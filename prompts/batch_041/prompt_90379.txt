Draft:

Everyone says culture evolves, and everyone reaches for Darwin. But its doing two different jobs in that sentence and people slide between them. Sometimes Darwinian just means changes over time and the changes stick, which is drift-talk and nearly vacuous. Sometimes it means there's a population of replicators, copying is faithful enough that lineages exist, and selection sorts those lineages. The meme is supposed to be the second thing. I want to argue that for most of what we call culture it's actually the first, and that the genuinely Darwinian part is much smaller and much stranger than either the meme's fans or its critics admit.

Here's the move. A cultural item reaches a new host either because the host reconstructs it or because the host copies it. Reconstruction is cheap and doesn't need lineages: give someone a hint of a tune, a rumour, a recipe, and their own priors will fill in the rest, and what comes out the other end is pulled toward whatever those priors already favour. Copying is expensive, because it needs a host willing and able to suppress their own priors and reproduce the item as given, warts and all.

Call the first kind a derivable item and the second a costly one. Derivability is a subsidy. A derivable item pays almost nothing to get transmitted, because the host's existing mental furniture does the carrying. But the subsidy comes with a catch: an item that's cheap because it's derivable is also cheap to alter, since whatever the host's priors push toward is, by definition, nearby. So derivable items spread like wildfire and never stay still. Costly items barely spread at all, but when they do they keep their shape, which is exactly what you need for a lineage, and therefore for selection to have anything to bite on.

That gives a clean split. Derivable items are not replicators. They're more like equilibrium points, stable features of the space of human priors, re-derived afresh by every host; their distribution tracks the shape of that space rather than the history of copies. Costly items are replicators, but they're fragile and only persist where something holds them in place: rote learning, ritual, institutions, professional gatekeepers, the threat of looking like an idiot. Which means the Darwinian story, properly told, isn't a story about culture in general. It's a story about the narrow band of culture that's expensive enough to need enforcing and valuable enough to be worth enforcing, liturgies, legal formulas, mnemonics, technical notation, the exact wording of a pledge.

Two consequences I like. First, the usual complaint that memetics can't explain why some things spread and others don't is aimed at the wrong target; for derivable items there's nothing to explain, because their spread is overdetermined by priors, and the interesting question is why the priors have the shape they do. Second, the interesting evolutionary question about costly items isn't why they spread but who pays the enforcement cost and why, which is a question about power rather than about fitness.

That's the gist. Is the derivable/costly split doing any work, or is it just the old copy-versus-reconstruct point in a new hat? And have I got the direction of the argument right, or should I be more concessive to the people who think culture really is a Darwinian process all the way down?
