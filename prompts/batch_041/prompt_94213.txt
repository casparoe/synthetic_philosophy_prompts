For the past two years I have lived in a country whose elected government was displaced from within — not by a foreign invasion, but by the man who then commanded its security services. He governs now by decree; the press is quiet, the courts do as they are told, and people I have known all my life have learned to talk in the street as though the walls had ears. My younger brother, who was a student organizer before all this, has fallen in with a small group that has concluded the only remedy left is to kill the man. They meet at the back of a bookshop and speak of it as an operation rather than a killing, which I find almost more troubling than the plan itself.

I am not asking you to settle an abstraction. I have to decide what to do, and every argument I might use has already been claimed by someone in that room. One of them maintains that because the ruler holds power without any lawful title whatsoever, killing him is not murder at all — that the ordinary prohibition simply does not reach a man who is, on this view, an occupier rather than a magistrate. Another, older, who has read a good deal of old writing about politics and the duties of rulers, insists that the group is collapsing two cases that the tradition he trusts keeps firmly apart: the ruler who has no claim to the office at all, and the ruler whose claim is perfectly sound but who governs like a beast. He says the two are not treated alike. He says further that even where the killing could be justified in principle, it is not for private persons to undertake it, but for whoever holds public authority, or for someone acting for the community rather than for himself. And he adds a third point that the students brush aside: that a ruler killed in this way is usually replaced by a worse one, which is why the same tradition has often counselled patience with a tyranny that is at least endurable.

I can see the force of each of these and cannot make them fit together. If the man has no title at all, does the question of who may act against him even arise, or does it simply drop out? If it does arise, how much turns on the fact that this group is not the public authority but a handful of people who have appointed themselves to speak for the country? And is the argument about what comes next a reason why the act would be wrong, or only a reason to expect that it will fail — and does the difference matter to what I may do? The students also say that the old man's whole framework was built for kingdoms and has nothing to say to a country like ours, and I do not know how to answer them.

I would like you to sort this out rather than reassure me: to say which of these arguments bear on what I am permitted to do and which are being asked to do work they cannot do, to identify what is really being disagreed about beneath the rhetoric, and to tell me what the most defensible course is — for the group, for my brother, and for me, given that I may be able to stop it by speaking to someone.
