I'm taking a seminar on Gödel's theorems and the philosophy of mind, and the final assignment is an open-topic essay. I've settled on a defence of a restricted version of the Lucas–Penrose argument, but the outline below contains three moves that I suspect do very different amounts of work, and I would rather find that out now than after twelve pages. Please comment on the outline.

Where I begin. Let S be an effectively axiomatized, consistent system strong enough for arithmetic, and let G_S be its Gödel sentence, unprovable in S and true if S is consistent. Lucas's claim is that a human mathematician can see that G_S is true, so no such S is the mathematician's capacity.

Move 1: the mechanist needs a standpoint he cannot occupy. The usual objection is that seeing the truth of G_S just is knowing that S is consistent — the Gödel sentence is provably equivalent to the consistency statement over elementary arithmetic — and no consistent system proves its own consistency, so the mathematician's alleged insight either lacks warrant or outruns her knowledge. My reply is that the mechanist is not offering S as a mere sentence generator; he is offering it as the mechanism of mathematical knowledge. Knowledge is factive, so the mechanist is committed to the soundness of S, and soundness is strictly stronger than consistency. But the soundness of S is not provable in S, and a theory asserting it stands outside S. So the mechanist's own position requires a standpoint external to the machine — precisely the standpoint Lucas claims for the mathematician. Either he asserts S's soundness from outside, conceding that such a standpoint is available and needed, or he declines to, in which case he has no reason to regard the machine's outputs as knowledge at all.

Move 2: the choosing capacity. The obvious counter is that this is self-defeating, since a mathematician who derives G_S from the soundness of S is reasoning in a system stronger than S, and that stronger system is itself a machine: add the consistency statement as an axiom and continue. The argument then shows at most that for any system there is a stronger one. Here I want to appeal to Turing's ordinal logics: a machine can be programmed to iterate consistency axioms along a sequence of ordinal notations, but how a given system is to be extended at each stage is not itself dictated mechanically, and it is this choosing capacity, rather than any particular system, that the anti-mechanist identifies with the mathematician's mind. The mechanist then owes an argument that the capacity is effective: if it is, it is a machine and Gödel applies to it too; if it is not, mechanism fails.

Move 3, which I take to be the weakest part. For the argument to deliver the human is not S, rather than the human is not S for each S, the insight must be uniform — one and the same capacity, exercised on any system presented to it. But uniformity seems to require an effective procedure for identifying the system and generating the sentence, which is exactly what move 2 denies. There is a harder version of the worry: the premise that the human can see the truth of G_S is a claim no actual mathematician can make good on for a system like ZFC, whose consistency nobody knows. If the premise holds only for toy systems, the argument looks like a claim about an idealized mathematician, and idealizations are the entities a mechanist is most willing to call machines.

What I would like from you: (1) whether the technical claims are right, especially the equivalence of the Gödel sentence with the consistency statement, and whether it is soundness rather than consistency that the mechanist's commitment to knowledge actually delivers; (2) whether move 1 escapes the consistency objection or merely relocates it; (3) whether the non-effective choosing capacity in move 2 is a coherent posit or a renaming of the mystery, and what it commits me to; (4) if you were defending the mechanist, which reply does the most damage, and is there a repair available to me; (5) whether move 3 is as damaging as I fear or whether I should be less concessive. I am not asking for reading suggestions — I need to know what is wrong with the argument.
