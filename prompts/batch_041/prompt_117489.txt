I need an in-class activity for a masters module I teach (logic and computation, 16 students, one 80-minute slot, room with movable tables), and I'm sick of explaining the Löbian obstacle off slides while everyone nods politely and nothing actually lands. I want them to walk into it with their own feet and only get the name afterwards.

Here's what I want them to run into, spelled out plainly so you know what to aim at. Take an agent whose rule is: I do the risky thing only if I can *prove* the risky thing is safe. The agent is well built, its rules are impeccable, and the bridge really is safe. The catch is that "my proofs are true" — the soundness of its own reasoning — is something it can only see from outside. It can't pick that fact up and use it as a premise, no matter how obviously true it is. And there's a sharper form of this (Löb): the conditional dodge doesn't help either. If the agent could prove "if I can prove P then P", it could already prove P — so the loophole that looks like it hands you self-trust is exactly as hard as the thing you couldn't do in the first place. That wall is the whole point, and I want it staged rather than asserted.

Three things I want them to feel, and I care about the order: (a) they can *see* the truth of the safety claim and can't get it into the system; (b) their reasoning is flawless and it buys them nothing; (c) the obvious escape hatch is a trap, and they should find that out by burning fifteen minutes on it rather than by being told.

My rough sketch, which you should feel free to throw out if you've got something better: they're split into small agencies, each agency may send a drone over a bridge only while holding a signed safety warrant, and a separate team of students plays the Warrant Office, which issues warrants strictly on presentation of a derivation from a fixed deck of premise cards. Soundness of the Office is something everyone in the room can see is true and nobody is allowed to write down. Add an Office rule that appears to license trusting your own certificates, so they chase it. Then open the envelope at the end and let them watch a safe bridge go unused. If you've got a cleaner mechanism — cards, a betting market, a referee game, a two-team delegation loop, whatever — take it, as long as the wall shows up in the rules and not in my debrief.

Constraints: no lecture up front, no naming the theorem, the modality, or any of the literature until the debrief. Everything must run with paper, pens and maybe index cards; no tech, no real money. It has to work for students who have done a first course in logic but have never seen provability logic.
