I'm a second-year PhD student in philosophy, working mostly on spacetime, and I have to turn a private obsession into a term-paper prospectus for a seminar on the philosophy of physics: about 6,000 words, one semester, and — this matters — a project that stays conceptual. I've had one semester of general relativity, enough to read a line element and to follow what a closed timelike curve is, but not enough to enjoy a project that turns on my deriving anything.

The obsession is Gödel's rotating cosmological solution of 1949 and the philosophical conclusion he drew from it: a universe of swirling dust, homogeneous and singularity-free, in which there is no global time order and in which some worldlines return to their own past — and from the possibility of such a universe he concluded that time, or at any rate the objective lapse of time, is ideal. Two things about the shape of the argument rather than its physics are what I can't let go of. First, the inference appears to run from the mere existence of a model of the theory with a certain temporal structure to a claim about the temporal structure of the actual world, and I suspect that if I could say exactly which modal premise it needs — consistency with the field equations, physical possibility, metaphysical possibility, or merely our present inability to rule it out — I could then say which of his critics has a real objection and what that objection amounts to. Second, I suspect the physics is doing less work than the rhetoric implies, and that what actually drives the conclusion is an unstated conception of what objective time would have to be: something like the requirement that reality consist of a succession of universal present moments, or that temporal order be globally definable and observer-independent. If that is right, I want to know whether the rotating universe does any work that the relativity of simultaneity could not have done by itself, and whether the same inference applied to other structures in a spacetime — the division of the manifold into space and time, the metrical structure, the asymmetry between the two temporal directions — would deliver conclusions nobody wants. If it would, that tells me something about the argument form rather than about time.

So: write the prospectus, two pages, with a month-by-month plan. I want the question stated precisely enough to have a determinate answer; an account of why the obvious positions on it leave it open, given in terms of arguments rather than citations — I'll put in the references myself, so no bibliography and no reading list; the method, meaning the order in which I would actually do the work, including what I would have to reconstruct by hand (the argument's premises, the general form of inference I suspect it belongs to, and the test cases I would run that form against, such as the hole argument about spacetime substantivalism or the conventionality of simultaneity); what a good outcome would look like and what would count as the project failing; and an honest assessment of scope, including whether this is really one project or whether the honest deliverable is a taxonomy of possibility-to-actuality arguments in the philosophy of physics with Gödel's as the leading case. If it is relevant, note the parallel with his other famous modal argument, the ontological one, which also moves from possibility to actuality, and tell me whether the parallel is a clue or a red herring. And if the thing that makes the project workable is also the thing that makes it thin, say so rather than dressing it up.
