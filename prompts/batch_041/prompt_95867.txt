I have no training in philosophy, but a problem I came across years ago has stayed with me, and I would like to see its two sides made to produce the document they should have produced long ago. The problem is the familiar one: six people need a drug; the whole dose will save one of them, the same dose divided will save the other five, and you cannot do both. Call the first camp the counters: five deaths are a greater loss than one, that loss belongs to nobody in particular but is real all the same, and so you should save the five; saving the one instead, when the five were there to be saved, is a serious mistake. Call the second camp the separatists: nobody dies five times; there are five people with five claims and one person with one claim, none of the claims stronger than the others, and the size of a group is not a reason any of its members can offer on their own behalf; so saving the one is not obviously a mistake, and if what you want is a fair procedure, a draw is at least as defensible as a headcount. (I believe the second camp is named after a philosopher called Taurek, whose paper I have never read — I know the argument only by hearsay, so please do not lean on the literature.)

Two things have kept this unresolved in my own head, and I suspect each camp answers only its own version of them. The first is the arithmetic. When I ask a counter why five deaths are worse than one, I am told that more people die, which is the very thing I asked about. When I ask a separatist why a draw is fair, I notice that under the draw each of the five carries a one-in-two chance of dying where one of the certain options would have left all five alive, and the single person carries a one-in-two chance of dying where the other certain option would have saved him — so nobody, looking at his own case alone, would prefer the draw to the option that saves him, even though no single certain option is better for all six. The second is what the choice is between. Is it about what is permitted, about what is required, or about what a decent person would do? Each camp seems to answer one of those and then reply to the other's example as though it were about its own.

I also want them to say what they make of cases the standard one does not reach, because I suspect the disagreement looks different depending on them:

- the one person I could save is the only person who could later save the five, so the choice that looks like counting is also the choice that saves the one;
- the five each stand to lose a limb while the one stands to lose his sight, so that counting and severity pull in different directions;
- the one is my child, or the five are, or I am one of the six;
- the choice is not a rescue at all but the allocation of a scarce medicine, where different hospitals and different countries seem to settle the same question by different rules.

Please write the joint statement the two camps could sign. I want: first, a numbered list of claims both would accept, about the base case, about what is being compared and by whom, and about each of the four cases above, with nothing so weak that it survives by saying nothing; second, a table of the disagreements that remain — the question at issue; each camp's own answer in wording that camp would sign; the sub-question that camp thinks the other must answer before it would move (the crux); and, for each crux, something both accept bears on it, whether an argument, a case, or a comparison, with each camp's prediction about how it comes out recorded in advance; and third, a closing note on which parts of the quarrel are about words rather than substance, if any, and why.

I cannot look anything up, and neither can you. Do not give me citations, article titles, or reading lists, and do not tell me what has been written on this. Where a claim is a matter of definition rather than of fact, say so plainly rather than dressing it up.

No more than 800 words, table included.
