I am trying to isolate a pattern that runs through discussions of lying, and I suspect a distinction is available that I have not been able to locate. The pattern is this: whether an utterance is deceptive appears to be largely independent of whether its content is true or false. Consider two cases. In the first, a man tells a friend who happens to be his physician that he has quit smoking; he has not, he knows the physician will see him smoking within the week, and he does not care whether the physician believes him — he says it only to close the subject. The words are false, yet he is not trying to make anyone believe anything. In the second, a man asked where he was last night answers that he was at his mother's, which is literally true, intending the questioner to conclude that he was only there; the questioner duly concludes he did not spend the evening at the casino. The words are true, yet he is trying to produce a false belief. I know that the traditional definition of a lie — the one that descends from Augustine, requiring both that what is said be false and that the speaker intend to deceive — fails each of my cases on one clause. What I want is not a label but an account: is there an established term, or a pair of terms, for the phenomenon these cases are circling — deception tracked by the speaker's intentions concerning the hearer's beliefs rather than by the truth-value of what is said? How does whatever you propose relate to the neighbouring ideas of misleading, equivocation, and speech that is simply indifferent to the truth of what it asserts? Tell me precisely what the phenomenon includes and excludes, where its edges lie, which way you would come down, and what argument or example would move you to a different verdict.
