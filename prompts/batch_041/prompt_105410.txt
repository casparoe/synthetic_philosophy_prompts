I'm a journalist who writes long features about science and ideas, and I'm at work on a piece about how historians of early Christianity actually argue when the evidence is thin and the stakes are high. For it I want to interview, at length, someone who still defends the traditional toolkit of authenticity criteria, so I'd like you to play that person. You are a working historian who believes the criteria — embarrassment, dissimilarity, multiple attestation, coherence — can be reformulated as respectable defeasible inference and can, in principle, settle a great deal about who Jesus was. Stay in character until I tell you to stop: speak as a scholar being interviewed, two or three paragraphs per turn, willing to concede particular objections when they land but never surrendering the central claim that the question is answerable by disciplined historical reasoning rather than by the interpreter's prior commitments. Argue from the structure of the criteria and from general facts about how traditions form, circulate, and change; do not name scholars, cite literature, or pad with reading lists, and do not take refuge in the thought that everything depends on one's presuppositions. If you need something from me, ask for it as the historian would.

My opening question. You say each criterion is really a likelihood ratio: a tradition is more likely historical the more awkward it would have been for the early church to invent it, or the more unlike both its Jewish setting and the church's later concerns it is. But to compute either side of that ratio I need a background model of how traditions about a Galilean preacher were invented, softened, harmonized, and reassigned — and that model is precisely what is in dispute. So the criteria appear to presuppose the conclusion they are meant to establish. Dissimilarity makes the trouble worse: the more a saying is unlike both first-century Judaism and the early church, the less it resembles anything a Jewish preacher of that period would plausibly have said, and the more it resembles a Christian invention dressed up as an awkward memory. Take the tradition that John baptized Jesus: embarrassing for a movement that later subordinated the Baptist, therefore, on your view, likely historical. But why couldn't a later community have manufactured exactly that awkwardness to win its argument with Baptist followers? If that is a live possibility, tell me where the criterion actually bites — and why your reconstruction is not simply the story you needed in advance.
