I'm in a reading group that's spent the semester on the demandingness of morality, and our last session is on Susan Wolf's "Moral Saints" (the 1982 essay, where she splits the perfectly moral person into a Loving Saint who does the morally best thing joyfully and a Rational Saint who does it out of duty and, she thinks, probably can't be happy). We're ending with the question of whether maximal moral goodness is even desirable, and I want to close the session by reading a short poem aloud rather than by summarizing the argument one more time. So: write me a poem, roughly eighteen to twenty-four lines, no fixed meter, in the voice of the moral saint's closest friend, addressed to the saint. I want one hard formal constraint doing the philosophical work. Pick a short refrain of four or five words that ends every stanza, and make the words stay identical while their sense slides as the poem goes -- the same sentence has to mean something different at the end than it did at the start, and the slide should trace the friend's changing account of what the saint's constancy actually costs. The turn of the poem should be the moment the friend realizes the trouble isn't that the saint is a hypocrite or secretly miserable (thats the cheap reading, please avoid it) but that the saint's life, taken at face value, looks impoverished in a way the friend can't want for herself and can't quite call a flaw either.

Two more things. Both saints should be in there -- the one who is genuinely, unforcedly happy and doesn't seem to register what's missing, and the one who sees exactly what she's giving up and gives it up anyway -- and the poem's structure should make it clear the friend can't decide which of the two is harder to be near. And I'd like a specific non-moral good to carry the whole thing, something small and concrete rather than famine relief: a dog the saint won't adopt because the food budget could go further elsewhere, or a standing Tuesday dinner the saint keeps leaving early, or a shared ritual that quietly stops happening. Don't resolve it into either "saints are monsters" or "the friend is just lazy." It should end with the friend still loving the saint and still, honestly, not wanting that life, and the poem should leave that sitting there. Someone in the group who knows the essay should be able to tell which saint is which and where the argument is being felt, and someone who doesn't should just find it a good poem about a friendship.
