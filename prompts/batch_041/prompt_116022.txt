I'm an undergraduate in a seminar on human origins and the deep history of inequality — eighteen students, one seventy-five-minute meeting, movable chairs, a whiteboard, and a projector — and I've been handed one class session on the question of whether the shift from foraging to farming was a mistake. I would rather run something than talk at them. Everyone in the room can already rehearse the two sides in the abstract: the picture of foragers as affluent, well fed, and lightly worked, associated with Marshall Sahlins, versus the argument that the Neolithic was a catastrophe for human welfare. None of that abstract knowledge has changed anyone's mind about anything, because the real difficulty in this debate is not in the positions but in what it takes to compare two ways of life at all. Please design the session as a game.

What I'm after is an activity in which the class, divided into households or bands, plays through a sequence of rounds with a scoreboard and runs into the trade-off in their own numbers rather than hearing about it. Build in a two-currency scoreboard — something like calories and free hours — so that total output and welfare can visibly move in opposite directions; a storage rule so that some households can carry food across rounds; a rule that lets stored surplus be converted into other people's labor; a rule about guarding or losing what is stored, so that protection becomes worth paying for; an inheritance rule; and, most important to me, a population rule that ratchets, so that once the group crosses a threshold the foraging strategy becomes unavailable and no one can go back. The exact numbers should be yours, chosen so the system neither collapses in round two nor has a trivially dominant strategy, and the rules and materials should be arranged so that nothing on the table names the debate we are having — the vocabulary of the argument should not appear until the debrief. Give me the full setup: roles or teams, materials I can actually assemble, the round structure, a payoff scheme that uses no real money, a timeline broken down by minutes, and a one-page facilitator sheet with the arithmetic precomputed so I can run it without fumbling.

Then the debrief, which is the part I care about most. Map specific moments in the game onto specific moves in the debate: which mechanic stands in for the counterfactual problem, that comparing the two ways of life requires a baseline nobody ever occupied; which stands in for the ratchet, that the transition may have been no one's choice and no one's to reverse; which stands in for the claim that inequality comes from storable and heritable surplus rather than from farming as such; and which stands in for the objection that the forager-affluence picture leans on modern foragers and on a contested reading of the archaeological record. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle each failure — I expect at least one group to optimize for the scoreboard and treat the metrics as the thing itself, and I want to know how to turn that into the discussion instead of letting it derail the class. Finally, be frank about what the activity cannot reproduce and that I will have to supply myself, and include two or three pieces of archaeological or ethnographic evidence that complicate the simple version of the story; since you cannot check sources, mark how confident you are in each rather than offering citations, and do not give me titles or references at all. Please write everything in American English.
