I am a doctoral student in formal epistemology, and I have been trying for some months to discipline a phrase that circulates in my field with what I can only call promiscuous confidence. In a reading group on the value of information, a visiting speaker brushed aside a colleague's objection — that acquiring a piece of evidence might leave a decision maker worse off — with the flat assurance that information can only help. I have since heard near-variants of the same line ("more information never hurts," "you can't be worse off knowing") used to foreclose worries about evidence-gathering in contexts ranging from experimental design to clinical disclosure, and I have come to suspect the sentence is doing several incompatible jobs at once.

What I want is a disambiguation of the slogan: an ordered menu of the precise claims it could be making, running from the nearly trivial to the genuinely radical, with a verdict on each. For every reading, state what exactly is being asserted, what it presupposes about the decision maker — whether she is Bayesian, whether she maximizes expected utility, whether the information is free or costly, whether she is choosing for herself or advising someone else, whether the setting is individual or strategic — and what is being valued: expected utility, accuracy of belief, welfare, or the mere possession of truth. I want the readings sorted so that I can see which are tautologies dressed as discoveries, which are substantive and defensible, which are false, and which are true only under conditions the slogan quietly suppresses. Two distinctions matter especially to me and should structure the menu. First, keep apart the value of information computed in advance of receiving it from the value it actually delivers once received; a claim that is true of the first is not thereby true of the second. Second, keep apart the decision-theoretic sense of information — an experiment, a signal, a sample — from the epistemic sense, a body of evidence bearing on a hypothesis.

Then tell me which of these readings the colloquium remark most plausibly traded on, and which of them, if any, would actually license dismissing the objection that new evidence can hurt. I have a nagging sense that the slogan is true in something close to the sense Good's theorem makes it true and false in most of the senses in which it is actually deployed, but I want the menu laid out rather than that single verdict, because the point of collecting the readings is to identify which one the argument in the room needed. Close with a short account of where ordinary use of the slogan has drifted furthest from the theorem, and say which drift is the most damaging.
