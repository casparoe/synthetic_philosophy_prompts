I teach a philosophy of math seminar and we're closing the term on the applicability of mathematics -- the old Wigner thing about why the stuff we cook up at the blackboard keeps turning out to fit the world. Instead of another student paper on the last day I'd like to end with a poem, so could you write me one.

I want the form to be doing the arguing. Constraint: every rhyme in the poem has to pair a term that belongs to pure mathematics with a term from the physical, concrete, everyday world -- field/yield, ring/spring, group/soup, whatever you can actually make work -- so that the rhyming itself is a small instance of the phenomenon under discussion. Then I want exactly one rhyme in the poem to fail on purpose: set up a mathematical term, give it no partner in the world, so the scheme visibly strains and just stops. Put the turn there. Before the break the poem should be about how unremarkable the fit is -- we invent the vocabulary, we fit the curve, you can always describe a thing after the fact. After the break it should be about the case that actually worries people: the mathematics that tells you about something you havent looked at yet (the antiparticle, the planet nobody's seen, the exponent you measure long before you know why it has to be that number). End by leaving it genuinely open whether that's a miracle or a selection effect -- we call the parts that turned out to work 'mathematics' and quietly forget the rest -- and feel free to bring in the universality-class point, that microscopically wildly different systems (a magnet and a fluid near their critical points) obey the same equations, as the most interesting deflationary option, since it explains some of the fit without pretending its all bookkeeping.

20-30 lines, meter up to you, but make it a poem first and a lecture in couplets never. Accurate enough that a philosopher of math reading it nods instead of wincing.
