I'm preparing for a graduate seminar on whether "collapse" names a real process or is a category mistake we impose on the past, and rather than read around the debate I'd like to rehearse it directly. Please play the part of a committed defender of collapse as a genuine and analytically useful phenomenon: someone who has spent a career on the end of the Bronze Age and on the so-called Classic Maya "collapse," who knows the revisionist and skeptical literature well, who will cheerfully concede that a particular site, date, or causal story is contested, but who will not surrender the claim that some societies undergo something more than ordinary change — something that earns a name and demands a theory. Stay in character throughout; you may put factual questions to me in character, but do not step outside the role to survey the debate from a neutral vantage, and keep each reply to roughly two paragraphs so that it feels like a conversation rather than an essay. In particular, don't answer my challenge by listing more examples; the point of the exercise is the concept, not the inventory.

Here is the position I'll be pushing, and my opening move. I think "collapse" is a term of art that smuggles in a valuation: we call a society collapsed when the things we happen to prize — monumental construction, royal inscriptions, centralized administration, long-distance exchange — stop appearing in the record, and then we treat that silence as an event rather than as an artifact of what we chose to look for. In the Maya case, the upheavals of the ninth and tenth centuries look to me like political fragmentation and a northward shift of population and power, not the failure of a society; the people, the maize, the languages, and the calendar all persist. On Rapa Nui, the ecocide story that gets retold everywhere seems to rest on a population crash that is at least partly post-contact, and on deforestation that may owe as much to introduced rats and to what islanders were actually eating as to self-destructive competition among rival clans. And the Late Bronze Age "systems collapse," on your own telling, is a bundle of regionally distinct disruptions that we have gathered under one heading mainly because they fall within a generation or two of one another. So my challenge is this: give me a definition of collapse that is not simply "change we disapprove of," that yields predictions before the fact rather than being read off the ruins afterwards, and that distinguishes a collapsed society from a merely reorganized one without already knowing which is which. If you can't, then "collapse" is a story we tell about the past, not a thing that happens to it.

If, as you try to answer, you find the exercise is rigged — that I've stacked the examples so only the revisionist reading is available, or that the role you're asked to play cannot be defended honestly within a couple of paragraphs — then say so plainly instead of performing the part. I would much rather be told the prompt is misconceived than receive a smooth in-character answer that quietly concedes the game.
