I have no academic training in philosophy, but I have been chewing on the Liar paradox on my own, and I want to write a satire about the two standard ways people try to get out of it — the one that forbids any statement from talking about the truth of a statement at its own level, so that truth-talk is only ever allowed one rung higher, and the one that declares the troublesome self-referential sentences neither true nor false. I would like the piece to take the form of an invented city's official policy document, deadpan throughout.

The setup I have in mind: after a scandal in which officials kept making pronouncements about the truth of one another's pronouncements, the city adopts a Grading Code for all municipal statements. A statement is "grounded" if it can be traced down, through the statements it rests on, to a statement that simply describes the world — the pavement, the budget, the weather. A statement whose support runs in a circle is "idling." A statement that assigns a grade to a statement at its own level is "out of order" and may not be issued at all; to say anything about the grade of a statement one must speak from the next level up, on the prescribed form. An Office of Grounded Statements adjudicates disputes.

Write the office's public FAQ: numbered questions and answers, about 650 words, in the flat, self-assured register of a municipal handbook. The comedy has to come out of the logic rather than out of gags. The FAQ is itself issued by the office and is stuffed with statements about the grade and the truth of statements; a citizen who asks the office to grade the sentence "this statement is idling," or "this statement is not grounded," forces a verdict the Code cannot give consistently; and the office must never concede that any of its own rulings is neither grounded nor idling. Someone who knows the literature should recognize the hierarchy fix and the truth-value-gap fix as the targets and find the exaggeration fair; a reader who has never heard of the paradox should still be able to follow the absurdity. Nobody should wink at the reader, and no one in the piece should ever call the situation a paradox.
