I run a fortnightly reading group for philosophy postgrads — usually about ten of us, 90 minutes a session — and we've just spent three weeks on grounding and fundamentality. By the end they can all recite that grounding is irreflexive, asymmetric, transitive and (probably) well-founded, and not one of them can feel why well-foundedness is a live constraint rather than a bit of book-keeping. I want to fix that with an activity rather than another handout.

The shape I have in mind, though change whatever you need: I hand out a deck of cards, each with a fact written on it, and the rule is that every card must be laid down pointing at another card that grounds it. No card may point at itself, and no loops allowed. They build the chain in silence for ten minutes, then we look at it. Predictably they run out of cards at the bottom and someone has to be left with nothing pointing at them, which is the moment I want. Then the twist: I let them add exactly one "bedrock" card that needs no ground, and they have to argue for it against the people who'd rather keep going down forever. I'm hoping that argument gets heated.

What I'd like from you is the whole thing, runnable as written. In particular:

- Build me the deck. Don't just give me physics-onion cards ("the kettle boils because the molecules are moving because..."), because they'll do that on autopilot. I want it to force the issue across at least two domains at once, and I want at least one case where the grounding is social and the obvious answer loops back on itself. The one I keep coming back to is a made-up island, say Marrow, where shells are used as money: a shell is money because everyone treats it as money, and everyone treats it as money because it's money. Invent the rest yourself rather than leaning on anything famous — I'd rather they couldn't reach for a half-remembered paper.
- Rules and materials, a rough 90-minute timeline, and what I say (and don't say) at the start.
- A debrief that maps specific moments in the activity onto the parts of the idea: well-foundedness, the option of infinite descent, brute facts, why "it's just turtles" is and isn't a cheap answer, and how this differs from the regress of reasons in epistemology. That last distinction matters a lot to me — I'm worried the whole thing will slide into being about justification, because honestly that's what the activity is made of.
- What will definately go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle it. My guess is they'll smuggle in causation and start talking about time, and that someone will nominate the Big Bang as bedrock and derail us into cosmology, but you'll probably spot other failure modes.
- An honest note on what this activity cannot get across and I'll have to supply myself. Don't oversell it.

Write it in British English please, and no reading lists or citations — half of them are working without library access this term anyway.
