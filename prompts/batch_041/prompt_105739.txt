The phrase is: it's not what you said, it's what you meant. I first ran into it properly when a mate and I were arguing about a company's advert — every sentence in it was literally true, and I pointed that out, and he came back with "it's not what you said, it's what you meant, and they meant to decieve". Fair enough, I thought. But then I heard the exact same sentance used in a totally different way in a reading group, where someone wanted to insist that some nasty bit of a word isn't really part of its meaning at all, it's "just" something the speaker meant — and the slogan got trotted out as if it settled that too. And a third time, in an argument about whether a witness who says something true but misleading has perjured himself.

These uses can't all be pulling in the same direction. If what matters is always what was meant, then what the words mean barely matters; but if what's meant is just a further fact about the speaker's intentions, then the slogan sounds almost trivial. I've got a vague sense this is all Grice territory — something about what's said versus what's implicated, and about implicatures being cancellable — and there's also some maxim about not multiplying senses beyond necessity that seems to pull the other way, but I couldn't tell you how they fit together.

Can you lay out the different precise things the slogan could actually be claiming, from the boring ones to the wild ones, and for each say whether it's a platitude, something substantive and defensible, or just false? I want a menu, not a single answer. And please say which of the readings is doing the real work in each of the three cases above — the advert, the slur, the witness — because I suspect the slogan only gets its punch from one specific reading and the rest is padding.

While you're at it, tell me where it's likely to mislead me: is there a reading on which it's trivially true but useless, and another where it's useful but false? And does the don't-multiply-senses thing genuinely conflict with it, or are the two just talking past each other?

Write it in British English please. No need for reading lists or citations — I just want the ideas sorted out.
