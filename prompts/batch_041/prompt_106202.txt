I'm writing a chapter defending futarchy, and my supervisor keeps offering the same argument: the case for letting prediction markets choose policy is just the socialist calculation argument run one level up. Central planning fails because the knowledge required to allocate resources is dispersed and largely tacit, and the price system solves the problem by collapsing it into a number that nobody has to understand; by the same token, no legislature can know the consequences of its policies while conditional markets can, so the state should let those prices decide. I want an audit of how far the analogy actually holds, not a restatement of it. Set out the correspondence explicitly -- what plays the role of the price, of the dispersed knowledge, of profit and loss, of the planner -- then say where the mapping breaks in ways that bear on the conclusion. I suspect two things matter. In the source argument a price is both a signal and the terms on which goods actually change hands, whereas the futarchy price is a pure signal whose causal power is manufactured by the decision rule. And the welfare metric the markets are meant to maximise has to be fixed by somebody, which looks like exactly the central plan the analogy was invoked to abolish. I'm also unsure whether the analogy supports the strong claim at all, or only the weaker one that markets should inform policy, and whether the reflexivity of a market that determines the outcome it is pricing is a fatal disanalogy or a manageable design problem. Tell me what survives, and what a better analogy for the surviving claim would be.
