ok so I've been messing around with an idea for an essay and I want a second oppinion before I sink more time into it. The topic is whether an ant colony can have a mind — you know, the old thought experiment where somehow you can sit down and have a chat with a whole colony. I don't want to just rehash that, so I've tried to push it somewhere a bit less trodden. Here's the draft, roughly:

The claim I want to defend is that a colony can have beliefs and desires in the full-blooded sense but can't have experiences. A mind with no inside.

Start with why I think the intentional states are real. A colony finds sugar to the east and over the next hour more and more foragers head east. Move the sugar and it keeps sending foragers to the old spot for a while, then corrects. Block a path and it reroutes within minutes, faster than any individual ant could have learned the layout. The best description of all this attributes content: the colony thinks the food is over there, it remembers the route, it's surprised when the food isn't where it left it. And crucially none of this is true of any single ant. No forager knows the route; the route lives in the pheromone gradients and in the statistics of who follows whom. So if you're willing to say a thermostat believes anything (and you shouldn't), fine — but the colony is a far better candidate than any of its parts. The unit that carries the content is the colony.

Now the swap test, which is where I think it gets interesting. Replace the ants one at a time with fresh identical ants and the colony's behaviour — its routes, its habits, its little quirks — all persists, the way your behaviour persists as your neurons turn over. So whatever the colony mind is, it isn't the ants. Good, that's just substrate independence, nothing new.

But now split the colony in two. Put a wall down the middle, or just move half the ants a mile away. Each half keeps doing something, and here's my claim: neither half is the original mind, and there's no fact of the matter about which half got it, because the mind was never located anywhere to begin with. Compare a split brain: we agonize over whether there are two minds or one, but we assume there's a fact of the matter. With the colony I want to say there isn't, and that this isn't a defect of the colony — it's the norm for anything whose mental states are spread across a population rather than gathered at a point.

And that, I claim, is exactly why the colony has no experiences. Experience, whatever else it is, is something that is had from somewhere — it comes with a point of view that can't be divided without a remainder. Beliefs can be diffuse and distributed; experiences can't. So the colony can think about the sugar and even, in a stretched sense, want it, but there's nothing it's like to be the colony.

Two things I'm not sure about. (1) Is the split argument doing any real work, or have I just defined experience as the thing that doesn't split and then patted myself on the back? (2) Is this genuinely diffrent from the standard easy/hard problem distinction, or am I just relabelling it with ants?

So what I want from you: poke holes. Where does the argument actually break? Is the split move legit or is it circular? And is any of this interesting to anyone who isn't already in the tank for this stuff, or is it just a cute reframing that collapses the moment you press on it? Don't be nice about it.
