I am putting together a single ninety-minute session for a second-year undergraduate seminar in the philosophy of science: fifteen students, a term of elementary logic behind them, no history of science, and little patience for being lectured at. The topic is the pessimistic meta-induction. I have taught it twice and both times the session died early: the students could reconstruct the argument within ten minutes, agreed with it, and spent the remaining eighty elaborating that agreement, because nothing in the session gave them a reason to feel the pull of the other side. What I want from you, therefore, is not an exposition of the argument but a plan for teaching it — the order in which the components must be introduced and what depends on what, the case that carries the whole session, the misconceptions to expect and where each must be met, and how I will know at the end whether the idea has landed or has merely been recited back to me.

Three constraints on the plan.

First, the session must turn on the distinction between those elements of a superseded theory that did predictive work and those that were merely carried along with it, and the students must reach that distinction by using a device of my own rather than by being told about it. The device is a two-column worksheet: down one side, the posits of an abandoned theory; the students sort them into those that were load-bearing for the theory's successful predictions and those that could have been exchanged for something else without disturbing the predictions, and then classify each load-bearing posit as retained by the successor theory, replaced by something structurally analogous, or simply dropped. I am not confident the worksheet is sound. I suspect it imports hindsight at the sorting stage and makes the outcome look tidier than it is. Tell me where it will mislead them, when in the session that will happen, and what I should say at that moment.

Second, the students' own resistance should be the engine of the session. They will insist that present theories are different in kind from the ones that fell, and I do not want that objection smoothed away or answered on their behalf; I want the plan to make them work through it themselves.

Third, the session has to reach the self-undermining worry — that the argument's historical premise is itself a claim we accept on the authority of the science it is used to impugn — and the students have to come away seeing it as a substantive objection rather than a debating trick.

I would also rather that the first half of the session never use the word realism at all. If you think that is unworkable, say so and tell me what you would put in its place.

Give me:

1. The order of the components, step by step, with a note at each step on what the students must already accept for the next one to be intelligible, and the single sentence that marks the pivot of the session. If the plan needs more than ninety minutes, tell me what to cut and what the cut costs.
2. The one case that should carry the session, with an account of what it must have — a posit that was indispensable to a prediction that still stands, and a successor theory that cannot simply be read off it — and what it must lack. If the obvious candidate is the wrong one, say so and give me the right one.
3. The misconceptions: those the students will arrive with and those they will form during the session, each with the point in the sequence where it should be headed off — and, separately, any misconception that cannot be headed off, which I should allow to surface and then leave standing.
4. A closing question that separates a student who has understood why the argument is worrying from one who has merely been persuaded by it.
5. What I should openly refuse to cover at this level, and the reason I can give the class for refusing.

No readings and no citations: the plan should stand on the ideas rather than on who held them.
