I am a novelist, and three weeks from now I am to give the speech at the dedication of a public sculpture in the river town where I grew up. It stands on the high side of the levee, looking across at the district the water took at three in the morning on a night in the spring I was thirteen. Eleven people drowned, one of them a girl I knew. The sculptor is a woman from another country who had never spent a night in that town; she was chosen on the strength of photographs and three days of interviews with people who were there, and she has made a large thing out of what they told her. I have seen the maquette, and a dozen photographs of the foundry. I have not seen the finished work, and I will not see it until the moment I am standing beside it with the sheet still over it, speaking to the mayor, the donors, the families of the eleven, two critics down from the capital who will write about the work and, incidentally, about me, and the sculptor herself, who is frightened and does not know that the town has never quite forgiven her for being given the commission.

I have written the speech four times and thrown it away four times, and I think I know why. Every version wants to say that the work is beautiful, and that it is true, and behind every version I hear a small objection that philosophers have made a career of, which they call the problem of aesthetic testimony. Put plainly: if a friend tells me the river broke the levee, then I know the river broke the levee, and testimony has done its ordinary work. If a friend tells me that a painting is magnificent, I have learned something about my friend and nothing whatever about the painting. Whatever makes a work worth standing in front of does not cross the gap between persons; it has to be undergone, and undergone by me. And yet we read criticism, we take recommendations, we let the taste of people we love decide where to spend a free afternoon, and some of the time that seems like the most reasonable thing in the world. So the real question is what testimony can honestly carry, and what it only appears to carry.

My difficulty is doubled, because I stand at both ends of it. The sculptor made her work out of what she was told, which is exactly what I am being asked to do. And I hold the one thing she lacks: I was there. But the night itself is no more transferable than the sculpture's beauty. I can tell her what happened, and I cannot give her what it was like, and she cannot check what she has made against my memory the way one checks a sum. Neither of us can certify the other. What I will not do is pretend. I will not stand up and pronounce on a thing I have not seen, and I will not let having been there pass for authority over how it ought to be remembered; the families would hear the pretense instantly, and they would be right to.

So I want the speech itself. It has to be honest about what I can and cannot know, warm to a room that is mixed and a little suspicious, and useful to people who lost someone without making use of them. It should leave the sculptor her work. It should find what a person actually can do from that position, which I suspect is closer to handing on the act of looking than to handing on a verdict, though I have never managed to write the sentence and would rather you found your own. Eight minutes or so, a thousand words. Do not quote anyone, do not invent quotations, and do not cite or name critics, scholars, or real people; anything that needs saying, say it in my voice. And do not write an essay about any of this: I want the difficulty present the way a skeleton is present, not mentioned. Give me the speech as continuous prose — no headings, no numbered points, no bullets, no bold, no tables; paragraphs only, the way one writes a letter.
