I'm a science journalist who writes about the life sciences and the ideas that shape them, and I've been asked to run a session at a week-long summer institute for graduate students in bioethics and philosophy of medicine — eighteen participants, ninety minutes, no assigned reading beforehand, and a room I have to fill on my own. I'm at work on a long feature about where the line between disease and ordinary human variation actually gets drawn, and what I keep noticing in my reporting is that the line moves for reasons that have nothing to do with the biology: the same measurements, the same mechanism, a different verdict, depending on who is being measured and who bears the cost of the label. I want the session to make the participants feel that, rather than hear me assert it. Could you design the activity for me — rules, materials, roles, a timeline, a scoring scheme that doesn't require money, and a debrief?

Here is what I have in mind, though I would rather you improve on it than follow it. The participants are a mock revision committee for an invented international classification of human functioning — invented so that nobody has to sit in a room classifying a real group of people, and so that no one can win by recalling how the real debates went. Twenty-four case cards, eight per subcommittee, with three subcommittees of six, each card carrying four things: a biological profile with concrete numbers (prevalence, a measurable parameter, a heritable component), a functioning profile (what the person can and cannot do), a short first-person testimony, and a social note describing how other people react to the trait. The committee has exactly four listing tokens, so it must cut half the cards, and to spend a token it must write both a dysfunction clause and a harm clause. The cards should be engineered so that the two clauses come apart in at least four different ways: a clear mechanism failure that harms no one and that the person would not trade away, a clear harm with no discoverable mechanism failure, a trait whose only harm is the social response it provokes, and a trait that is a mechanism failure in one reference population and statistically ordinary in another. Two mechanics should do the real work. First, at the midpoint I want to hand out a revision memo that replaces one card's biological profile with an identical profile drawn from a different population, so that the abnormality disappears while the functioning profile and the testimony stay exactly the same, and the subcommittees must re-vote that card alone. Second, every condition that gets listed should impose a cost — a point, a lost speaking turn, something small — on a participant drawn at random and revealed only after the listing is final, so that the committee is deciding about a cost that will land on one of them and cannot know which. At the end I want a debrief that maps each mechanic onto the position it dramatizes: the two-clause rule onto the harmful-dysfunction structure and the naturalist's hope that the dysfunction clause can be filled without values; the reference-population swap onto the biostatistical theory and its dependence on a reference class that biology does not itself select; the random cost onto the normativist claim that the extension of disease tracks who has standing to complain. I should say plainly that I want the words naturalism, normativism, Boorse and Wakefield kept out of my mouth until the debrief, and I want a historical epilogue at the end — a real classification that once listed a condition whose only harm clause was social, and one that delisted a condition whose dysfunction clause never changed — supplied by me, not discovered by them.

Tell me where the design will break: which mechanic the participants will game in the first ten minutes and how to close the loophole; what happens if the group decides the honest move is to list nothing or everything; how to handle the moment someone imports a live political controversy despite the fictional cards; and whether the random-cost rule is more likely to produce cynicism than insight, with a fix if it is. Finally, be honest with me about what the activity cannot reproduce and I will have to say out loud — I suspect it is the metaphysical question of whether a value-free notion of dysfunction is possible at all, since a determined naturalist can always say my committee simply had bad data about reference classes, and the semantic question of whether the concept of disease is itself evaluative, which no amount of voting will settle.
