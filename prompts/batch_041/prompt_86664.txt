I am a retired experimental scientist. I ran a physiology laboratory for thirty-four years, spent the last decade of that trying to get other people's published findings to hold up under my own hands, and have spent the five years since my retirement reading philosophy of science with more appetite than training. My philosophical education is self-taught and uneven, and I mention this because it is the reason I would rather have the reasoning than the conclusion.

I have been asked to chair an advisory panel for a foundation that will commit a fixed sum over three years to improving reproducibility in biomedical and behavioral research. The board consists of working scientists who are, collectively, impatient with philosophy: whatever the panel recommends must be defensible as something that would change what researchers actually do. The budget is enough for one large multi-site replication consortium or for a larger number of smaller methodological projects, but not for both.

What I need from you is not a recommendation between those two shapes, but a defended ranking of the conceptual disputes that should be allowed to constrain the choice, together with the disputes I should keep off the board's agenda because, whatever their philosophical interest, their resolution would not change what anyone does.

The candidates I have collected are these, though I would welcome additions and would especially welcome being told that one of them is confused:

- whether the familiar division between frequency-based and Bayesian inference is even the right axis, or whether the failures that motivate the reforms are failures of measurement, incentive, and publication rather than of inference, in which case most of the foundational debate is beside the point;
- whether the Bayesian insensitivity to stopping rules — the posterior, I gather, does not depend on the intention with which data collection was halted — removes a problem the reforms were designed to address, and whether that makes pre-registration a covertly frequency-based commitment;
- whether the freedom to choose a prior is a researcher degree of freedom of the same species as those the reforms exist to eliminate, so that Bayesian methods relocate the flexibility rather than remove it;
- whether the replication enterprise rests on an unexamined homogeneity assumption — that there is a stable effect there to be replicated — and whether that assumption is empirical, conceptual, or both;
- whether the error-statistical tradition has a resource the Bayesian framework lacks, in the form of a notion of severity: a test counts in favor of a hypothesis only if it would probably have detected a real effect of the relevant magnitude;
- whether the measured replication rate is itself a statistic exposed to the same selection and publication pressures, so that the empirical case for the crisis is either partly self-undermining or partly self-reinforcing.

For each candidate, I want to know why it is or is not upstream of the practical reforms; how tractable it is for people who are not going to become statisticians; whether settling it would alter what the consortium does or merely how the final report is worded; and what we might see in the next two or three years that would move it up or down the list. I want a defended ordering, not a survey, and I have no use for a reading list.
