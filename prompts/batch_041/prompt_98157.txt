I'm trying to pin down the principle behind my own judgments about which strategies get eliminated, step by step, when I reason through a game. Below are five games; in each cell the first number is the row player's payoff and the second is the column player's. Assume common knowledge of the matrix and of both players' rationality, and treat each game on its own. After each matrix I give my verdict and how sure I am of it.

Game A. Rows T, B; columns L, R.
(T, L) = (2, 0); (T, R) = (2, 1); (B, L) = (1, 1); (B, R) = (0, 0).
Verdict: cross off B. T does at least as well as B no matter what the column player does, and strictly better in one case. No hesitation.

Game B. Rows T, M, B; columns L, R.
(T, L) = (3, 2); (T, R) = (0, 0); (M, L) = (1, 1); (M, R) = (1, 1); (B, L) = (1, 0); (B, R) = (4, 2).
Verdict: cross off M. It is strictly worse than a fifty-fifty mix of T and B against either column choice, even though no single pure strategy beats it on both columns. T and B stay. I am confident, but notice that this verdict forced me to compare a strategy with a mixture rather than with another pure strategy, and I want the principle to say why that is legitimate.

Game C. Rows T, B; columns L, R.
(T, L) = (2, 0); (T, R) = (1, 1); (B, L) = (2, 1); (B, R) = (0, 0).
Verdict: do not cross off B. T is at least as good as B against everything the column player can do (equal against L, better against R). But if I had reason to expect the column player to play L, B would be exactly as good as T, so playing B is not a blunder. Crossing off a strategy that can still be a best reply feels wrong to me.

Game D. Rows T, B; columns L, R.
(T, L) = (1, 1); (T, R) = (2, 1); (B, L) = (1, 2); (B, R) = (1, 1).
Verdict: here I want to cross off both B and R — T is at least as good as B for the row player, and L is at least as good as R for the column player. But I cannot. If I remove R first, B is no longer beaten by T, so B stays; if I remove B first, R is no longer beaten by L, so R stays. The answer depends on the order, which seems absurd, and either way I can never remove both.

Game E. Rows T, M, B; columns L, C, R.
(T, L) = (5, 2); (T, C) = (4, 1); (T, R) = (1, 0); (M, L) = (3, 2); (M, C) = (2, 1); (M, R) = (3, 0); (B, L) = (1, 0); (B, C) = (1, 2); (B, R) = (0, 3).
Verdict: this one resolves cleanly and I am sure of the survivor. Remove B (T beats it in every column), then remove R and C (with B gone, L beats both for the column player), then remove M (with only L and C left, T beats it). What remains is (T, L). Unlike Game D, the order did not seem to matter here.

The pattern I think I see is this: cross off a strategy when it is strictly worse than some alternative, pure or mixed, evaluated against the opponent's remaining strategies, and repeat until nothing more can be removed. That gets A, B, C, and E — note that it keeps B in C, because against L, T merely ties B, so B is not strictly worse than anything. And the procedure appears to be order-independent, which is what I want, since D shows what happens when it is not. But it cannot remove B and R in D, because in D neither of those is strictly worse than its rival; each merely ties in one column. So D is the single verdict this pattern cannot deliver.

The only way I can see to get D is to let weak dominance count, crossing off a strategy that is merely never better and sometimes worse. But that is exactly the move my C verdict forbids, since in C, B is weakly dominated by T and yet I refuse to cross it off. So I am stuck: strict dominance saves C and loses D; weak dominance wins D and loses C, and even then D has no unique answer, because the result depends on the order of removal.

Here is one more idea, to see whether it rescues D without costing C. Do not fix an order: consider every possible elimination order, see what survives under each, and keep only the strategies that survive under all of them. In D that leaves T for the row player and L for the column player, so both B and R are gone, matching my verdict. But in C it still removes B, since B is weakly dominated there and every order removes it. So this idea does not save C after all; it looks like weak-dominance elimination with a tie-breaker, and I am back to the same trade-off.

So: what principle, or small set of rival principles, actually captures the verdicts I have given? State it precisely; say which game forces each clause; say which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate; and say what it predicts for cases I have not listed. I would especially like to know: (a) whether there is a principled reason to let mixtures into the elimination test, or whether restricting it to pure strategies is the more honest version; (b) why the iteration is order-independent when the test is strict dominance but not when it is weak dominance; (c) whether "strictly dominated by some mixture" and "never a best reply to any belief" always agree — I have heard they coincide in two-player games but can come apart once there are three or more players, and if that is right, I want to know which way they come apart and what it does to my verdicts; and (d) whether the "survives every order" idea can ever yield an empty or otherwise incoherent set, and what that would tell me.

Reason it out rather than reciting known results, and write in American English; no citations needed.
