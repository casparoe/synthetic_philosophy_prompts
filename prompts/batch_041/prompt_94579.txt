I chair the patient-safety committee at a mid-sized teaching hospital — I'm a hospitalist by training, not a lawyer, though I've sat on this committee long enough to have absorbed more causation doctrine than any clinician should — and I have to circulate a short written opinion before Thursday's meeting on a case that has split us five to four. My sister teaches torts and we have been arguing about the word "cause" at Thanksgiving for a decade, which is probably why I am the one who keeps slowing these discussions down. The facts are not in dispute. A sixty-one-year-old man on the surgical ward, second day after an uncomplicated bowel resection, bled slowly overnight and arrested at 6:40 a.m.; he was transfused forty minutes later, too late. Two people could have caught it. The night nurse's standing instructions required her to page the on-call resident once a patient's systolic pressure fell below a set threshold on two consecutive readings, and she watched it fall without paging, because the man was also restless and she read that as pain rather than bleeding. The resident, paged at 5:10 a.m. about an unrelated patient two doors down, walked past the room, did not pull the chart, and so did not see the trend. Either the page or the chart review would have led to a transfusion in time; neither was necessary, because the other would have sufficed. Our charter defines a death as "preventability-reportable" when "a lapse in care delivery was a necessary element of the outcome," and adds a rider that "lapses consisting in an omission count only where the omitted act was a specified duty of the role at the time." Counsel read the charter aloud to us in about ninety seconds, said it is simply the but-for test with a duty gloss, and suggested we stop overthinking it. I would like you to help me not stop overthinking it.

What I need is a genuine argument rather than a survey — something I can hand to nine people who will read it once, on the way into the room. Begin with whether an omission can stand in the causal relation at all: the resident's silence did not open a vessel, and the man died of the bleed, so is our talk of an omission "causing" the death a real claim about the world or a way of marking conduct we disapprove of? If omissions can be causes, tell me what test we should be applying here, given that the but-for test collapses on the very structure this case has — two lapses, each sufficient to avert the death, neither necessary — and say what work the charter's duty rider is actually doing, since it looks to me like a rule about who we may blame smuggled in as a rule about what counts as a cause. Then take seriously the pattern that bothers me most about this committee: we reliably locate causes in the omissions of individuals, who have names and duties, and reliably fail to locate them in the omissions of the institution — the monitor that was never installed on this ward, the ratio of nurses to beds that no one in this room ever set, the discharge plan nobody wrote. I want to know whether that asymmetry reflects a difference in the causal facts or merely in the availability of someone to hold responsible; and if it is the latter, I want the memo to say so plainly rather than reassure us that we establish causation first and assign responsibility afterwards.

Close with a concrete recommendation I can move on Thursday: whether to report the death to the state as preventable, whether to open a peer-review file on the resident or the nurse or neither, whether to refer the charter's standard back to the board for amendment and on what timetable, and one sentence I can say to the family that is neither a lie nor a legal formula. Give the strongest case for whichever outcome I end up recommending against, stated in a form the colleague who will make that case in the meeting could recognize as fair — she is not a fool, and I would rather lose to her argument in writing than win by leaving it out.
