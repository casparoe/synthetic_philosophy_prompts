I spent thirty-odd years as a working scientist and have spent the last five trying to catch up on philosophy, mostly through a Thursday reading group that has somehow become the best part of my week. There's a woman in it, Ruth — retired actuary, sharpest person I have ever argued with — whose views on long shots I have never been able to reduce to a rule. I've got a case I want to put to her on Thursday and I'd like to know her answer before she gives it, so I'm asking you to do the reducing for me.

Here's her record, as best I can reconstruct it. A certain £1,000 or a one-in-a-hundred shot at a million: she takes the shot. A certain £10,000 or a one-in-a-million shot at £10^12: she takes the shot. A certain million or a one-in-10^20 shot at £10^60: she takes the shot, and when I tell her this is absurd she says the smallness of the probabilty is a red herring so long as the prize is large enough, and that if the arithmetic produces absurdities then that is the arithmetic's problem. In all three of those, note, the worst that can happen to her is that she ends up exactly where she started.

Then she turned down a deal I was rather pleased with. A certain 100 units of good, or a gamble with one chance in 10^12 of 10^12 units of harm and otherwise 200 units of good. The gamble's expected value is 199 against 100 for the sure thing. She refused before I'd finished the sentence and wouldn't do the sum. The harm there is a catastrophe by anyone's reckoning.

One more, which I suspect is a seperate matter but I'll mention it in case it isn't: I asked her about a wager with a one-in-two chance of an outcome she'd be willing to call infinitely good, against a certain but finite good of any size you like. She refused — not because the odds were unattractive, but because she says that once infinite values enter the ledger the thing stops being a decision problem: any positive chance of an infinite good beats every finite alternative, and a rule that tells you to stake everything on a coin toss is a reductio of the rule, not an answer from it.

The case for Thursday. Two options. The first is a certain gain of 1,000 units of good. The second is a gamble that pays 2,000 units of good unless a one-in-a-million event occurs, in which case she ends up 500 units worse off than she is now. So the gamble's expected value is about 2,000 against 1,000 for the sure thing, and its worst case is a modest loss rather than a catastrophe.

What I want: first, a prediction, with reasons, of what she'll say on Thursday. Second, your best diagnosis of the rule she is actually running — straight expected value maximisation, expected value maximisation with some floor under the probabilities, a rule against ever accepting a gamble that can leave her worse off than she started, a rule that refuses only the gambles that could ruin her, or something else entirely — saying what each candidate predicts about Thursday and which of them survive the rest of her record. Third, if Thursday leaves it open, the case that would settle it. Please commit: give me a definate prediction and a definite diagnosis and tell me what you'd bet on and why, rather than laying out the possibilities and leaving me to choose. British English please — I've gone native on the spellings and it upsets my grandchildren.
