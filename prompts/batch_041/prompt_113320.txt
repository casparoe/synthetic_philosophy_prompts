I'm taking this seminar on philosophy of economics and I keep running into something I can't square, and honestly my friends are tired of hearing about it. For like a week we did revealed preference — the idea that you don't get to say what someone "really" wants or how much utility they "really" get out of something; all you have is their choices, and "utility" is just whatever ranking of options makes the best sense of those choices. Fine, clean, I get it. But then I look at how regulators actually do cost-benefit analysis and it seems like a completely different animal. They put dollar figures on things nobody buys or sells — a cleaner air, a slightly lower chance of dying in a crash — and that number for a human life, the one that's in the millions, comes from how much extra pay people demand to take riskier jobs. Then they add up all the dollar benefits across millions of different people and compare the total against the cost. A friend of mine who works in a regulatory shop says this is just "applied revealed preference," which I don't buy for a second, because to add benefits up across people you have to be comparing how much better off each person is, and revealed preference is supposed to be allergic to exactly that — it gives you a ranking within one person, and not even a how-much ranking, just a better/worse one.

So what does the practice actually presuppose about what utility is? It looks to me like it needs utility to be a measurable thing you can compare between people, which is the one thing the theory it claims to be built on says you can't have. And there's a second half to the puzzle. These same legal systems are perfectly happy to let you get paid to box, or work on a trawler, or do a risky drug trial for cash — all exactly the kind of choices revealed preference wants to price. But you can't sell a kidney, and in alot of places you can't be paid to carry someone else's child, and obviously you can't sell your vote. When I ask why, the answers I get don't sound like "we know better than you what's good for you" and they also don't sound like "someone else gets hurt" — they sound like a third thing I can't quite name. And the thing that really gets me: when economists go measure this stuff, people turn out to want several times as much to give something up as they'd pay to acquire it, which if preferences were the clean stable ranking the theory says they are shouldn't happen at all.

So I'd like someone to lay out what picture of utility and welfare this whole package — the dollar numbers, the summing across people, the bans on a handful of voluntary deals — actually commits us to. Which bits are strictly implied and which would survive if we swapped in some other theory of welfare? Is there a reading where it all hangs together and I'm just being dense? And if the honest answer is that the practice quietly needs a richer notion of utility than revealed preference can supply, what's the smallest patch — do we say there are "true" preferences hiding behind the revealed ones, or that welfare isn't preferences at all, or something else? And what would actually change on the ground if we took the revealed-preference story seriously — would the bans have to go, or the dollar figures?
