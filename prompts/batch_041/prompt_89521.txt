I chair the review panel for our foundation's programme on the limits of formal reasoning. We meet on Friday to decide the fate of a single shortlisted proposal, and the decision letter goes out the following Monday, so I need to circulate a written opinion to the panel and to the trustees before then. Please draft that opinion.

The proposal, submitted by a philosopher of mathematics with a co-investigator in machine learning, asks for three years of support for what the applicants call the blind-spot thesis. In their own summary, the argument runs as follows. Any formal system rich enough for arithmetic, if it is consistent and its axioms can be effectively listed, contains sentences it can neither prove nor refute. Any machine that does mathematics is such a system — the applicants argue that this holds for every artificial reasoner, however it is built. It follows, they say, that there will always be mathematical truths a machine cannot reach but a human mathematician can see; and from this they draw a policy conclusion: funders and research councils should not treat automated theorem proving as a replacement for human mathematical work, and should keep a protected share of mathematics funding for human researchers. The proposal also includes a public engagement strand whose centrepiece is a claim the applicants intend to place in a museum exhibition and a schools pack — that there are mathematical truths that no method whatsoever can ever prove.

Two referee reports are before the panel.

The first is broadly favourable. It concedes that the policy conclusion does not follow from the logic alone, but argues that a weaker conclusion does: any fixed machine will have blind spots relative to a stronger system, and that alone justifies keeping humans in the loop. It adds that the public engagement strand is precisely where the work of correcting popular misunderstandings will happen, and that a funder's job is to support the work rather than to guarantee the slogans.

The second is hostile, and its objections are the ones I expect to be decisive. First, it says the argument trades on the order of quantifiers: what the incompleteness results deliver is that for every such system there is a sentence that system cannot decide, whereas what the argument needs is a single sentence that the human mind can prove and that no such system can. The first claim is compatible with the mind simply being one of the systems, and the second does not follow from it. Second, it says the argument's crucial step — that we can see the undecidable sentence to be true — requires knowing that the system is consistent, and the second incompleteness theorem says the system cannot establish this for itself. So either our confidence in that consistency comes from a stronger system, in which case the same difficulty recurs one level up, or it comes from something that is not proof at all, and the proposal owes us an account of that something. Third, it says the whole argument concerns an idealized reasoner that is consistent, never mistaken, and equipped with a fixed stock of axioms, whereas real mathematicians contradict themselves and publish errors; the proposal must therefore say which subject its conclusion is about. If it means the real mathematician, the conclusion about seeing unprovable truths does not apply; if it means the idealized one, the conclusion has no bearing on funding for the mathematicians we actually employ.

I need the opinion to do the following. Reach a verdict — fund as submitted, reject, or fund subject to specified conditions — and give the reasoning for it in terms a trustee with no logic training can follow, without softening or fudging the technical points. If you recommend conditions, make them conditions the applicants could plausibly accept without abandoning their thesis, and say what should happen if they refuse. Address separately the sub-question of the museum and schools claim about truths that no method whatsoever can prove: whether the panel should require it to be dropped or reworded, and on what grounds, given that we are a funder and not the applicants' peer reviewers and that they will fairly invoke their academic freedom. Treat the strongest version of whichever position you come out against rather than a caricature of it, and be explicit about which specific inferential steps in the applicants' argument you accept as sound and which you reject; I would much rather have an opinion that says plainly which steps fail than one that observes in general terms that incompleteness theorems are widely misused. No literature review is needed — the panel has no appetite for one, and we will not be adjudicating priority disputes. Roughly a page and a half, in the register of a formal committee paper.
