Interpretability folks seem to do two things that don't obviously fit together. When a linear probe reads a concept off a layer's activations, they'll say the model "represents" that concept. But when you point out that you could probably probe a randomly initialized network for the same thing, or that the info might just be sitting there unused, they back off and say decodability isn't representation -- you need a causal test, ablate or patch the direction and check the behavior moves. So in one breath representation is linearly decodable information, and in the next it's causally used information. Then there's the newer move where you train a sparse autoencoder on activations, get a pile of "features," and start talking about them as the model's concepts, even when a single feature fires on a grab-bag of unrelated stuff.

What does this practice actually presuppose about what a representation is -- that it's decodable structure, that it's a causally active direction, or that it's something more like a human concept with a content? Which of those does the practice strictly need, and which is just along for the ride? And if you swapped the implicit theory out for a deflationary one where "representation" is only a useful bookkeeping device and there's no fact about what the network really represents, what would have to change about how interpretability work is done and reported?
