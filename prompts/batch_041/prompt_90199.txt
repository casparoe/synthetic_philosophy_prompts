I've been going back and fourth with a friend about credences and I want to nail down what I actually think, so here's my gut on a pile of cases, and I'd like you to tell me what principle, if any, is behind them.

An unfamiliar coin: minted last week, never flipped, I know nothing about it. I don't think "my credence in heads is somewhere in [0,1]" is the right thing to say. I think 0.5 is the credence, and having something else is a mistake, not just a permissible option.

The Ellsberg urn thing, 30 red balls and 60 that are black or yellow in some unknown split. I'd rather bet on red than on black, and I'd rather bet on red-or-yellow than on black-or-yellow, and I don't think I'm being irrational when I do. That feels like a real preference I'm entitled to, not a bias to be corrected.

A friend hands me a coin she made herself and says only that it isn't fair, with no reason to think it leans heads rather than tails. Next flip: 0.5 heads, 0.5 tails, happy to say that. But I also think two heads in a row is more likely than heads-then-tails — not by much, and I couldn't tell you the number. Both of those feel right, and I have a suspicion they shouldn't both be.

A coin that's either 40% heads or 60% heads and I don't know which. Is my credence in heads 0.5, or is it the interval [0.4, 0.6]? If someone offers me even money on heads, I feel like I have to produce a single number to say yes or no, but I also resent being made to pretend I know which coin it is.

The one that really bugs me: I have a coin whose bias I've narrowed to somewhere between 30% and 70%, and separately a fair coin. Someone flips the fair coin and tells me it came up heads, which tells me nothing about the first coin. But take the proposition "exactly one of the two coins comes up heads." Before I hear the fair coin's result I'm at a clean 0.5 on that. After I hear it, I can only place it in a range, and the range is wider than the point was. Learning something irrelevant to a question shouldn't make me more ignorant about that question, but I can't find where the reasoning goes wrong. (I might be garbling the setup.)

Grains of sand in a sealed jar: is the number even? There I'm perfectly happy to say my credence is anywhere in [0,1] and no particular number is required. Why is that different from the coin?

Practical version: as a bookie I'll take either side of an even-money bet on the unfamiliar coin. But on the urn I want the red side and not the black side, at the same odds. Structurally those look like the same situation to me.

The half-principle I keep forming is that a sharp number is right when the situation is symmetric, and an interval is right when what I'm missing is a fact about the world that someone better informed would just know. Trouble is, it treats the unfamiliar coin and the urn's black exactly alike — in both, what I'm missing is a fact — and my verdicts there come apart. Same with the not-fair coin.

So: is there a principle that gets most or all of this, and where does it have to give? Which of my verdicts do you think I should drop, and what does the surviving principle say about a case or two you make up that I haven't mentioned?
