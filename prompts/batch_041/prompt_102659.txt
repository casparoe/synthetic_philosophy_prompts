I run operations and hiring at a midsize nonprofit, and we recruit for the same three roles on a rolling basis — applications arrive continuously, we interview as candidates come in, and I make a call within a few weeks of opening a search. A bad hire costs us most of a year, so I can't just grab whoever is available, but I also can't keep searching forever: I have roughly fifteen hours a week for a search, offers above a set salary band need board sign-off and the board only meets monthly, and once I decline a candidate I can't go back to them — though we do keep a file of strong runners-up we're allowed to re-approach if they haven't taken another job.

What I want is a decision procedure I can actually run, built on the satisficing tradition in bounded rationality — the idea that a boundedly rational agent sets an aspiration level and accepts the first option that meets it rather than optimizing over the whole space. Translate that theory into something usable by a hiring manager who has never read it, keeping what the theory actually says and not just restating generic hiring advice. Give me the steps in the order I should run them; for each step, what counts as a sufficient answer; where the procedure stops with a yes and where it stops with a no; how I set the aspiration level in the first place and how I am supposed to update it as I learn what the pool looks like; how to tell when my aspiration level is miscalibrated rather than the pool being genuinely bad; what changes when the cost of continuing to search goes up or down; and where the procedure hands the decision back to a person because the question is one of values rather than facts.

I'd like you to distinguish, in the course of this, between satisficing and optimizing, between a stopping rule and a choice rule, and between searching a stream of candidates one at a time versus choosing among several I already have in hand — because my search is mostly sequential but the silver-medalist file partly breaks the usual assumption that a declined candidate is gone for good, and I want the procedure to say what to do about that.

Then run it on three cases. First: an early applicant who clearly clears the bar on paper but has an unexplained two-year gap and lukewarm references — do I stop and hire, or is a cleared bar not yet a sufficient answer? Second: a long drought in which every candidate is mediocre and I can feel myself wanting to lower the bar just to end the search. Third: a candidate who is outstanding on dimensions I hadn't been weighting, so that she doesn't obviously clear my stated bar but is plainly the strongest person I've seen. At least one of these should come out in a way I won't like, and I want you to say so rather than soften it.

Finally, be honest about which steps are judgment calls dressed up as procedure, and tell me what the procedure cannot do — in particular, whether it can ever instruct me to keep searching past a candidate who has already cleared the bar, and whether that would be a failure of the procedure or a genuine feature of satisficing. Please write the answer in American English.
