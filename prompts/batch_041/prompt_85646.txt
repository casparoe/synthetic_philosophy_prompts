I'd like you to act as a philosopher of mind who works on mental causation and is practiced at explaining the debate to people outside the field. I've been reading about the causal exclusion argument on my own — the line of reasoning that runs roughly like this: every physical effect already has a sufficient physical cause; mental properties are not identical to physical properties but are realized by them; so if a mental property were also a cause of the resulting behavior, the physical cause would already have done all the work, leaving the mental cause either redundant or in competition with it. I don't want a survey of the literature, a reading list, or citations; I want you to think carefully, and before you answer, to spend a moment setting out for yourself exactly what the argument's premises commit one to.

What I'm after are the most important implications — the downstream consequences that someone who takes the argument seriously, or who rejects it but wants to know what is at stake, should care about. Please work this out concretely rather than gesturally, and address three things in particular. First, what follows for the special sciences — psychology, but equally something like ecology or economics — if their proprietary properties are all multiply realized in physical ones; can they still make genuine causal claims about their own entities? Second, what follows for the ordinary claim that a person's reasons caused her action, and what would that mean for the practice of holding her responsible? Third, are the everyday personal-level explanation of behavior and the neuroscientific explanation competitors, or can both be true at once? I am especially interested in the possibility that the argument, if it succeeds for the mental, succeeds just as well for every higher-level property whatsoever, so that its real cost is much larger than it first appears — please say whether you think that generalization goes through and, if it does, what the single most important consequence is. Where an implication depends on a premise I might not accept, flag which premise, so that I can see which implications survive if I give that premise up. And please don't simply tell me that it depends on one's theory of causation; if it does, tell me what each major theory of causation implies for these three questions.
