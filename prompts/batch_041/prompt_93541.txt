I teach an upper-level seminar in decision theory and I'm writing the final exam. I want one essay question about games whose expected value is not well-defined, and I want it to resist the usual St. Petersburg reflexes. Use this game, which I've invented so that nobody can have memorized a discussion of it: a fair coin is tossed until it first comes up heads, and if that first head lands on toss N you are paid (−1)^(N−1) · 2^N / N dollars, so the amount is negative, meaning you pay, whenever N is even. Have the students determine whether the game has a well-defined expected value; explain why the obvious computation yields a tidy finite number and why that number may be an artifact of how the outcomes are ordered or truncated; and say what a risk-neutral expected-utility maximizer should conclude, in particular whether it would pay a small positive fee to play and whether an agent can even be said to have well-defined preferences over a game like this. Ask them to confront the natural objection that the sample space already fixes an ordering, so the tidy finite number really is the expected value, and to take a stand on it rather than survey the options. Also ask them to distinguish this game from the St. Petersburg game, since students tend to blur the two. Then give me a model answer of about 700 words and a marking scheme out of 20 that names the specific moves a first-class answer must make and the specific errors that should cost marks. Write everything in continuous prose, no headings, bullet points, bold, or tables, the way you'd write it out in a long email. No citations or reading lists, and nothing in quotation marks.
