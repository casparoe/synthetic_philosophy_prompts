A colleague of mine, a historian of environmental politics, has a line he deploys whenever the Anthropocene comes up: the quarrel over when it began is like a quarrel over whether the speed limit should be thirty or thirty-five. Both numbers keep the traffic safe, one of them gets picked, and the picking is done by whoever has the authority to do the picking. On his account the long dispute over the start date — the proposal to anchor it to the plutonium fallout from atmospheric bomb tests recorded in the sediments of Crawford Lake in Ontario, and the 2024 decision by the bodies that ratify such things to reject that proposal — shows that the Anthropocene is a political category rather than a scientific one: there is no fact of the matter about when it began, only a decision about how to use a word, made by a committee whose say-so is the whole of its authority.

I want to know how far the analogy actually holds. Lay out the correspondence explicitly — what plays the role of the road, the traffic, the number, the legislature, and the enforcement — then tell me where the mapping fails, which features of the speed limit get quietly imported into the geological case, and whether his conclusion survives once the disanalogies are on the table. I have a suspicion that the argument proves too much, in that the same reasoning would make every boundary in the geological time scale a mere convention, but I can't tell whether that is a real objection or just a debating trick; say which, and if the speed limit can't carry the weight, tell me what analogy would do better.
