I'm in a reading group on moral responsibility, and we keep circling something I can't get past: nearly every account of when a person has or lacks the standing to blame seems to be reverse-engineered from one favorite case, and the case is almost always the hypocrite. I want to stop collecting theories and get the specification first. So please take the role of a philosopher who has worked seriously on blame and accountability, and think carefully before you write. Begin with a short brainstorm in which you lay out for yourself the full range of cases that bear on standing, because I suspect the usual debate has narrowed the menu. Then give me the desiderata that any adequate theory of standing to blame must satisfy.

For each requirement, give me the case or consideration that motivates it and why it counts as a genuine demand rather than a prejudice of one camp. The list should cover at least: the cases the theory must get right (the hypocrite, obviously, but also the blamer who is not a hypocrite yet is complicit in the wrong she condemns; the reformed wrongdoer who has made amends and now blames someone for the same act; the blamer who benefits from the practice he criticizes; the third party who blames on behalf of a victim; the compromised blamer whose criticism is nonetheless true; and the case in which whether someone has standing seems to turn on facts she could not have known); the distinctions the theory must not collapse (standing versus blameworthiness versus the truth of the criticism; standing to blame versus standing to hold someone to account, if those are different; forfeiting standing versus never having had it; the appropriateness of a blaming attitude versus the efficacy of a blaming utterance); and the constraints it must respect (for instance, that standing not turn on unknowable facts, that it not be impossible for everyone, and that it not reduce to hypocrisy alone). I'm especially interested in the ways standing might be regained or laundered through ritualized self-criticism, since none of the accounts I've seen says anything about that.

Then, and this is the part I most want, mark which requirements are non-negotiable and which are preferences of particular camps, and flag any pair or subset that looks jointly unsatisfiable — if some of what people demand of a theory of standing cannot all be had at once, that result is more useful to me than any scorecard. Tell me which single requirement does the most to exclude the most popular theories, and finish with a brief pass over how the leading families of view do against the list. Keep the weight on the criteria and their motivating cases; the scoring is secondary, and a table is fine if it helps.
