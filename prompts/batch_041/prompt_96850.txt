A popular book on skepticism that I have been reading devotes a chapter to a dispute among philosophers, and I would like to know how much of its account I can trust. The account runs roughly as follows: 'Reliabilism holds that a belief is justified when it is produced by a process that reliably yields true beliefs, and it does not require the believer to have any independent evidence that the process is reliable. The trouble is that this makes knowledge too easy. The reliabilist is committed to saying that you can establish that your eyes work simply by using them: this looks red to me, and it is red; that looks blue to me, and it is blue; and so on, until you conclude that your vision is reliable. Critics call this the bootstrapping problem, and their objection is that the reasoning is circular — you are already trusting your eyes to certify your eyes — so it cannot yield knowledge. The real disagreement, then, is whether we can have any noncircular assurance that our faculties are reliable at all: the reliabilist says we cannot, and cheerfully accepts what it calls easy knowledge, such as knowing that the cup you are looking at is not really white under a red lamp without bothering to check the lighting, while the conservative insists that knowledge of the world must rest on something firmer. In the end the book treats the whole thing as an old worry in new dress: you cannot prove that reason is trustworthy by reasoning, and the same goes for the senses.'

I suspect the book has the two sides of the dispute tangled together, but I cannot tell where; I am inclined to think the critics are onto something, though I cannot see what would be wrong with the argument if it is not circular. Could you sort through the account and separate what someone working on these questions would accept, what is a loose but defensible simplification, and what is simply wrong? I would also like to know whether the book's closing dilemma — that the choice is between easy knowledge and no knowledge at all — follows from the parts of the account that are accurate, and what a careful reliabilist would say in response.
