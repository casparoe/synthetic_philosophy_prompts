Having spent my working life in the natural sciences and only recently turned to philosophy, I have been trying to compare two impossibility results that are often mentioned together but seldom set side by side with any care: the no-free-lunch theorems and the problem of induction. Please write that comparison for me as exactly five numbered items, each a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion. I am especially interested in the version of the no-free-lunch theorem which states that, averaged uniformly over all target functions, every learning algorithm has the same expected error, and in the distinction between that theorem and the stronger, and I suspect often conflated, slogan that no inductive bias can be universally better. In the first four items, compare the two results on: what each identifies as impossible; what each shows to be an unavoidable assumption (a prior, a uniformity of nature, an inductive bias), and whether the uniform distribution over target functions in the no-free-lunch theorem is itself an assumption of the same kind; whether the no-free-lunch theorem is best read as a formalization of the classical problem of induction or as a different, algorithmic rather than epistemic, impossibility; and what the existence of a free lunch for restricted problem classes implies for the demand for a non-circular justification of induction. In the fifth item, state how confident you are in the comparison as a whole and single out the point you are least sure of.
