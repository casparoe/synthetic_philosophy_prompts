I'm a software engineer, not a philosopher, and I've been arguing about sex-work policy with friends long enough that I want to check my own position for internal consistency before I go any further. Here is roughly where I've landed. I hold all of the following:

1. Selling sex should never be a criminal offense. The person who sells should face no arrest, no fine, no criminal record.
2. Buying sex should be a criminal offense. The end-demand or "Nordic" approach — go after the buyer, leave the seller alone — seems right to me, because the demand is what pulls the whole thing along.
3. Sex work is work. It's a legitimate service occupation, not inherently degrading, and the people who do it are workers like any others.
4. The full-decriminalization approach, where the state simply treats it like any other legal service and regulates it lightly, is a mistake. In practice the industry runs largely on trafficking and economic desperation, and declaring it legal mostly launders that.
5. I'm anti-paternalist about this: the state has no business policing what adults do with their own bodies, and the law should be judged by whether it actually protects the people doing the work.
6. Laws here should aim at the exploiters — traffickers, pimps, the people running the operations — and never at the workers themselves.

My instinct is that these six are a coherent package and that the people who tell me otherwise are just pattern-matching me to a camp. But I can't actually produce the argument, so I'd like you to do the audit. Which of these commitments genuinely pull against each other, and which pairs only look like they conflict? For the genuine ones, rank them by how badly they clash and tell me what would have to give and what it would cost me — the cheapest repair, not just "drop your whole view." And if there's a way to read one of these more narrowly so that the set survives intact, say which one, and whether that reading is honest or just a dodge.
