Some years ago a man I never met left his estate to a small charitable trust with a single condition attached: that the money be spent on abolishing suffering. Not on relieving it, or on making it more bearable, but on abolishing it — using whatever technology would do the job — and not for human beings alone but for every creature that can feel anything, including animals on farms and animals in the wild. I am one of three trustees. We meet four times a year, we have roughly £120,000 a year to give away in one-year grants, and between us we have no scientific, medical or philosophical training. The proposals arrive a fortnight before each meeting, and anything we fund has to be agreed unanimously.

We keep having the same argument. One of us will say a project is obviously good because it takes suffering away; another will say it is a horror; and neither of us can say what the disagreement is really about, because we have no method. I have been going round in circles about it on my own for months.

What I want from you is a procedure we can actually run: the questions to ask, in order; what would count as a good enough answer to each; the point at which the procedure comes to a stop and the answer is no; and the point at which it has to be handed back to us, because the question is one of values rather than of facts. It has to be usable by three non-specialists in a single evening, and it has to hold to the two clauses in the deed that we keep tripping over:

(i) nothing may be funded whose purpose or principal effect is to kill the creatures whose suffering it is meant to relieve;
(ii) nothing may be funded whose most likely eventual use is to make an existing harmful practice cheaper, or easier to defend.

Then run the procedure you have written on three proposals that have actually come to us, and give me the results straight:

- A laboratory wants to breed a line of farmed chickens that cannot feel pain, so that the birds can be kept in the conditions they are kept in now, with the suffering subtracted.
- A group wants to develop a contraceptive bait for a wild mammal that lives at very high densities and, so far as anyone can tell, mostly dies young and painfully; their aim is that fewer of these animals are born, and so that there is less suffering in the world, though the species itself would dwindle.
- A group wants to develop a stunning method that would reliably make cattle unconscious before they are killed, and to press abattoirs to adopt it; the intention is to spare the animals pain in their last minutes.

Tell me where the procedure gives an answer I will not like — I would rather hear it than not — and where it cannot give an answer at all. I would also like to know which of its steps are really our own preferences dressed up as reasoning, so that we at least know when we are deciding rather than deducing.

Please do not give me a reading list, references or suggestions for further study; I will not follow them up, and what I need is the procedure itself. Write the whole answer in British English.
