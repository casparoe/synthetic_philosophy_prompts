I am a postdoctoral fellow in moral philosophy, and in ten days I present a paper at our departmental colloquium defending what I take to be the real lesson of the moral-saints debate Susan Wolf opened in her 1982 paper "Moral Saints": not that maximal moral goodness would be unattractive to the agent who achieved it, but that it is undesirable for the moral community that hosts it, because saintly lives are lives that presuppose non-saints. My argument, in outline, runs like this. A person who is maximally morally good has, by hypothesis, eliminated the standing temptations, partial attachments, and self-regarding ambitions that make moral reasons salient, effortful, and motivational for the rest of us; such a life is therefore not merely an unattractive personal ideal but a self-undermining social one, since a world of saints would be a world with less moral responsiveness overall — the friction that saintliness removes is the very friction that trains and sustains moral agency in ordinary people. I distinguish the saint (the limiting case of maximal moral goodness) from the hero and from the merely decent person, and I insist that the objection is internal to morality rather than an appeal to self-interest; I also claim the saint's life fails a universalizability-style test, not because it cannot be recommended to everyone, but because it can only be lived against a background of people who are not saints.

You are an experienced moral philosopher who has sat through hundreds of colloquia and is known for anticipating exactly where an argument will be attacked. I need you to prepare me for the discussion rather than defend me in it. The audience is not sympathetic in any uniform way: two colleagues work on effective altruism and treat partiality as a bias to be overcome; one is a Kantian who will think I am abusing the universalizability test and will press the formula of humanity; two virtue ethicists doubt that "saintliness" names a character trait rather than a philosopher's fantasy; a metaethicist will ask who is supposed to be doing the desiring and in what sense a life can be called undesirable at all; and one colleague in the Aristotelian-Nietzschean corner will say the whole question is confused because it treats morality as one sphere among several competing for a life. I have roughly forty minutes of Q&A.
