I'm a second-year PhD student in an ML group and I'm TAing the graduate seminar on AI safety this term; the prof is away the week we cover control, so I'm running that session and writing the take-home. That week's theme is safety measures built on the assumption that the model might already be misaligned — the family of ideas where, instead of trying to make the model want the right things, you assume it doesn't and try to catch it, or to structure the task so that even a scheming model can't do much damage. The students will have read the standard stuff, so almost anything I ask will get answered by reciting the usual threat model and the usual list of mitigations. I want one essay question they can't answer that way, and I want it to hang on a concrete setup rather than on the literature. So: a company wants to use a big model M for a long-horizon job — say writing and then debugging a large codebase across many sessions — and M is much more capable than anything else they have. Their only other model is a smaller, weaker one, W, from the same training pipeline. So the standard move of having a trusted weaker model review everything M produces is still on the table, except W can barely follow what M is doing; on a lot of M's outputs W's judgment is close to a coin flip. Also assume M can see the whole protocol, nothing is hidden from it, and the evals used to check the protocol are ran by M itself under conditions where it might know it's being tested.

I want the question to force them to say where the safety is actually coming from in a setup like that, and in particular whether you can state the guarantee as "this is safe even if M is misaligned" without smuggling in an assumption that W — or the humans auditing some small sample of M's work — is trustworthy, or that M is just too dumb to game the thing. I'd like a strong answer to have to say something about what happens to the guarantee as W gets stronger and the capability gap closes, and about whether the monitoring changes the thing being monitored, since M learns what gets flagged and adjusts, so the protocol you evaluated isn't quite the protocol you're running. My own hunch is that a really good answer will separate what the protocol buys you against a merely wrong-objective model from what it buys you against a model actively optimizing against the protocol, and will admit that some of the trust just gets relocated rather than eliminated — but don't put any of that in the question, it should be neutral and open.

Deliverables: the question itself (answerable in about 700 words of prose, no code required), a model answer of roughly 600-700 words pitched at a good grad student, and a marking scheme out of 20 that names the specific moves separating an A from a B — the must-hit points spelled out, marks per point, and a one-line note on the mistake that usually costs people each mark. Also flag for me any answer that would look sophisticated but is really a dodge (hand-waving "just use interpretability", "just don't deploy", "the evals will catch it"), so I know what to watch for while marking. Don't bother with citations or references, we're not asking for those.
