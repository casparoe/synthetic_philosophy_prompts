You are a philosopher specializing in the theory of value, with a precise command of the logic of comparative relations. Think carefully, and begin by brainstorming the structure of the case before committing to any goods, numbers, or ordering.

Construct a thought experiment to order: a spectrum case engineered to put pressure on the transitivity of the all-things-considered "better than" relation. The purpose of the case is to test whether transitivity is a conceptual truth about betterness or a substantive constraint that could fail, so a case that merely stipulates intransitivity, rather than generating the tension from independently plausible verdicts, does not count.

Specifications the case must satisfy:
- The alternatives form a chain of at least four items that differ along a single dimension only, with everything else held fixed.
- Each adjacent step is a strict, determinate improvement (or worsening): the difference is clearly noticeable and is not a matter of vagueness, of borderline cases, or of a "just noticeable" gap that could reasonably be called a tie.
- The endpoints are ordered so as to generate the puzzle: the local steps imply that the last item is better than the first, yet it is intuitively not better — or is clearly worse.
- Use ordinary goods a person could actually face. No infinity, no "mere addition" or population-ethics scenarios, no science fiction, and no appeal to values that are incomparable with one another.

Then do the following:
1. Set out the chain and state the verdict at each adjacent comparison.
2. State exactly the inconsistency: which premises — transitivity, the individual step verdicts, and the endpoint verdict — cannot all hold together.
3. Identify which premise is doing the work, and say what happens to the puzzle if that premise is removed, or if the small steps are made large.
4. Give the strongest version of each of the three standard replies — reject transitivity, reject one of the steps, reject the endpoint verdict — and say which you would endorse and why.
5. Say whether your case threatens only the transitivity of "better than" or also that of "at least as good as," and whether that distinction matters here.
6. Finally, adopt the perspective of someone who treats transitivity as non-negotiable: what is the most damaging objection they can raise against your case, and does the case survive it?
