I'm a journalist at work on a long feature about what philosophers call "rational irrationality" — the idea that it can be rational to make yourself the kind of agent whose actions are not, at the moment of action, rationally required. The centerpiece of the piece is Parfit's hitchhiker case from Reasons and Persons: you are stranded in a desert, a driver offers to take you to safety if you promise to pay when you arrive, and you sincerely promise; once you are safe, paying would cost you and gain you nothing, so a strict self-interest theorist says you have no reason to pay. But if the driver knows you are a strict self-interest theorist, they would never have picked you up, and it was the promise that saved your life. I've been interviewing two philosophers who have argued about this for years. The first is a committed self-interest theorist — the view Parfit labels S — who thinks practical rationality is exclusively about maximizing one's own self-interest over time; the second is a committed present-aim theorist — the view Parfit labels P — who thinks an action is rational if it serves the aims the agent has at the time of action, whatever their content, and who therefore regards the self-interest theorist's verdict about the hitchhiker as an artifact of a decision procedure that cannot be endorsed from within the situation. They keep talking past each other about whether the hitchhiker has a reason to pay, whether it was rational to make the promise, and whether the driver's refusal to pick up a self-interest theorist is a fact about rationality or merely about reputation.

I want to commission the document they should have produced together. Please draft the joint statement of their adversarial collaboration: the claims both can sign, about the structure of the case, about what makes the promise credible, about what the driver's willingness depends on, about what is and is not at stake once the hitchhiker is safe, and about the relation among the rationality of the disposition, the rationality of making the promise, and the rationality of keeping it. Then the precise formulations of their remaining disagreement, each phrased so that its own side would accept the wording as a fair statement of its view. Then the cruxes: the sub-questions such that settling one would move at least one side, including whether the self-interest theorist is offering a criterion of rightness or a decision procedure, whether the hitchhiker's promise is binding independently of its consequences, and whether the case is a counterexample to self-interest theory or merely a demonstration that the theory is self-effacing. For each crux, give the case, argument, or variant that both sides agree bears on it — for instance, a variant in which the driver is also a self-interest theorist, a variant in which the hitchhiker can secretly signal his type before the ride, a variant in which the promise is made to a driver who will not survive to collect, and Kavka's toxin puzzle as a control case — and record each side's prediction about what follows. Format the document as a numbered agreed-claims section followed by a disagreements table, and close with a short section on any part of the dispute you conclude is merely verbal, saying why.
