I retired three years ago after forty years in atmospheric science — fluid dynamics, and toward the end a lot of nonlinear stuff, predictability horizons, that sort of thing. Since then I've been reading philosophy and history in a fairly undisciplined way, and I keep hitting a problem I don't know how to state properly.

The other evening I got into it with a friend of mine who teaches history. I'd made what I thought was a modest point — that some large political outcomes are best explained by long-run structural pressures, and that the particular way things fell out was more or less accidental — and he cut me off with the for-want-of-a-nail-the-kingdom-was-lost line. As if that settled it. It's come up twice more since, once in a book review I was reading and once from my son-in-law, who used it to argue that nobody can explain anything because everything is chaos. So the same sentence is doing at least two different jobs in my life and I don't think they're the same job.

What I want from you is a menu. Take that one line and lay out the genuinely distinct precise claims somebody could be making by it — separated by what they actually commit you to, not by how they feel. For each one, tell me whether it's a platitude nobody could deny, a real empirical or metaphysical claim that could turn out either way, or something that sounds deep and is actually false. And where a reading needs an extra assumption to do any work — an amplification mechanism, a measure over possible histories, a claim about what we would have observed had things gone the other way — say what that assumption is and how strong it has to be.

Then tell me, for the two uses I described — swatting away a structural explanation, and the everything-is-chaos one — which of the readings actually supports the use. I have a suspicion the first use needs a reading the proverb doesn't obviously give, but I'd rather you tell me than me tell you.

A few things I'd ask you to keep straight, because old habits die hard. My field had its own version of this slogan for a while — tiny perturbations, exponential divergence, the butterfly thing — and I've never been sure whether that supports the historian's point or undercuts it. Deterministic systems can be wildly unpredictable, and wildly unpredictable is not the same as undetermined, and I want to know which of the readings trades on that confusion. Also, most perturbations in most systems I ever worked with damp out; the ones that grow need the system sitting somewhere special. If that's the right analogy, what does it imply about how often a nail actually loses a kingdom, and does the proverb as usually meant claim frequency or just possibility? And I've noticed we only ever tell the story of the nail that mattered, never the thousand that didn't — tell me whether that's an argument against the proverb or just a complaint about historians' taste.

Lay it out as a numbered list I can hand to my friend. No reading list please, I won't read it. Keep it concrete — if a reading only works under a specific picture of how historical explanation works, spell out that picture, and if two of the readings collapse into each other once you state them carefully, say so instead of padding the list. I'm not trying to win the argument, I'm trying to find out what I've been agreeing to all these years when I nodded along.
