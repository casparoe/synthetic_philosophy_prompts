I'm in this small reading group that's been working through Berkeley's The Querist — the 1735 book where he just asks a few hundred questions in a row about Ireland's economy and never answers any of them — and honestly it's messed with my head a little. I keep opening my banking app and thinking: this is a screen of numbers, and the numbers are just a promise that other numbers can move. Berkeley says the true idea of money is "a ticket or counter," that it's useful only insofar as it "stirreth up industry," and that the four elements plus human labour are the real source of wealth. He also asks whether the real end and aim of men is power rather than money, and — this is the one that gets me — whether creating new wants might be the likeliest way to make a people industrious. So the ticket is worthless, the ticket is everything, and the wanting might be the actual product. I have to open our last session and I'd rather not do it with a summary, I want to do it with a poem.

So: write me a poem, somewhere between 16 and 20 lines, and make it entirely questions — no declarative sentence anywhere in it, not one. The form is the point: Berkeley's whole method is inquiry that never lands, because on his view the value of money is never actually located in the money, it's always somewhere else, in the next exchange. Each question should push one step further than the last, roughly in this order: the coin, then the token, then the credit, then the want the token manufactures, then whether the wanting itself is the wealth — and then the last question has to turn back on the first one, so the poem loops the way a note gets passed around a room. The word "money" should show up only once, and not until the second half.

Two things to avoid. First, don't write it as 18th-century pastiche — no "thee," no "doth," no faux-bishop voice; I want it in plain contemporary English, the way someone would think while staring at a receipt. Second, don't let it settle into either "money is nothing" or "money is everything" — the ending should leave the loop standing, unresolved, since that's honestly where our discussion is stuck too. No citations, no name-dropping Berkeley or anyone else inside the poem; someone who's read the Querist should be able to tell that the ticket/counter idea and the labour-not-bullion idea are in there doing work, but nobody should feel like they're being quizzed.
