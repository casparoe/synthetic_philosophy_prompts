I'm taking a course on laws of nature and scientific modality, and we've spent the term on the Humean best-system picture — laws as the theorems of whatever deductive system best balances simplicity and strength in describing the total pattern of occurrent facts — and on the anti-Humean alternative, on which laws genuinely govern or necessitate their instances rather than merely summarizing them. I'm writing a paper and I've reconstructed an argument that I now find suspicious, because every step seemed innocuous as I set it down but the conclusion is one I can't accept. I'd like you to diagnose it.

(1) A law of nature must be more than an accidental truth. It is not a coincidence that it holds, and this shows up in the counterfactuals it supports: had things been relevantly different, the law would still have held.

(2) To say that a generalization would still have held under relevantly different conditions is to say that it holds not only in the actual world but throughout a class of possible worlds, namely those that are nomically like ours.

(3) So a generalization is a law only if it holds in every world in that class.

(4) Fix our laws. The worlds nomically like ours are then just the worlds that share those laws, and every true generalization of ours holds in every such world. A world that shares our laws and yet violates one of our true generalizations is not a coherent thing to describe.

(5) Therefore every true generalization of ours holds in every world nomically like ours.

(6) By (3), every true generalization of ours is a law.

(7) So there are no merely accidental generalizations. Every regularity is a law — including the most parochial ones, such as the fact that every coin currently in my pocket is a quarter.

The conclusion is plainly false; accidental generalizations exist, and I would sooner keep that claim than abandon the thought that laws support counterfactuals. But I can't locate the slide, and I suspect that once I do, it will tell me something about what each side owes. Which step do I reject? Explain why it looked fine when I wrote it, and then tell me what rejecting it costs — in particular, whether the cost is paid differently by the Humean who treats laws as summaries of the occurrent facts and by the anti-Humean who treats them as governing. If you think I should give up a claim I said I wanted to keep, say so and make the case.
