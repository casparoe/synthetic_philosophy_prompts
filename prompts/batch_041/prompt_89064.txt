I am a novelist, and I am at work on a book about two people who make a single thing together — I have been imagining a pair of instrument makers, though the trade matters less than the problem. The fiction keeps running aground on a question I can only answer philosophically: when are two people doing one thing, rather than two things that happen to fit? I have written the short essay below to think it through, and I would be grateful for your comments. I am not asking for a reading list or for references; I have no way to check them, and what I want is engagement with the argument itself.

**Answerability, not shared intention**

The philosophy of collective intentionality has largely agreed on the shape of its central problem: what is it for several agents to intend to do something together, and how can an intention be, in some sense, one? The answers have been given in terms of we-intentions, shared intentions, joint commitments, and the common knowledge that binds them together. I want to suggest that all of these put the emphasis in the wrong place. What distinguishes a joint action from a merely coordinated one is not the structure of the intentions involved but a normative relation between the parties: mutual answerability. Agents act jointly when each is accountable to the others for the contribution they make — accountable in a sense that makes the reactive attitudes apt. Resentment, reproach, apology and excuse belong to joint action; prediction and readjustment belong to coordination. Where you may only be disappointed, you were not acting together.

Consider a stagehand and an actor who have never met. A producer's schedule assigns them the same moment: the actor's entrance, the stagehand's cue to open the door. Each knows what the other will do; each knows that the other knows; each knows that the other knows that he knows. Suppose the door does not open. The actor is inconvenienced, and he may complain to the producer, but it would be strange for him to feel wronged by the stagehand. Now compare the same actor with his dresser, with whom he has worked for a decade, and who both know will be in the wings at the cue. If the dresser is late, the actor is entitled to be hurt; the dresser owes him an apology, and owes it to him rather than to the producer. Nothing about their intentions differs. What differs is who owes what to whom.

Answerability does not require a shared intention. A hired mover who helps carry a piano to the van need not care whether the piano arrives; he need not intend the moving of the piano as an end. He intends to do his part, and to be paid. Accounts that make the shared intention the constituent of joint action must either argue that he intends the end in some thin sense, or deny that he is a joint actor at all. On the account I am proposing, he is answerable for his part and that is sufficient; his indifference to the whole is beside the point. He can be reproached for dropping his end of the piano, and the reproach is owed to his partner rather than to his employer.

Nor is answerability merely the agreement to be answerable. If it were, joint action would reduce to promising, and two people who have never spoken could not act together. The condition is instead uptake: I can hold you accountable for a contribution, and if you accept the holding, we are answerable to one another whether or not we have agreed to anything. This is why relationships can generate joint action without explicit arrangement, and it is also why the account is not circular: it takes answerability for a part, not for the whole, as primitive.

The account has costs, and I will name three. First, a flash mob: fifty strangers who have never met dance the same dance at the same time, each having learned it from a video. Many would say they are acting together. On my account this is coordination at scale, since no one is answerable to anyone, and I accept that verdict, though it is a cost. Second, and sharper, a musician improvising with a partner she believes to be in the room, when in fact she is playing to a recording. From the inside her case is indistinguishable from a duet: she listens, she responds, she expects to be answered. On my account there is no joint action, because there is no one to whom she is answerable. I think that verdict is correct, and that it shows joint action to be a matter not of how the activity feels from within but of the standing the parties have toward one another. Third, a musician forced at gunpoint to accompany another. She is excused rather than answerable, and so on my account they are not acting together — which sits badly with the thought that a coerced duet is still a duet. Perhaps answerability can be present and merely defeated; but I do not yet see how to say that without weakening the criterion to the point where the stagehand and the dresser are no longer distinguishable.

I would like your comments on this. In particular:

1. Is the distinction between answerability and shared intention a genuine improvement, or a restatement of the shared-intention view in other words — for instance, the flip side of a joint commitment? If it collapses into an existing position, say precisely where the collapse happens.
2. Do my cases discriminate between the accounts, or do they beg the question by describing the parties in my preferred vocabulary?
3. Which of the three verdicts is most costly, and is the cost worth paying?
4. Where is the argument most likely to fail, and what would change your mind about its central claim?
5. Give me a plain verdict on whether the thesis is true, and tell me where it is genuinely interesting rather than merely tidy.

I should add that the scene in which my two characters first become collaborators depends on getting this right, so I care more about the weak joints than about the flattering ones. Please argue with me rather than survey the field, and do not give me references or a reading list.
