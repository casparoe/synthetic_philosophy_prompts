I am teaching Kierkegaard's Fear and Trembling next term and find myself unable to give a stable account of the central move in Problema I. As I reconstruct the passage, Johannes de Silentio argues that the ethical is the universal, that the universal is the telos of everything, and that the tragic hero — Agamemnon, Jephthah — remains within the ethical because he sacrifices for the sake of the universal. Abraham, by contrast, acts for the sake of an end that is not the universal, and so the ethical is teleologically suspended. My difficulty is that these claims seem to pull against one another. If the universal is the telos of everything, nothing can be higher than it; and if the universal itself can demand the sacrifice of a child, then the difference between Abraham and the tragic hero cannot be that Abraham kills and the hero does not, nor that Abraham's act is unjustifiable, but only that Abraham's justification is not universalizable. Does the teleological suspension therefore suspend the universality of the ethical rather than the ethical as such? And if so, what prevents the position from collapsing into the claim that the ethical is merely provisional for anyone who takes himself to stand in an absolute relation to God — a collapse Johannes seems to want to block by calling the position a paradox rather than a doctrine? I would welcome a careful reconstruction of the argument at this point, with particular attention to what work the term teleological is doing: is the suspension justified by the end it serves, and if so, what kind of end could be higher than the ethical without being simply a competing ethical end?
