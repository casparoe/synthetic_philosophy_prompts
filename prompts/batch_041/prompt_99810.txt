I teach a 90-minute session on decision theory and paradoxes to a mixed group of about 18 advanced undergraduates in philosophy and economics, and I want an in-class activity that makes them feel the two-envelope paradox in their hands rather than hear it explained. The setup I want them to be given is minimal: pairs of envelopes, one containing twice the other, a participant opens one, and may then swap for the other. I want the group to generate the tempting expected-value argument for themselves -- small groups decide on a strategy in writing, with a justification, before any envelope is opened, and most of them should talk themselves into "always swap" without me prompting it. Then the rounds run, with points as stakes rather than money, and the always-swap crowd should finish behind, or at least fail to beat, the people who commit to something more cautious.

Please design the whole thing: the materials, how the amounts are generated, the payoff scheme, the pairing or role structure, a minute-by-minute timeline, and how many rounds to run so that the aggregate totals carry the lesson rather than the noise. I want at least one structural feature that turns the screw beyond the textbook version -- for instance a round in which two participants each hold one of a pair and each is separately offered the swap, so the group has to stare at the fact that both of them reason their way to swapping and both expect to gain; or a phase in which they may condition their choice on the observed amount using a cutoff, and can watch a threshold strategy actually earn more than either always-swap or never-swap. The debrief should walk back through what happened and name the machinery: the conditioning error of treating the observed amount as fixed while the other envelope is random, the role of the prior over amounts and why the flat-prior version is incoherent, where the genuine improvement lives, and why "swapping is always bad" is not the same as dissolving the paradox. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time -- participants suspecting the deck is stacked, high variance letting a few reckless players win by luck, people refusing to commit to a strategy, the group overgeneralizing from one session -- and how to handle each. Be honest about what the activity cannot reproduce and that I have to supply it myself, including any version of the puzzle in which a properly randomized rule beats a coin flip even without opening an envelope, and the fully general statement of why the naive argument has no well-defined expected value to compute.
