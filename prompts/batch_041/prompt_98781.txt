So I'm a novelist and I've ended up applying for one of those small arts-and-humanities grants — six months, not much money, and they want me to dress it up as research. Which is fine, I sort of am doing research, I just don't have the vocabulary for it. Heres the thing I keep chewing on and can't get straight.

I've been reading about the Confucian picture where you don't have a self sitting underneath your roles — you're a son, a mother, a friend, a neighbor, and that's what you are, there's no little person behind the curtain working the levers. People call this role ethics. The more I read it the more I think it's true about actual human beings and completely useless to me as a fiction writer, because every character I've ever cared about is someone who breaks a role. The daughter who won't do what the family needs. The soldier who walks off. The priest who loses it. And if the self just is its roles, then breaking one looks like either bad role-performance or like an appeal to some inner true self that role ethics says was never there. So which is it?

Here's what I want a proposal for, narrower than all that. Is there a coherent third thing between performing a role and exiting it — call it role distance, where you're genuinely in the role but standing a little apart from it, watching yourself do it — and can Confucian role ethics make room for that without smuggling back in the liberal individual it was built to argue against? And if it can't make room, is that a problem with role ethics or a problem with me wanting it?

For the proposal itself I need the usual bits. State the question so it actually has an answer, not just a mood. Say why the normal ways of reading role ethics don't already settle it — I notice people run together the claim that we in fact are our roles and the claim that we should cultivate them well, and I suspect role distance bites on the first one and not the second, but you tell me. Say how I'd actually spend six months on it, and be honest about what I can and cant do: I can read closely and I can write scenes and test the idea against characters, I can't do Sinology and I'm not going to learn classical Chinese. Say why that approach and not just, you know, reading more philosophy. Tell me what a real result would look like and what would count as the whole thing failing. And give me a scope that fits six months of a novelist's attention, not a dissertation.

Don't hand me a reading list or citations, I'll find my own texts — just talk about the positions and the arguments in plain english. Also the funders are not philosophers so keep the middle of it readable.
