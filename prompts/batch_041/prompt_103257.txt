I'm giving the commencement address next month at a joint ceremony for our philosophy and psych graduates. Half the room are psych people who know the situationist literature cold -- the seminary students in a hurry who step over the groaning guy in the alley, the dime in the payphone, all of it -- and the other half are grandparents who just want to hear something nice about their kid. Heres my problem: I actually believe in character. I've taught Aristotle for thirty years and I think these graduates can become better people, not just get lucky about which situations they end up in. But I'm not going to stand up there and pretend the situationist critique doesn't exist, because the psych half will definately check out and I'd be lying to them.

Write me the speech, ten minutes or so. It has to take the critique seriously and still say something real about character. And please don't do the cheap move of "well, situations matter and so does character, it's a bit of both" -- that's what everyone says and it's empty. I want it to actually commit to a line about what character can and can't be counted on to do. The one version I can half-defend, and I'd like you to build on it rather than swap it out, is something like: character isn't a store of willpower you draw on in the moment, it's the part of you that quietly arranges your life so the right thing is the easy thing -- so say what follows from that for someone starting a job, a marriage, a city, and be honest about where it fails. No study names, no citations, nobody's taking notes. Keep it warm, not lecturing, and in American English.
