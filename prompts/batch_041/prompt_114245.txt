I'm a third-year undergraduate, and I'm taking a moral philosophy course this term. My partner and I are deciding whether to start trying for a child now or to wait about two years. If we have a child now, I'll still be on a medication that carries a substantial chance of a condition that would make the child's life harder — not unbearable, but harder than it would otherwise be. If we wait, I can switch medications first, and the child we have then would very likely not have the condition. My partner thinks the timing is morally free. The argument is that the child we'd have now and the child we'd have later are different people: the later child is not harmed by our waiting, because in the world where we wait the earlier child never exists; and the earlier child is not harmed by our not waiting, because in the world where we don't wait the later child never exists. Since no one is worse off either way, there is nothing to weigh. We also already have a two-year-old, and having another child now would mean less money and attention for her.

In class we discussed a principle a visiting speaker proposed, which I'll paraphrase: an action is permissible if there is no one for whom it is worse than under some alternative the agent could have chosen — call it the no-losers test. My partner treats the test as settling the matter. If the test is right, though, it seems to also permit having a child now whose condition would be considerably worse, so long as the child's life would still be worth living; I can't tell whether that is a reductio of the test or a bullet I should bite. My own reaction is that knowingly choosing the harder life for the child we would actually have still stands in need of justification, and I would like to know whether that reaction can be defended.

Please advise me on how to think this through: what the no-losers test gets right and where it goes wrong; how our existing daughter's interests bear on the decision; and what, if anything, we owe the child we would have now.
