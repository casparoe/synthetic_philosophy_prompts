Bit of background so you know who's talking: I took early retirement from a career in geochemistry a couple years back and have been trying to teach myself philosophy since, mostly by sitting in on seminars at the local university and arguing with people over dinner. Lately I've gotten stuck on meritocracy and desert, and I've built an argument that I'm pretty sure proves too much, but I can't find where it breaks. I'd like you to find it for me.

Here's the argument, laid out the way I'd write a lab protocol:

1. To deserve credit or reward for an outcome, you have to be responsible for that outcome.
2. Being responsible for an outcome means being responsible for the things that produced it. If the factors that did the heavy lifting were things you had no hand in, then the outcome belongs to them as much as to you.
3. The factors that produce any achievement -- your talents, your temperament, your ability to sit still and work, your nose for a good opportunity, even your willingness to try -- were themselves produced by your genes and your upbringing, and you chose neither.
4. So you aren't responsible for the factors that produced your achievement.
5. By (2), if you aren't responsible for the factors, you aren't responsible for the outcome.
6. So you aren't responsible for your achievements.
7. So you don't deserve anything for them.
8. So meritocracy -- handing people more because they did better -- isn't merely imperfect, it's unjust in principle.

Why I'm suspicious: this looks airtight to me, and yet it seems to take down things I very much want to keep. If (7) holds, then the man who tortures a cat for fun doesn't deserve any blame either, since blame is just the negative version of the same claim, and my revulsion at him isn't fitting so much as a fact about my wiring. I don't believe that. But I also can't find the bad step.

Three people have offered me fixes and I understand none of them. The first said I'm confusing being the *origin* of a thing with being the *author* of it, which sounds to me like words rather than an argument. The second said desert attaches to the quality of will an act expresses -- so someone who acts out of contempt deserves blame for it even if she never chose to be contemptuous -- and that I'm treating responsibility like a fluid that has to flow all the way up the pipe to the source before any of it can be called yours. The third said there are two different things people mean by desert: the kind that's internal to a game (the runner who crosses the line first deserves the medal, by the rules, whatever it was that made her legs) and the kind that's supposed to reach back behind the rules to the person herself, and that my argument only kills the second one. But if the second is the only kind that could make meritocracy just in the first place, then killing it seems to be exactly the point, so I don't see how that's a rescue.

In my own field we'd say the argument is definately confusing the whole causal field with the one cause you select to explain something, but I don't know if that translates out of the sciences.

So: which step do I give up, and what does giving it up cost me? If I drop (2), what stops me from sliding into "well then anybody who did anything at all deserves everything"? If I keep (2) but reject (5), what is responsibility if it isn't transmitted up the causal chain? And whichever premise you tell me to drop, do I get to keep blame and punishment, or do I loose those too?
