I run a monthly "systems night" at the company I work at — mostly engineers and PMs, no philosophy training, roughly 20 people, 90 minutes in a room with a whiteboard and a projector. I want to run something that makes people actually *feel* a multipolar trap / race to the bottom — the thing where everyone acts sensibly, no villain anywhere, and the whole group still gets dragged somewhere none of them wanted, and where the obvious fix (everyone just agrees to stop) keeps collapsing for reasons that have nothing to do with anyone being a bad person. I've read enough to know this stuff gets filed under "Moloch," but I don't want a lecture about that and I definately don't want the overfishing or arms-race examples, everyone's heard those and they'll just nod along and learn nothing.

Here's the wrinkle I want the game built around, because it's the part I find genuinely interesting and I've never seen it made concrete: I want the trap to come from a *ratchet* rather than from a one-shot dominant strategy. So whatever move a player makes, they can never take it back — once you've escalated, or undercut, or spent the resource, that option is gone, everyone can see it's gone, and the only moves left point further in the same direction. On top of that, I want players to have a real way to try to form a pact: they can all publicly commit to holding a line, but make the pact fragile in a specific, structural way — say it only holds if it's unanimous and everyone can verify that everyone else held, and verification is costly or delayed. I want to watch a room full of smart adults try to hold a line and see whether the ratchet plus the verification problem beats them, and I want the debrief to be honest about which of those two did the actual work.

So: rules and materials, the payoff scheme (no real money), how many rounds and how much is revealed between them, the exact instructions I read aloud at the start, a timeline that fits 90 minutes with the debrief included, and then the debrief itself — which should map specific moments from the run onto the parts of the idea (ratchet vs coordination failure vs the verification problem), and should seperate what's actually settled from what's genuinely contested among people who study this stuff rather than presenting one camp's view as the consensus. Tell me honestly what a game like this structurally *can't* reproduce about real races to the bottom and what I'll just have to say out loud in the debrief, and what predictably goes wrong the first time I run it (my guess is someone tries to be clever and "win" it — how do I handle that without killing the vibe). Last thing: keep the word "Moloch" out of the whole thing until the very end, I don't want them pattern-matching to the meme before they've felt the thing.
