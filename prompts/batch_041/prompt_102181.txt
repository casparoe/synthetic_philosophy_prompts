I am a fourth-year PhD candidate in intellectual history, and I have been asked to deliver the keynote at the 150th anniversary dinner of the Wrenfield County Historical Society. The society is a small, underfunded institution with a divided board: the old guard wants a speech that celebrates the founders, the mill owners who endowed the archive, while a newer group of trustees—many of them descendants of mill workers and of the dispossessed Nipmuck families whose land the mills occupied—wants the speech to reckon with what the official history has left out. The dinner will be attended by both groups, plus local teachers, a few professional historians, and donors who have threatened to withdraw their support if the evening becomes, as one trustee put it, an exercise in self-flagellation. The occasion is also the unveiling of the society’s new negative inventory: a small exhibit of empty shelves and blank labels, each one marking a record that was never made, was destroyed in the 1971 fire, or has never been donated—ledgers, photographs, oral testimonies, and a set of deeds that the founding family still keeps in a private vault. The board has asked me to speak for about ten minutes, and I want the speech to be honest without being unusable.

I have been reading Nietzsche’s essay on the uses and disadvantages of history for life, and I want the speech to be structured by the three ways he says a person or a culture can relate to the past: monumental history, which finds models and inspiration in great deeds; antiquarian history, which preserves and reveres the ordinary and the local; and critical history, which puts the past on trial and tries to break with it. I do not want the speech merely to mention these three modes as a taxonomy. I want each mode to do structural work: the speech should have a movement that embodies monumental history and then exposes its characteristic failure—it mythologizes, it flattens, it excludes the unheroic; a movement that embodies antiquarian history and exposes its failure—it hoards, it turns piety into paralysis, it confuses the relic with the life; and a movement that embodies critical history and exposes its failure—it can slide into resentment, it can forget that some of the past is still living and still owed something. The negative inventory should be the central object of the antiquarian movement, and the board’s fear of donor backlash should be the pressure that makes the critical movement risky rather than merely rhetorical. Assume most of the room has not read Nietzsche and would resent a lecture; the ideas should be audible in the structure and the examples, not in the terminology. The speech should not quote Nietzsche or any other text; if you invoke him, paraphrase. It should be written for the ear, about 1,100 to 1,300 words, and it should end in a way that a divided room could actually applaud—not by resolving the tension between the three modes, but by making the tension itself the thing the society is being asked to inherit.
