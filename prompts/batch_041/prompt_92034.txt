Got into a four-hour argument with a friend last night about Marx's base and superstructure, and now I've somehow agreed to teach the session on it next week, so I'm cashing in on you.

Setup: second-year social and political theory course, seminar of 16, one 80-minute block, and I dont want to stand at the front and tell them what the thesis is. I want them to walk into it.

Here's what I actually want them to feel. There are two claims that everyone runs together. One is that the economic base causes the rest — somebody, a ruling class, a state, intends the laws and the religion and the ideas, and produces them in its own interest. The other is that the rest is explained by its function for the base — nobody intends any of it, the ideas that don't serve the material process just don't survive, and that counts as a perfectly good explanation on its own. The second claim can be true while the first is false, and I want sixteen undergrads to feel how strange that is in their bodies, not just agree with it on a slide.

So design me the game. 16 players, 80 minutes including the debrief, no money, materials printable or scroungable. The rules should do the work: some ongoing material process with real scarcity they're competing over (production, exchange, whatever you like), and an ideational layer sitting on top of it where they have to argue, justify, legislate, tell stories. The ideational layer has to have real consequences, otherwise nobody cares — but the things that survive in it should be the things that keep the material process running. Run it long enough that the pattern shows up on its own and somebody in the room says it out loud before I do.

Three things I'd like in there if you can:

- A variant, or a mid-game switch, where in one condition the idea-players genuinely choose freely and we watch what they pick, and in the other they're equally free but the payoffs mean the ideas that serve the material side get reinforced and the rest wither. Same people both times, so they can feel the difference between choosing something and being selected for it.
- At least one round where the ideational layer changes the material payoffs, so the feedback loop is real. The honest version of the thesis has the economy determining only in the end, with the rest reacting back and having effects of its own, and I want them to see the loop and then have to argue about whether it breaks the one-way story.
- If you can build in a layer where the material conditions fix which arena the fight happens in that round — not who wins it, just where it's staged — then please do, because that's the version of the claim I find hardest to teach and the most defensible.

And the usual stuff I always forget to ask for: exact rules, setup, materials and payoffs, written so I can run it cold. Where it predictably breaks the first time and what I do when it does. The debrief, structured so they get there themselves and I only supply the names at the very end. And be straight with me about the part the game can't do. My worry is this: I'm the one building the payoff structure, so of course the ideas that survive are the ones that serve the material side — a student is going to say the game assumed the conclusion, and they'll be right. Tell me how much that matters, whether there's a version that dodges it, and what I have to say out loud to cover the gap.
