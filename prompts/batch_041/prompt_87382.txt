I chair the Professional Conduct Appeals Panel of our chartered institute. On Thursday we hear an appeal that has turned, unexpectedly, into a dispute about the theory of meaning, and I would like a decision memo to circulate to the panel beforehand.

The facts. Dr. R., a member, wrote a reference letter for a departing postdoc who was applying for a lectureship. Every substantive sentence of the letter was, in its literal content, true. It confirmed that the candidate had met each deadline that had been fixed in writing; that no formal concerns about data handling had been recorded during the candidate's three years in the group; and that the candidate "would be well suited to a post with clearly specified and closely supervised tasks". The receiving department read the letter as a warning and did not shortlist the candidate; one of its members later described the letter to the candidate as "a knife wrapped in cotton wool". The candidate complained. Our Conduct Committee found a breach of the Code, which provides that a member "shall not, in any professional communication, mislead, whether by express statement or by implication", and imposed a reprimand. Dr. R. appeals against both the finding and the sanction.

The submissions. Counsel for Dr. R. argues that nothing misleading was stated, since every sentence was true; that any suggestion of unsuitability was drawn by the reader rather than conveyed by the writer, and that a regulator which punishes a member for an inference in a third party's mind punishes thought-reading; that Grice's distinction between what a speaker says and what a speaker implicates tells in the member's favour, since conversational implicatures are cancellable — the member could have added, without contradicting himself, that he meant to suggest nothing at all — and so cannot be treated as content he conveyed; and that a Code reaching cancellable suggestions gives members no fair notice of what is forbidden. The Institute's presenting officer replies that cancellability shows only that the suggested content was not part of what was said, not that it was not meant; that on the Gricean account a speaker means what he intends his audience to recognise that he intends; that the extreme narrowness of each sentence was plainly deliberate and plainly meant to be noticed; and that a Code which could only reach the reference that says something false would be a dead letter, since the whole art of the damning reference is to say nothing false at all.

What I need. Draft the decision memo: a clear recommendation, the reasoning the panel could actually publish, and an honest treatment of the strongest consideration against your recommendation. Decide the disposition — uphold the finding and the sanction, overturn them, or substitute something else (a lesser sanction, a finding without sanction, or guidance with no finding) — and say concretely what should happen next and by when. Because this appeal will be cited to future panels, you must also state, in a single sentence, the test we should apply to the words "or by implication" in the Code, and defend that test against the two obvious objections: that it is too vague to give members fair notice, and that it is too demanding of members who are merely being scrupulously accurate. Keep the memo under about eight hundred words, and do not cite statutes, decided cases, or secondary literature; the panel needs its own reasoning, not authority.

Then give a JSON object with exactly the following fields and nothing else:

- "disposition": one of "uphold", "overturn", "substitute".
- "outcome": the concrete order you recommend, including any time frame, e.g. when a substituted sanction takes effect.
- "governing_test": the single-sentence test for "or by implication".
- "utterance_analysis": an array with one object per substantive sentence of the letter, each containing "sentence" (a short label), "classification" (one of "entailed", "conversationally implicated", "conventionally implicated", "reader inference only"), "speaker_meant" (one of "yes", "no", "unresolved"), and "confidence" (a number between 0 and 1).
- "strongest_counterargument": one or two sentences.
- "guidance_for_future_cases": an array of strings.
- "overall_confidence": a number between 0 and 1.
