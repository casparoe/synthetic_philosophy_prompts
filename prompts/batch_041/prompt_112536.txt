I spent my working life as a geophysicist and have spent the years since retirement teaching myself philosophy of science, Bayesian confirmation theory in particular. I have read enough of the literature on the problem of old evidence to see the shape of the debate and, frankly, to be puzzled by it, because it has the look of a dispute that cannot end. The problem itself I understand: a body of data a scientist has known all along can evidently raise the credibility of a hypothesis, and the standard Bayesian apparatus seems unable to say how, since those data are already certain for her and conditioning on a certainty leaves her credences untouched. What I cannot see is why the replies have not settled the matter.

As I read them, they fall into a few families. One says: ask what the agent's credence would have been had she not known the data; the old data then confirm exactly as new data would. Another says: a rational agent's probability space already includes all the logical truths, so if the hypothesis was entailed or supported by what she knew, she should have been conditioning on that all along, and her failure to do so shows only that she was not the ideal agent the theory describes. A third relocates the issue, treating the scientific preference for novel predictions as a methodological or sociological fact about inquiry rather than a constraint on credence, so that no Bayesian obligation is violated when old evidence does the work. Each reply seems to answer the others' objections by changing the question, and the argument is now decades old with no sign of convergence.

I want a diagnosis, not another survey, and I don't need the bibliography. Is this a substantive disagreement, a verbal one, or several different questions sharing a single name — one about how credences should change, one about what makes a hypothesis well supported, one about why scientists prize novel predictions? Is a hidden formal question doing the work, namely whether any diachronic norm can license old-evidence confirmation without licensing too much? Or is the impasse a symptom of an idealization — logical omniscience — that any formal model of a bounded agent must make and that no amount of tinkering will remove? My own hypothesis, offered as a hypothesis, is that the parties disagree less about confirmation than about what the Bayesian formalism is a theory of, and that the counterfactual reply and the logical-omniscience reply give opposite verdicts about whether a scientist who raises her credence on old evidence is irrational, which is why neither side moves.

Here is the case that made me care. For thirty years I carried in my head a numerical agreement between two independently measured quantities in my field and regarded it as a curiosity. It became evidence for a particular mechanism when a colleague showed that the mechanism predicted the agreement from parameters fixed elsewhere; nothing about the measurements changed, yet my confidence in the mechanism jumped. Tell me what each of the standard replies says happened to me, and whether any of them describes the episode rather than redescribing it. Then tell me what would count as progress — a distinction, a formal result, a change of question — or argue that none should be expected, and why.
