I am drafting a short essay on Maimonides' treatment of the sacrificial laws and would like a critical reader's comments before deciding whether to develop it. The draft follows. I am not looking for sources or a reading list; I want the substance of the objections, an honest verdict on whether the central problem is real, and an indication of which of my repairs is worth keeping.

Draft: "The Weaning Problem"

1. Maimonides' best-known explanation of the sacrificial commandments is a concession theory, but the concession is not, as it is usually reported, an appeal to the weakness of a particular ancient people. In the Guide he begins from a general principle of governance, illustrated first from the development of animals (the newborn mammal cannot take dry food, so milk is provided until its limbs harden) and then from the Torah's own account of the exodus, where God avoids the short Philistine road because the people would turn back at the sight of war. The principle is stated flatly: "It is, namely, impossible to go suddenly from one extreme to the other." Applied to the sacrifices, the claim is that the Israelites were raised in a world in which worship simply was the slaughter of animals at temples housing images, with incense, a priesthood and its dues; God therefore "allowed these kinds of service to continue" and "transferred to His service that which had formerly served as a worship of created beings."

2. He anticipates the obvious objection (why not command the thing itself, and give the people the capacity to receive it directly?) and answers it with the precedent of the detour: a route that would fail is not a route. The detailed prescriptions are means, and means are vindicated by their working, not by their deducibility from a principle.

3. This is where I want to press. The animal-development analogy is a maturation analogy, and maturation has an exit: the point of milk is that the child is weaned. If the sacrificial service is milk, then the defence of it also predicts its supersession, and Maimonides cannot accept that, since he holds that the Law is not to be replaced. The details pull the same way, because in the following chapter the reasons he gives for them are frankly historical: the three species are commanded because the Egyptians reverenced the ram and abstained from killing sheep, some among the Sabeans worshipped demons they imagined as goats, and most idolaters objected to killing cattle; leaven and honey are forbidden and salt required because the idolaters offered leavened and sweet things and used no salt; and the point of it all is stated in medical terms, as when he says that "evil principles, the diseases of the human soul, are cured by other principles which are diametrically opposite." So the institution is defended by an analogy that implies outgrowing it, the details are fixed by what idolaters happened to do, and the whole is held to be permanent. A fourth loose end: Maimonides himself observes, of the offerings that are not obligatory, that "the omission of the sacrificial service on our part will not be reckoned to us a sin," which is an odd thing to say of an indispensable vehicle of the transfer.

4. Three repairs suggest themselves. (a) Transfer, not stage: the rite is not provisional material to be outgrown but an inherited vocabulary re-indexed to a new addressee, the same act directed to a different recipient; the milk analogy then covers only the manner of the Law's delivery, not the content of the rite, and nothing needs weaning because nothing was a stage. (b) Two tiers of reason: the reason for the institution (therapeutic, historical) is not the reason for its form (perfection and dignity in what is offered to God), so the details can be historically motivated while the institution's standing is not hostage to the history that first motivated it. (c) Bite the bullet: permanence is a property of the regimen as a whole, a community that must always be led from where it is, and not of each element being necessary in every possible world; the Law could have been otherwise in its particulars and still be, once given, irrevocable.

5. Nachmanides rejected the whole family of readings, in his commentary on Leviticus 1, and his objections matter because my repairs have to survive them. Of the account he says: "But these words are mere expressions, healing casually a severe wound and a great difficulty, and making the table of the Eternal polluted"; and, of the suggestion that the offerings exist to remove false belief: "Far be it that they should have no other purpose and intention except the elimination of idolatrous opinions from the minds of fools!" He gives three arguments: sacrifices were brought before idolatry existed, by Noah and Abel, so idolatry cannot be their reason; if the aim was to cure the reverence felt for these animals, slaughtering them to God would honour them further, whereas eating them (which the Law forbids) would have been the better cure; and Scripture calls the offering God's food, a pleasing odour, which is not the language of an instrument for curing a false belief. His own account is that the offering is for the offerer's sake, engaging "thought, speech and action," so that the one who brings it is formed by bringing it. My reply in the draft is that the first and third objections attack a psychological thesis stronger than Maimonides needs, since what matters is the public addressee of an established practice rather than the beliefs of any generation of worshippers, and that the second objection is a genuine difficulty for the account as he states it, since it shows the choice of species cannot be explained by its expected effect on the idolaters' minds.

What I want from you. (i) A verdict on whether the weaning problem is real or an artifact of my pushing an analogy past what it will bear; in particular, does the milk analogy in Maimonides carry a commitment to supersession, or does it concern only the manner of instruction? (ii) Which of (a) through (c) is the strongest repair, and whether any of them is actually available to Maimonides rather than to a tidier philosopher I have invented. (iii) Whether my answer to Nachmanides' species argument is a reply or merely a restatement, and if a better answer exists, what it is. (iv) The sentences you would cut, and any place where I overstate what the texts say. (v) Your overall judgment: is this a thesis worth developing, or a clever point that dissolves once the analogy is read correctly? I do not need references; I need to know where the argument is weak.
