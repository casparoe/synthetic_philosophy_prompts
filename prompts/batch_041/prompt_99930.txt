I've gotten into building historically faithful reproductions of antique furniture — reclaimed wood of the right species, hand-cut dovetails, hide glue, and I've taught myself to fake an old patina and even the faint wear a century of use leaves on a drawer runner, so that a piece I've made can pass a curator's eye. This has turned into a long-running argument with a friend who is convinced that the whole notion of an "original" is a superstition, and he has finally put his case in a form I can't wave away. Here it is. (1) Whatever value an object has for us must be grounded in something we could in principle come to know or to experience about it; a value resting on nothing knowable or experienceable would be a value we could not have. (2) So if a reproduction is indistinguishable from an original in every respect we could ever know or experience, the reproduction cannot be worth less than the original. (3) And for any original, such a reproduction is possible in principle — a copy that no test, no expert, and no instrument could ever separate from the original. (4) Therefore no original is worth more than its perfect double, and anyone who pays more for the original, grieves its loss more, or refuses to trade it for the copy is simply making a mistake; provenance, signatures, first editions, relics, and the whole apparatus of authenticity are sentiment we would be better off without. (5) But the same reasoning would apply to a perfect duplicate of a letter in my grandmother's handwriting, to an atom-for-atom rebuilt version of a house that was demolished, or to a wedding ring swapped overnight for an identical one — and I don't believe that preferring the real thing in those cases is merely a mistake I could be argued out of.

He says the argument is airtight and that the only honest move is to accept (4) and stop caring about originals. I say something has gone wrong, but when I try to name the bad step I end up either rejecting (1), which seems to cost me the entire idea that value is anything more than a private feeling, or rejecting (3), which I can't defend now that I've seen how convincing the fakes can be. Can you tell me where the argument breaks? I'd like to know which step you would give up, why it looked so plausible in the first place, and what I would have to accept about value, knowledge, and the identity of objects if I gave it up — and, if the argument really is valid and (1) is the price of honesty, what exactly I lose by paying it. I care most about keeping the ordinary preference in (5); I'm willing to give up more than I'd like elsewhere, but I want to see the bill before I do.
