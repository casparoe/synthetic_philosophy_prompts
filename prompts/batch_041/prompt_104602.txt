I'm a second-year undergraduate in a seminar that pairs the philosophical literature on weakness of will with the behavioural-economics literature on hyperbolic discounting, and I've been asked to run the session that tries to connect the two. I'd like to build a decision tree for the handout: it should take a reader from one starting commitment to a named position, with every branch node phrased as a question a reader can answer without already knowing which position it leads to.

The starting commitment is this: I accept that someone can sincerely judge that, all things considered, she ought to do A, and yet voluntarily and knowingly do B instead. Readers who reject that premise should also have somewhere to land, so the tree needs an intellectualist leaf reached by denying it (the view on which no one acts against what she genuinely thinks best, and apparent cases are failures of knowledge).

Please build the tree from the logical space first and from names second: tell me the questions that carve up the space and which combinations of answers they permit, and only then place the actual positions in it. I want at least these four branch questions, in an order you justify rather than just list:

1. Is the act done for a reason at all, or is it more like a lapse that bypasses the agent's agency?
2. Is the judgement she acts against a full belief about what she ought to do, or only an expression of what she wants, or a belief she holds without being moved by it?
3. Is the conflict located inside a single moment of choice, or across time, between an earlier judgement and a later act?
4. Does the theory treat the agent as one agent with competing motivations, or as a plurality of temporally separate selves whose preferences can diverge?

Two stipulative distinctions I want the tree to use, because the course readings lean on them and I haven't found settled names for either. Call a case thin when the judgement and the action concern the act under the same description, and thick when they concern it under different descriptions — she judges that she ought to study, and what she actually does is open the app. Call a case diachronic when the judgement was formed at an earlier time than the action, and synchronic when the two are simultaneous. I suspect most textbook examples are thin and synchronic, and that this is why the two literatures talk past each other, so I want the tree to make that visible.

The leaves should include, at minimum: the intellectualist denial; Aristotelian weakness proper and its impetuous counterpart as two separate leaves; the line associated with Davidson, on which the akratic act is fully intentional and caused by a reason, but not by the agent's all-things-considered judgement; the Frankfurt-style line about first- and second-order desires and the unwilling addict; a planner-doer or multiple-selves line in which diachronic conflict is handled by precommitment; a line on which akrasia is simply time-inconsistent preference and hyperbolic discounting is the whole story with no separate failure of will; and a deflationary line on which nothing here deserves a distinct name. Also include one unnamed leaf for a position I'll describe, and say whether it matches anything you know or is genuinely unoccupied: the view that the failure is not motivational but one of uptake, where the judgement simply is not present to the agent at the moment of action and no struggle occurs.

These cases must appear as nodes or as the tests that separate leaves, not just as illustrations: a person knowingly deletes her own website blocker the night before a deadline; a person acts against her all-things-considered judgement with no felt struggle at all; a person is glad afterwards that she was akratic; the thick case above, where judgement and action concern the act under different descriptions; and a case where an observer predicts her behaviour better from her past choices than from her stated judgement.

Two more requirements. Flag any pair of leaves the tree cannot separate, rather than papering over it — I'd rather know which distinctions are doing no work. And list the combinations of answers that no position occupies, with one line each on whether that's because the view is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored.

Aim for eight to ten leaves, written for second-year undergraduates, and give each leaf one sentence on its most awkward commitment.
