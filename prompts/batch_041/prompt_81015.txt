I sit on the research ethics committee of a rehabilitation hospital, and I have to write the committee's opinion in a case that has become personal: the patient is a man I have known for twenty years, and although I disclosed that and offered to recuse myself, the chair wants me to draft it because I am the only member who understands the device. He has advanced ALS and communicates through an implanted decoder that converts his neural activity into a small set of words. The question before us is whether an affirmative he produces that way can authorise anything — withdrawing a treatment, enrolling him in a further trial — or whether it is a reading of his brain rather than a statement by him. One member holds that a signal means what it was selected to carry, and concludes that a decoder built by engineers rather than shaped by evolution carries nothing at all; another holds that a signal means whatever a competent interpreter can reliably read off it, which would let the clinical team's own confidence settle the matter. The family says he authorised this mode of consent years ago, while he could still speak, and one member thinks that is the only legitimate route to a binding answer. Draft the opinion I will circulate before Thursday: a recommendation the committee can vote on, the reasoning behind it, and a fair statement of the strongest case against the position I am rejecting, written so that members without the theoretical vocabulary can still use it. It should say what would have to be true before a decoded affirmative could bind us, including what would count as the device having misreported him.
