I'm a novelist, and I'm writing a book set in the years before 1914 whose spine is the Anglo-German naval race — not the diplomacy, which I have, but the mechanics by which two sensible governments kept buying ships that neither could afford and that, in the end, neither could use. I want you to read that race through the dollar auction and then tell me how far the reading actually holds.

The auction as I understand it: a dollar is put up, bidding proceeds in small increments, the highest bidder pays what they bid and takes the dollar, and the second-highest bidder also pays what they bid and receives nothing. Once two bidders are in past a few cents, the second-highest is facing a certain loss that can be escaped only by raising, and the increments are small enough that each raise is individually defensible. The auction ends in the ruin of at least one bidder. I have also read the literature on escalation of commitment, which looks to me like the same trap described in softer language; tell me if that conflation is unfair.

Treat the following as given, since I've done the reading and don't need it handed back to me: the Royal Navy was built to a standard of parity with the next two fleets combined; Germany's successive navy laws were aimed less at beating Britain at sea than at making an alliance with Britain worth Britain's while; the arrival of the all-big-gun battleship reset the race to zero and made every existing ship obsolete; Britain answered German programmes with more ships than Germany laid down; the two governments came close to a negotiated ratio of fleets and failed to sign it; and the German fleet was largely idle after Jutland and was scuttled by its own crews rather than surrendered.

Now do the mapping properly — who the bidders are, what the dollar is, what the increments are, and above all who the auctioneer is, because I cannot find one and I suspect the absence matters more than the analogy admits. Then say where the model misdescribes the case. Two features I especially want pressed: in the auction the prize is fixed, whereas in a naval race the prize is a ratio to what your rival spends, so a higher bid may buy nothing; and the auction has no stopping point short of ruin, whereas this race stopped. Say what the case does to the theory in return — confirmation, anomaly, or a case the theory never anticipated.

Take a position rather than presenting the options: is the dollar auction the right frame for this race, or is escalation of commitment doing the real work while the auction is a metaphor earning its keep by doing none? Lead with your verdict in the first sentence, then give the reasoning. No preamble, no restating of my question.
