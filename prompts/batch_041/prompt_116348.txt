Over the holidays I got into it with my brother-in-law about whether Jainism's many-sidedness idea (anekantavada — I've seen it spelled anekāntavāda too, sorry if I'm mangling it) is a real philosophical position or just the ancient Indian version of everyone's entitled to their opinion. His line was that it's relativism with better branding: no view is the whole truth, every view is partly right, so who's to say, etc. I couldn't really answer him. Then I read somewhere that Jains also believe the enlightened ones — the Jinas, the kevalins — perceive everything at once, every side simultaneously, and that seems to cut the other way. If one viewpoint can be the complete picture, then the rest of us aren't holding equally valid truths, we're holding partial ones, and there's a direction in which they're incomplete. He said that's just a priestly escape hatch, that the many-sided part does all the real work and the omniscient part is decoration. I said maybe the doctrine was never about truth being multiple in the first place, but about us being stuck seeing one face of the thing at a time — more like humility than like pluralism. There are apparently two related doctrines, something like syadvada (about statements being true only in a certain sense) and nayavada (about standpoints), and I honestly can't tell whether that distinction settles the question or just relabels it.

So what I actually want is an essay on this: is anekantavada a genuine pluralism about truth, or is it a doctrine about the limits of our knowledge that quietly keeps one privileged standpoint — the omniscient one — in reserve and never gives it up? Take a side and defend it; don't do the both-readings-are-valid thing, since that's exactly what I'm suspicious of. And I'd like you to say something about whether the two readings even come apart in practice: if reality-has-many-sides and we-can-only-ever-see-one-side lead to the same advice about how to argue with somebody, maybe the whole distinction is academic. I'm not religious, I just like this stuff, so don't pull your punches.

Write it as an essay. Don't bother citing texts or naming scholars — I won't check and I don't care, it's the arguement I want. Start the essay with your verdict in the first sentence, then give the reasoning, no throat-clearing and don't restate what I asked.
