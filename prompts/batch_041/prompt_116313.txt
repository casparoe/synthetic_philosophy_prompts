I run a departmental reading group — eight or nine participants, mostly first- and second-year philosophy graduate students with a few visitors from law and cognitive science — that meets for seventy-five minutes, and nobody will have read anything beforehand. The topic is the problem of the wrong kind of reasons. I want a plan for the session, not an explanation of the problem. Lay out the order in which the components have to be introduced and why each depends on the ones before it; the one example that should carry the discussion and what it must have and lack to bear that weight; the misconceptions the group will predictably arrive with or develop, together with the exact point in the sequence where I should head each one off; a closing check that would show me whether they have understood the problem rather than merely learned its name; and a short list of questions I should explicitly decline to settle at this level. The plan must not depend on any assigned reading.

Use the following case as the carrying example. A junior editor at a publishing house is told that she will be promoted to a senior position — which she badly needs and cannot get elsewhere — only if she comes sincerely to regard her employer's unpublished novel as a work of real literary merit. She can read it repeatedly in the most sympathetic light and avoid the colleagues who mock it; no one is asking her to lie, and the promotion turns on what she actually comes to think. The novel is competent and unremarkable, and she cannot tell either way with confidence. The session should end with the group able to see why this case bears on the thesis that something is good just in case it is fitting to value it, without being told that the case settles that thesis.

Two further requirements. First, the sequence must include a contrast case that strips away one feature of the main case at a time, so that the group can see precisely which feature makes the offered consideration a reason to bring the attitude about rather than a reason bearing on whether the attitude is correct. Second, at some point the plan must handle the reply that the offered consideration is simply a reason of another kind — a reason to want the attitude, or to do something — and must show where that reply helps and where it leaves the hard question untouched. Finally, because the reason in the main case is self-interested, it invites the mistake of thinking that wrong-kind reasons are just selfish ones; include a variant in which the consideration pushing the editor toward the attitude is other-regarding instead, and say what that variant is meant to expose.

Write your answer in American English.
