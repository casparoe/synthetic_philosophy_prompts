I spent thirty-five years as a behavioural ecologist, most of that time writing and defending protocols for work with animals, and since retiring I have been reading moral philosophy with more appetite than method. For the past two years I have also sat on my old department's animal care and use committee, and I have come to think that our hardest problems are philosophical rather than veterinary.

The committee is required to be consistent, and is audited against its own record. We work from a handbook: the standard framework of replacement, reduction and refinement, plus a growing list of standing decisions about housing, anaesthesia, humane endpoints and so on. That list fails us in a particular way. Again and again the feature that decided a case one month is present in the next case and points the other way. Pain is a cost, except when the alternative is a slow death from the very condition under study, or when the pain is momentary and the animal's species-typical life is otherwise undisturbed. Social housing is a benefit, except for a species for which enforced proximity is itself the chronic stressor. An aversive procedure is a harm, except when it is the only route to a result that would spare a great many animals later. I recognise this pattern from my own trade: the rules of method I taught for decades were not exceptionless, and the exceptions could not be listed in advance, only handled well or badly by someone who knew what the case was.

The position I keep finding myself drawn to is that moral reasons are context-dependent, that the very same feature can count for and against depending on what else is true in the case, and that no finite list of principles will capture this. Its opponent, as I understand it, need not be a rule-worshipper: a principle can be hedged, defeasible, and applicable only with judgment. So the practical question is not whether to have rules but how much of our reasoning can be codified, and where the codification should stop.

Please write the procedure. It should tell the committee, for a single protocol, the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops with a refusal, and where it hands the decision to a person because the question is one of values rather than facts. It should say what weight a past decision carries, whether precedent is a reason or only a reminder, and how we are to notice that a feature has changed polarity in the case before us. It should say when deliberation is finished. And it should mark, without flattery, the steps that are judgments dressed up as procedure. Constraints: we meet monthly, we have roughly twenty minutes per protocol, and the members are a veterinarian, two bench scientists, a statistician, a community member and me; the reasons we write down become the precedent we are later held to.

Then show it working on three cases, giving each as the written decision we would issue: single-housing a gregarious primate for a behavioural study; a learning paradigm requiring repeated brief aversive stimulation in a species we have approved it for before, under circumstances that were not these; and lethal sampling from a declining wild population where the science is genuinely good. At least one of these should come out in a way I will not enjoy defending to my colleagues. Close by telling me whether what you have written is a particularist procedure at all, or whether I have quietly become a generalist in order to have one. I don't want a reading list; I want the thing itself.
