Hey, I need a hand with something that I'm pretty sure is a philosophy question wearing a work problem's clothes, so I'm hoping you can put on the hat of a philosopher of history rather than a generic advice column. Before you answer, spend a few lines brainstorming the different things a person could mean when they say a piece of writing about the past is objective — I want to see the options laid out before you commit to one, because I suspect they come apart. Then give me your actual view. Take your time with it, I'm not in a rush.

Here's the situation. I've been asked to write the short official history of a local firm for it's 150th anniversary booklet — the old family-run works that used to employ basically the whole town before it shut in the 90s. The historical society's director is my de facto editor, and she's adamant that it be strictly factual: dates, decisions, documents, no adjectives, no editorialising, and above all no narrative — she has a real bee in her bonnet about historians who sneak a story in where there ought to be just the record. She keeps saying the booklet should show how things actually were. I'm half with her and half convinced she's asking for something that can't exist, and I can't tell which half is right.

Now the bit that's got me stuck. The firm's famous big bet in 1958 — the one every obituary credits to the founder's foresight, the decision that supposedly saved the place — I've been through the minute books and the letter files, and it looks like he made the call for a daft unrelated reason, a spat with a supplier, and it only worked out because an export order landed two years later off the back of a personal favour, and because their main competitor burned down. So the celebrated decision was, on the record, a poor decision that happened to pay off. Do I write that? And if I do, am I being more honest, or am I just swapping one tidy story for a different one — a cynical story about luck — and calling it objectivity because it feels less sentimental? Her rule, no story, just the record, also bothers me in a way I can't articulate: a mate of mine who teaches philosophy reckons that even a bare year-by-year list of events is already a kind of story, since somebody had to decide where the list starts and stops and which happenings count as events worth listing. That sounds right to me and also like it might be a cheap gotcha.

So: what would it even mean for this booklet to be objective? Is her demand coherent, or is sticking to the record itself a stylistic choice like any other? What do I do about the 1958 story, and is there a real difference between being fair to a dead man and being accurate about him? And practically, what should I write, given that the family's descendants fund the society and get to see the draft before it goes to print? I'd definately rather have your actual view than a both-sides summary.
