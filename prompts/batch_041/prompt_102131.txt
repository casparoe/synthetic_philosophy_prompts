A colleague of mine is a philosopher of physics and a committed panpsychist: she holds that experience is a fundamental and ubiquitous feature of the physical world rather than a special product of brains, and she has thought harder than most about what that implies for ethics. Over the years she has given me off-the-cuff verdicts on which physical systems have a welfare of their own — which things can be wronged. They are:

- a single atom ionized in a gas: cannot be wronged;
- a boulder split by frost: cannot be wronged;
- a household thermostat cycling to hold a set point: cannot be wronged;
- a virus sitting inert outside a host cell: cannot be wronged;
- a tree girdled so that it slowly dies: can be wronged;
- a nematode recoiling from a noxious chemical: can be wronged;
- a rat in pain: can be wronged;
- an adult human being in good health: can be wronged;
- a large language model producing a paragraph: cannot be wronged;
- a Bose–Einstein condensate of a million atoms: cannot be wronged.

I have to take a position on a new case in a paper we are co-writing, and I would rather predict her reaction than guess it. The new case: a self-sustaining, self-repairing pattern of the kind found in a Belousov–Zhabotinsky reaction — or, if that example is too contrived, a candle flame — which holds itself far from equilibrium by continuously consuming fuel and dissipating energy, and which re-establishes its own form after small perturbations.

Do not simply hand me a verdict. I want the candidate criteria that fit her existing verdicts equally well; what each candidate predicts about the new case; which criterion she is most likely actually running, with the reasons for preferring it; which of her earlier verdicts is the closest analog to the new case; and a further case, different from the new one, that would most cleanly separate the criteria that survive. State how confident you are in each of these claims, and single out the one point you are least sure of.
