I work on the practical-assessment side of a training programme that certifies wind-turbine maintenance technicians, and I have been handed the job of rebuilding our competency sign-off sheets. The problem is that our current sheets blur two very different things. A trainee can pass the written and oral portions — reciting torque sequences, describing lockout-tagout order, explaining why a gearbox overheats — and then, on the tower, stand at the hatch unable to move, or torque a bolt in the wrong order the moment the light is bad and someone is waiting on the radio. The reverse happens too: we have a technician who cannot tell you in words why he does what he does, who has never once been able to explain his reasoning, and who nevertheless walks past a nacelle and says the bearing is going and is right. Our sign-off sheet treats the first as competent and the second as a problem case, and I no longer believe either call is defensible. I have read a little about the distinction between knowing how and knowing that, and about the philosophers who insist the distinction collapses because knowing how is just a special case of knowing that something is so, and I have the nagging sense that this is exactly the tangle our sheet is caught in. What I want from you is not a summary of that debate but a working instrument.

Please act as both a philosopher of knowledge and an assessment designer, and think carefully before you write anything — start by laying out for yourself what the live positions actually are and which of their distinctions can survive contact with a 45-minute practical assessment run by an assessor who has no philosophy training. Then give me a decision procedure my assessors can actually run, task by task: the questions to ask in order, what counts as sufficient evidence at each one, which steps are answered by observation and which by interview, where the procedure stops with a no, and where it must hand the decision to a human because the question has turned into one about what we value (reliability under pressure? breadth across models? the ability to teach an apprentice?). It should say plainly which of its steps are judgment calls dressed up as procedure, and it should be honest at the end about what it cannot do. Ground the whole thing in what the philosophy of knowledge genuinely has to say about the difference between being able to do something and knowing that something is the case — the regress worry about rule-following, the cases where someone seems to know how without being able to do it and to be able to do it without knowing how, and the question of whether an ability has to be reliable or transferable to a new situation before we call it knowledge. Assume the assessor has one shift, no video review, and no second opinion available, and that the programme director signs off on whatever the sheet produces.

Then run the procedure end to end on three trainees. First, one who can recite every torque specification from memory and freezes at the hatch. Second, the one who diagnoses a failing bearing by ear and cannot explain himself. Third, one who performs flawlessly on our shop rig but has only ever touched one turbine model. I expect the procedure to give me at least one answer I will not like, and I would rather you tell me where it does than smooth it over. You do not need to cite anything; I want the reasoning, and I want it usable by someone standing on a service lift in the wind.
