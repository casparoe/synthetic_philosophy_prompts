I was at a dinner party last week with two friends, Rosa and Dev, and the conversation turned to a decision our mutual friend Sam had just made. Sam had been offered a job in another city that would pay enough to cover the cost of an experimental treatment for his younger sister; accepting it meant leaving the neighborhood legal-aid clinic he had founded and run for years, which would almost certainly close without him. He took the job. Rosa and Dev, who both know Sam well, disagreed sharply about whether it even made sense to evaluate his choice.

(1) Rosa said the two things at stake—his sister’s health and the clinic’s survival—are incommensurable. There is no common currency in which to add them up, so there is no fact of the matter about which choice was better. He faced a hard choice, not a wrong one. (2) Dev replied that incommensurability does not mean the choice is beyond rational assessment. Sam still had reasons, and some reasons can be more urgent than others without being measured on a single scale. His sister’s need was a directed reason: it was addressed to him in particular, not merely a fact in the world. Directed reasons can have priority without a common measure. (3) Rosa answered that this is just a ranking in disguise. If you say his sister’s claim was more urgent, or that it had priority, you have put the two claims on the same scale—just with a different unit. And if you refuse to rank them, then the choice was settled by something outside the reasons, like his preference or his gut. (4) Dev said not every comparison requires a common measure. You can say one painting is better in color and another in composition without assigning either a single score. In the same way, you can say his sister’s claim had a kind of priority that the clinic’s claim did not, without claiming that both were being weighed in the same units. (5) Rosa responded that the analogy describes a comparison; it does not make a decision. At some point Sam had to act, and acting requires a verdict. If the verdict does not follow from a common measure, it is either arbitrary or it is produced by a commitment that is itself unranked. And if commitments can settle choices without being ranked, then any choice can be rationalized after the fact by whatever commitment the person happens to have. (6) Dev said commitments are not arbitrary. They have histories and structures. Sam’s relationship with his sister is a lifelong relation of dependence; the clinic is a project he started. Those are different kinds of reasons, and the difference can guide choice without a single scale. Sam did not compare and then choose; he chose in light of who he already was and what he was already responsible for. (7) Rosa said that is still ranking by history, which is still a ranking. She does not see how Dev gets a rational verdict out of different kinds of reasons without either a common measure or a decision that is not fully rational. (8) Dev replied that Rosa is assuming rational choice must be codifiable in advance, like a formula. Practical reason can be uncodified and still responsive to reasons. The fact that we cannot state the exchange rate does not mean there is no rational difference between the options.

Please adjudicate this exchange move by move. For each numbered move, say whether it is clearly mistaken, partly right, or clearly right, and briefly justify your rating. In doing so, please distinguish among (a) the claim that the values are incommensurable, (b) the claim that the choice is therefore not rationally assessable, and (c) the claim that some reasons can have priority without a common measure. Then say who, if either, is closer to right overall, and identify what both of them are missing. I am reporting this as a bystander; I do not have a stake in either position.
