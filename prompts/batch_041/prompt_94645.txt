I'm building a case for a paper on whether Solomonoff induction actually vindicates Occam's razor, and I need it constructed to order rather than borrowed from the literature. Design a scenario in which a device emits a single binary stream, observed up to some finite point, and two rival predictions about the next bit come apart as follows.

(i) The prediction favoured by shortest description — the minimum two-part/MDL code, and equally what informal Occam's razor would pick — must lose under Solomonoff's universal mixture, which must place more than half its posterior weight on the other prediction.

(ii) The divergence must come from the mechanism I care about: the losing prediction is carried by a single hypothesis, the winning prediction by a family of mutually distinct programs, each individually longer than that single hypothesis, whose weights sum to more than its own. Give explicit lengths in bits — shared core, per-member parameter, member count — and exhibit the sum against the single program's weight, so that the domination is checkable arithmetic rather than a gesture.

(iii) Everything must be a genuine program: no oracles, no undecidable shortcuts, no members that differ only in unobservable padding, and each member must actually output the observed prefix before making the prediction you attribute to it.

(iv) The observed data should be short enough to write down, even if the parameter lengths are large.

Then, for the case as built: give the shortest-description verdict, the mixture verdict, and the reason they part company; say which feature of the construction is doing the work and what becomes of the divergence when that feature is removed; say whether the divergence could arise between two singleton hypotheses, and why; and give the strongest reply open to someone who holds that these two criteria cannot come apart, with an honest note on what that reply costs.
