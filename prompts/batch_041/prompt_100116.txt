ok so I'm putting together a poetry-and-economics evening for my reading group and I want you to write the centerpiece poem. You're a historian of economic thought who also happens to write verse, so bring both hats, and before you draft anything spend a beat just brainstorming the stages the poem has to move through, then write it. Think carefully — the structure here is doing real work, not decoration.

The subject is Hume on money and trade. Specifically the thing he worked out about the balance of trade: he first floated it in a letter to Montesquieu in 1749 and then argued it in the essay "Of the Balance of Trade," in the Political Discourses of 1752, aimed at the mercantilists who thought a nation should hoard bullion and run a permanent export surplus. Hume's move was that the hoard defeats itself. A country running a surplus pulls in gold; the extra gold bids up domestic prices; dearer exports and cheaper imports follow; so the gold drains back out again, and the balance comes home to zero. He also saw that it isn't instant — money isn't simply neutral in the short run, an inflow can actually stir industry for a while before prices catch up, so there's a false spring before the correction bites. And he liked to think of money as the oil that keeps the wheels of trade turning rather than as one of the wheels. That last bit matters for the poem: the treasure the merchant is so proud of is just the stuff that lets the real trade move.

I want a villanelle. Two refrains, and they should be the two poles of the argument — one is the mercantilist's conviction (a nation's wealth is its bullion, hold the gold), the other is the correction (the gold you hold is the gold that leaves). But don't let them be slogans. The whole trick of a villanelle is that the same line keeps coming back and its meaning drifts each time it does, and I want that drift to be exactly the lag Hume describes: the first time the correction-refrain lands it should sound like a warning nobody's listening to, and by the end it should sound like a receipt. When the two refrains meet in the last two lines they shouldn't just sit side by side — they should turn out to be one sentence, the hoard and its undoing.

Accuracy bar: someone in the group who's actually read the Political Discourses should nod rather than wince. They should be able to trace surplus, inflow, rising prices, dying export, outflow, balance, stage by stage, inside the poem. But don't let it read like a textbook with line breaks — I'd rather it be about a merchant's certainty and its slow humiliation, or about the gold itself, whatever voice makes the mechanism feel like a fate rather than a diagram. Meter and rhyme can be a little loose if the refrains carry their weight.

Keep it in the weeds but legible to people who've never heard of the price-specie-flow mechanism — render it in the poem's own images, don't name-drop the quantity theory like everyone's supposed to know it. And please don't quote Hume at me in quotation marks; I don't want to get his wording wrong in front of the group. Your own words for his idea is the whole point.
