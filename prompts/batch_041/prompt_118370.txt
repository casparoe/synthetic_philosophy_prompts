I'm writing a long essay for a political theory module on whether national character is a coherent notion at all, and whether it can ever be acceptable to generalise about peoples, and I've got stuck on a reconstruction exercise that I'd like you to help me think through properly. The exercise is to work out what Hume would say about questions that did not exist in his lifetime. This is not a request for his recorded opinions, since he never had any on most of these matters; it is a request to derive the most defensible position from his principles and from the one essay in which he treats the topic head-on, "Of National Characters." In that essay he separates moral causes of national manners, such as the nature of government and the plenty or penury in which people live, from physical ones, such as climate and air; he is sceptical about the physical causes and leans almost entirely on the moral ones; and he holds that men of sense reject undistinguishing judgments while still allowing that "each nation has a peculiar set of manners, and that some particular qualities are more frequently to be met with among one people than among their neighbours." Given that starting point, I want his likely verdicts on five things that only arose later: first, the twentieth-century anthropological tradition that tried to characterise whole nations by their child-rearing practices and which has since been largely abandoned; second, contemporary cross-national survey and personality research, where country-level differences are statistically detectable but small, often unstable, and frequently fail to replicate, and where some critics argue the instruments measure the peculiarity of the sampled populations as much as anything about the nations themselves; third, the use of group-level statistical generalisations in insurance pricing, credit scoring, policing, and immigration screening, where the generalisation is actuarially useful but distributively unfair to individuals who happen to fall on the wrong side of it; fourth, the legal regime that has grown up around this in many liberal states, in which some group generalisations are permitted in commercial and policy contexts and others are prohibited outright; and fifth, the nationalist move, which postdates him by roughly a generation, of arguing that shared manners or a shared culture ground a claim to political self-determination.

I'd like the answer to do three things. First, give his likely verdicts case by case, then state the principle that unifies them, and keep two questions separate throughout: whether national character is a coherent descriptive notion, and whether it is ever permissible to act on such a notion once one has it. Second, tell me where the reconstruction is doing real work rather than merely reporting Hume, marking clearly which parts are direct inferences from the essay, which are extrapolations from his wider philosophy, and which are your own guesses about the man and his temper. Third, tell me what would change your mind: which further text, which argument, or which consideration about the limits of his moral-causal framework would push you towards a different reconstruction, and where in your own reasoning you think you are most likely to be going wrong. Please don't supply a bibliography or citations; I can find those myself, and I would much rather you reason from the position than gesture at sources.

One further wrinkle I want handled honestly. The body of the essay condemns undistinguishing judgments, and yet Hume later attached a much harsher footnote on racial differences to the same essay, one that many readers now regard as straightforwardly racist. I'd like you to say whether that footnote is continuous with the essay's argument or a departure from it, and what that tells us about how far his principle about national character can be pressed. Please do not reproduce the footnote's wording unless you are confident of it; if you are unsure what it says, reason about the tension in general terms rather than reconstructing it. Write in British English.
