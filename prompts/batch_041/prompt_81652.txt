I've been running a fortnightly reading group for about two years — a dozen or so regulars, mostly the sort of people who read rationalist blogs, a few with formal training in maths or philosophy. For our next ninety-minute session I want to do something on the difference between working inside a system and stepping outside it — what I've seen called "jootsing", jumping out of the system — and I want to use the MU-puzzle as the vehicle.

The setup, in case you want it stated plainly: you begin with the string MI, and the only legal moves are four rewrite rules. (1) Any string ending in I may have a U appended. (2) From Mx you may move to Mxx — the part after the M is doubled. (3) Any occurrence of III may be replaced by a single U. (4) Any occurrence of UU may be deleted. The challenge is to produce MU. It cannot be done, and the proof that it cannot be done is not a move in the game at all: it is an observation about every string the game can ever reach, namely that the number of I's is never a multiple of three. MU has no I's, so it lies outside the reachable set — but no sequence of legal moves will ever tell you that, and an exhaustive search would run forever without discovering it.

That is the thing I want people to feel in their bones: the wall you hit is not a hard search problem, it is a different kind of fact, invisible from inside, obvious from outside — and there is a specific relief that comes with the jump. I do not want to give a lecture, and I do not want the invariant named until the very end of the debrief, if at all.

Design the session. What I need from you:

- A runnable activity with rules, roles (if any), materials, and a minute-by-minute timeline for ninety minutes and roughly fourteen people. Physical or paper-and-pen is fine; assume a room with a large whiteboard or a wall we can cover in paper.
- Some stake, or some way of making the failure bite, without real money — a scoring scheme, a forfeit, a leaderboard, whatever does the work.
- The idea has to show up in what the group does, not in what I say afterwards. Ideally they arrive at the impossibility themselves, or at least feel it before I name it. Tell me how the staging achieves that and where the seams are.
- A debrief that maps particular moments in the activity onto the inside/outside distinction, the role of an invariant, and the difference between "I cannot find a proof" and "there is no proof" — and that connects it, without overclaiming, to the broader theme of systems that cannot see their own limits.
- What will predictably go wrong the first time I run it — including the failure modes I am least likely to anticipate — and how I should handle each.
- An honest note on what the activity cannot reproduce and that I will have to supply myself. I am aware the MU-puzzle is a toy, and that the real Gödelian twist (the unprovability itself being provable within a stronger system, self-reference, and so on) is nowhere near it; I would rather you tell me plainly where the analogy stops than let me walk into the debrief thinking I have staged more than I have.

Two things about the framing, since I have seen how these go. First, do not build in an "aha" that is really just me telling them the answer more slowly; if the only way the group can reach the invariant is by being nudged to it step by step, say so and own it. Second, if the whole design is a bad fit — if this puzzle cannot be staged as a group activity that produces the feeling I am after, or if the "jump" I am describing is really just ordinary mathematical problem-solving dressed up in dramatic language — tell me straight rather than designing around it. I would rather get a "this will not do what you want, here is what it will do" than a polished activity that quietly assumes the premise. Where you are making a judgement call about how much of the idea the activity can carry, flag it as a judgement call and say how confident you are.
