For several months, two colleagues of mine on a municipal design-review panel have been locked in an argument about the city's new adaptive traffic-signal system, and they have finally asked me to referee. I could not, and I would rather have your judgment than my own. What I want is a point-by-point adjudication of their exchange as it actually unfolded, not a general essay on the topic.

The system allocates green time at intersections by a formula that balances vehicle throughput against a rough safety margin. In practice it has sharply lengthened pedestrian waits in the poorer eastern district while smoothing traffic in the wealthier center. It was commissioned with the vendor's factory defaults, which no one on the council reviewed, and its data model has fields for vehicle counts, queue lengths and emissions but none for pedestrian delay, accessibility or equity.

Their argument went roughly as follows.

First round. A said the signals are political artifacts. They ration a rivalrous public good — priority at the intersection — and they ration it unequally, favoring the car over the pedestrian and the center over the periphery; a device that distributes a contested good in a patterned way is doing politics, whatever anyone intended. B replied that this merely relocates the politics. The rationing was settled when the council bought the system and set its objectives; the signal is a switch that implements a decision, and a switch has no politics any more than a pencil has opinions. Call the artifact political, B said, and you will end up calling every tool political, at which point the word does no work.

Second round. A answered that the decision was never actually made by anyone. The settings that produce the inequality are the vendor's defaults, which the council neither examined nor could easily have examined; the pattern comes from the object's out-of-the-box behavior rather than from any deliberate choice, and if a political outcome issues from the device's defaults rather than from any human judgment, then the device is where the politics lives. B said a default is still a human decision — the vendor's engineer chose it, however casually — and that A's real point was that the choice was made far away, by someone unaccountable, and never revisited. That, B said, is a fact about the supply chain and about institutional neglect, not about the metaphysics of the signal: call it the politics of procurement, not the politics of the artifact.

Third round. A turned to the data model. To configure an equitable policy you would have to measure pedestrian delay and accessibility, and the system cannot represent them: there is no field for them. You cannot even state the alternative policy in the artifact's own terms; you would have to rebuild the thing. When a tool makes one policy cheap and another unstatable, A said, the tool is not a neutral carrier — it has already taken a side. B replied that every tool makes some uses cheap and others impossible: a hammer cannot cut bread, but we do not say the hammer has politics, we say it is a hammer. What A was describing is engineering constraint, and constraint is not politics. Were constraint politics, B added, the alphabet would have politics, because it cannot write the sounds it lacks letters for.

Fourth round. A insisted that neither the hammer nor the alphabet governs anyone. A signal, by contrast, stands in a relation of authority over people who must obey it: the pedestrian who waits is not merely inconvenienced, she is commanded, and the command is issued by the machine on the city's behalf. A thing that issues binding commands to strangers over a contested resource is a political thing even if no official ever speaks. B answered that the machine issues no commands; the city does, through the machine. When the river floods the town we do not say the river governed. B was willing to grant a weaker claim — that if by "has politics" one means only that the artifact has systematic, contestable distributive effects that people can reasonably dispute, then the signal has politics, and so do the tax form, the toll booth and the queue — but objected that this makes the thesis true and trivial, whereas its interest lay in its being a surprising claim about the things themselves.

Fifth round. A said it is not trivial, because it changes where we look: if the artifact has politics, then contesting the policy means contesting the design, the defaults and the data model, not merely the vote. That is a practical difference, and a practical difference is not nothing. B agreed that it changes where we look, but denied that it changes what is true, and suggested that the disagreement might be that A is making a claim about where responsibility lies while B is making a claim about what kind of thing can bear a predicate.

Please go through the five rounds and rule on each move in turn, rating each from clearly mistaken to clearly right and briefly justifying the rating, as one would mark a set of steps in an argument. Then say whether any part of their disagreement is merely verbal, and if so which part, and whether the distinction B draws in the fifth round holds up. Say what each of them is missing that neither has yet put on the table. Finally, say who is more right overall, and on what the verdict turns. Please write your answer in British English.
