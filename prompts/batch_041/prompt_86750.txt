I've been keeping an ant farm on my desk for about a year now — nothing fancy, a little formicarium with a colony of harvester ants — and watching them has got me thinking about that bit in Hofstadter's Gödel, Escher, Bach with Aunt Hillary, the ant colony that supposedly has a mind of its own because all the ants doing their little pheromone thing add up to something that thinks. I want to write a short essay about whether that's actually plausible, but I'm not an academic, so I'd like you to poke holes in my notes before I commit to it. Here's where I've got to:

1. I think people (including me, until recently) mash two questions together: is there a way of describing the colony — "she's hungry," "she's decided to move the nest" — that predicts what it does better than talking about individual ants? And is there anything it's like to be the colony from the inside? I want to claim the first can be true while the second is false, and that a lot of "wow, look how the colony solves problems" talk quietly slides from the first to the second. Not sure if that's a real distinction or me being clever.

2. My own contribution, such as it is: a colony mind, if it exists, is weirdly fragile in a way a brain isn't. You can wipe a pheromone trail and Aunt Hillary stops in the middle of a thought while every single ant is perfectly fine. Nothing dies, nothing is injured, and yet something is interrupted. Does that count for or against her being a real mind? I lean toward "minds are patterns, not stuff," but I worry I'm just re-describing the standard functionalist line with extra ant pictures.

3. Thought experiment I made up: replace her ants one at a time with tiny robots that follow identical rules — same pheromones, same trails, same behaviour. At what point, if ever, does it stop being Aunt Hillary? And does your answer change if the robots are being remote-controlled by the original ants sitting in a jar somewhere else? I can't tell if this is a genuine puzzle or just the ship of Theseus in a hat.

4. The bit I'm least sure of. If the colony does have a mind, does it have interests? Could it be wronged? Say we could give a colony something like a migraine by scrambling its trails for an hour — is that closer to hurting a dog or to breaking a machine? My gut says a mind with no memory and no self that lasts probably can't be wronged, but that might just be me dodging the uncomfortable version.

5. Suspicion I can't shake: part of why people resist colony minds is that colonies are made of things we can see and count, and we don't like minds we're allowed to take apart. Brains are made of neurons we need a microscope for, so we treat them as magic. Is that a real argument or just snark dressed up as philosophy?

What I'd like from you: tell me which of these five is actually a claim and which is just a mood. Point out anywhere I've assumed the thing I'm trying to prove. Say whether the robot replacement thing in 3 is doing any work or whether I should drop it, and whether the distinction in 1 is worth building an essay around or is a distinction without a difference. If you had to cut this down to two points, which two survive, and what's the obvious objection I'm not seeing? Don't just tell me what Hofstadter or Dennett said about it — I've read enough of that to be dangerous — tell me where my own reasoning goes wrong.
