I'm putting together a short piece for a reading group on "consciousness as a user illusion" and I'd like comments before I commit to the line I'm taking. Draft below, warts and all.

[start]

The phrase "user illusion" is borrowed from interface design, and I think the borrowing has been under-examined. In HCI a user illusion is a useful fiction: the desktop, the trash can, the folder. Nobody's confused about the metaphysics of a trash-can icon — you know it's pixels — and the fiction is exactly what makes the machine usable. The interface hides the implementation, and that hiding is the point.

Now the illusionist about consciousness wants to say phenomenal properties are like that: the felt redness isn't a real property of anything, it's the interface through which the brains processing gets presented. Fine. But notice the disanalogy. In HCI there's a user who is fooled by the interface, and that user stands outside it, using it. In the consciousness case there's no user outside. The brain isn't a user sitting at a screen; if there's a user at all, it has to be manufactured by the very illusion in question.

So the metaphor, taken literally, either (a) posits a homunculus, which is precisely what the view was supposed to avoid, or (b) forces the illusionist to give an account of a "virtual user" — a self that's the output of the interface rather than its consumer. I want to defend (b). Call it the virtual-user reading. On this reading the illusion has two layers. First, transparency: the medium (the neural goings-on) is invisible and only the content shows. Second, self-reference: the system's model of itself as a unified subject is delivered through that same transparent medium. The self isn't the one being fooled; it's part of what the fooling produces.

The payoff, if it works, is that it dissolves a standard objection — "an illusion has to be an illusion for someone." The reply is that the someone is a position inside the illusion, like the implied viewer in a perspective painting, not a person standing in front of it. Whether that reply is more than a verbal trick I'm honestly unsure about.

Second thing I'm unsure of. I want to claim the user illusion is really a transparency illusion, and that these come apart: you could have full transparency with no user at all (a thermostat's "reading" of the room, if you squint), and you could have a user-model with no transparency (a system that explicitly represents its own states as representations). On my story it's the conjunction that generates the feeling of phenomenal presence, and neither alone does. That feels like the load-bearing claim and also the one I could most easily be bullshitting myself on.

[end]

So — comments please. Specifically: is my reading of the HCI metaphor fair to the illusionists, or am I setting up a strawman by treating the metaphor as load-bearing when they might only mean it loosely? Is the two-layer split (transparency + self-reference) actually doing work, or am I just relabeling? And is the "position in the illusion" move legitimate, or does it just redescribe the homunculus with extra steps? Don't worry about style, I'll polish. I mostly want to know if the central analogy holds up, whether the transparency/user-model conjunction is a real distinction or a distinction without a difference, and where the weakest joint is.
