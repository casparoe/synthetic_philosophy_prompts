I'm a novelist (two books, both with a lot of interior monologue) and I'm applying for a one-year fellowship at a small humanities center that likes cross-disciplinary projects. They want a two-page proposal, and I've got a half-formed idea I'd like you to turn into one. I've been reading about eliminative materialism -- the view that beliefs and desires aren't real inner states but part of a folk theory we'd be better off dropping -- and my first reaction was, fine, but what does that mean for what I do all day? My characters are basically bundles of beliefs and desires; if those are theoretical posits with no genuine referent, am I just writing elaborate falsehoods? But then I thought maybe the mistake is mine: maybe belief-talk in fiction isn't descriptive at all. When I write that a character believes her brother will come back, I'm not making a claim about a hidden causal state; I'm giving the reader a rule for how to imagine her, a way to make her actions cohere. I've been calling it the directive view in my notes (not a real term, just my shorthand). The question I want the proposal to nail down is something like: if eliminative materialism is true, does it undermine the novel's practice of attributing beliefs and desires, or does it only undermine a certain philosophical theory of what that practice is doing? And if the directive view is right, what exactly is the novelist committed to when she says a character believes something -- what has to be true for the attribution to be apt, and what would make it a failure?

Could you write the proposal? I need a precise statement of the question (maybe split it into two: one about the metaphysics, one about the practice), an explanation of why the existing positions -- the eliminativists, the folks who think belief-talk is indispensable, the as-if instrumentalists -- don't already settle it, and then a method. I don't want a literature review or citations; I'll add those later if I get the fellowship. What I really need is a month-by-month plan for what I'd actually do: I imagine a first month getting clear on the distinction between descriptive and directive uses, a second month building a small taxonomy of the roles belief-attributions play in fiction (prediction, explanation, moral evaluation, narrative alignment, maybe unreliable narration), a third month stress-testing the taxonomy on scenes where characters are wrong about their own beliefs or where the narrator's belief-attributions conflict with the character's actions, and a final month drafting a long essay that could also double as a craft manifesto. I'm definately not a philosopher, so also say why this is a conceptual and critical project rather than, say, an empirical study of readers, and why that approach is better than just surveying what novelists say about their own craft. Say what would count as the project failing -- I'd rather know now if the whole thing collapses into a terminological reshuffle. And be honest about scope: is this a one-year project or am I kidding myself? Also, if you can, tell me which part is the riskiest and what I could cut if the fellowship turns out to be six months instead of twelve. I was at a residency in the Catskills last summer and got into this argument with a poet who thinks all fiction is just seperate from metaphysics, and I'd love to have something that answers her.
