I'm a novelist — my last book was a triptych about people who work in standards offices, so I have a type — and I'm two stories into a collection where each story is built around a piece of conceptual machinery that everyone has heard the name of and nobody actually understands. The one I'm stuck on is the Church-Turing thesis, and what I want the story to be *about* is its weird epistemic status. Here's what I mean by that, and I'm telling you because I want you to have it in hand, not because I want the story to explain it: the thesis isn't a theorem. It says that anything a person could compute by mechanically following a finite set of rules — the kind of recipe you could hand to a clerk who understands nothing but the instructions on the page — can be computed by a Turing machine. Church and Turing and a few others all landed on the same boundary in the 1930s from different directions (recursion, lambda calculus, abstract machines, string-rewriting), and that convergence is basically the whole case for it. Nobody ever proved it, and there's a reason: one side of the equation is a vague everyday notion, and you can't prove things about a vague notion without first sharpening it — and the moment you sharpen it you've either assumed what you wanted (if you sharpen it into "Turing-computable") or you've smuggled in a substantive claim about the physical world (if you sharpen it into "whatever any real device can do"). There are people who think it's an empirical hypothesis about physics, people who think it's a definition, people who think it's true by the meaning of the word "procedure," and people who think the convergence is fake because all those formalisms are cousins and never really left the family. I dont need any of that in the story. I need it behind the story.

So: write me a short story. Setting is a licensing office — a small bureaucracy that certifies procedures, a patent office crossed with a notary's office, in a country that doesn't exist, sometime in the middle of the last century. The protagonist is a young examiner, two years in, whose job is to decide whether a submitted "procedure" counts as one; she has a feel for it by now, the way a proofreader has a feel for a typo. Then somebody submits a recipe for something no machine can do, and she has to rule on it. I don't want a lecture in the dialogue and I don't want the resolution to be "the thesis was wrong" or "the thesis was right." I want the story to turn on the moment she realises the only way to rule on the case is to have already decided what a procedure is — and that the office has been quietly deciding that all along, case by case, for decades, without ever writing it down. Maybe there's a older colleague who's been there thirty years and has a rule of thumb he can't justify and won't defend. Maybe the file she pulls to check precedent is mostly empty, and the emptiness is the point.

Give me somewhere between 1,500 and 2,500 words, close third person on the examiner, and if you can manage it I'd rather not see the phrase "Church-Turing thesis" or the word "computable" anywhere on the page — I want the idea carried entirely by the plot and the filing system.
