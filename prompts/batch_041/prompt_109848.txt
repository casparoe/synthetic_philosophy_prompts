I have been turning over a sentence I heard during an argument about a forged painting, and I would like help taking it apart. A museum had discovered that a picture it displayed for years as the work of a famous hand was in fact painted decades later by someone else. A friend's reaction was: if nobody could tell the difference, then there was no difference. He offered this as though it settled the matter — as though the only thing anyone could sensibly care about in a painting is what it looks like, so that once two objects look the same there is simply nothing left for the word "difference" to latch onto. I don't think he is obviously right, but I also cannot state cleanly what he is claiming, or what I would be claiming if I resisted him.

What I want is a menu, not a verdict delivered from on high: all the distinct precise claims that one sentence — if you can't tell the difference, there is no difference — could be making, sorted from the ones that are nearly empty of content to the ones that are substantive and contestable, each with your assessment. I am especially interested in the gap between differences in the object, differences in the experience of it, differences in its value, and differences in what it is legitimate to believe or say about it, and in whether any reading of the slogan actually licenses my friend's shrug.

A few things I have already been chewing on, in case they help you frame the options: the possibility that two paintings could be identical down to the last brushstroke and the last molecule, so that no test could ever separate them; the thought that part of what moves me about a real painting is something I cannot see — that it came from a particular person's hand at a particular moment, which is a fact about its history rather than its surface; the odd fact that a forgery can be more beautiful or better composed than the thing it imitates, and that we still seem to rank them the other way; and the suspicion that the difference might lie not in either painting but in what we are entitled to believe and to say about it, which would make it a difference in us or in our practices rather than in the canvas.

Please lay your answer out under these headings, in this order:

- Bottom line: one short paragraph, no more than a few sentences, saying whether any reading of the slogan makes it true and whether any of them supports my friend.
- The slogan and the work it was doing: about one paragraph on what claim the argument needed the sentence to carry.
- The candidate readings: the main body — a numbered list of the distinct precise theses the sentence could express, each explained in two to four sentences and given a short label.
- Verdicts: for each numbered reading, a few sentences saying whether it is trivial, plausible, or false, and why.
- Which reading the slogan needs: roughly a paragraph on which reading, if any, would actually support the inference "so there was no difference," and whether that reading is defensible.
- What would settle it: a short paragraph on what kind of evidence or argument would decide between the live readings.
