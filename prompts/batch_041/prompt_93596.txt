Ok so I'm a novelist, which means my whole job is deciding which bits of a made-up world are doing the real work and which are decoration, and for the last few months I've been sitting in on a philosophy reading group and I've hit a wall I can't get passed. The fight is about grounding — the "in virtue of" relation, the thing that's supposed to hold between what's more fundamental and what's less. There's a camp that says there's exactly one such relation, that it has a single formal profile (irreflexive, asymmetric, transitive, hyperintensional, and so on), and that when people think they see different kinds of dependence — a set depending on its members, a whole on its parts, a mind on a brain, a truth on its truthmaker — that's one relation wearing different masks. Then there's a camp that says no, that's an artifact: grounding is just a word we use for a grab-bag of genuinely different dependence relations, and the formal unity the first camp loves is bought by making the predicate so coarse it stops tracking anything real. And then there's a third group that says the whole thing is a non-starter because grounding isn't a relation in the world at all — it's explanatory or expressive machinery we project onto the world.

They've been at this for years and nobody has moved an inch. I want a diagnosis, not a survey and not a verdict about who's right. My own hunch, and I'd like you to test it hard, is that the monist/pluralist half of this is a verbal dispute in disguise — like arguing whether "ancestor of" is one relation or a family of relations, when the answer just depends on how coarse you make the predicate — but that the third position is a real, substantive disagreement with the other two about what metaphysics is allowed to posit. Is that the right cut? Or is something else doing the work — different standards of evidence, a hidden question about whether grounding is even supposed to name a natural kind, a disagreement about which cases count as data (does one counterexample to transitivity sink the monist, or do you get to keep the formal profile and reclassify the case as a different phenomenon)?

And here's the bit I actually care about, because I keep trying to build a test that would separate them. The idea is to hold the formal properties fixed and swap the relata around — take a case where a set depends on its members and a case where a whole depends on its parts, keep everything about the formal profile identical, and ask whether the kind of dependence is still the same. My instinct is that if you can vary the kind while holding the profile fixed, the pluralist wins; and if the profile can't be held fixed across the swaps, then the monist's "one relation" is doing no work. Does that test actually cut anything, or does it just restate the arguemnt in new clothes? And if it's the latter, tell me what kind of disagreement this really is, and what — a distinction, a formal result, a change of question — would count as progress here, or whether I should just accept that this one is never going to resolve and why.
