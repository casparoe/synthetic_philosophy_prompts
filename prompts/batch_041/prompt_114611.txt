I'm in a philosophy of language seminar this term, and the professor is a dyed-in-the-wool Gricean — the Cooperative Principle is basically his religion — and for the last few weeks we've been going around the room classifying cases. He asks exactly one question each time: did the speaker implicate the proposition in question, yes or no. I've been keeping a tally, mostly out of boredom, and I want you to predict what he'll say about a case we haven't done yet. The tally so far: (1) "Did everyone get the memo?" / "The grad students did" — yes, implicated that not everyone did. (2) A referee for a job candidate writes "he was never once late in four years, and he never lost a set of keys" — yes, implicated that the candidate is weak. (3) A classmate's fussy use of the subjunctive leads another student to conclude he's a snob; the speaker wasn't trying to convey anything about himself — no, not implicated; the professor was adamant that if the speaker didn't mean it, it's not in the running at all. (4) "Fantastic, another two-hour delay," said cheerfully while stuck on a platform — yes, implicated that the delay is bad. (5) "The window's still open," said while shivering — yes, implicated a request to shut it. (6) "She's a widow" — no, not implicated that her husband died; he said that's just part of what she said, so it can't be an implicature.

Next week's case is a line in a student essay: "The proof is elegant but useless." The question on the table is whether the author implicated that the proof is useless — or, really, whether the but-half counts as an implicature at all. Predict his verdict, but honestly I care less about the verdict than about the rule he's running, so please lay out the candidate rules the tally is consistent with and tell me which one he's actually running. I can see at least two: the strict line, where an implicature has to be a conversational one — worked out from the maxims, cancelable without landing you in contradiction — so the but-bit gets disqualified because it rides on the word itself and you can't take it back; and a looser speaker-meaning line, where anything the speaker meant that isn't part of what the sentence literally says counts, which would make it a yes. There may be a third I'm missing. Say which you'd bet on and why, and name the one follow-up case that would actually settle it — I've got a tenner riding on this with a classmate, and alot of the seminar thinks the tally already underdetermines the answer, which I suspect is right but want spelled out.
