EVERYDAY CONVENTIONS AND THE ETHICS OF THE OUTSIDER

The question whether everyday conventions are moral obligations is usually run as a question about strength: are these mere customs, or do they bind? I want to argue that this is the wrong axis. The right axis is what a convention is doing, and once we separate two functions the single question dissolves into two much more tractable ones with different answers.

1. Two functions

Some conventions allocate a cost that has to fall on someone. Queueing allocates waiting time. A tip, in a wage system where the tip is priced into what the worker is paid, allocates the cost of service labour. Right-of-way rules allocate risk and delay. Escalator etiquette allocates a scarce lane. In each case there is a quantity of something unpleasant that must be distributed among people who all want less of it. The convention is one distribution rule among many, and it functions because everyone expects everyone else to be using it.

Other conventions signal something about the relationship: that you recognise someone, that you are not contemptuous of them, that you belong. Forms of address, dress, whether you greet the person behind the counter. These cost little materially and their violation costs the other party little materially, but they carry information.

2. Obligations attach to losses, not to denied gains

Violating a burden convention typically imposes a loss on an identifiable person: the person behind you waits longer than they would have. Violating a marker convention typically denies someone an expected benefit, the recognition they were anticipating, without imposing a loss: they are no worse off than they were before you acted. I take it that we have obligations not to impose losses and much weaker reasons to supply benefits. So burden conventions can ground obligations, while marker conventions ground reasons of a different and weaker kind, reasons of non-contempt, which is to say reasons not to let your conduct express that the other person does not count. That is not the same as an obligation to conform.

3. Conditions on the obligation

I take the obligation to attach only when three conditions hold: the cost of compliance is low relative to the burden being redistributed; the burden of non-compliance falls on an identifiable party; and the convention is the operative coordination point, meaning there is no other salient rule that everyone is already using. Where the third condition fails, the obligation evaporates. Jaywalking is the test case. Where a rule is universally violated and universally known to be violated, the operative coordination point is the pedestrian's own risk assessment, not the statute. The statute is a dead letter, and violating it imposes no loss on anyone who was relying on it, because no one was. I want to resist the conclusion that this licenses jaywalking. It licenses nothing; it relocates the question to risk imposition, which is a different question with its own answers.

4. Aggregation

The obvious objection: trivial losses aggregate. If a thousand pedestrians each cost a driver two seconds, the total is real. I want to say that trivial losses imposed on unconnected strangers do not aggregate into an obligation, because the aggregate has no bearer: there is no one who lost thirty-three minutes. They do aggregate when the same party is repeatedly on the receiving end, which is why queue-jumping, where the same queue is raided over and over, is a more serious matter than jaywalking, where the losses fall on a rotating cast. This is the passage I trust least.

5. The visitor

Now the second question: must visitors follow local conventions? The standard answers are that you must, because you are a guest and entering a place binds you to its customs, or that you need not, because obligations cannot be generated by geography. I want a third answer built on an asymmetry in the cost of adjusting. For burden conventions the visitor should follow local practice, and the reason is not deference but predictability. The locals have built their expectations around a rule they cannot renegotiate with you in the moment. Your obligation is to be predictable, not to be local. This binds you even when you think the rule is silly, and binds you most strongly when your non-compliance is invisible to you but visible to them, as when a visitor stands on the left of an escalator without knowing that anything has been done. For marker conventions the asymmetry runs the other way. The host can read a visitor's foreignness more cheaply than the visitor can acquire a local repertoire of small signals, and the host knows that non-conformity here is evidence of unfamiliarity rather than contempt. So the host owes interpretive charity, and the visitor owes at most legibility: to be visibly trying. A visitor who bows badly is doing the thing. A visitor who will not try is doing something else.

6. Two hard cases

Tipping. A visitor from a country without a tipping norm, in a place where the tip is priced into the worker's wage. The non-compliance imposes a loss on an identifiable person, so this is a burden convention and the visitor is bound. But the visitor may not know the rate, and the rate is not posted. I claim the obligation survives the ignorance, since the loss falls either way, but that culpability is low, and that the local who knows the visitor is foreign and says nothing is also doing something mildly wrong.

Milling at a counter. At a busy counter, locals cluster rather than form a line; the visitor forms a line, is served last, and feels wronged. On my account the visitor has no complaint. No convention was operative that anyone was relying on, and the locals imposed no loss: they used a different and equally serviceable rule that happened to disadvantage a newcomer. I dislike this conclusion, which is one reason to distrust the account.

7. What this leaves

If the account is right, the moral weight of everyday conventions is not a function of how deeply felt or how widely shared they are, but of who bears what when they are broken. That predicts that the conventions people defend most heatedly, the markers of respect, will often be the ones with the weakest claim on outsiders, and that the ones people barely notice, the queue, the tip, the escalator lane, will be the ones that bind. That inversion is the part I most want to be told is wrong, if it is.
