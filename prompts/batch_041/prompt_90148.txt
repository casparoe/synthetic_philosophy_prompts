I'm a final-year philosophy student, and I've been asked to give the student address at my college's graduation this summer. The audience is mixed: my classmates, their families (some religious, some not), and a few faculty who will know exactly what I'm doing up there. I want the speech to turn on a problem I've been working on in a metaphysics seminar — whether the badness of humanity's coming to an end is fully accounted for by the deaths it would involve, or whether there is a further loss that isn't anybody's death: the loss of there being anyone left at all to care about anything. The thought experiment I keep returning to is a world in which every person dies painlessly and at the same instant; I think we sense a catastrophe there that can't be reduced to the sum of the deaths, and I'd like the speech to make that felt without stating it as a thesis. My own view, which I want the address to embody rather than defend, is that our projects matter to us in a way that quietly depends on a human future we won't be part of, and that this gives us reason to care about that future without pretending it is a way of surviving. Write the address. It has to comfort people who are carrying losses of their own, yet it must not offer the standard consolation that the dead live on in those who come after — I think that consolation is false and I don't want to trade on it — and it must land for the non-philosophers in the room without flattening the idea into a platitude. Please write in British English, keep it to 500 words at most, and end with something a graduating class can actually carry out of the hall.
