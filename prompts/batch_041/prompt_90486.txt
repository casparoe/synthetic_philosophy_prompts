I run the help centre and the community forum for a small software company: every page a customer reads when something has gone wrong is written by me or by the two colleagues I train, we publish several items a day, and people act on what we write with their money and their data. I have no academic training, but I have been thinking about one question I cannot settle: when is one entitled to state something flatly, rather than hedging it, attributing it, or giving the reader the odds? It is plainly not enough that I believe it, and I am not convinced it is enough that it is true — I can write a sentence that is literally accurate and still leave a customer with a false picture of their situation. Nor does certainty look like the test: on our uptime figures it is about as near-certain as anything gets that the service will be up tomorrow, and I still would not put that sentence on a status page. I understand philosophers have offered truth, belief and knowledge as rival standards for assertion, and I want whatever survives of that debate turned into something we can actually run, in twenty minutes, with no lawyer in the room.

Please give me a procedure for deciding what may be published as a flat claim: the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, the point at which the procedure returns a no and what to publish instead (hedge, attribute, quantify, or stay silent), and the points where I am fooling myself if I treat a judgement call as mechanical. Then run it on three cases — (1) 'Your files are never shared outside the company', true as written; (2) a fix that cures the crash for roughly nine users in ten; (3) the uptime sentence above — at least one of which should come out against what I would like to publish. Finish by saying what the procedure cannot decide.

Write in British English.
