I'm a graduate student in a science, technology, and society seminar, and I keep running into a move I can't quite pin down. We were discussing our university's adoption of a retention-prediction system — software that scans student records and flags undergraduates as likely to drop out so that advisors can intervene — and a classmate defended the whole thing by saying that technology is neutral: it's just a tool, and everything depends on how people use it. When I asked about the pattern such a tool would inevitably absorb from historical data, he sharpened the point: the system only surfaces a probability and leaves the decision to a human advisor, so whatever happens downstream is the advisor's doing rather than the tool's, and the politics, if there are any, live entirely in the use. He then added, more or less as a closer, that you cannot uninvent a thing once it exists, so the only real question is how to live with it. I suspect the slogan is doing several different jobs at once and that at least one of them is false, but I can't say which, and I don't want to simply assert that artifacts have politics without knowing what that would commit me to.

Could you lay out, as a menu rather than a single favored reading, the distinct precise claims that "technology is neutral" could be making — ranging from the trivial (a hammer can be used to build or to bludgeon) through the substantive to the plainly false — and for each say what it actually asserts about where values, agency, and responsibility reside: in the artifact, in its design, in the intentions of users, in the social arrangements surrounding it, or nowhere in particular? Please keep apart the claim that a technology is value-free, the claim that it is value-neutral, the claim that its effects are underdetermined by its design, and the claim that its development is autonomous or inevitable, since my classmate seems to slide between these without noticing. Then tell me which reading is the one actually doing the work when someone uses the slogan to deflect criticism from a design decision, and whether that particular reading is defensible. Finally, say which of these readings are compatible with the thought that artifacts can themselves have politics and which contradict it, so that I know what I would be taking on if I pressed that line in next week's discussion. I am not after a reading list; I want the conceptual map and your verdict on each branch of it.
