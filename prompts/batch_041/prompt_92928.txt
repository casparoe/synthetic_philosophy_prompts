I'm a third-year philosophy student currently taking a course on language and ethics, and as part of the peer-assessment component I've been asked to write a first-pass grade on a classmate's problem-set answer; I'd like a second opinion before I submit it. The question was worth 20 points and asked students to apply the distinction our instructor circulated in the Week 6 handout, "Saying and Steering." The handout introduced two working terms: an utterance displaces an audience when it brings them to believe something false, and it diverts them when it steers them away from a true belief without installing any false belief in its place. The handout stressed that these are categories of effect rather than of act-type — the same utterance may do both, either, or neither — and that whether the speaker lied is a logically separate question from which effects the utterance had. The question presented two cases. In the first, Nadia's supervisor asks whether Ravi has submitted his section of the report; Ravi has not. Nadia replies, "Ravi told me he was nearly finished." That is true — Ravi did say exactly that, three weeks ago — but Nadia knows he has since gone silent, and she wants the supervisor to take her remark as evidence that Ravi is close to done. In the second, Tom, a company spokesperson, is asked at a press conference whether the firm's chemical storage complied with regulations. He says, "It did." He believes it did not, and he also knows that every journalist present already knows it did not, since the noncompliance has been widely reported; he says it anyway because the CEO has ordered all staff to deny it. Students were asked to say, for each case, whether the speaker lied, whether the utterance displaced, whether it diverted, and whether the lying/misleading distinction marks any moral difference here.

Here is my classmate's answer, reproduced in full:

"Both cases turn on the familiar idea that lying requires a false assertion while misleading can be done with truths. Nadia does not lie: her sentence is true, and on any standard definition a lie must be false. What she does is mislead by implicature — she invites the supervisor to infer that Ravi is nearly finished, and since she could have added 'but that was three weeks ago' and didn't, the implicature is clearly cancellable. Applying the handout's terms, her remark diverts the supervisor: it moves him away from the truth, and because she never actually asserted anything about Ravi's current progress, no false belief is installed. It is therefore a case of pure diverting, and the handout's warning that displacing and diverting are different effects supports treating it as less serious than a straightforward lie; misleading is really just a species of lying in which the falsehood is left implicit rather than stated. Tom's case is harder. He asserts something he believes to be false, which is the core of what it is to lie. But the journalists already know the truth and cannot be taken in, so no one is actually deceived. Since the handout says that deception requires that the audience actually be misled — an utterance that fails to mislead is not deceptive — Tom's statement does not count as a lie, and it displaces no one. It is, if anything, a purely symbolic act of institutional loyalty. This is why the lying/misleading distinction does no moral work here: what matters morally is whether someone is actually led into error, and in Tom's case nobody is. Where the distinction does matter is Nadia. Because the supervisor is genuinely misled, she has done something wrong, and the wrongness consists in the deception's success rather than in any property of her sentence. Note that Tom's statement would have been a lie if the journalists had believed him, which shows that the difference between lying and misleading is not intrinsic to the act."

I'd like a grade out of 20 and the specific comments that justify it, going through the answer claim by claim so I can see exactly where credit is earned and where it should be withheld, and what a full-credit answer would have needed to say that this one doesn't.
