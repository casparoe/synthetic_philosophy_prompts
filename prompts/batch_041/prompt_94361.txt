I am a fifth-year PhD student in decision theory and formal epistemology, and I have been trying to reverse-engineer my own intuitions about gambles. Please act as an expert in expected utility theory and in the growth-based or geometric approach to rationality that descends from the Kelly criterion. Treat the verdicts below as data about me, not as claims I am defending, and tell me what principle, if any, could generate them.

Before you answer, brainstorm. Restate each case in your own notation, compute what the obvious candidate principles say about it, and only then look for the pattern. Reason carefully and show your work; I would rather have a slow, honest derivation than a tidy verdict. I do not need a literature review or a reading list — I need the argument.

My verdicts. Every stake is a fraction of my current total wealth, and I am considering each gamble in isolation unless I say otherwise.

1. An even-money bet on an event I put at probability 0.6, any stake permitted: I would stake about a fifth of my wealth.
2. The same even-money bet with the probability at 0.51: I would stake a couple of percent.
3. A ten-to-one bet — a win gains me ten times what I staked, a loss costs me the stake — with the probability at 0.5: I would stake just under half.
4. Double or nothing at probability 0.6: I decline.
5. Triple or nothing at probability 0.5: I decline, even though the expected value is positive.
6. A thousand times my wealth or nothing at probability 0.5: I accept.
7. The same as 6 with the multiplier at a hundred: I cannot make up my mind, and my verdict flips with my mood.
8. Case 5 again, but stipulated to be the last decision of my life, with no repetition and no compounding: I still decline.
9. A wager whose payoff is not money at all — a coin flip that either lands me a permanent academic position or ends my career in the field: I evaluate it in a way that will not map onto any of the above, and I suspect that is because I cannot say what my bankroll is.

The principle I suspect. I suspect my verdicts are just expected-log-wealth maximization — the Kelly rule — dressed up. Verdicts 1 through 3 track the Kelly fractions almost exactly, which makes me worry that I am reporting a formula back to myself rather than consulting my gut. On this hypothesis I treat wealth multiplicatively, I care only about the fraction at stake, and I treat ruin as infinitely bad. Two things I have read are supposed to support it: that the logarithm is the unique utility that turns multiplication of wealth into addition of utility, and that it is the unique utility whose rankings are invariant under rescaling wealth. I cannot see how the second claim could be true, since any power of wealth seems to me to rank gambles the same way after rescaling. If I am wrong about that, I would like to know where.

What I want from you. (a) State the best principle, or small set of principles, that fits my verdicts, precisely enough that I could apply it to a new case without asking you again. (b) For each clause, name the verdict that motivates it. (c) Say what your principle predicts for at least two cases I have not listed, choosing cases that separate your principle from plain expected-log-wealth maximization. (d) Tell me honestly which of my verdicts the principle cannot accommodate. If it can accommodate all of them, tell me what it had to give up in order to do so, and whether a simpler principle that drops one verdict would be better. I am prepared to hear that some of my verdicts have to go; I want to know which ones and why.
