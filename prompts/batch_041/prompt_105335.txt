I'm an undergraduate research assistant on a project assembling Bongard-style concept-induction problems for a machine-learning course, and I've been asked to write the certification procedure we will run on every candidate problem before it enters the set. Each problem shows six images on each side of a panel, and the solver must recover the rule that separates the two sides. All of our problems are built from a fixed vocabulary of primitives -- counting, symmetry, convexity, containment, connectivity, and reflection -- every intended rule must be written as a Boolean combination of those primitives, and we score candidate rules by description length over that vocabulary. Please turn the theory of induction from a few examples into a procedure I can actually run: the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, the test that decides whether a problem is underdetermined (some other rule in the vocabulary fits all the examples), how many examples a problem needs, and where the procedure must stop and hand the decision to the instructor because the remaining question is one of taste rather than fact. Then run it on three candidates: one whose intended rule is convexity; one where a shorter rule fits every example but is not the one I meant; and one where my rule turns out to be equivalent to a rule already in the set. I have about twenty minutes per problem and the instructor signs off on the final set. Finally, tell me what the procedure cannot do -- in particular, whether it can ever certify a unique natural solution rather than merely a unique solution within our vocabulary -- and separate what is settled about concept induction from what is genuinely contested among people who study it.
