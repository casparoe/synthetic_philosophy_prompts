My study group has been working through the classical deterrence literature, and one of my friends has latched onto the game of chicken as the master key to the whole subject. On his telling, two drivers race toward each other on a single-lane road; whoever swerves first is humiliated, and if neither swerves they both die. Everything he thinks matters about nuclear politics follows from this. The winning move, he says, is to make your own swerving impossible — to tear out the steering wheel and throw it out the window where the other driver can see it — and so it can be rational for a nuclear power to cultivate a reputation for recklessness, to let launch authority sit with people whose grip on it is uncertain, and to prefer arrangements that leave the final outcome to accident rather than to anyone's deliberate decision. He points to crises in which leaders visibly let events slip toward the edge as proof that this is how the game is actually played, and he concludes that the only stable policy is to be the side that credibly will not swerve; anyone who talks about mutual restraint or accommodation, he says, simply has not understood the game.

I want an audit of the analogy rather than an argument for or against his politics. Begin by setting out the correspondence explicitly — what plays the role of the drivers, the road, the swerve, the crash, the onlookers, the steering wheel, and the single moment of decision — and then tell me where the mapping actually breaks. I am especially interested in the payoff ordering, because it seems to be doing all the work: in chicken, the worst outcome for each driver is to be the one who swerved while the other did not, which is ranked below the mutual crash, and it is exactly this ranking that makes the no-swerve commitment rational. Tell me whether that ordering is preserved when the crash is a nuclear exchange, or whether the analogy has quietly assumed it. Then sort the disanalogies into those that matter for his conclusion — that the only stable policy is to be the one who will not swerve — and those that are merely colorful, and say whether the conclusion survives once the whole list is on the table. I would also like you to separate the parts of his argument that genuinely depend on the analogy from the parts that would stand without it.

Finally, if there is a better comparison for brinkmanship than chicken, say what it is and what it buys: an auction in which both bidders can be ruined, a war of attrition in which each side pays while waiting for the other to quit, or a shared dial of risk that both sides can turn but neither controls. For whichever you favor, tell me what it captures that the chicken picture misses and what it in turn distorts. And address the two specific policies he draws from the analogy — the cultivation of a madman reputation and the delegation of launch authority to the uncertain — and say whether the chicken picture supports them or only supports the weaker and much less controversial claim that commitments and reputations matter in a crisis.
