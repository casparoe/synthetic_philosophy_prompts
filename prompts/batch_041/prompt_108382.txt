I write a humor column for a small fortnightly newsletter that circulates among people who work in finance and economics, and I am assembling an issue built around parody. What I would like from you is a mock personal-finance advice page — the syndicated kind where strangers send in their money troubles and a brisk, omniscient expert settles each one in four sentences and a cheerful sign-off. My invented columnist is Dr. Miriam Fisk, who writes a column called "Value Judgments"; she is a sincere, unrepentant expected-value maximizer, and the calculation of expected monetary value is her entire apparatus for giving advice. The joke I am after is not that she is a fool. It is that she is exactly right on her own premises, all the way down, while her readers keep flinching. So keep her inside her position from the first line to the last: she may be brisk, impatient, or condescending, but she must never quietly help herself to risk aversion, to diminishing marginal utility, or to any other premise that would let her off the hook, and no narrator, footnote, or fifth character may step in to tell the reader that she is wrong. The absurdity has to be visible without anyone in the piece announcing it, and the last word belongs to her. She is also a newspaper columnist rather than a scholar, so she will not cite papers or attach footnotes; nothing outside the letters and her replies should appear.

The engine of the column is the St. Petersburg game: a fair coin tossed until it lands tails, with the prize doubling for each head that precedes it, so that a tail on the first toss pays two dollars, a tail on the second pays four, a tail on the third pays eight, and so on without end. Please write four short reader letters, two or three sentences each, and four replies of a paragraph or two, roughly 1000 to 1200 words altogether, and give her a single formulaic sign-off that she repeats every time. The letters should drag in the standard misgivings in the voices of ordinary people rather than of textbooks. One reader wants to know whether to pay fifty dollars for a single play. A second is sure that money must be worth less to him the more of it there is, and adds that the house has only so much in its vault in any case. A third has heard that the game is worth infinitely much, cannot understand why nobody will buy his own copy of it for more than twenty dollars, and mentions that he is well behind after playing it a dozen times himself. A fourth has been offered a variant — I have seen it discussed as a cousin of the original — whose prizes alternate in sign and shrink as they go, so that the series of outcomes converges, but only conditionally, and adding the terms in a different order can make the total come out to whatever number you please; he wants to know what the thing is worth.

Answer each letter the way an honest expected-value maximizer really would, not the way a caricature would, and let the replies carry the remaining objections as well: that a rule which tells you to pay any finite amount gives no advice at all about how much, and that the prizes which make the expectation infinite are ones no one will ever live to see. Where the honest answer is a shrug — where her rule genuinely does not distinguish between one price and another, or where the total depends on how you add it up — she should shrug, confidently, and move on to the next correspondent. If she wants to insist that she is in the business of telling readers what things are worth rather than what to want, let her, but the piece must not treat that as a solution. She should also give at least one piece of advice that a sane reader would find reckless, and give it without a flicker of hesitation. The mathematics has to be exactly right throughout, since the readers who will laugh at this are the ones who will notice if the series is handled sloppily.
