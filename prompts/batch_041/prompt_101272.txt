I am preparing a seminar for final-year undergraduates on the integrity objection to impartialist moral theories, and I want to set them a question that forces them to distinguish the objection itself from a cluster of nearby but different worries. Consider the following case. A person has been persuaded by the objection that, in a rescue scenario, it would be alienating to save one’s spouse because one has first reminded oneself that partiality is morally permitted. He therefore resolves to act without any such mediating thought: he simply saves her. A critic observes, however, that his resolution not to have the mediating thought is itself a kind of deliberation, and that he is now acting from a conception of what a good husband’s thought ought to be. Please give an example of a conceptual issue that arises here, one that would be obscured if we treated the integrity objection as merely another way of stating the act/omission distinction or the doctrine of double effect. I am particularly interested in whether the issue concerns the content of the agent’s reasons, the causal history of his action, or the transparency of his motives, and in how the example would help students see the difference. Please answer in British English.
