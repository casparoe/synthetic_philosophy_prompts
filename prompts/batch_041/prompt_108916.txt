I'm a software engineer by training, with no formal philosophy background; my day job is in distributed systems, so I spend a lot of time reasoning about causal chains that are long, interleaved, and nearly impossible to trace to the end. A friend recently walked me through the debate over consequentialist cluelessness, and it lodged in my head precisely because it seems to describe something I already live with at work. The worry, as I understand it, is that the consequences of almost any action ripple outward indefinitely, so that even if consequentialism is the correct theory of right action, we cannot estimate the expected value of our options well enough for the theory to actually tell us what to do. I keep encountering a distinction between a shallow version of the problem, in which we know the relevant outcomes but cannot do the arithmetic, and a deeper version, in which we cannot even enumerate the outcomes or determine their signs, and I also keep hearing the suggestion that if the uncertainty is roughly symmetric it ought to cancel out, so that the real bite comes only from systematic bias. That last move sounds to me like a claim about noise versus bias in an estimator, and I would like to know whether the analogy holds up or quietly papers over something important. For what it is worth, I am not asking you to resolve the debate; I want to understand its shape well enough to know which parts of it are load-bearing.

What I actually want from you is an agenda rather than a survey. Suppose a small funder -- enough to support three or four people for a couple of years, no more -- wanted to make real progress on cluelessness and asked you to allocate that effort. Which open questions should they take up, and in what order? Please defend the ordering against the criteria you think are genuinely the right ones: which questions are upstream of which, which are tractable without anyone first having to settle the deeper metaphysics of value, and which look central but would turn out not to change what any consequentialist actually does. Tell me explicitly what would move a question up or down that list -- what a decisive result would have to establish, and what evidence over the next year or two would legitimately shift the ranking. I care most about the questions that would change practice, in the sense that a person deciding where to donate or how to spend a career should behave differently depending on how the answer came out, so please say plainly which candidate questions survive that test and which are, however intellectually interesting, action-irrelevant. And if the honest conclusion is that the whole literature is less consequential for practice than its prominence suggests, I would rather you tell me that than assemble a tidy list of open problems.
