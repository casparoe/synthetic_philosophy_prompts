My physio has a slogan she uses on basically everyone in the chronic pain group I go to: it's not the pain, it's how you respond to it. She means it kindly, and for some people it does seem to help -- she'll say the sensation is one thing, and what you do with it, the fear and the bracing and the catastrophizing, is another, and that second part is the part you can actually work on. Fine. But I mentioned it to a friend from a philosphy of mind reading group and he got weirdly excited about it, saying that taken literally the slogan is just the truth about pain and that there's a patient population that proves it. He was describing people with certain kinds of brain damage -- I think around the insula, and I think he said some were patients after old-style psychosurgery -- who will tell you that the pinprick hurts, that they can say how sharp it is and where, but that it doesn't bother them at all. They don't pull their hand back, they don't grimace, some of them laugh, none of them ask for anything to make it stop. He also keeps going on about pain having "components" -- the sensation, the unpleasantness, the urge to get away -- and says this syndrome (he called it asymbolia, I think) is a case where the components come apart.

I don't think he's exactly wrong, but I also don't think he and my physio are saying the same thing at all, and I don't want to just assert that. So: can you lay out the precise claims the slogan could be making? I want a menu, not one reading explained. The flat physiological one (a nociceptor firing isn't the same thing as suffering), the component-separation one, the one where the badness of pain is extrinsic -- it's bad because we mind it, and minding is a response -- the full stoic one where nothing is bad except your judgement, the clinical one about what's treatable, and whatever else is hiding in there that I've missed. For each, say whether it's trivially true, a real substantive claim, or actually false, and whether someone who thinks pain is bad in itself, in a way that doesn't depend on how you take it, has to deny it.

Then tell me which reading my physio needs for her advice to be fair to a patient who is suffering, and which reading my friend needs for the brain-damage cases to prove what he says they prove. And push back on him a little on my behalf: does someone who says it hurts but doesn't mind really show pain without its badness, or is there a respectable reading on which what they've lost is part of what makes the state pain in the first place, so the cases don't establish what he wants? My nagging suspicion is that the slogan is doing two completely different jobs in the two conversations and everyone's happy because nobody says which one they mean. Also flag any reading where the slogan comes out true for free, since I suspect that's most of what's going on.
