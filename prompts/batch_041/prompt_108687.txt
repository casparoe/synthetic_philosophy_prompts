A friend who's a doctor keeps getting into it with me about whether 'disease' names a natural fact or just encodes what we don't like, and I'd rather hand her the spec than another slogan. So: write the desiderata any theory of disease (and of health, since the two come as a pair) has to meet. The cases it has to call right in both directions, the ones that have embarrased everybody (homosexuality, and the 19th-century diagnosis for slaves who ran away, on one side; grief, and the contested chronic pain and fatigue syndromes, on the other), the malfunctions that harm nobody and the harms that involve no malfunction, the predisease states we now medicate for (cholesterol, prediabetes, osteopenia), infertility -- a clear failure of a biological function that's devastating to some people and irrelevant to others -- whether it has to cover a tree and a dog or only people, and the reference-class mess: aging is universal and nearsightedness is now the majority condition among young adults in much of urban East Asia, so how much is left of 'statistically abnormal'? With each requirement, the case or consideration behind it, and whether it's a genuine constraint or just a commitment of one camp. Then the part I actually want: which of them can't all hold at once -- in particular, can you keep disease value-free, keep it meaning something bad for the person, and keep it as the thing that licenses treatment? -- and which single requirement does the most to kill off the usual candidates (the biostatistical line, the harm-plus-dysfunction line, and the flat normativist line that it's whatever the culture pathologizes).
