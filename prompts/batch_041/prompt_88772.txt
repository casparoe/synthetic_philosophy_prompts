Next month I am giving a paper to a joint session of our logic seminar and the cognitive science group, and I need a question-period prep sheet rather than a defence. My thesis is a narrowed version of the Gödelian argument against mechanism, in the tradition of Lucas and Penrose, and it runs roughly like this. Gödelian reasoning does not refute computationalism as such; it is entirely open that for every human mathematical performance there is some Turing machine that matches it. What it refutes is the stronger and, I claim, more interesting doctrine that there is a machine whose programme we could in principle write down, inspect and endorse as sound, and whose proof-theoretic reach is the reach of human mathematical reason. Call this surveyable computationalism. The conclusion is therefore an epistemic anti-mechanism: mechanism may be true, but no machine whose soundness we could ever certify could be our exact computational equal, since the act of specifying it hands us, by Gödelian means, a sentence it cannot prove and which we can see to be true. I am aware that the concession in the first step may be thought to drain the argument of its force, and that the whole thing turns on an idealised mathematician who can survey proofs no actual person could survey; assume that objection will be put to me.

The room matters. The logic side consists of a proof theorist who works on ordinal analysis and is therefore acutely sensitive to what a theory can and cannot prove about its own consistency, and a recursion theorist who thinks in terms of the arithmetical hierarchy and Turing jumps. The cognitive science side consists of a functionalist philosopher of mind who takes computationalism about cognition more or less for granted, a neuroscientist who regards the whole debate as armchair wordplay, and a graduate student who arrived a Penrose enthusiast and has read considerably more of the secondary literature than the rest of us. Prepare me for the question period: the seven or eight questions I will actually get from this room, not the ones I would get from a general philosophy audience, ordered by how much damage they would do to me rather than by how likely they are, each phrased in the vocabulary of the person who will raise it, each with a two-minute reply and a frank note on where that reply is soft. Include the question I am hoping nobody asks. Do not give me references, reading lists or suggested citations; I know the literature, and what I need is what to say, not what to point at.
