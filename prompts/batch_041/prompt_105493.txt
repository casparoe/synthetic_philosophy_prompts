I would be grateful for help settling a dispute that has become a standing argument at my Thursday-night game, and that bears on a decision I actually have to make.

One of the players has invented a wager and is offering it to me. A fair coin is tossed until it lands heads. If the first head appears on toss k, then he pays me 2^k/k dollars when k is odd, and I pay him 2^k/k dollars when k is even. So: heads on the first toss and I win $2; on the second, I lose $2; on the third, I win $8/3, about $2.67; on the fourth, I lose $4; on the fifth, I win $6.40; on the tenth, I lose $102.40. He will let me play once for a fee of 65 cents, and he will also offer me, if I prefer, a version of the same wager in which no single payment in either direction can exceed $50,000.

Two of my friends have been arguing about what the wager is worth, and I cannot adjudicate between them.

The first says the wager is plainly in my favor. Take the tosses in pairs. The first two tosses make up a sub-wager whose average contribution is 1 − 1/2, which is positive; the next two contribute 1/3 − 1/4, also positive; and so on without end. Since the whole wager is just these favorable sub-wagers added together, its average must be favorable. He adds that if you cut the game off after any fixed number of tosses — five, or a hundred, or a million — the average is positive, and it settles toward 69 cents as the cutoff grows. On what principle, he asks, could it be rational to refuse a wager whose average is positive however far you truncate it?

The second friend says the first is making an arithmetic mistake that happens to look like an argument. The average of the wager, he says, is not 69 cents and not any other number, because the list of possible payoffs, each weighted by its chance, does not settle on a total: the total depends on the order in which you add the entries up. Written in the order the tosses arise, it comes to about 69 cents; written as two wins to every loss, it comes to about $1.04; written with the losses first, it runs away to a loss without limit. The order of bookkeeping is not a feature of the wager — the money I actually receive is fixed by the coin — and yet the order decides the answer. So the wager has no average, he concludes, and no argument that appeals to its average can tell me whether to accept it. He insists this is not the same as saying the average is infinite; it is to say there is nothing there to be infinite.

My own reaction is that the second friend has found a real defect but has drawn the wrong lesson from it, though I could not say what the right lesson is. What I want to know is this: should I pay the 65 cents? Is there a defensible way to put a value on this wager, or a principle that governs choices when the usual arithmetic refuses to produce a number? Does the capped version change the answer — so far as I can tell it has an entirely ordinary average — and if the capped versions are all favorable, does that tell me anything about the uncapped one? And a third player has suggested that I simply play fifty times and see how it comes out; I would like to know whether that settles anything or only obscures the question.

Please tell me which of my friends has hold of the truth, where the other one goes wrong, and what I should do. I would rather have the real structure of the problem than a verdict, but I would like both.
