Next month I give the commencement address at the small school I have led for eleven years, and I have to do it without pretending that this year's faculty vote did not happen. After half a century of trusting students entirely -- no proctored examinations, an honor code, open dormitories -- the faculty voted to proctor the final examinations while leaving everything else on the honor system. I want to build the address around the old quarrel between Mencius and Xunzi over human nature: whether we are seedlings that grow straight once the soil is right, or boards that stay warped until someone steams and presses them. I keep reaching for the easy line that the vote proves Xunzi was right, and it is both too simple and an insult to the students sitting in front of me. Write the address, six to eight minutes. Make the distinction earn its place: say what the vote actually commits us to, make sense of the asymmetry between the examinations we now supervise and everything else we still leave to trust, and be honest about which of the two thinkers the school is now following. The audience is a third faculty who teach these texts, a third parents who have never heard of either philosopher, and a third eighteen-year-olds who will remember one sentence. End on something true rather than something rousing.
