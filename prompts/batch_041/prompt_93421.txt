I am a third-year undergraduate in a seminar on consciousness and the philosophy of perception, and I would like your help diagnosing an argument that a classmate advanced in our last meeting. I will set the argument out as precisely as I can, and then ask you to take it apart.

By way of background: in the color phi phenomenon, two spots are flashed in succession at different locations on a screen and in different colors. At the right spacing and timing, viewers do not report seeing two spots; they report a single spot that appears to travel from the first location to the second and to change color about halfway along its path. The striking feature is that viewers report the color change as occurring *during* the illusory journey — before the second spot has been presented — even though the second spot's color is not available to the visual system until the second flash occurs. The report of a color change at an early moment therefore depends on information that arrives only later.

My classmate used this to argue as follows. He defends each step separately and has a reply ready for the obvious objections. He introduces one piece of terminology of his own: a *phenomenal timestamp*, by which he means the felt time at which an experience is dated — when, from the inside, the experience seems to be happening.

(1) In the color phi display, viewers genuinely undergo a visual experience as of a spot that moves and changes color between the two flash locations.
(2) That experience carries a phenomenal timestamp earlier than the second flash: the color change is experienced as occurring before the second spot is presented.
(3) The information required to specify the color change — the second spot's color — is unavailable in the visual system until the second spot is presented.
(4) No experience can have a content that is fixed by information that did not yet exist when the experience occurred.
(5) So the phenomenal timestamp on the experience of the color change is false: the experience is dated earlier than it actually occurred.
(6) If an experience's apparent time of occurrence can postdate its actual time of occurrence, then the felt order of our experiences is not a reliable guide to the order in which they occur.
(7) If the felt order of experiences is not a reliable guide to their actual order, then there is no experiential fact of the matter about the order of mental events. In particular, the old dispute over whether the mind revises experience itself (the 'Orwellian' option) or only the memory of it (the 'Stalinesque' option) is not merely experimentally undecidable but empty.
(8) Hence the apparent stream of consciousness is a construction that can be written and rewritten, and nothing in experience tells us what is happening in our minds at the moment we are having it.

When I objected that step (2) seemed to confuse the time at which an experience occurs with the time that the experience represents, he replied that this only relabels the difficulty: if the whole experience occurs only after the second flash, then even the experience of the *starting* location is postponed, so some part of what the viewer takes to be an experience of an early moment is occurring later after all. He calls (4) his 'no-antecedent-information' principle and (8) his 'rewritable stream' conclusion.

I would like you to act as an expert in the philosophy of mind and the cognitive neuroscience of time perception — someone fluent both in the empirical work on apparent motion and in the conceptual debates it has provoked. Please begin by brainstorming the possible fault lines in the argument before committing to a verdict. Then give me:

(a) a step-by-step assessment, rating each of (1)–(8) as clearly mistaken, defensible, or clearly right, with a brief justification for each rating;
(b) your verdict on the single step (or pair of steps) at which the argument actually breaks, together with an explanation of why that step looked plausible in the first place;
(c) a statement of what a defender of the argument would have to surrender in order to accept your diagnosis, and what you would have to surrender in order to accept his conclusion — and, if the argument contains an equivocation, the term that slides between two readings and what those two readings are; and
(d) a direct answer to the question I would most like settled: I am attached to (1)–(3), which strike me as just the experimental facts, and I am unwilling to accept (8); if I must choose, which should I keep, and what does keeping it cost me?

Please do not simply tell me that (8) is too strong or that consciousness is 'constructed.' I want the argument dismantled joint by joint, with the cost of each option made explicit.
