I retired about two years ago from a career in field ecology — four decades of counting things in ponds and hedgerows — and Ive been spending the freed-up time reading philosophy, mostly philosophy of science with a bit of metaphysics on the side. Ive got something I keep going round on and I'd rather you treat it as a real piece of reasoning than a trivia quiz.

Here's the view I've talked myself into. Everybody treats the Fermi silence as a fact about the galaxy: no signals, no probes, no giant engineering projects, so something must be culling civilizations or life must be rare. I think that's a mistake, and I think it's a mistake an ecologist spots straight away. In my old line of work, when a species came up absent in a survey, the prior was almost never that it was extinct — it was that our traps were wrong, our transects were in the wrong place, the thing was cryptic, and it was rare. The default reading of an empty-looking habitat is a bad sampling protocol, not a global catastrophe. I want to say the same about the galaxy: the silence is a fact about us, about what we've actually looked for and how, and we've got no business inferring a filter from it.

And I've got a second hunch that either strengthens this or just makes it stranger. The usual story is that civilizations get louder as they get more capable — more energy, more waste heat, a bigger footprint. I think that might be backwards. Computation gets more efficient per joule over time, so a civilization that matures might get quieter rather than louder, radiating as little as thermodynamics will allow. The most advanced things out there could be the hardest to see, which would mean the energy ladder everyone draws is pointing the wrong way.

So: what would count against this? Not the standard stuff about why nobody's out there — I know the standard stuff, I used to inflict it on undergrads. I mean what observations and arguments should actually move me, sorted by how decisive they'd be. What does my view predict that the rare-life and the filter views don't, and vice versa? What would the world have to look like for me to be wrong — which findings would be decisive, which merely awkward? If a defender of my line had to give up one of the two claims, which goes first, and which one is doing the real work? And be straight with me about the thing that worries me most: we just haven't looked hard enough can be said forever, so have I built myself a view that can't lose? If so, is that fatal, or is it the kind of thing I should just own up to?

One note on how to weigh things, since it's your call: I'm a sucker for arguments from sampling and detection. Tell me my survey design is biased and I'll believe you faster than if you tell me my ontology is wrong.
