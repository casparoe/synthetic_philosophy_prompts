I built the following argument for a graduate seminar on the extended mind, and it now troubles me: each step seems to follow from the one before, yet the conclusion is far stronger than anything I set out to defend. I want to know which step to give up and what giving it up costs.

(1) An external resource belongs to a subject's mind when it plays, for that subject, the functional role an internal resource would play: reliably available on demand, consulted without deliberation, and automatically endorsed, so that losing it leaves the subject cognitively diminished in the way Otto is diminished when his notebook is taken.

(2) Renata has worn a device — call it the Beacon — for eleven years. It overlays the names and salient facts of the people she meets directly onto her visual field. She does not decide to consult it; its deliverances are simply there, as her own recollections are there, and she is disoriented when it fails.

(3) The Beacon satisfies the condition in (1) at least as well as Otto's notebook satisfies it for him. So the Beacon is part of Renata's mind.

(4) The Beacon is not self-sufficient. It draws its content from a company's servers, refreshed nightly, and the refreshes are chosen by a recommendation model trained on the activity of millions of other users. Renata has no access to the servers and no knowledge of the model; she receives only what they place before her.

(5) If the Beacon belongs to her mind, it belongs because of what it does for her, not because of where it sits. The servers do for the Beacon what the Beacon does for Renata: they supply, reliably and without her deliberation, the content she then endorses. A resource that counts by virtue of what it supplies is therefore satisfied by the servers as much as by the Beacon.

(6) And if the servers, then the model that governs them; and if the model, then the stored activity of the millions of strangers that trained it, without which it would supply nothing. So Renata's memory includes the recorded behaviour of people she will never meet.

(7) The same reasoning applies to language, which is likewise reliably available, consulted without deliberation, and endorsed; so every speaker of a language shares a memory with every other speaker, and there ceases to be any principled line between one mind and the next, or between mind and world.

(8) A theory that dissolves the boundary between minds, and between mind and world, is not a theory of mind.

(9) Therefore (1) is false, and no external resource belongs to a mind — not even Otto's notebook.

I would rather keep (1), and I am fond of the Otto case, but I do not accept (7) or (9). Is there a step that does not actually follow from what precedes it, or is one of the early premises simply too costly to hold? If something must go, tell me what it is, why it looked innocent, and what each of the familiar ways of drawing the mind's boundary has to pay in order to stop the slide.
