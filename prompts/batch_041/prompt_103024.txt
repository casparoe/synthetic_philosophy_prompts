Construct a thought experiment to specification, on the norms of assertion.

The object to be built is a single assertion, made by a single speaker in an ordinary setting, on which the three standard candidate norms — the knowledge norm (assert only what you know), the truth norm (assert only what is true), and the belief norm (assert only what you believe) — do not converge but deliver two verdicts against one. The required pattern is this: the truth norm permits the assertion, the belief norm permits it, and the knowledge norm alone forbids it. The case is wanted in order to test whether the knowledge norm can be pulled apart from the truth and belief norms by the social dimension of knowledge alone, rather than by falsity, irrationality, or luck.

The specifications are as follows. (1) The speaker asserts a proposition that is in fact true, and that the speaker genuinely believes; the belief is formed from a source that is in fact reliable and is not the product of guessing, wishful thinking, or a Gettier-style coincidence. (2) What the speaker lacks is knowledge, and the entire difference between the speaker's position and knowledge must be a single isolable feature: the speaker's possession of a reason against relying on that source — a reason the speaker holds but neither acts on nor attends to. Distinguish this, in your exposition, from two neighbors: a reason the speaker merely ought to have and does not, and a doubt the speaker consciously feels; the case must turn on possession-without-attention and not on either neighbor. (3) The setting must be ordinary: no lotteries, no fake barns, no brain-in-a-vat or demon scenarios, no science fiction, no appeal to the size or statistics of a population, and no more than a single paragraph of scenario. (4) The verdicts must be generated by the norms rather than stipulated; if any verdict depends on a contested meta-theory, say which and flag it.

Then supply: (a) the scenario itself; (b) the verdict of each of the three norms, with the reasoning that produces it; (c) the identification of the single feature that does the work, together with a removal test — delete that feature and show that the three verdicts collapse into agreement; and (d) the strongest line of resistance available to a defender of the knowledge norm, stated in the defender's own terms.

Finally, attempt the reverse divergence as a second, harder case: an assertion that the knowledge norm permits but the belief norm forbids — that is, knowledge without belief. Keep it ordinary, make it your own invention rather than a reproduction of any published example, and state plainly whether it succeeds. If it cannot succeed, identify exactly the premise or platitude that blocks it, and say why the block is a genuine impossibility rather than a failure of imagination.
