alright, essay time. Goodhart's law: I want you to actually argue, not just survey. The question is wether Goodhart's law is a real, substantive regularity, or whether it's basically either a tautology or a relabeling of ordinary incentive theory with a spookier name.

But skip the standard tour — you know the drill, nail-factory quotas, cobra bounties, the Soviet stuff. Everyone writes that essay. Instead I want you to build the whole thing around a distinction I think is doing all the work and is almost never made explicit:

- the *decay* reading: the metric really was a decent proxy for the goal, and it degraded because it became a target. That's the causal claim — that's what the "law" is supposed to be.
- the *exposure* reading: the metric was never a good proxy; targeting it just revealed that the correlation was flimsy or non-causal all along. Nothing decayed, because nothing was ever there.

My hunch is that most alleged instances of the law are actually the second thing wearing the first things clothes, and that the honest version of Goodhart's law is way weaker than the slogan suggests. But push back on me if I'm wrong — maybe there's a clean case of genuine decay, and if so, say what makes it clean and what it would take to tell decay from exposure in the wild.

Things I want you to actually deal with:

1. If the decay reading is right, what's the mechanism? Is it that optimizing pushes the system into a regime where the old correlation stops holding (some selection/regression effect), or is it something more like the metric changing the world it was supposed to merely measure? Those feel different to me and I don't think people keep them apart.
2. Can a measure ever be constitutive rather than descriptive — i.e. bring into being the very thing it claims to track? Think about how the idea of the economy as one measurable object came to exist. If measures can do that, does it break the decay/exposure distinction or just complicate it?
3. Take one fresh, non-classic case and run it through the distinction in detail. Not the usual suspects — school league tables, hospital waiting-time targets, credit ratings, h-indexes, pick one and commit. Show which reading fits and why, and be honest if it's a bit of both.

End with a real verdict: is Goodhart's law (a) a genuine law-like regularity, (b) a tautology, or (c) a useful heuristic that's been oversold — and defend it. Don't wimp out with "it depends."
