I am a novelist, and the protagonist of my current book is an epistemologist of a particular and rather austere persuasion: she holds that the only thing that makes one credence epistemically better than another is how close it lies to the truth, and that the ordinary canons of rationality — that credences obey the probability axioms, that they be revised by conditionalization when new evidence arrives — are not independent principles but theorems, derivable from that single commitment by dominance arguments. The shape of the argument, as I understand it, is this: for any credence function that violates the axioms there exists a rival that is more accurate in every possible world, where accuracy is measured by a strictly proper scoring rule, that is, one that an agent who cares only about accuracy minimizes in expectation by stating her true credences. She is a fictional character and I am not a philosopher, so I would rather learn where the position is strong and where it is thin than invent a defence for it. Please give me your credence in the following proposition, and then the reasoning that produces it.

The proposition: that this accuracy-first program can be extended to accommodate the plain fact that a finite mind cannot hold credences about everything, and can do so without surrendering its claim that accuracy is the sole fundamental epistemic good. Take the extension I have in mind to be the following. Replace per-proposition accuracy with a single score for an entire cognitive state: the accuracy of the credences the agent actually holds, weighted by the share of her finite attention each proposition receives, so that one state is better than another when it is more accurate across the propositions to which the agent in fact attends. Call this the attention-weighted reading. Stated precisely, the proposition is that the attention-weighted reading preserves the program's central results — the dominance argument for probabilism, and the corresponding argument for conditionalization — and that it does so without introducing a second, non-accuracy epistemic good, such as the significance or the relevance of a proposition.

Decompose your credence. I want the sub-questions the number rests on, a rough probability for each with a line of justification, and how they combine. The sub-questions I can see are these, though you should add or replace them as you think right: whether accuracy-first epistemology is committed to evaluating credences one proposition at a time, and so cannot be globalized at all; whether a global, attention-weighted score admits of a dominance argument, given that the options being compared now include allocations of attention and not merely assignments of credence; whether any such extension must covertly rank propositions by something other than accuracy, and so is a second good in disguise; and whether the whole question is misfiled, attention being a practical matter of how to spend a finite budget rather than an epistemic one. Tell me which of these terms your final number is most sensitive to, and what argument or result would move it furthest.

If you judge that the proposition as I have stated it is not the one I should be asking — if, say, the attention-weighted reading is a distraction and the real difficulty lies elsewhere — then say what the right proposition is and give me your credence in that instead, with the same decomposition. What I need in the end is a single number between 0 and 1 that I can put in a notebook, together with a short list of the joints at which it could break.
