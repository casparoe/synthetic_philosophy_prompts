I am a philosophy professor at a small liberal arts college, and the graduating class has voted me their commencement speaker — largely, I suspect, because they want someone to say something about AI that is neither a policy memo nor a reassurance. I have eight minutes at the podium. In the room: the graduates, a good half of whom have used an AI assistant for nearly every paper they have written since their second year and are not sorry; their parents, some of whom believe the faculty let this happen; and a board of trustees that adopted a permissive policy over my dissent. Write the address.

I do not want to make the cheating argument or the authenticity argument; I think both are dead ends, and I would rather lose the room than make them. What I believe, and want the speech built around, is this: the thing at risk is not the answers. People have always gotten their answers from other people, and we call that an education. It is the relation between the answer and the person holding it. In April I asked my seminar to state, in three sentences and from memory, the thesis of the essay they had submitted in December; nine of the twenty-two could not. I do not think that number means what my colleagues take it to mean. I think the loss appears first not in what they cannot do but in what they cannot want: a question you have never been stuck on is not a question you have, and if that is right, then the erosion is invisible from the inside, which is exactly why it is worth eight minutes of a commencement address.

Make it a speech and not an essay — speakable aloud, celebratory, and honest about how much I do not know. It must not name a single philosopher or cite anything, and it must not rest on the claim that machines cannot understand; I do not know that, and I will not say it. The heavy users in the audience should leave with a question rather than an accusation, and their parents should leave with something true.
