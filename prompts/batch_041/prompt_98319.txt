Okay so I've had this short-story idea rattling around for a while and at this point I'd rather you just write the thing than help me outline it. Premise: about forty years out there's a massive prediction market, basically a stock exchange, except the assets you trade aren't companies but open claims in math and science. You can buy a share that pays out $1 if the Riemann hypothesis is true and $0 if it's false, and meanwhile it trades at whatever the market thinks, somewhere strictly between. Same deal for whether P = NP, whether some half-formulated physical conjecture holds, you get the picture. The exchange employs auditors whose entire job is to find market makers whose posted prices can be gamed: if your prices let some clever adversary assemble a portfolio of logically related claims that's guaranteed to pay off no matter how the math actually falls out, then you're the irrational one, not the trader cleaning you out. That's the test they use, and I find it interesting because it never asks "are your probabilities correct" (nobody can check that for an unproven theorem) — it only asks "can a sharp counterparty build a sure-profit strategy against your book." I read about something like this once, some people call the underlying idea logical induction, I don't remember the formal details so just use the framing loosely however it's useful.

Here's the philosophical knot I actually want the story to chew on, because I think it's genuinely unsettled and not the kind of thing you can resolve with a slogan: is it even coherent to have a *graded* belief about something that logic has already settled? A coin that's already landed is 0 or 1, no in between, and a theorem that's true was always true — so a purist says any price other than 0 or 1 is just ignorance wearing the costume of uncertainty. But obviously no trader can post 0/1 on everything, because they don't know which way each claim goes, and the exploit-test framing seems to say that's fine as long as your in-between prices hang together in the right way. So what is the honest description of what a 0.7 on an open conjecture even *means*, if there's a fact of the matter out there that's either 1 or 0? I'd like the story to let a character actually wrestle with that instead of having someone deliver a neat answer in dialogue.

Now the plot, and please keep these beats. Protagonist is a young auditor, a couple years out of school, sharp and a little arrogant, who gets handed a contract the senior people keep passing around and refusing to sign off on. It's self-referential: a share whose payout is defined in terms of its own trading price — roughly, it pays $1 if this very share never trades above 0.5, and $0 otherwise. She has to work out whether any pricing of it is un-exploitable at all, and what introducing a claim that refers to itself does to the coherence of the whole book around it. Give her a wry older colleague who's seen markets do stupid things and a rival trader who's clearly already sniffing the same contract. Mood: quiet, technical, a bit melancholic, closer to a detective story than a thriller — fluorescent light, late nights, coffee, the hum of the exchange. Somewhere between 1500 and 2500 words, and resist the urge to end on a tidy moral; I'd rather it close on the unresolved ache of the thing. Don't lecture at me through the narrator, dramatize it.
