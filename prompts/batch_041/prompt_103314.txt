I am a longtime reader of the rationalist blogosphere, so I tend to think of this as a sequence-prediction problem: there is a hidden rule generating the labels, I have a small training set, and the danger is overfitting a rule so baroque that it fits every holding but predicts nothing. I have only the record I give you below; do not assume access to the Court's actual opinions.

Background. I have been following the Constitutional Court of the Republic of Caldera, a small parliamentary democracy with a written constitution. Article 9(2) of that constitution provides that the Court shall not substitute its judgment for the legislature's on questions of conscience, but shall not defer on questions of competence. Nobody has ever given a clean account of where conscience ends and competence begins, and the Court has never adopted a single test in so many words. What it has done is decide a string of cases. I paraphrase the reasoning rather than quoting the opinions.

1. The Court upheld a statute that criminalized assisting another person to die. It reasoned that the statute reflected a contested moral judgment about the value of life and that the legislature was the proper forum for that judgment.

2. The Court upheld a statute that imposed a mandatory waiting period before a divorce could be finalized. It treated the matter as one of conscience and deferred.

3. The Court upheld a statute that banned a religious minority from performing a particular ritual slaughter. It described the ban as a moral and religious question on which the legislature's judgment was final.

4. The Court struck down a statute that raised the threshold for a new political party to enter parliament from two percent to twelve percent of the vote. It reasoned that the change insulated the established parties from competition and made it practically impossible for new entrants to win seats.

5. The Court struck down a statute that transferred the power to appoint and remove the chair of the electoral commission from a cross-party panel to the prime minister. It reasoned that the change gave the incumbent government control over the administration of the very elections in which it would compete.

6. The Court struck down a statute that gave the government the power to license which civil-society organizations could receive foreign funding. It reasoned that the licensing scheme was not narrowly tailored and that it burdened the ability of opposition groups to organize.

7. The Court struck down a statute that required a new political party to obtain the government's permission before it could register. It reasoned that the requirement was a direct barrier to political competition.

Next week the Court hears a new case. A statute, the University Governance Act, gives the government the power to appoint and remove the heads of all public universities. The statute was passed after extensive committee hearings, includes a sunset clause that will repeal it in five years unless parliament renews it, and does not touch elections, party registration, campaign finance, or the electoral commission. The government says the Act is a matter of competence, namely public administration and the stewardship of state-funded institutions. The challengers say it is a matter of conscience, namely the freedom of the academic community to govern itself, and that in any event the government is seizing a lever of cultural influence. The Court must decide whether the Act is constitutional.

What I want from you:

(a) A prediction, stated as a verdict, of how the Court will rule and why. Give me a confidence level if you like, but give me a verdict.

(b) The set of decision rules that fit the seven earlier decisions. I expect more than one; spell out at least three, and be explicit about what each one implies for the University Governance Act.

(c) Your best guess at the rule the Court is actually running, with the reasons for preferring it over the others, and an honest note on what would falsify your guess.

(d) The single future case that would best discriminate among the surviving rules, one the Court has not yet heard, and what verdict under each rule would tell us which rule is doing the work.

(e) If the precedents genuinely underdetermine the new case, say so, and state what principle the Court would be adopting with each available verdict, so that I can see the cost of each choice.

Prefer the simplest rule that survives the data, but tell me where the simplicity is bought at the cost of explanatory power. Do not hedge at the end: pick the verdict you would bet on.
