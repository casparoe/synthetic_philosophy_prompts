I'm teaching a seminar on social epistemology and the philosophy of democracy -- 18 students, one 90-minute session -- and I want to build the whole session around a game that makes them feel the force of Condorcet's jury theorem rather than just hearing me state it. Condorcet's result (1785, the essay on applying probability analysis to majority decisions) says that if each voter is a bit better than a coin flip and their errors are independent, the majority gets more reliable as you add voters, converging on certainty; and if they're worse than a coin flip, adding voters makes things worse and the best jury is one person. I don't want to lecture that up front. I want the room to *run into* it.

You're an expert in social epistemology and an experienced seminar teacher who designs classroom simulations. Please think carefully and start by brainstorming two or three candidate mechanics before you commit to one, then write me the final activity in full.

Here's my rough sketch, improve on it freely. Prepare a deck of ~20 binary items with determinate answers that individuals get right only most of the time (mix easy perceptual judgments with mildly tricky logic/fact items; aim for individual accuracy around 65-70%, which I can calibrate in advance by piloting on a few colleagues). Round 1: everyone answers privately on cards, staking points on their own answer; we tally individual accuracy vs. majority accuracy and compare. Round 2: same items, but before answering, everyone hears the same "expert tip" from me, right some of the time and wrong the rest. Now the errors are correlated and I want the majority to end up no better than, or worse than, the average individual -- that's the moment the whole thing turns on independence and not on crowd wisdom as such. Round 3: I want a set of items where individual competence is deliberately pushed below chance (obvious-looking answers that are wrong), so they see that more voters can be actively harmful.

Give me: the exact rules, materials, the payoff/points scheme (no real money), a minute-by-minute timeline for the 90 minutes, and a debrief that maps specific moments in the game onto the theorem's assumptions -- independence, identical competence, p above or below 1/2, and the monotonicity result. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it (I expect someone will notice they can free-ride on the group and start copying, which is itself worth discussing). And be honest about what the activity can't reproduce: we're 18 people in a room, so I'll have to supply the asymptotic convergence myself, and the theorem also assumes there's a determinate fact of the matter for each item, which is exactly what's contested for most real political questions -- say how to handle that gap in the debrief without deflating them.
