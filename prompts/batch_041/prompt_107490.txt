I am a novelist, about a third of the way into a book whose central character is a moral philosopher — a man of genuine rigor and a certain coldness — and I have given him a position that leaves me half-persuaded and half-suspicious, which is precisely the trouble. He holds, roughly, that an individual's ordinary contributions to large-scale collective harms — a few transatlantic flights a year, a meat-heavy diet, a single vote, a household's worth of plastic — are morally inert. His argument runs in two steps. First, the difference-making premise: for any such act, there is no outcome that would have been different had the agent acted otherwise, because the harm in question is produced by millions of acts and is insensitive to any one of them. Second, the responsibility premise: a person is blameworthy for a harm only if her act made a difference to whether, or how much, that harm occurred. He concludes that the guilt people feel about these things is a superstition of scale — a category error rather than a moral failing. I would like you to tell me what would count against this position: not what a polemicist would shout, but the arguments, cases, and possible findings that ought to genuinely move someone who holds it.

Please be concrete, and please sort what you give me. I am especially interested in pressure on the difference-making premise rather than mere denials of it. Consider the case where one's act is the trigger that tips a collective over a threshold — the last person onto the bridge, the signature that meets the quota; the case where the harm is divisible and one's share, though tiny, is genuinely nonzero; the case where the relevant difference lies not in the outcome but in the probability of the outcome, so that a risk-imposing act can be wrong even when the world happens to be insensitive to it; and the case where a single act propagates through imitation or market signals, so that its expected marginal effect is not zero after all. Which of these can a defender of the position absorb simply by clarifying what he means by "difference," which force a real revision of one of the two premises, and which — if any — refute him outright? I also want the mirror-image worry pursued honestly: the position seems to exonerate almost everyone for almost everything done in concert, including the firing squad in which only one rifle is loaded. Is that a reductio, or is it the correct conclusion, and does the answer turn on whether we are asking about blameworthiness, about causal responsibility, or about what one ought to do rather than what one deserves?

And finally, the question I actually need settled before I can write the chapter: is this position well insulated — has the difference-making premise been quietly weakened to the point where nothing could count against it — and if so, is that a defect or merely the mark of a modest thesis correctly stated? Tell me what the strongest version of his view is, the one worth putting in a character's mouth, and what evidence or argument would most move a careful holder of it. I would rather my philosopher be defeated by something real than by rhetoric.
