I am a second-year PhD student in political theory, and next month I am presenting the central chapter of my thesis — a forty-minute paper — at a small two-day workshop on the epistemic credentials of democracy. I know most of the audience in advance, and I would much rather have the questions these particular people will actually ask than a general list of the standard objections to my topic.

The paper defends what I call the dispersion thesis about the Condorcet jury theorem. The theorem is normally read as a large-number result: if each voter is more likely than not to be right, and votes independently, then the majority is right with a probability tending to one as the electorate grows. I argue that the asymptotic half of the theorem is a red herring for institutional design and that its normative content lies in the finite case. Two claims carry the paper. The first is that, at finite n, the reliability of majority rule is not determined by average competence: so far as the limit goes, all that matters is whether average competence clears one half, but away from the limit the shape of the competence distribution does independent work. The second is that expansion is not reliability-preserving. My illustration is this: a three-member body whose members are each right with probability 0.9 is right by majority about 97 per cent of the time; add two members who are right with probability 0.45, and the five-member body is right by majority only about 78 per cent of the time, even though the mean competence is still a comfortable 0.72. From this I conclude that the theorem cannot underwrite any general presumption that larger or more inclusive bodies are epistemically better, and I propose that the condition an epistemic democrat actually needs is not that the average member beats chance but that no substantial bloc of the body falls below chance — a condition I readily admit is easier to state than to satisfy. I close with the implication for sortition: a citizens' assembly of a few hundred is nowhere near the limit, so its reliability turns on the shape of its competence distribution rather than on its size, which makes the Condorcetian case for sortition weaker than its friends suppose.

The room will contain: two social choice theorists, who will want to know whether my claims are theorems or illustrations, and whether they survive replacing independence with exchangeability; a deliberative democrat who holds that competence is produced by deliberation rather than given before it, and who will say I have assumed away the very thing the theory is meant to explain; an experimentalist who thinks real electorates are correlated through partisanship and shared information, and who will say the model is a toy; a Bayesian epistemologist who will ask what competence is a competence at when there is no settled fact of the matter and no common prior; and a legal theorist of the legitimacy-not-truth school who will say that the whole epistemic framing is a category error.

Please draft my Q&A prep sheet: the eight or so objections I will actually get from these people, ordered by how much damage they would do me, each phrased as the person raising it would phrase it, with a two-minute reply and a note on where the reply is soft. Tell me what to concede in my opening remarks before anyone asks for it, which single reply has to land for the paper to survive the session, which of the objections I should treat as fatal if I cannot answer it, and which point in my own position I should prepare to defend rather than hope nobody notices. Finally, tell me the question I am hoping nobody asks.
