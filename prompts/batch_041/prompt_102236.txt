I'm running a 90-minute session for a upper-level epistemology seminar — 14 students, mostly majors, a couple of grad students — and I want to teach the wrong kind of reasons problem as an activity rather than a lecture. The thing I want them to feel is the specific queasiness of a reason that attaches to an attitude because of what having the attitude does for you, not because the attitude fits its object. I'm especially interested in the graded case: not 'believe p because you'll get paid,' but 'have a credence above some threshold in p because you'll get paid if you do,' and I want the payoff to be verified by a self-report plus a quick confidence check so they can't just lie. I have a projector, index cards, and a bag of poker chips; no real money. No lecture up front — they should run into the problem in the game before it gets named.

Design a game where they stake credences under a payoff scheme that rewards a credence the evidence doesn't support, and ideally build in the nasty recursive wrinkle where the payoff itself is weak evidence about p — so the pragmatic reason can't be cleanly separated from the epistemic one. Then a debrief that maps the moments onto the object-given/state-given distinction, the fittingness of credences, and the question whether credences can be reasons-responsive at all. The activity should make them feel the pull of 'I have a reason to raise my credence' and then the vertigo of asking whether that reason is the right kind. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time — my guess is they'll treat it as a betting game and not notice the attitude/reason mismatch — and be honest about which feature of the problem the game can't reproduce, e.g. the full debate about whether wrong-kind reasons are genuine reasons at all or just reasons for wanting or preferring an attitude.
