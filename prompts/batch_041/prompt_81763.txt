You are a philosopher of cognitive science with a specialist's command of the symbol grounding debate and of the competing theories of meaning -- referential, definitional, inferentialist, use-based, teleosemantic. I want you to audit an analogy that is doing real argumentative work in a discussion I am part of, and to be strict about what that analogy can and cannot carry. Begin by brainstorming: list the elements of the source domain, list the elements of the target domain, and only then decide what maps onto what. Think carefully at each step; a fluent answer that skips the mapping work is worth less to me than a slow one that does it. I do not need citations, a reading list, or a survey of the literature -- the argument is the deliverable, not the bibliography.

The analogy comes from Harnad's 1990 paper on the symbol grounding problem, which frames the difficulty like this: "The problem is analogous to trying to learn Chinese from a Chinese/Chinese dictionary alone." The picture is of an agent who knows no Chinese, is handed a Chinese-to-Chinese dictionary, and is told to acquire the language by working through the entries. Every definition resolves into further Chinese words, so the reader circulates among symbols and never reaches anything that is not a symbol. The moral drawn is that a system whose tokens are manipulated solely by their shapes can have meaning only parasitically, on the meanings already in our heads.

In my reading group this analogy is deployed to license a fairly strong conclusion about large language models: that a text-only system trained on text cannot mean anything at all, and therefore that scaling such systems is beside the point, since more of the same ungrounded symbols will never add up to understanding. I am partly sympathetic and partly suspicious, and I want to know which parts of that argument are being carried by the analogy and which are being carried by something else.

Please do the following.

1. Reconstruct the argument in its strongest form, so that the analogy's work is explicit: state exactly which premise the dictionary picture is supposed to establish, and what further premises are needed to get from there to the conclusion about language models.

2. Give me the correspondence table. What in the target plays the role of the symbol token, of the definition, of the dictionary as a whole, of the reader, of the reader's own knowledge of the script and of the world, of the lexicographers, and of the speech community that keeps a dictionary honest? Be explicit about whether each slot has a genuine occupant in the target or is simply empty.

3. Say where the mapping breaks, and separate the breaks that matter for the conclusion from the ones that are merely decorative. I am especially interested in three things: whether the analogy establishes that grounding must be sensorimotor, or only that it cannot be purely symbolic; whether the picture presupposes a definitional theory of meaning and so begs the question against inferentialist or use-based accounts on which a model's competence might legitimately count as meaning; and whether the source domain is quietly working through features the target lacks -- a reader who is already a grounded perceiver, a finished artifact rather than an ongoing process, a single agent rather than a population of speakers.

4. Tell me whether the conclusion survives. If it does, state precisely what survives and in what weakened form; if it does not, say which premise fails. Do not rescue it out of charity, and do not refute it out of pedantry.

5. If there is a better analogy for what is actually at stake here, give it, and say what it gets right that the dictionary gets wrong.
