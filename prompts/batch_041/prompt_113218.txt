I'm in a reading group that has spent the term on Madhyamaka, and for the final session I want to close with a poem rather than another summary. What I want the poem to carry is Nāgārjuna's tetralemma — the four-cornered analysis that runs a claim through the options that it is so, that it is not so, that it both is and is not so, and that it neither is nor is not so — and, more than the four options themselves, the way they function in his hands: not as four candidate answers among which one is finally correct, but as a device on which each candidate, stated fully and in good faith, comes apart in the mouth of whoever states it, so that no fifth position is left standing either. I do not want a poem that lists four views and shrugs; I want the form to do the work.

So please write a poem in four numbered sections, one for each corner, built around a single refrain — one line that closes every section. The discipline I care about is this: the refrain must not change by a single word from first to last, and yet it should read in the first section as a confident affirmation, then shade into denial, then into something that hovers between the two, until by the fourth section it no longer asserts anything at all. Let the sections be short, four to six lines each, and give the poem a brief envoy at the end, addressed to the reader's itch to ask which corner is finally true. Keep the register plain and unhurried — this is for people who have been arguing for three months and are tired of it — and anchor it in some ordinary, concrete claim rather than in abstraction, so that there is something for the analysis to circle. Someone who knows the tetralemma should feel each corner being taken seriously and then quietly losing its footing, and should recognize that the ending refuses the fifth option rather than supplying it; someone who does not know it should still come away with a good poem. Please keep it from drifting into either a lecture on emptiness or a fog of mysticism — the whole point is that ordinary things keep going at the level of everyday practice even as the analysis dissolves — and do not lean on Sanskrit beyond what is needed or add footnotes.
