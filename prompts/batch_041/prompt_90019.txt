I'm finishing a chapter in a philosophy-of-science project on a phenomenon I've come to call the premature-refutation problem: a theory issues a prediction whose characteristic timescale is longer than the period over which we have actually been able to observe, so that an apparent refutation may be nothing more than an artifact of the window we happen to be looking through. Malthus is my anchor case — the claim that population tends to outrun the means of subsistence, and the awkward fact that the predicted catastrophe has not arrived in the places and periods where the theory was supposed to bite — but I want to treat him as a specimen rather than litigate the exegesis. The general question is when an apparent refutation of a long-run prediction should be taken at face value, and when it is legitimate to rescue the theory by revising auxiliary assumptions, extending the timescale, or redescribing what the theory was really claiming. I keep running into the usual machinery — Duhem–Quine underdetermination, Lakatos on progressive versus degenerating research programmes — and it has started to feel like these supply diagnoses rather than decision procedures.

Now suppose I'm advising a small foundation that can support roughly five researchers for three years on precisely this cluster of questions, with the money restricted to conceptual and methodological work rather than new empirical history. Which open questions should they put first, and in what order? I care more about the reasoning than the list: for each question, why it is upstream of the others, how tractable it is for philosophers rather than for economic historians or demographers, and what would move it up or down the ranking — say, a defensible account of when a timescale extension is ad hoc rather than principled, or a demonstration that the whole problem collapses back into ordinary theory choice. A few candidates I've been kicking around, which you should feel free to reject, merge, or replace: Is there a principled line between the verdict that a theory was refuted and the verdict that it is merely early? Does that distinction even survive contact with theories whose predictions are probabilistic or conditional rather than dated? And is there any way to score a long-run prediction's track record without quietly importing our own present as the privileged vantage point from which the scoring is done?

Please close by naming the single question you would fund first, and saying what evidence over the next three years would make you change your mind about that choice.
