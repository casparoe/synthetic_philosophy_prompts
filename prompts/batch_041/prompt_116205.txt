I am a statistician by training and I spend my working life on causal inference -- directed acyclic graphs, potential outcomes, do-calculus, mediation and sensitivity analysis -- and I have fallen into a recurring argument with a colleague in the philosophy department about the Frankfurt cases. I have an undergraduate acquaintance with the free-will literature but I have never worked through the secondary debate, and my colleague's talk of alternative possibilities and flickers of freedom tends to slide past me. I know the shape of the cases well enough: a counterfactual intervener waits in the wings, prepared to make the agent perform the action if the agent were about to refrain, but the agent performs it on their own, so the intervener never acts; the intuition is that the agent is nevertheless responsible, and the moral drawn is that responsibility does not require the ability to have done otherwise, so the principle of alternative possibilities is in trouble. What I would like from you is a faithful restatement of that debate in the vocabulary I actually use, followed by an honest residue report. Render the ability to have done otherwise as carefully as you can in causal terms: what is the treatment, what is the outcome, where does the intervener sit in the graph, what exactly is held fixed when we say the alternative was unavailable, and what happens to the analysis when the intervener fires only probabilistically rather than with certainty. Do the same for the response that relocates the agent's freedom from the action to the decision itself -- the thought that a flicker of freedom survives even though the action was guaranteed -- and for the distinction between causal responsibility and moral responsibility. I want to know which parts of the debate map cleanly onto counterfactual dependence under an intervention, which parts survive only in a distorted form, and which parts have no counterpart at all in do-calculus or potential outcomes.

My own suspicion, which I would like you to test rather than flatter, is that the ability to have done otherwise is just counterfactual dependence, that the interventionist reading is the natural one, and that much of the debate is a confusion about which counterfactual is under evaluation -- the one that holds the agent's deliberation fixed versus the one that intervenes on the agent's decision. If that suspicion is right, say so and show the translation. If it is wrong, I want to know precisely where my vocabulary runs out: is the missing piece something a causal model ought to be able to express but does not, or is it something that was never a causal claim in the first place -- a claim about reasons, capacities, or desert that no graph can carry? I would rather have the residue than a tidy correspondence, so please do not smooth over the places where the analogy breaks.
