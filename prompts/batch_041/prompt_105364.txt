My reading group has been grinding through Plato's Meno for weeks (we're slow, and we keep getting derailed), and last night two of them — Ravi and Cass — went at each other over the Meno problem itself, i.e. why knowledge is supposed to be better than mere true belief, and I lost track of who had the better of it. Ravi started with the obvious answer: Socrates's bit about true opinions being like Daedalus's statues that run off unless you tie them down, and knowledge being the tied-down version — so knowledge is better because it's stable and keeps paying off, whereas the lucky true belief you might lose at any moment. Cass said that can't be the whole story, because "keeps paying off" is just a claim about future usefulness, and she could stipulate a true believer who's totally confident, will never be shaken, and so gets exactly the same practical mileage out of it as the knower; and if the extra ingredient in knowledge doesn't make the belief any truer — the truth was already there, that was the setup — then she couldn't see where the extra value was coming from at all, and said the answer had to be something non-instrumental or there was no answer.

Ravi took that up: fine, non-instrumental — knowledge is creditable to you in a way a lucky true belief isn't. The lucky believer just happened to be right; the knower earned it, and that's why we'd rather know. Cass said credit comes apart from getting it right in both directions: someone can use a method that's unreliable in general and still get full credit when it works, and someone can know and yet be so muddled about how they got there that it isn't really theirs. She also said credit looks like it's about the believer's scorecard rather than about the belief being better, and the Meno question was about why knowledge is better, not why knowers are better off. Ravi then tried: maybe the value is social — you can ask a knower for their reasons and they can give them, so we can rely on them and hold them to account, whereas the mere true believer has nothing to offer when pressed. Cass said that's just justification doing the work again, and Ravi had stipulated the true believer is right, so what's the complaint; and she added that we don't even always prefer knowledge — the patient who merely believes, truly, that they'll pull through might be better off than the one who knows the odds — at which point Ravi said that's a different question entirely (whether you'd want the belief at all, not whether knowledge beats true belief), and they went around again.

I couldnt referee it. Could you go through their exchange move by move and say who's right at each step — not just who wins overall — and where each of them is missing something? I'd rather have the specific moves rated (clearly mistaken up to clearly right, with a sentence of justification each) than a verdict that just says they're both partly right, and if there's a distinction they're both fumbling toward without quite naming it, tell me what that is.
