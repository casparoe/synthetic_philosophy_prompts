I'm helping run a small reading group on autonomous systems and accountability, and every paper we read seems to be arguing past the others. Before we read any more I want the spec, not another theory: what does a theory of responsibility attribution for autonomous systems actually have to deliver? Give me the requirements list — the cases it has to get right, the distinctions it can't be allowed to collapse, the facts about how these systems really work that it can't contradict — with the consideration or case behind each requirement. Then tell me which of the things people demand are genuine requirements and which are just habits of one camp (I suspect half of them are criminal-law reflexes).

I don't need a literature review, just the criteria and why each one is on the list. The cases I care about most, roughly:

- The relay: the model that makes the call was trained by one party on another party's operational data, fine-tuned by a third, deployed by a fourth, and keeps updating on live data after deployment. Nobody can point to who authored the policy that produced the harm, or even to what the system "decided."
- Harmless culprits: every component and every human step is defensible in isolation, and the harm is emergent from the interaction. Nobody's behaviour was even sub-standard.
- The well-behaved swarm: the operator knew the fleet would cause some number of harms a year, took the cheapest of several available precautions, and the precaution wasn't enough. No individual harm was foreseeable or avoidable.
- Drift: after deployment the policy shifts in a way no human could have anticipated or vetoed in the time available, so "they could have stopped it" is false at every moment.

One thing I want the theory to take a stance on rather than assume: whether there's really a gap at all, or whether ordinary responsibility concepts just stretch to cover these cases. Make that a requirement, not a premise.

Distinctions I want either respected or explicitly rejected (say which and why): causing a harm vs answering for it vs bearing its cost; blame for what happened vs responsibility for what happens next; individual vs corporate/collective responsibility; responsibility for a token harm vs responsibility for a pattern or practice; having responsibility vs how much and owed to whom.

Some candidate principles I keep hearing and can't tell if they're real desiderata — rule on each, and say whether they can be held together:

- no orphans: every harm has to land on at least one agent who at some point could have done otherwise;
- no new agents: we don't get to invent a responsible machine just to plug the hole;
- substrate neutrality: the verdict shouldn't change just because the decision-maker is silicon rather than carbon, absent a principled reason;
- don't let the responsibility question dissolve into a cost-internalization question — if the theory just says "price it in," it has dodged the thing I asked about.

Also two things I'll treat as fixed: we already hold corporations responsible without thinking they're moral agents, and responsibility doesn't vanish into thin air just because it's hard to place. If a theory can't live with those, say so and why.

Finally: which single requirement does the most work at excluding candidates? And is there a subset here that can't all be satisfied at once — if so that's the result I actually want, so say which ones and why they clash. Then a short pass over the three families we keep circling — (i) just distribute it to the humans who built, deployed or operated the thing, (ii) extend agency to the system or its corporate owner, (iii) the gap is real but only bites for blame, not for liability or regulation — and tell me which survives the list best and where it fails. Keep the weight on the criteria; the scorecard is secondary.
