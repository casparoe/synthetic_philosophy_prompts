I'm the secretary of a small housing cooperative — about thirty units, a volunteer board, and a set of bylaws that everyone treats as the last word on how decisions get made. Our bylaws say they can be amended by a two-thirds vote of the membership at a general meeting, and they say nothing else at all about amendments. Last month a faction that had been pushing for years to lower the threshold got their two-thirds, and passed an amendment rewriting the amendment clause itself: from now on bylaws can be changed by a simple majority, and a further sentence was added saying that this new procedure cannot itself be amended for ten years. It went through cleanly under the old rules. Now we're stuck, and the meeting we're holding next week is already contentious. Some members say the change is plainly valid, because it was adopted exactly as the old rules required; others say it cannot be, because a bare majority should not be able to strip away the protection the two-thirds requirement existed to give, and a rule that tries to lock itself in place is attempting something a rule just can't do. There are wrinkles I keep turning over on my own. If the new procedure is invalid, then the meeting that adopted it was, under the old rules, an ordinary meeting that did something it had no power to do — but then which rules govern the vote to undo it? If the new procedure is valid, it looks as though we could amend it anyway, ten-year lock or no, since the lock is itself just a bylaw provision and the amendment clause now says bylaws need only a majority. And whichever way I go, I notice I'm leaning on the idea that the two-thirds rule had some original authority behind it — which only pushes the question back a step, to whatever rule authorized *it* in the first place.

Could you help me see what's actually going on here? I'd like to understand the real options for making sense of a rule that governs its own change: which of my neighbors' positions, if either, is defensible, whether the ten-year lock changes anything, and what a small organization should actually do in practice when it lands in this position. Please explain it in plain language and don't assume I know any technical vocabulary; if there is a standard way of framing these puzzles, tell me what it is and how the different framings differ, but the goal is to understand the situation, not to be handed a citation or told to consult a lawyer. I'd also like you to be candid about your confidence: say which consideration would push you toward a different answer than the one you give, and where you think your own reasoning is most likely to fail.
