I teach philosophy and sit on the ethics committee at the hospital we're affiliated with, and Thursday's case is turning into a fight. Woman in her late 70s, diagnosed with early dementia about three years ago, signed an advance directive back when she was still sharp: no antibiotics, no hospitalization, no artificial nutrition, daughter named as proxy. She now has aspiration pneumonia that would probably clear up with a course of antibiotics. On the ward she's cheerful, oriented to person and place most days, says she wants the antibiotics, and doesnt remember signing anything. Her daughter says the directive is the whole point, that her mom watched a parent linger and was adamant about it. Two of our nurses think the daughter is overriding the patient she has now; one of our physicians says the cheerful version of her isnt competent to weigh her own future and the earlier self should govern. Then the wrinkle: the directive has a line the patient insisted on adding, saying that if she ever seemed content and asked to be treated, that should count as a change of heart. Some of the committee reads that as decisive for treatment; others say a demented person's contentment cant revoke a considered directive.

Draft the opinion I'll circulate before the meeting. It should recommend a concrete course of action with a time frame, and it should give the strongest argument on the losing side a fair hearing rather than just declaring a winner. Skip the legal boilerplate, counsel handles that. I want the philosophical shape of it: what, if anything, makes the earlier self's authority bind the later one, and how much weight the current self's expressed preferences and apparent contentment should get.
