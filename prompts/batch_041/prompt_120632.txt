You are a political theorist trained in the philosophy of the social sciences, especially the problems that arise with thick, partly evaluative concepts and their measurement. Write a dialogue between two equally competent interlocutors on whether democratic backsliding names a real phenomenon or is a partisan label in disguise.

Give them a specific standard to fight over, stipulated here rather than attributed to any existing author: the handover test, on which an institutional change counts as backsliding just in case it reduces the likelihood that an electoral defeat would in fact cost the incumbent power. The defender holds that the test rescues the concept — it is behavioral, applies symmetrically to any party, and yields determinate verdicts in Hungary, Poland, Turkey, and the United States. The skeptic holds that it only relocates the problem: credibility is an observer's judgment, the counterfactual cannot be falsified before the fact, and the test's apparent neutrality conceals a substantive commitment about which kinds of entrenchment are legitimate — measures a reforming coalition takes against a court captured by its predecessor look like entrenchment too, so either the test condemns them or the defender must smuggle in a normative distinction between entrenchment that serves electoral competition and entrenchment that defeats it.

Neither side may be a strawman, and each must answer the other's strongest point in the other's own terms rather than talking past it. Let them run the handover test on at least two of the four countries and see whether it survives. Each side should concede something real; the skeptic should offer a positive alternative rather than a shrug. End by narrowing the disagreement to one precise question rather than dissolving it. Before writing, spend a few sentences brainstorming the best objection available to each side.
