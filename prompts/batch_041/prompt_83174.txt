I have been asked to give the commencement address at our community college's graduation this June, and I would like you to write it. The cohort is unusual: about a third of the graduates are over sixty, most of them people who returned to school after a divorce, a layoff, a retirement, or the death of a spouse. I teach philosophy here, and most of the older graduates have sat through my introduction to ethics. The faculty in the room will know Cicero's Cato Maior de Senectute; the families will not. The graduates themselves are religiously mixed, a few are visibly frail, and at least one has a diagnosis she has not disclosed to the college.

I want the address built around the argument of that dialogue rather than around the usual commencement furniture about journeys and open doors. As I understand it, the elderly Cato is made to answer four complaints about old age: that it removes us from public affairs, that it weakens the body, that it takes away sensual pleasure, and that it brings death close. His reply is that most of these are faults of character rather than of age, that the old can still act through counsel, authority, and judgment when strength and speed fail, and that the loss of appetite is a liberation rather than a deprivation. Correct me in the appended note if I have misstated the structure; the address itself should carry no footnotes, no citations, and no quotation from the text, and should not use Latin words unless they are glossed immediately.

Two features of the dialogue should do structural work in the speech rather than being decorative allusions. First, Cato concedes that in extreme poverty old age cannot be light even for a wise person. That concession is uncomfortable in a room where some graduates are poor, and the address should not smooth it over. Second, the whole speech is put into the mouth of a notoriously severe censor at eighty-four, with his own death close, by a Cicero of sixty-three writing in the year of Caesar's assassination, with his own life nearly over. The equanimity is therefore both borrowed and under pressure, and the address should let that show without becoming a lecture on the text.

The hardest part is the fourth complaint. Some of these graduates buried spouses this year. I do not want to promise what I cannot promise, and I do not want to be merely stoic in a way that insults people who are grieving. Cicero's Cato ends by holding out the soul's survival in a manner that refuses to be a proof, treating it as something he would not surrender even if it turned out to be an error. I would like the ending built as a stance rather than as a claim.

Write the address at roughly nine hundred to twelve hundred words, in a register suitable for reading aloud, warm but not sentimental, and intelligible to someone who has never read a page of Cicero. Do not invent specific anecdotes about real members of the audience; where you need a concrete detail, keep it generic or clearly hypothetical.

After the address, add a short note addressed to me rather than to the audience. In it, do three things. Identify where the speech leans on a reading of De Senectute that a classicist might dispute, and describe what the speech would look like under the rival reading. Say where the speech has softened, compressed, or invented something, and why. Finally, say which consideration or argument would most likely lead you to handle the fourth complaint, or the poverty concession, differently, and be specific about where the reasoning in the address is most likely to fail with this particular room.
