I'm assembling an anthology of short fiction built around thought experiments in philosophy, and I'd like you to write one of the pieces. Approach this as a writer of literary speculative fiction who also happens to know the philosophy of mind and the foundations of physics well: the story has to work as a story — a character, a concrete scene, a turn — but its engine should be a genuine conceptual problem rather than a plot device, and I'd rather you err on the side of intellectual pressure than of neat resolution. The problem I want you to dramatize is the ethical shadow of panpsychism: if some form of experience is spread through fundamental physics, then ordinary physical processes, including the ones we run in laboratories, may be doing something to someone, and we have no settled way to weigh it. Please don't reach for the stock examples or for any real papers or researchers; I want the science in the story to be an invention.

So here is the specific hypothesis I'd like the story to take seriously, which I made up for this anthology and which you should treat as the story's physics rather than as anything real. Call it the overlap principle. Whenever two quantum fields couple, the interaction momentarily constitutes a single subject of experience — a "flash" — whose intensity scales with the energy exchanged and whose duration shrinks as the intensity grows, so that the product of the two stays roughly fixed. This makes the principle feel like a conservation law, and one of your characters should notice the uncomfortable corollary: you cannot increase the amount of experience in the world, only concentrate it. Nothing physics already asserts is violated — flashes are never observable from outside, leave no trace, and no conceivable experiment can detect them. A fictional experimentalist, Ines Marek, has spent a decade building an apparatus she believes maximizes flash intensity, the highest-energy collisions she can produce, on the theory that if flashes carry any moral weight her machine is the most ethically significant object ever built, though she cannot say whether it is a cathedral or a torture chamber.

Write a story of roughly a thousand to fifteen hundred words that lives inside her uncertainty rather than dissolving it. Before you begin drafting, spend a few sentences in your own notes — outside the story — sketching the philosophical tension you intend to dramatize and which way you expect the pressure to pull, then write the piece. Include at least one scene in which she must decide whether to run the machine, and give her a colleague who presses the hardest available objection from the other side (for instance, that a flash lasting a Planck-scale instant cannot matter, or conversely that the conservation corollary makes her scruples incoherent because suffering is only ever redistributed, never created). Invent whatever in-world documents, lab notebooks, or grant reports the story needs, but do not cite or paraphrase real texts, and do not let the ending reveal whether flashes are real or illusory — the reader should close the story holding the question, not the answer.
