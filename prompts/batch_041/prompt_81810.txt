I'm a software engineer with no formal training in philosophy, and I've been trying to work out my own views on free will by running through a set of cases and seeing where my gut actually lands. I'd like you to tell me what principle, if any, unifies my verdicts, and to be honest about the ones it can't save.

Here is where I come out. (1) A colleague was raised in a rigid religious community that shaped nearly every value she has; as an adult she still acts on those values, and when she does I think she acts freely and is responsible for it. (2) A man whose behavior changes because of a brain tumor, who becomes cruel in ways that match nothing about him, strikes me as not responsible, or much less so. (3) Someone given a post-hypnotic suggestion who then, on cue, does something she would never otherwise do — I think she is not free in doing it, even though she experiences herself as deciding. (4) If physicists announced tomorrow that the universe is fully deterministic, I don't think I would change a single verdict above: the ordinary person who deliberates and endorses her own values is still free, the tumor patient still isn't. (5) But when I imagine a machine that first simulates my brain completely — computes exactly what I would decide, down to the felt experience of weighing reasons — and only then writes that decision into my brain, so that I go through the motions of deliberating and then arrive at the pre-computed outcome, I think I am not free in that case, even though the outcome is the one I would have reached anyway and feels entirely mine. (6) A decision caused by a genuinely random event, a quantum coin flip wired into my brain, seems to me no freer than a deterministic one; randomness is not freedom either.

My tentative principle is something like: an action is free when it flows from the agent's own settled character and reasons, and the agent would have acted differently had her reasons been different, and it does not matter whether that character and those reasons were themselves caused by something outside her. I'll call this authorship without origination. But I can see it cannot handle everything: cases (3) and (5) both seem to be actions that flow from the agent's character and reasons, yet I don't call them free, while case (1) is a character imposed from outside, yet I do call it free. Case (5) is the one I keep going back and forth on.

So: what principle captures the most of my verdicts, and which of them does it force me to abandon? For whichever principle you favor, please state it precisely, tell me which of my cases motivates each clause, and say what it predicts for cases I haven't listed — for instance, an autopilot that has been given a rich model of its goals and reasons and reliably acts on them, or a person who fully and reflectively endorses the person she has become even though she can see exactly how she was made that way. I would also like to know whether I am really a compatibilist or an incompatibilist, because I genuinely cannot tell which label fits the mess above.
