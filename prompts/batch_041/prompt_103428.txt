OK so I've been going down a rabbit hole on French laïcité and I keep hitting the same wall. The official story is that the state is rigourously neutral about religion: pupils in state schools can't wear conspicuous religious signs, the state recognises and funds no faith, and religion is supposed to be a purely private affair. Fine. But then you look at what actually happens. The state pays the salaries of teachers in private schools that sign a contract with it (overwhelmingly Catholic ones), it pays chaplains in prisons, hospitals and the army, it maintains the churches that were built before the great separation law, and in parts of the east, around Alsace and the Moselle, it still pays priests, pastors and rabbis out of the public purse and puts religious instruction on the school timetable. Meanwhile it goes to enormous trouble to conjure up a single official body to represent Muslims, so that it has somebody to negotiate with. So on one hand it won't touch religion; on the other it funds it, houses it, organises it and presides over its institutional shape.

What I want to know is what theory of religion, and of the state, this whole patchwork presupposes. Does it presuppose that religion is at bottom a private conviction, like a taste or an opinion, which the state can just bracket and leave alone? If so, why does the state care so much about the *form* religion takes — why insist on one representative body, why pay the clergy, why keep the buildings standing? Or does it presuppose the opposite, that religion is a public social force that will organise itself into a rival community unless the state gets in first and shapes it? Can one practice coherently hold both, or is one of them doing the real work?

I'm also puzzled by the conspicuousness bit in the school rule: a large crucifix or a headscarf is out, a small pendant is fine, and nobody pretends this is about the state endorsing a doctrine — it's about display, visibility, pressure on other pupils. That seems to presuppose that what's objectionable about religious expression in a state school is not its content but its public visibility, i.e. that the harm is to other pupils' sense of belonging rather than to truth or to neutrality. Is that right, and does it sit consistently with the face-covering ban in public space, which gets justified in terms of living together and public order rather than religion at all?

Then contrast the Anglo-American multicultural model, where the state is also meant to be neutral but cashes neutrality out as accommodation — keep the headscarf, allow time off for prayers, offer the halal option. That looks like it presupposes religion is closer to an identity, something constitutive of who you are, which the state has a duty to recognise rather than to ignore. So what exactly do the two arrangements disagree about — the nature of religion, the nature of the state, or what equality requires?

What I'd really like: reconstruct the implicit theory behind the French arrangement, tell me which bits of it are strictly presupposed and which are just historical accidents or are equally licensed by several different theories, say whether the thing is internally consistent, and then tell me what would have to change if you swapped in the multiculturalist premise. And please don't just tell me France is inconsistant — everyone says that. I want to know what it would have to believe for the whole thing to hang together.

British English please. Cheers.
