I spent thirty-five years as an experimental physical chemist and retired three years ago; since then I have been reading philosophy with more appetite than method. I have agreed to open a session of our reading group on dispositions, and before I do I would like my own position audited rather than defended. Please tell me which of the commitments below genuinely pull against one another, which pairs only appear to, how serious each real conflict is, and what the least costly repair would be in each case. Rank the real conflicts by severity instead of treating them all as equally bad, and finish by naming the single revision that would clear up the most. I have no need of a reading list or citations; I want the audit. Please write in American English.

1. Dispositional predicates in the sciences name real features of the things that bear them. Soluble, brittle, infective, and carcinogenic are not disguised reports about observers, instruments, or laboratory protocols.

2. At the bottom there are no powers. Mass, charge, and the rest are occurrent quantities, and everything about what a thing would do follows from those quantities together with the dynamical laws. Whatever dispositions there are must be fixed by that base.

3. A disposition is individuated by its trigger and its manifestation: same trigger, same manifestation, same disposition. What makes a thing soluble is what it does on meeting a solvent, not the particular structure that makes it do so.

4. The simple analysis of dispositional talk is wrong — the one on which saying that a thing is disposed to M when C comes to saying that it would M if C. What kills it are cases in which something interferes. Two from my own bench: a salt whose lattice energy and hydration energies are, so far as I can measure, those of its soluble congeners, but which rearranges instantaneously on contact with water and seals itself against further hydration, so that it never dissolves; and a reaction mixture that would proceed briskly on its own, except that the buffer contains a scavenger that mops up the reactive intermediate the instant it appears, so that the mixture never reacts.

5. On the first of those cases I say the salt is still soluble: nothing about its structure before contact distinguishes it from its soluble cousins, and what defeats the dissolving is the very event that ought to have triggered it. On the second I say the mixture is still reactive, for the same reason. On a third case I say the opposite: a mixture that contains no reactive species at all, but in which warming manufactures one within microseconds, so that the product appears — the same product, on the same trigger, as from a mixture that was reactive all along. That mixture, I say, is not reactive. Reactivity has to be in the mixture, and not conjured up by the trigger.

6. A disposition ascription is acceptable only if some occurrent structural feature of the bearer accounts for it: no difference in dispositions without a difference in structure. If I can describe no mechanism, even in principle, I will not call it a disposition. That is a rule I brought from the bench, and I am not inclined to relax it.

7. Dispositions are intrinsic to their bearers. Whether a thing is soluble, brittle, or infectious is a fact about the thing itself and not about its surroundings.

8. I am a realist about chemistry and biology in the way I am a realist about physics: the patterns those sciences describe are not going to turn out to be convenient fictions.

I have been told that the masking cases show dispositions to be relational rather than intrinsic, and I confess I do not follow the argument. Set me straight on that if I have it wrong, but do not let it substitute for the main audit.
