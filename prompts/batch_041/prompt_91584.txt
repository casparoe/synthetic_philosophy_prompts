I'm the instructor for a small grad seminar on practical rationality — the back half of the course is all preference, choice, and time, and I've got to set the take-home final this week. I want a single long essay question on money-pump arguments, and I specifically don't want students to just recite the stock story (roughly: intransitive or incomplete preferences make you exploitable in a sequence of trades, therefore such preferences are irrational). What I want them to grapple with is whether the pump actually earns that conclusion. In particular I want them forced to take seriously the line that the pump only bites against an agent who is naive or myopic about the sequence they're being walked through, so that a foresighted agent with cyclical preferences could just refuse to enter — and to say what that does and doesn't show. I'd also like the question to make them distinguish two things I think get sloppily merged: an argument that a preference ordering is defective versus an argument that a decision procedure or a set of dispositions is defective. And I want the synchronic/diachronic distinction to do real work in the answer, not just get name-checked.

So please write (a) the exam question itself, pitched so a strong student needs the full time, (b) a model answer of about 800 words, and (c) a marking scheme out of 25 that names the specific moves a top answer has to make and the specific errors that should lose marks — e.g. treating the pump as a deductive proof that the agent is irrational, or quietly assuming the agent must accept every trade offered, or collapsing the ordering/procedure point. One more thing on the marking scheme: be explicit about which claims here are settled among people who actually work on this and which are genuinely contested, and dont let the model answer dress up a contested reading as the consensus. Students won't have internet or notes, so the question can't lean on them tracking down any particular paper.
