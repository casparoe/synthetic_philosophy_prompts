I spent my career in the natural sciences and have only recently come to political philosophy, so I am drawn to positions that make themselves vulnerable to refutation. I now hold that there is a strong presumption in favor of open borders: a state's right to exclude is not a basic right but at most a delegated instrument, justified only when exclusion is necessary to sustain the liberal and democratic institutions that make self-government possible. I want a map of what would count against this view. What would have to be true for the presumption to be false, and which empirical findings, conceptual distinctions, or possible cases should move me? I am particularly concerned with the distinction between a right to enter and a right to belong, and with the case of a polity that admits all comers while denying any path to citizenship, so that open borders becomes a durable system of disenfranchised labor. What would defenders of the presumption themselves concede as its costs? Does the view make risky predictions that its rivals do not, or has it been qualified into unfalsifiability? Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, and say which are live rather than merely logically possible.
