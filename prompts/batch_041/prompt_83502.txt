I am a novelist, and I have just finished a book about a man who at seventeen was driving when his closest friend was killed, and who at sixty has been forgiven by everyone whose forgiveness he could reasonably want — the dead boy's mother, his wife, the court, the priest — and who is the only person in the novel who has not let himself off. To mark the publication I have agreed to interview, on stage at a festival, a philosopher whose new book, provisionally titled *The Unpayable Debt*, argues roughly this: that guilt is built like a debt — it names an act, it can be discharged, and the discharge is what allows everyone, wrongdoer included, to stop thinking about the wrong — whereas shame names the person and cannot be discharged, which is precisely its moral advantage; that shame's need for an audience is not heteronomy but the correct register for creatures whose standing is constituted by recognition, guilt being the emotion of a ledger kept by nobody in particular, which is why guilt cultures produce the curious figure of the man who has settled his account and now demands readmission; that cultures cannot choose their dominant moral emotion directly, only their repertoire of practices for handling the irreversible, and shame-based cultures tend to have the richer repertoire because they do not believe the ledger ever balances; and finally, by way of provocation, that the tidy formula — shame is about who you are, guilt about what you did — is an artifact of a particular moral vocabulary rather than a fact about the emotions, its boundary drawn by institutions rather than by psychology.

I would like eight to ten questions that make her work rather than summarise it. Three of them must do specific jobs. First, press the non-dischargeability claim: I want to know what distinguishes keeping a wrong in view from mere rumination, and what her theory says about my man at sixty — whether she thinks he is right, and whether a self that cannot be forgiven is a moral achievement or a symptom. Second, press the recognition claim: if the audience can be internalised, does shame collapse into a private ledger and so into guilt under another name; if it cannot, what becomes of the person whose community is gone, or was always hostile to him. Third, press whether a culture can choose: what is the actual mechanism, and does she accept the implication that the organised shaming of offenders — public naming, exposure, the pillory — is a tool she ought to welcome. Add one question on her claim that the shame/guilt boundary is drawn by vocabulary rather than psychology, asking how she would distinguish a society that lacks the words from one that lacks the emotion.

For every question, give me the follow-up I will need if she answers as I expect her to. Do not ask her what drew her to the subject, or for definitions she has already given in the book, and end with the one question that makes her characteristic deflection — that shame is a practice and not a theory — hardest to use.
