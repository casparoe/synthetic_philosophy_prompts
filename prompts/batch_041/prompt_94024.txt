I need a two-page research proposal for a one-year, part-time fellowship application, built around a half-formed idea of mine that I have not seen developed anywhere. The project is conceptual: no experiments, no new data, and no dependence on anyone's forecast about when or whether transformative AI arrives.

The idea. Arguments about AI takeoff speed are usually sorted into fast and slow camps, and each side treats takeoff as a single event with a single rate. I suspect much of the disagreement comes from one word doing three jobs at once. There are at least three thresholds a system or a civilisation can cross, and they can come apart in time:

(i) the capability threshold — the point at which the system can carry out the cognitive work of AI research at least as well as the humans who built it, so that further capability gains can in principle be self-generated;

(ii) the control threshold — the point at which no human institution can any longer reliably audit, veto, or reverse what the system does, not because control was formally handed over, but because oversight has become slower than what it oversees;

(iii) the lock-in threshold — the point at which the resulting distribution of power, values, and institutions can no longer be altered by any realistic human effort.

On inspection, fast-takeoff arguments look like arguments about how quickly (i) leads to (ii); slow-takeoff arguments look like arguments about how quickly (i) leads to (iii), often leaning on physical bottlenecks — fabrication capacity, energy, supply chains — that bear on (iii) while barely touching (ii). If that diagnosis is right, then much of the apparent disagreement is not about facts, or about the shape of returns to cognitive investment, at all, but about which threshold each side has silently made the referent of takeoff.

Turn this into a proposal with the following parts.

1. The question, stated so that it has a determinate answer. My provisional version: once takeoff claims are indexed to a particular threshold, does any substantive disagreement remain between the main fast and slow positions, or do they turn out to be compatible claims about different thresholds? Sharpen this into whatever formulation is actually answerable.

2. Why the current positions leave it open.

3. The method, concretely. My sense of the order of work: define each threshold in an operational rather than stipulative way, so that the definitions do not simply assume the answer; characterise the possible shapes of the returns-to-cognitive-reinvestment curve and of the deployment-bottleneck curve; then determine, for each combination, which threshold binds first and how far the three can be pulled apart. Say what the obvious rival approaches are and why this one is better — in particular, why the project should not simply argue for fast or for slow takeoff.

4. What a result looks like, and what failure looks like. A success would be a defensible mapping from kinds of takeoff claim to thresholds, together with a verdict on whether the remaining disagreement is substantive. State plainly what would make the project a failure — for instance, that the thresholds cannot be defined without circularity, or that every plausible pair of curves collapses them into one, so the distinction does no work.

5. Why it is worth doing, and a scope that fits one year at part time, with milestones.

Two constraints. Do not include a bibliography or any citations; refer to positions and arguments by their content rather than by who holds them, and I will supply the references myself. And keep the proposal neutral between empirical forecasts about AI timelines — neutrality there is the point of the exercise, not a gap in it.

Begin your reply with the proposal itself. No preface, no commentary, no restating of this brief. Let the proposal's first sentence be its bottom line — the question and the angle in one sentence — and let everything that follows earn that sentence.
