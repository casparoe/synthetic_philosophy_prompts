Gary Gilmore was shot by a Utah firing squad in 1977 — two murders, and he dropped his appeals and insisted on being executed, which made him the first person killed in the US after the death penalty came back. I want to use him to test Camus's case against capital punishment. As I remember it, Camus deliberately refuses the usual moves: he won't rest abolition on deterrence (unproven) or on the risk of executing an innocent (that only shows courts are fallible, and quietly concedes that an infallible court would be entitled to kill), and instead goes after the thing itself — the premeditation, the ritual, the reduction of a person to an object, and the fact that the state now does it behind prison walls instead of in the square, which he reads as the state's own bad conscience. So map Gilmore onto that and tell me where it bites and where it slides off. The awkward part: the guy consented, so if the objection is the horror inflicted on the condemned, consent looks like it dissolves it — unless Camus's complaint was never really about the suffering, in which case say what it is about and whether it survives contact with a willing victim. Then run it the other way: what does this case expose about the arguement that Camus didn't anticipate — is consent to one's own execution ever real once the state's machinery is involved, or is that just another way of being killed? Does he owe us an answer to that, or can he stay silent? And be straight about where the theory runs out of gas rather than stretching it to fit the case.
