I've spent the past year covering a public inquiry — a harbour, a failed alarm, a report that everyone reads differently — and for the piece I'm filing now I want to close with a poem rather than a summary. Twelve to eighteen lines, no fixed form. Build it on the thing the inquiry kept bumping into without ever naming: the same antecedent and consequent put twice, once as an open conditional about what actually happened (if the gauge was faulty, the alarm sounded — and we still don't know whether it was) and once as a counterfactual about what would have happened otherwise (had the gauge been checked, the alarm would have sounded). Set it out in pairs, one line each way, and let the rhyme or a repeated phrase hold each pair together while the mood pulls it apart, so the form itself shows that these are two different animals sharing a grammar. The turn should be the moment a reader sees that one member of a pair can be settled while the other cannot. Include at least one pair where the counterfactual flips depending on how much of that night you hold fixed — whether the faulty gauge stays in place or the silent alarm does — and leave that one unresolved, since that is where the inquiry left it. No names, no doctrine, no quotations. A philosopher of language should recognize the machinery, and a reader who has never thought about any of it should still find a good poem about a harbour and a report.
