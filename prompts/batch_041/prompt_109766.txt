our reading group has spent the whole term on david lewis's analysis of convention and we've ended up split into two camps that can't hear each other. the first camp says a convention is a regularity in a recurrent coordination situation where everyone conforms, everyone expects everyone else to conform, everyone prefers to conform provided the others do, and all of that is common knowledge — so it's mutual expectation, salience and shared reasoning all the way down. the second camp says that's a description of the end state, not an explanation of it: what actually makes and keeps a convention is population-level dynamics — imitation, success-biased copying, reinforcement, drift — and you can get a perfectly stable convention in a population of agents who know nothing about each other's minds, never reason about each other at all, and may not even have the conditional preference the first camp insists on. they've been argueing for six weeks and mostly past each other.

i want the document the two of them should have written instead: an adversarial-collaboration joint statement that one committed member of each camp could actually put their name to. no citations or reading lists please, i'm not interested in who said what — i want the argument.

Structure it like this:

1. Agreed claims — numbered, 8 to 10 of them, one or two sentences each. Claims both sides can sign, and i want them to be substantive rather than truisms: at least a few should say what a convention isn't, or what the stock examples (driving on one side of the road, money, language) do and don't establish.
2. Where we still part company — 3 or 4 disagreements, each one stated twice, once in the wording the first camp would accept for its own view and once in the wording the second camp would accept for its own, with no strawmen.
3. The cruxes — 3 or 4 sub-questions such that settling one would actually move at least one side.
4. What would bear on each crux — for each one: the case, argument or evidence both sides agree is relevant, then each side's prediction, written down in advance. a table is fine.
5. Is any of this just verbal? — where the two camps are describing the same thing in different vocabularies, and where they genuinely contradict each other.
6. Where i'm most likely to have got this wrong — about 150 words. which of the agreed claims do you think one side would refuse to sign, and what would show that my drafting of the disagreement was slanted.
7. What would change my mind — what argument or finding would make you conclude the dispute isn't substantive after all, or that it's more substantive than you've made it sound, and where your reasoning is weakest.

Two cases have to appear somewhere in there, since they're the ones that split us:

(a) the repealed law. everyone conforms to a regularity, everyone prefers to conform provided the others do, and everyone expects everyone else to conform — but the expectation is false at the level of the explanation: they all believe the others conform because of a law that was repealed years ago and that nobody has ever checked. so the regularity is stable and the conditional preferences are real, but the mutual expectations that are supposedly holding it up are mistaken. does camp one still call that a convention, and does camp two care about the difference?

(b) risk versus payoff. a recurrent two-option coordination situation with two equilibria, one risk-dominant (safer against a partner who does the other thing) and one payoff-dominant (better if both get it right), in a population where agents copy whoever seems to be doing well and nobody has any common knowledge of anything. what does each camp predict the population converges on, and would the outcome count as evidence for either of them? if the camps make the same prediction here, say so, and say what that shows.

british english please, and don't hedge everything into mush — i want the formulations sharp enough that they could turn out to be wrong.
