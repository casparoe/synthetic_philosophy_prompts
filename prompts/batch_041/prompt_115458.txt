I have no philosophical training, but for about a year I have been working an idea out on my own, and I would now rather see it handled satirically than explained to me again.

The idea, as I have reconstructed it, is that a piece of behaviour can be *for* something — can have a point, a rationale — without anyone holding that rationale in mind, and indeed without there being any mind involved at all. A vine turns toward the light. A bacterium swims up a sugar gradient. A thermostat trips the furnace. In each case, if you ask why the thing does what it does, the most natural answer cites what the behaviour is for, and yet there is nobody there to have that reason. The phrase I have seen used for these is 'free-floating rationales'. I have taken to calling them nobody's reasons, which is my own coinage rather than a term of art, so use it if it is useful and drop it if it is not.

What interests me is that as soon as I accepted the idea, I started manufacturing nobody's reasons for everything: my houseplants, my car's warning lights, the queue at the post office, the way my neighbour's fence leans. Some of these are surely nonsense. The trouble is that I cannot tell in advance which ones are, and when I try to state the criteria I find I cannot do it cleanly. Something needs to be said about whether the behaviour has a history that would explain why it persists, whether the rationale I am citing is the one actually doing the explaining or merely one that fits, whether a rationale can be correct even when the behaviour fails to achieve what it is for, and whether two rival rationales can both be right about the same behaviour.

What I want from you is a satire, not an exposition. Specifically, a mock advice column of roughly 700 words: a page from an agony-aunt column, with three or four short letters from readers and the columnist's replies, in which the columnist is a wholly straight-faced practitioner of the idea above and the readers are ordinary people who have begun finding reasons in things that have no minds. Some correspondents you might use: a man who cannot stop apologising to his thermostat; a woman who has concluded that her garden is negotiating with her; someone convinced that the pricing algorithm at work holds a grievance against him; someone who suspects a river of having designs on the property line. Invent better ones if you can.

The constraints I care about:

- The columnist must never wink. The humour should come from taking the idea seriously and applying it competently to domestic trivia, not from the columnist being whimsical.
- Every diagnosis must be defensible on the merits. A reader who knows this idea well should be able to tell that the columnist knows it too, including the parts of it that are uncomfortable: that a rationale need not be represented by anyone, that it can be present even when the behaviour fails, and that it is suspiciously easy to generate one for anything that happens to work.
- At least one reader must be told, correctly, that there is no nobody's reason in their case, and the columnist must justify the refusal in a way that distinguishes a genuine rationale from a regularity that merely looks like one.
- At least one verdict must be uncomfortable for the reader — a case where the answer is yes and the reader would very much prefer it to be no.
- Do not let the column resolve into the moral that it is all just physics and everyone should stop being fanciful. The difficulty of drawing the line should remain visible without ever being stated.
- No citations, no reading lists, no appeals to what the literature says.

Keep it readable by someone who has never encountered any of this.
