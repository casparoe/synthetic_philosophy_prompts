Last night I lost the better part of an evening to an argument with a friend — she works on causal inference for an epidemiology group, I come at this from philosophy — and the thing we never got past was her opening claim that the counterfactual theory of causation and the interventionist theory are the same theory wearing different clothes. Her case, as I reconstruct it: the counterfactualist says X causes Y just in case Y would not have occurred without X, and then has to earn the right to set aside the counterfactuals that backtrack or that quietly hold every other potential cause fixed; the interventionist says X causes Y just in case some possible surgical intervention on X would change Y, and "surgical" is itself defined in terms of what is a cause of what, so the account pays for its precision with circularity. I think the two come apart, and I want to see exactly where, in a form I can keep and reread.

So: write me the dialogue we failed to have. Two theorists, one defending the counterfactual-dependence account and one defending the interventionist account, roughly 900–1300 words, at least eight substantive exchanges. Both should be first-rate — no strawman, no cheap shots left unanswered: if one of them has an obvious move available, the other should anticipate and block it, and every reply should engage the point actually just made rather than sliding to a neighbouring one. Neither should cite literature, name papers, or gesture at "the literature"; I want the arguments themselves, and I don't want either of them sheltering behind a citation I can't check.

Anchor the whole thing in a case, because I suspect the difference only shows up in the weeds. The case: a water utility's reservoir overflowed during a storm and flooded a low-lying neighbourhood, and the post-mortem turns up five factors, each of which would on its own have prevented the flood — the tank's level sensor had drifted out of calibration over the preceding month; the outflow pipe was partly blocked by sediment; the backup float switch had been tagged out for maintenance three weeks earlier and never re-enabled; the operator on shift was a trainee in her second week; and the storm was the largest in the gauge record. The regulator asks the but-for question — would the neighbourhood have flooded had the float switch been live? — and the utility's lawyers ask a different one: was the storm the cause? Have the two theorists fight about this: whether the storm counts as a cause at all, whether "the cause" is a well-posed question or a pragmatic one, and whether the interventionist's relativization to a choice of variables is a virtue (it explains why our causal talk is purpose-relative) or a confession that the theory cannot answer the question it was built to answer. Close with each of them stating what the other would have to concede for the dispute to be settled, and which single feature of the case — if I deleted it — would make the two theories agree. Then finish with a short paragraph in your own voice saying which of the two did better on this case, and why.
