For the better part of a year I have been writing a novel about a woman who comes out of an insular stroke with what my notes call pain asymbolia: she still reports pain — she can point to it, grade it, describe its character — and it does not distress her. Her hand leaves the hot pan on its own; she says, without complaint, that it hurts. Pinned above my desk, against her, is the definition of pain that nursing hands down — "whatever the experiencing person says it is, existing whenever the experiencing person says it does" — which I first met on a laminated card taped above a triage desk, where a nurse was using it to overrule a colleague who suspected a patient of exaggerating. I understood the impulse, and still do. But my character says she is in pain and means it, and the sentence seems to tell me something about her that I cannot square with what the case reports describe, and I have begun to suspect I am asking one sentence to do the work of four or five.

What I want is a map of that sentence. Lay out, as a numbered menu running from the nearly empty to the startling, the distinct claims it could be making. Is it offering a definition of pain, or of the clinical problem called pain; a rule about who is authoritative on the subject; a rule about what counts as evidence; an instruction to treat the report; a prohibition on substituting your own judgment for the sufferer's; or a claim about wherein the badness of pain lies — whether the hurt and the harm are one thing or two, and whether the unpleasantness is intrinsic to the sensation or consists in something further, a dislike, an aversion, a motive to get away? For each reading, say what it commits you to, whether it is trivial, defensible, or false, and what sort of person — nurse, neurologist, patient — would have to be assuming it.

Then tell me which of those readings would actually license the nurse's move of using the sentence to shut down a suspicion of exaggeration, and which would not; and which readings my asymbolic character's testimony threatens, which it supports, and which it leaves entirely untouched. If her case is real, then something in the sentence must be either false or much narrower than it sounds, and I would like to know which.

One further thing, meant as a constraint rather than a courtesy. Separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who work on this, and do not hand me a contested reading as though it were the consensus. A friend of mine, meaning well, told me with great confidence that the asymbolia cases prove pain can exist without being bad; I have since gathered that this is one position among several, argued over by people who know far more than he does, and resting on a very thin base of cases. I would rather have the disputes, and some sense of how much weight the evidence can bear, than a clean answer I will have to unlearn.
