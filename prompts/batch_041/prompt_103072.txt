I'm a third-year student in a seminar on transitional justice, and our final assessment is a simulation. The class is constituted as a national commission of inquiry for an invented country emerging from a twelve-year civil conflict, and I have been assigned to draft the procedure by which the commission will adjudicate applications for amnesty. The mandate we were given is deliberately ugly: eighteen months, roughly forty thousand named alleged perpetrators, and a political settlement that the outgoing security leadership signed only on the assurance that the process would not become a victor's tribunal — while the victims' associations have made equally clear that anything resembling a blanket amnesty will be received as a second betrayal. I do not want an essay on the justice-versus-peace tradeoff; I want the instrument itself, and I want it grounded in what the actual commissions got right and wrong (the conditional amnesty that required full disclosure in South Africa, the oscillation between trials and amnesty laws in Argentina, the community-court route in Rwanda), though please do not give me citations or a reading list — I need this to stand on its own.

What I need is a decision procedure a case officer with no theory training could run on a single application in about an hour and hand to a panel for signature. Give me the questions in the order they should be asked, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, the point at which the procedure stops with a refusal, and the point at which it must hand the file to a human because the remaining question is one of value rather than fact. It should sort every applicant into a small number of outcome categories — for example referral to prosecution, amnesty conditioned on some act by the applicant, amnesty without condition, or no decision — and it should say what makes a case fall out of the scheme altogether. Two things I find genuinely confusing and would like the procedure to resolve rather than dodge: first, I keep being told that amnesty conditioned on disclosure is a form of accountability rather than its abdication, and I cannot see how a procedure could make that more than a slogan when the commission has no way to verify most disclosures; second, I cannot tell whether the severity of the act, the rank of the perpetrator, or the willingness of the perpetrator to tell the truth should be doing the real work, and I suspect that choosing among those three is already a political decision disguised as a test.

Please then run the procedure on three applications: a mid-level commander who gives a complete and apparently candid account, names his superiors, and personally ordered two massacres; a conscripted teenager who participated in one killing and has been in therapy since; and a senior architect of the policy who refuses to disclose anything, whose faction's cooperation the settlement still depends on. Tell me plainly which of the three the procedure handles worst, and where it produces an answer I will not like. Finally, be honest about what the procedure cannot do at all, and about which of the theoretical distinctions you had to flatten in order to make it usable — and which ones you refused to flatten, because a case officer who could not see them would get the case wrong.
