Hi — I'm a reporter, I cover science and ideas, and I'm buried in a feature about whether it means anything to say that one person's suffering is worse than another's. It started as a story about QALYs and cost-benefit analysis and it's turned into something much more annoying, so I'm outsourcing part of my thinking to you.

Here's the proposition I want a number on. There is a fact of the matter — in principle, for at least some pairs of people and some pairs of experiences — about whether A's state at one time is worse for A than B's state at another time is for B, and that fact doesn't depend on whatever scale or convention we happen to use to measure it. Not "we can measure it accurately," not "we can all agree on a number." Just: there's something there to be right or wrong about.

Three ground rules for the exercise, because otherwise I'll get a shrug:

- Assume we already have a decent cardinal measure of intensity within a single person — that my 7 is twice my 3.5, whatever that amounts to. I want to know what's left once you cross persons, not whether "twice as painful" is meaningful for me in the first place.
- Keep the level question and the unit question apart: whether we can say whose state is worse, versus whether we can say how much worse, and whether either is possible without the other. Someone told me that distinction does most of the work here and I want to know if that's true.
- If your answer changes depending on what the currency is — how bad it feels, versus how well it satisfies the person's preferences, versus some list of objective goods — give me a separate number for the readings that come apart.

Then give me your credence in the proposition, decomposed: the sub-questions it breaks into, a rough probability for each with a line of why, how they combine into the final number, and which term the number is most sensitive to.

Two specific sub-questions I want you to price separately, because I keep running into them:

1. Somebody I interviewed has a proposal he's very proud of. Pick one physically specified anchor experience — same stimulus, same intensity, administered to everybody — and have each subject locate their own states relative to it. He thinks that hands us a common scale for free. What's your probability that this kind of anchoring, working exactly as advertised, would establish the fact of the matter in the proposition, rather than just a very good convention that everyone could live with?
2. Somebody else says you can back out each person's marginal utility from how they bet under uncertainty, and that this is all the comparability anyone needs for policy. Same question: does that get you the fact of the matter, or only a useful number?

And one more thing I'd like your read on. There's an old argument that if you insist preferences can't be compared across people, you can't aggregate them in any way that's both non-dictatorial and respects unanimous improvement, so you're stuck. Is that an argument that comparability is real, or just that a certain austere methodology is a dead end? Those seem like diffrent claims to me, and I think alot of my interviewees are sliding between them.

Last thing: my credence in the proposition is about 0.7, my editor's is about 0.25 and she thinks the whole thing is a word game. If we're both being sincere, which sub-question are we most likely actually disagreeing about, and what would move your number most — something in psychophysics, something in social choice, something in the philosophy of mind, or just a clearer story about what we mean by worse?
