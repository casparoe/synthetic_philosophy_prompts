During a reading group session on the mathematics of the infinite, a participant with finitist sympathies repeatedly deployed a single line against every argument we considered. Presented with Hilbert's hotel, and with the proof that the even numbers can be put in one-to-one correspondence with all the natural numbers, he would produce the same sentence as though it were a trump card: "The whole is greater than the part." (I later learned that the sentence is Common Notion 5 in Euclid's Elements.) He treats it as an axiom of common sense that no theory of collections may deny, so that any theory countenancing a collection equinumerous with one of its proper parts — as these examples appear to — has refuted itself before it begins.

I believe something is wrong here, but I cannot say exactly what. Please set out for me the distinct precise claims that sentence could be making, ranging from the trivial to the radical, and give a verdict on each. I would like the readings separated along at least these lines: what "greater" could mean (proper containment or parthood as such; greater cardinality; greater magnitude or measure; the existence of a nonzero remainder when the part is removed; greater ordinal rank or position in a well-ordering); whether the principle is a stipulation about the meaning of "greater," an axiom that fixes what "whole" and "part" mean, a theorem that follows from a definition of finiteness, or a substantive metaphysical constraint on what collections there can be; and whether the slogan read as a claim about composition — a whole exceeding the sum of its parts — is the same principle or a different one that has been conflated with it. For each reading, say whether it is trivially true, true but confined to finite or measurable cases, substantive and defensible, or simply false. Then say which reading the slogan needs in order to function as a genuine objection to Hilbert's hotel and to the equinumerosity of the naturals with one of their proper parts, whether that reading is available, and whether it begs the question.

When pressed, he adds two further remarks: that one cannot take an infinite collection away from an infinite collection and be left with anything determinate, and that infinity names a process of endless addition rather than a completed object. I would also like to know whether those remarks are independent arguments or merely restatements of the same reading of the slogan. Finally, tell me which reading the people who repeat the line are most likely to have in mind, since that is the one I will have to address.

Lay the readings out as a menu rather than as a defense of any one of them. Please answer in American English.
