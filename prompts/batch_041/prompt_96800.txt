I am in the final year of a doctorate in the social sciences, and a month ago a fellow student and I analysed the same survey data and reached opposite conclusions about whether a particular intervention worked. Our supervisor has decided the disagreement is interesting enough to publish jointly, which means that what was a private worry is now something I have to defend in print. I would like to rehearse my position against the best version of the view I am resisting, rather than against the version I find it easy to knock down.

Please play a committed conciliationist about peer disagreement: someone who holds that when two parties who are epistemic peers — comparably competent, comparably informed, neither obviously biased — reach opposed conclusions on the same evidence, each is rationally required to move substantially toward the other's view, and to do so because the disagreement itself is evidence that one of them has gone wrong. Play the position at full strength. Concede what a careful defender concedes — that peerhood is hard to define, that the view seems to threaten itself when the peers disagree about how to respond to disagreement, that moving toward an average is not obviously the right operation on credences. Do not concede the central claim, and do not escape into the suggestion that we are simply engaged in different projects. Argue from the materials of the case rather than by appeal to named authorities; if you want a principle, state it and defend it. Write in the first person, in continuous prose, one paragraph per turn, and stay in character until I say stop.

My opening move. I want to distinguish two kinds of disagreement. Call a disagreement traceable when the parties can point to the exact step at which they diverge — this control variable, that coding decision, the choice of estimator — so that the disagreement can be inspected, argued over, and if necessary re-run. Call it opaque when they cannot point, when the divergence is a difference in how the whole material strikes them and neither can say where it entered. My claim is that conciliationism draws all of its force from the opaque case. Where a disagreement is traceable, the fact that you disagree with me is not evidence that I am wrong; it is a pointer to a joint we can now examine, and the evidential work is done by examining it, not by my lowering my confidence because you are standing beside me. And here is where my own case bites: our divergence is traceable — we have located it in a modelling choice that the data do not settle — and yet I expect you to say that this makes it opaque after all, since there is no procedure for resolving the point. So tell me what makes a disagreement opaque: the parties' inability to point to the divergence, or the absence of a shared procedure for settling it once it has been pointed to? If the first, conciliationism is far narrower than it looks and my case falls outside it. If the second, then every judgment call is an occasion for conciliation, and I want to know what that does to a discipline that is largely judgment calls.
