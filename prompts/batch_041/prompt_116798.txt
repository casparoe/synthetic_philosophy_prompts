I'm in a seminar on early Chinese thought and we've just finished the Zhuangzi, specifically the death bits — his wife dying while he squats there banging on a tub and singing, the skull he sleeps on that tells him the dead have no rulers above them and no subjects below and nothing to envy about the living, the dying man who says the same thing that gave him a body has been tiring him with life and will now put him to rest. I got stuck with reconstructing the argument behind all this for class, and I came out with the thing below, and every step looks fine to me and the conclusion is hideous. Which usually means I've done something wrong, but I can't see where. My TA has a name for the crux, she calls it the no-witness problem — the idea that grief needs somebody who is worse off, and after the scattering there's nobody on the scene to fill the role.

(1) A person isn't a thing that has a life and then loses it. A person is a temporary gathering of stuff, and dying is the coming apart, on all fours with the seasons handing off to each other. (2) Grief is fitting only where something has been lost. (3) Something has been lost only if there is someone who is now worse off than before. (4) After the coming apart there is nobody who is worse off — the dead person isn't deprived of anything (the skull is pretty clear on this, and Zhuangzi treats death as a rest from the labour of having a body). (5) Nor is the survivor worse off, because the survivor's grief presupposes exactly the sort of fixed self standing over against a fixed other that the butterfly dream and the whole equalising-of-things chapter are out to dissolve. (6) So there's no loss and no one to bear it, so the grief is a mistake, a failure to understand fate — which is more or less what he says about himself, that he caught himself wailing and thought, this shows I don't get it, and stopped. (7) So tub and song are the correct response to a death and tears are an error. (8) But (7) is monstrous. If a friend's kid dies I'm not handing them a tub. And I notice the argument eats everything: if the seasons turning isn't a loss then a birth isn't a gain either, so I shouldn't be glad when a friend's cancer goes into remission, and Zhuangzi definately wants me to be glad about things, he's the happiest writer in the canon.

So which step do I drop, and what do I pay for dropping it? I'd like to keep (1), it's the bit I actually find consoling rather than creepy, and I'd rather not have to conclude he was just wrong about death. But if (1) stays and (7) has to go, something in between has to give, and I want to know which of the remaining options is least bad and why. I have a feeling the problem is hiding in the word loss, but every way I try to say that out loud sounds like I'm just refusing to follow the argument. I don't need a reading list, just the diagnosis.
