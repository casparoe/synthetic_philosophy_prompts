I recently became the outside ethicist on the safety-review board of a company that operates autonomous delivery robots on public sidewalks, and I have to give the board a usable answer rather than a seminar. What I keep circling is whether the so-called responsibility gap is a genuine moral gap or merely a legal lag, so here is the proposition I want your credence on: when a certified autonomous system operating within its design envelope seriously injures a bystander with no human in the loop, it is false that any particular human is morally blameworthy for that injury.

Give me a number, then decompose it. I would like rough probabilities for at least these terms, each with a one-line justification: (a) that the correct account of blameworthiness really does require control over, or knowledge of, the relevant details; (b) that under such an account every human in the chain—designer, trainer, certifier, operator, deployer—fails the control condition in a typical case; (c) that role-based or assumed duties, such as a deployer's duty of care, can ground blame even without control; (d) that blame can attach to a collective or a distributed process without any individual being apt for it; (e) that the harm is almost always traceable to some upstream human choice—reward specification, training data, or the deployment decision itself—that does satisfy the control condition; and (f) that the gap claim is an equivocation between blame and mere accountability. Combine the terms the way you think correct, then flag the one your final number is most sensitive to and say what evidence would move it most.

Two constraints: keep it in American English, and keep the number about moral blameworthiness rather than legal liability—the board already has lawyers for the second question.
