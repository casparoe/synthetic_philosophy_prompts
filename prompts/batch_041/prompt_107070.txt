I'm trying to work through the rotating-bucket passage in the Scholium that Newton places after the Definitions at the start of Book I of the Principia, and I keep losing my grip on what the passage is entitled to conclude. Here is the stretch I mean, copied from a rendering of the 1846 revision of Andrew Motte's translation:

"If a vessel, hung by a long cord, is so often turned about that the cord is strongly twisted, then filled with water, and held at rest together with the water; after, by the sudden action of another force, it is whirled about in the contrary way, and while the cord is untwisting itself, the vessel continues for some time this motion; the surface of the water will at first be plain, as before the vessel began to move; but the vessel by gradually communicating its motion to the water, will make it begin sensibly to revolve, and recede by little and little, and ascend to the sides of the vessel, forming itself into a concave figure [...]"

I don't need the standard moral that this refutes the thesis that all motion is merely relative. What I want is an account of the argument's actual reach. Notice the shape of the evidence: two stages that are relationally alike in one respect come apart in another, and it is the stage in which the water is at rest with respect to the vessel that exhibits the effect, while a stage in which the water and vessel are again in relative motion also exhibits it. So the surface cannot be tracking relative motion to the vessel. But the sentence as written says the vessel "communicates" its motion to the water, which sounds like a causal claim rather than a kinematic observation. Does the inference therefore presuppose some prior notion of what it is for motion to be communicated or transferred, and if so, is the argument circular when used to establish absolute motion? Second, and more important to me: does this passage really support substantivalism about space as an entity, or does it establish only the weaker claim that rotation has a privileged state that cannot be analysed as rotation relative to any body appearing in the experiment, while saying nothing about absolute velocity or about space as a container? I would like those two claims kept apart, with a verdict on which one the passage as written can bear. And if it falls short of full substantivalism, I want to know what a relationist who grants the phenomenon is still obliged to explain, and whether anything in the passage would have to be rethought if the vessel and the water were the only two things in the universe.
