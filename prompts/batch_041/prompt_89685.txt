I write fiction, and when I decide whether a character is answerable for what he has done I find I am working from a set of intuitions I cannot reduce to a rule. Most of my cases turn on a backstop — some person or mechanism standing ready to guarantee the act if the character were ever about to balk. My verdicts are these.

(a) A man embezzles from the charity he runs, out of ordinary greed and exactly as he had long planned; unknown to him, his accountant, who wanted the theft to occur, had arranged a hypnotic trigger that would have compelled him to embezzle had he hesitated. I hold him fully answerable.

(b) A woman knows that her partner has rigged a device that would compel her to reveal a friend's secret should she waver; she reveals it herself, for her own reasons and partly to show she needs no prodding, and the device never fires. I hold her fully answerable.

(c) A man resolved to stay silent about a crime is overridden by an interrogator's device and made to confess. I hold him not answerable for the confession.

(d) A man with no backstop of any kind, and every opportunity to refrain, acts on a desire implanted in him the previous night by a surgeon; had the desire not been implanted he would never have done it, and nothing else about him was altered. I do not hold him answerable.

(e) A man stays in a room and does harm there because he chooses to; the door was in fact locked and he could not have left, though he never tried it. I hold him answerable.

My tentative rule is that a character is answerable for an act just when the act issues from his own settled reasons, and that whether he could have done otherwise is beside the point. Cases (a), (b), and (e) support it, and (c) fits it, but (d) worries me: the act does issue from a reason he holds, yet the reason is not his in the way that matters, and I cannot say what "his in the way that matters" means without smuggling in the very thing I was trying to explain. Please test the rule against all five cases, state precisely whatever repaired principle or small set of candidates the cases will bear, tell me which of my verdicts I must abandon, and say what the principle predicts for a case I have not put to myself: a man who gives generously to a stranger with a dormant backstop that would have forced him to give had he refused. Answer as exactly five numbered items, each a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion.
