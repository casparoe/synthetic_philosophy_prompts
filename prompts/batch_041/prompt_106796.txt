Ok so I'm in this population ethics seminar and we just finished the mere addition paradox, and honestly the part that wrecked me wasn't even the famous repugnant conclusion itself, it was the follow-up where you add a huge number of lives that are barely worth living *plus* a bunch that aren't worth living at all, and the total still comes out better than a small world where everyone is happy (I think thats Arrhenius's "very repugnant conclusion", we read a chapter on it and I couldn't sleep). Anyway the last session is a bring-something-creative-instead-of-a-paper thing and I want to do a poem. Specifically a villanelle, because the form is exactly the trap: two sentences that each sound completely reasonable on their own keep coming back no matter what you do, and by the end they're standing right next to each other and you realize they've been walking you to Z the whole time. 19 lines, two refrains, five tercets and a closing quatrain, you know the drill.

Here's what I actually want from it: the poem should be spoken in the first person by one of the Z people. Not a philosopher, not a narrator looking down at them from the A valley — someone with a job and a favorite meal and a bad knee, whose life the theory has quietly appraised as barely worth living. The two refrains should be the platitudes the theory keeps saying *about* her, which she ends up saying herself, as though they were obviously true; something in the family of "nobody is worse off for one more happy person" and "if everyone alive is glad to be alive, more of us is better". By the last two lines I want those sentences to have folded into each other, so the reader feels the conclusion arrive without the poem ever naming it. I'd definately rather it be quiet than clever.

Some constraints, because I don't want this turning into a lecture: no jargon — no "utility", "welfare", "aggregate", "quality of life", "QALY", "maximize" — and please don't say "repugnant conclusion" or name Parfit inside the poem. The Z world has to be shown through three or four plain concrete things (what she eats, what the shift is like, the music they pipe into the shop, something she can't afford) rather than adjectives about how drab it is. Actually I'd rather she not come off as miserable at all — her life is fine, that's kind of the whole point, that's what makes it unbearable. And no resolution, no final stanza where the poet steps in to say "and yet we must choose" — leave her holding both sentences at once. Maybe give it a flat, bureaucratic title.
