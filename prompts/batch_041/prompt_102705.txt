You are grading a short essay in an advanced undergraduate course on perception and cognition. The exam question and one student’s answer are below. The course has covered the distinction between cognitive penetration and top-down attention, the difference between perceptual experience and perceptual judgment, and the modularity tradition’s notions of informational encapsulation and cognitive impenetrability. Grade the answer with a letter grade (plus/minus allowed) and then give specific comments that justify it. Identify each substantive claim, say whether it is correct, imprecise, or incorrect, and explain why. Attend especially to inferences that do not follow, distinctions that are conflated, and alternative explanations the student has not ruled out. Do not rewrite the essay; assess it. The question is self-contained; no references are needed.

Exam question:
A research team reports that when participants are primed with the concept danger, they judge ambiguous shapes as more angular, and when primed with the concept comfort, they judge the same shapes as more rounded. The effect persists when participants are asked to report only the physical shape and are offered a monetary incentive for accuracy. The authors conclude that concepts penetrate perceptual experience. A commentator replies that the result is just a response bias or a shift in judgment criterion. Evaluate the authors’ conclusion. In your answer, distinguish (a) cognitive penetration of perception from top-down attention, (b) perceptual experience from perceptual judgment, and (c) informational encapsulation from cognitive impenetrability. Then state what further evidence would be needed to support the authors’ conclusion.

Student answer:
The authors’ conclusion is largely supported. Cognitive penetration is when a cognitive state changes the content or character of perceptual experience. Top-down attention is different: it selects or prioritizes a stimulus, but it does not by itself alter what the stimulus looks like. Here the manipulation is a concept prime, not an attentional cue, so the effect is not merely attentional. The fact that the effect survives an accuracy incentive also rules out response bias, because a mere shift in response criterion would be washed out when participants are motivated to be accurate. So the effect is genuinely perceptual.

The commentator is right that we must separate experience from judgment. If participants are only reporting what they believe they saw, the data could reflect a change in judgment rather than in experience. But the accuracy incentive addresses this, since it makes judgment more accurate and thus more likely to track experience. Also, informational encapsulation is the claim that a perceptual system cannot be influenced by information outside its own domain, while cognitive impenetrability is the claim that it cannot be influenced by cognition. The modularity tradition holds that perception is encapsulated and therefore impenetrable. But that tradition also allows that attention can modulate perceptual processing, so it is not really opposed to the authors’ conclusion. In fact, because the prime is conceptual, the study shows that encapsulation is false.

To strengthen the conclusion, the authors could use a perceptual adaptation measure or ask participants to match the shape to a physical template. If the prime shifts the matching point, that would show a genuine perceptual effect.
