A colleague in my reading group argues that what people call a multipolar trap, or Moloch, is at bottom just a prisoner's dilemma — sometimes he says the tragedy of the commons — and that the remedy is therefore the familiar one: get the parties to sign a binding, monitored agreement, or install an authority that can punish defection. He trots this out about arms races, bidding wars, and the race to deploy AI systems. I suspect the diagnosis is too quick, but I want it tested properly rather than dismissed. Lay out the correspondence as explicitly as you can: what plays the role of the prisoners, their strategies, the payoffs, the dominant strategy, the jointly optimal outcome, and the enforcer standing outside the game. Then tell me where the mapping breaks — whether the parties in these races are choosing rather than being selected for, whether the set of players is fixed or open to entry, whether there is a jointly optimal outcome for them to move to at all, and who is supposed to police the agreement from outside. Finish by saying which of the disanalogies bear on his conclusion and which are merely decorative, whether the proposed remedy survives once they are on the table, and what analogy would carry the point better.
