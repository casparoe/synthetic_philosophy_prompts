I write about science and ideas for a monthly magazine, and I've been asked to run a ninety-minute session at a summer school on democratic innovation: twenty-four participants, mostly graduate students in politics and philosophy plus a few journalists, mixed prior knowledge, one large room, no laptops. My current feature is on sortition, and the thing I keep failing to get across in prose is what it actually feels like to hold power you did not compete for and cannot be re-elected into — the odd vacancy where a mandate should be, the way accountability stops biting when nobody can be punished at the next round, and the mirror-image pull of the elected chamber that has every incentive to be responsive and none to be right. I want the session itself to manufacture that feeling rather than explain it, so that by the time I name the trade-off the participants have already been living inside it. Please design me a runnable activity: rules, materials (assume paper, a large room, no technology), roles, a timeline that fits the ninety minutes including a debrief, and a payoff scheme that needs no money — small stakes, standing, or the power to shape what happens next will do.

One design constraint matters more to me than the rest. I want the allotted body chosen not from the whole room but from a smaller pool of self-nominated volunteers, stratified so that the sample is descriptively representative of that pool rather than of the room; and I want the review or appeal stage to be a second sortition drawn from those who lost the first one. My hope is that participants discover for themselves that a lottery can be scrupulously fair in one sense — equal chance, no favouritism, verifiable procedure — while quietly laundering a selection effect upstream, and that the two halves of the room end up defending incompatible intuitions about what political equality is for. The idea should never be announced from the front; it should surface only in the debrief, which I'd like mapped moment by moment onto the parts of the concept each episode corresponds to, along with an honest account of what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it (I expect at least one faction to game the nomination stage, and I'd like to know how to handle that without turning it into a lecture) and a frank note on which feature of the case for lottocracy the activity simply cannot reproduce and that I will have to supply in words myself — I suspect it is the timescale over which real lot-based institutions build or squander legitimacy, and the fact that nothing here actually costs anyone anything, but tell me if I'm wrong. I'll be writing the session up afterwards, so I'd rather it generate a few genuinely awkward moments than arrive at a tidy conclusion.
