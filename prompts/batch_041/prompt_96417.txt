I teach a 90-minute upper-level seminar in philosophy of mind and cognitive science for about 16 undergraduates, mostly juniors and seniors with some background in either philosophy or psychology but almost none in either Dennett or evolutionary theory. I have been assigning the four grades of creature that Dennett distinguishes in his writing on the evolution of minds -- the Darwinian creature that comes with its competences built in and cannot learn; the Skinnerian creature that can be shaped by reinforcement from the environment, at the cost of having to act and be punished or rewarded before it learns anything; the Popperian creature that can run the trial in imagination and let its hypotheses die in its stead; and the Gregorian creature that adds culturally transmitted thinking tools, from arithmetic and writing to institutions and software, that live partly outside the individual head. Every year the students nod along in class and then conflate Popperian with just being clever and Gregorian with just using a computer, and the whole point of the sequence -- that each grade is a new way of avoiding the cost of real-world trial and error, and that the last one relocates part of the intelligence out of the organism -- never lands. I want them to run into the distinctions with their hands rather than hear them explained, so please design an in-class activity for this group in this slot that does that. I am happy to bring in simple materials, to rearrange the room, to let them use paper and pen and whatever is already in their bags, and to spend real money only in trivial amounts.

The design should have the four grades show up in what happens to the participants rather than in anything I say up front, and I would rather not name any of the four until the debrief; ideally they should reach the end of the activity having felt a difference they cannot yet name and then get the names as a payoff. Give me the rules, the materials, the roles if any, the incentive or payoff structure (I would prefer not to use actual money, so tell me what to use instead and how to make it bite), a minute-by-minute timeline that fits the 90 minutes including setup and debrief, and a debrief script that maps specific moments in the activity back onto the four grades -- including at least one moment where a participant will have gotten the right answer for what feels like the wrong reason, and one where a tool that was supposed to help only works if enough other people are already using it. I especially want the boundary between the Skinnerian and the Popperian grades to be felt as a real change in what a person is allowed to do rather than as an increase in intelligence, and the boundary between the Popperian and the Gregorian grades to be felt as a change in where the thinking is happening, not just in how much of it there is. Tell me honestly what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this, how to handle it in the moment, and what I should change before the second run.

Finally, be frank about which of the four grades the activity cannot actually reproduce in a classroom and that I will have to supply by other means, and say so explicitly rather than papering over it -- I suspect the Darwinian grade is the hard one, since real selection across generations will not fit in a 90-minute session, but I would like your judgment rather than mine. If you conclude that one of the four grades cannot be staged at all without distorting it past the point of usefulness, say which one and what I should do instead of staging it. Write the whole thing in American English, and write it as a plan I could hand to a co-teacher who has not read Dennett, so define any term the activity depends on at the point where it is needed.
