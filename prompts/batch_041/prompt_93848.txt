I'm a science journalist — I write a column on research culture and the politics of expertise — and for the past two years I've sat as one of three non-scientist members on the governing council of a midsized scientific society: about 4,000 members, split between a large molecular-and-cellular wing, a smaller field-and-ecology wing, and a newer computational-and-data wing that is small, vocal, and growing. Our president's term ends in June, and for the first time anyone can remember the election is genuinely contested. Three candidates will not step aside: A, a senior figure identified with the molecular wing; B, identified with the field wing; and C, an outsider reformist who has campaigned on data-sharing and open methods, who is the first choice of the computational wing, the second choice of the field wing, and the last choice of the molecular wing. The council has to fix the election method at its next meeting, because our bylaws require the method to be in place before the ballot goes out, and the two factions have each quietly floated the rule that favors their candidate. I'm the member who is expected to be able to explain whatever we decide to outsiders, and since I cover this society professionally I need a document I can defend in print without looking like I'm carrying water for anyone. So what I want from you is the written opinion I'll circulate before Thursday's meeting: one clear recommendation with a concrete course of action — which method, and the exact wording of the ballot instructions — together with the reasoning and an honest account of the strongest case against me.

Here is the material. A non-binding straw poll of the membership (100 respondents, weighted to match section sizes) produced three ranking blocs: 40 respondents rank A above B above C; 35 rank B above C above A; 25 rank C above A above B. The proposals on the table are plurality, a two-round runoff, the Borda count, approval voting, a Condorcet-completion rule (ranked pairs or Schulze — pick one and say why), majority judgment, and STAR. Two constraints matter as much as the theory. First, roughly a third of the membership votes on paper, and the last time we put anything more complicated than three names in ranked order in front of them, turnout in that group collapsed; so cognitive and clerical simplicity is not a trivial consideration, and neither is the fact that a result nobody can verify by hand will be treated as rigged. Second, adopting a method takes a two-thirds vote of a council that is split roughly along the same lines as the membership, so a recommendation only one faction can love is a recommendation that fails — 'this is the theoretically correct rule' will not get us to two-thirds.

Start by brainstorming the candidate rules against those constraints, then do the arithmetic yourself and show your work: the pairwise results, the plurality and Borda outcomes, what a runoff would produce, and what the likely approval and majority-judgment outcomes would be if voters behave in the obvious way — and tell me if I've gotten the numbers wrong, because I assembled them quickly from section-level tallies. Then put on two hats in turn — a social choice theorist who knows exactly what Arrow's and Gibbard–Satterthwaite's results do and do not establish, and a working journalist who has to defend the decision to readers who have never heard of a Condorcet cycle — and write the memo. Keep it under about 1,500 words, define any term on first use, and make the recommendation specific enough that the council could vote on it as written. I don't want a literature review or a reading list; I want something I can hand to colleagues who don't know this field and a bottom line I can live with. The section I care most about is the one where you make the best case against your own recommendation — including the possibility that no counting rule is defensible here and the honest move is to change the ballot question rather than the counting method — and then say plainly why you still recommend what you recommend.
