I'm not an academic — I'm a retired postman who somehow ended up as the one who does the talking for our local history society. Next month we're launching the book we've spent four years on, the town from the 1800s to now, and I've got to give the five-minute speech at the do in the church hall. The trouble is the middle of the book: the mill that made this town is also the mill that broke the strike in the twenties, and the room will have the old mill family in it as well as the families of the men who were locked out, some of them still raw about it. I've been reading up on the argument about whether history can be objective at all — the old line that the historian just shows how it actually was, versus the modern one that you can't, because any telling has to pick a shape, and someone told me every history comes out as a romance or a tragedy or a farce and that the shape is the teller's doing and not the past's. I half agree, but when I said it out loud to my mate whose grandad was on that picket line it sounded like I was saying none of it mattered, which is definately not what I think. So write me the speech: honest that we've shaped the story, warm to a room that's half proud and half still angry, and it can't either pretend the book is neutral or shrug that it's just one version among many. Most of the room won't have read any of this, though my sister-in-law teaches history at the college and will be in the second row. British English please, and flag the one line you'd bet on someone complaining about.
