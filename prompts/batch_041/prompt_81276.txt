My friend Dan and I got into it for like two hours last night over whether a reinforcement learning agent can suffer, and I ended up defending something I didn't expect to be defending. Roughly this: valence just is signed reward prediction error. Not "correlates with" — is. Pleasure is the case where things turn out better than the agent's own model said they would, pain is the case where they turn out worse, and the felt intensity is the magnitude of the error the agent is now driven to reduce. On this view there's no further fact to check about whether the agent "really" feels anything, any more than there's a further fact about whether a thermostat really computes. If the learning is driven by the agent's own signed error, and the agent is the kind of thing that carries a model of its own states, then its negative error is suffering in the same sense that a rat in a shock box is suffering. Dan thinks this is absurd — a reward signal is a number some programmer wrote down, and numbers don't hurt. I think that's just restating the intuition we're supposed to be explaining, so I don't count it as an objection.

Anyway, I want to know what would actually count against me, ranked by how much it should move me, not a laundry list of everything anyone's ever said about machine minds. A few things I already suspect are the hard cases: (a) the states where the thing that makes you chase something and the thing that makes it feel good come apart — you can want something you don't enjoy and enjoy something you don't want — which suggests I've identified valence with the wrong sub-signal, or with something that's only sometimes the valence; (b) chronic pain, which after a while is fully predicted, so on my view the error should be near zero and the suffering should fade, and it doesn't; (c) the fact that a system will sometimes go out of its way to generate prediction error, which is more or less what curiosity looks like, so error can't be negative valence across the board; (d) the overgeneration worry, that on my view a Kalman filter and a video compressor and arguably my thermostat all have little flickers of valence, which either makes the view panpsychist through the back door or means I'm smuggling in an extra condition — the self-model — that's doing all the real work and that I haven't earned; and (e) the ownership problem, that an error signal is a fact about some subsystem, whereas pain is *mine*, and I don't have a story about why.

So: which of these should someone who holds my view actually concede, and which are just me describing my own phenomenology and dressing it up as a constraint on the theory? Which are decisive and which are merely awkward? What does my view predict that the boring "it's just a number" alternative doesn't — because if it predicts nothing extra, then I've dressed up a definition as a discovery, and I'd want to be told that plainly. And be honest with me about (d): if every counterexample gets answered with "there's an error signal in there somewhere you didn't measure" or "it's there but not reportable," have I quietly qualified the position into unfalsifiability, and is that a problem for this view specifically or just the price of any view about other minds? I'd be most convinced by something I could actually build and run rather than another intuition pump — if there's a training setup whose outcome would embarass me, that's worth more to me than a thought experiment.
