A colleague of mine has a manuscript in the works arguing that moral norms are just conventions in Lewis's sense -- equilibria that got locked in because they solved some recurring coordination problem -- and that the felt non-arbitrariness of morality is a byproduct of how the equilibrium got selected, nothing more. She's the guest on our department podcast in a few weeks and I'm doing the interview. I want to actually press her, not give her a runway to restate the thesis, so draft me eight to ten questions.

Stuff I definitely want hit. First, the obvious one: Lewis-style conventions need a real coordination problem with more than one equilibrium plus a conditional preference to conform once others do, and I want to know why "don't kill people who haven't wronged you" is supposed to be that rather than just the one salient answer sitting there by itself -- and a follow-up for when she says the multiplicity is in the details (who counts as having wronged you, how much compensation, etc.) rather than in the headline norm. Second, the reason-vs-prediction gap: does the convention give her an actual reason to comply, or only a prediction that she'll be left out or punished if she doesn't? Anticipate her saying the two come apart and press on what's left of the normativity. Third, her most likely clever move -- that enforcement is itself a second-order convention, so she never needs an external enforcer -- and what that move costs her, because I suspect it either pushes the bump up a level or quietly smuggles in a non-conventional reason to keep the higher-order convention going. Fourth, the arbitrariness worry: conventions are things we could have settled otherwise, and moral norms don't present themselves that way, so is the phenomenology just a bug in our self-reporting? Fifth, the evolution story, since "it evolved" can mean weak selection on a population of imitators, payoff-based reinforcement learning, cultural group selection, or a few other things, and I want her to commit to which mechanism she's actually running and to say whether the arbitrariness survives it.

Then a couple of sharp ones to close. One on whether her view is really just the old error-theory line -- moral judgements are systematically false because there's nothing there for them to be true of -- dressed up in coordination language, and if not, what the difference is supposed to be. And one on defeat: what would the world have to look like for norms not to be conventions? What's the observation or the argument that would actually move her, as opposed to the standard hand-waving that some norm or other resists the analysis.

For every question give me a follow-up keyed to the answer she's most likely to give, and flag anywhere I'm about to walk straight into a reply she's clearly got loaded and ready. Keep it tight -- I dont want to be reading off a list of fifteen questions on air.
