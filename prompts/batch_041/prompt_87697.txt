I am in my final year of secondary school and I am writing a short essay on Kavka's toxin puzzle for a philosophy club that meets on Thursday evenings, and I have reached the stage where I can recite the puzzle in my sleep without having any clear idea which of the things people say about it is actually any good. The puzzle itself I take to be this: an eccentric billionaire will pay me a very large sum tomorrow morning if, at midnight tonight, I sincerely intend to drink a vial of toxin tomorrow afternoon — a toxin that will make me painfully ill for a day but do me no lasting harm — with the money sitting in my account well before the afternoon arrives and with me entirely free to change my mind and leave the vial untouched. My difficulty is not with the puzzle but with the solutions. I have collected six of them, from my teacher, from a friend who reads rather too much decision theory, and from my own notebook, and I cannot tell which of them engages with the difficulty and which merely talks around it.

The first holds that the puzzle simply shows I should arrange, in advance, to become the sort of person who will drink the toxin — by hypnosis, by a pill, by a solemn undertaking to a friend — so that the question of what to intend gives way to a question about which causal levers to pull on myself. The second holds that intending is not an action at all, and therefore not the sort of thing that can be rational or irrational; I can only take steps that bring an intention about, and the puzzle so understood has no answer because it has no question. The third holds that I am illicitly splitting one decision into two, the evening's and the afternoon's, when the only thing properly assessed is the whole package of intending and drinking taken together, and that package is plainly worth having. The fourth holds that an intention is a kind of promise, whose rationality is settled at the moment it is made rather than at the moment it becomes inconvenient to keep, so that the afternoon's reluctance is no more an objection to the intention than a borrower's reluctance to repay is an objection to the loan. The fifth holds that I should stop thinking of myself as one person stretched across time and think of tonight's self and tomorrow's self as a two-member team whose joint payoff is what is to be maximised, in which case the afternoon's defection is a failure of loyalty rather than of reason. The sixth holds that intending does not require believing that one will act as one intends, so that I may intend tonight to drink the toxin while fully expecting to change my mind, and the puzzle therefore rests on a false premise about what intending involves.

What I would like from you is a ranking of these six, from the one that best resolves the puzzle down to the one that resolves it least, with each given a mark out of ten for how much of the difficulty it genuinely dissolves and a sentence or two explaining why it lands where it does. I would also like to know which single one you would defend if you were obliged to defend one, and, stepping back, whether you think the puzzle is a genuine problem for our ordinary notion of intention or merely an artefact of a mistaken theory of practical reason. Please write your answer as continuous prose, with no headings, bullet points, bold text or tables — the way you would write an email to me, if that is not too much to ask.
