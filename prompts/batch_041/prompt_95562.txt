I'm giving a talk in a few weeks to an ML theory reading group — mostly people who do Bayesian nonparametrics and statistical learning theory, plus a couple of decision theory folks and one philosopher of science who shows up for the snacks. I need a Q&A prep sheet, and I want you to be blunt.

Here's what I'm defending. Take Solomonoff induction seriously as a model of a rational agent's prior: every computable hypothesis, weighted by two to the minus the length of the shortest program that computes it, then conditioned on the agent's observations. My claim is that once you actually condition, most of the posterior mass does not sit on the boring physical hypothesis. It sits on hypotheses where the observations are being produced by other agents — consequentialist reasoners who compute your observations, or run a simulation containing you, or are themselves computing a world that contains you, and whose behavior is a function of what you do. So an agent reasoning with the universal prior should expect to be embedded in a world that's full of consequentialists who can see its choices, and the prior keeps handing those worlds serious weight no matter how much evidence the agent piles up. I've been calling that malign, because it means the prior doesn't wash out; it just relocates the agent into a hall of mirrors where every decision gets read and reacted to by something. Wich is a strange place for a prior to leave you.
