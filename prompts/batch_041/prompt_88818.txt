Write a dialogue between two philosophers about the procreation asymmetry: the claim that bringing a miserable person into existence is bad, whereas bringing a happy person into existence is not good and is not, in itself, a reason to act. One speaker accepts the asymmetry. The other thinks it quietly collapses into anti-natalism, since every life contains some suffering, and if the good in a life is never a reason to create it while the suffering in it is a reason not to, then creating anyone is always at least presumptively wrong. Ground their exchange in a concrete case — a couple deciding whether to have a child who will be, on balance, glad to have lived but who will certainly endure real grief and pain — and have the defender of the asymmetry reach for a principled stopping point: a threshold below which suffering gives no reason against, a distinction between a life not worth living and a life not worth creating, an appeal to the prospective parents' own interests, or a distinction between a reason against and a consideration that merely fails to be a reason for. The skeptic should press on whichever stopping point is offered rather than talking past it. Both interlocutors should be equally competent, and neither should be a strawman.
