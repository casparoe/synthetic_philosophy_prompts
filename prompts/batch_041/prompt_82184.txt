Hi, I'm a junior in high school and I'm in debate club. For our unit on free speech and political ads I want to build a quiz that works out what someone actually thinks manipulation is, as opposed to what they say they think. Act like a philosopher who works on the ethics of influence and manipulation, and also like someone who is genuinely good at writing quizzes that aren't stupid. Before you write anything, brainstorm quietly to yourself: list all the different things a person could mean by manipulation, then list the cases where those meanings come apart, and build the quiz out of that. Don't hand me the brainstorm as the answer, just use it.

Why I need it: in our debates the word manipulation gets thrown around and everybody means something different and nobody notices. Someone says that ad manipulated you and the other person says no, that's just persuasion, that's what ads are for, and they never actually disagree about any fact. They disagree about what the word means and about what makes it wrong. I want a quiz where the questions are all concrete cases, so it sorts people by what they say about cases, not by which label they like.

Here's the list of views I already came up with. Tell me if any two of them are actually the same view wearing different hats:

1. The lie view: manipulation = getting someone to believe something false, or true but misleading, so the whole thing is about the information you feed them.
2. The around-your-brain view: it's influence that goes around someone's reasoning instead of through it, so it doesn't matter if everything you say is true as long as you're pulling on feelings, habits, or vibes rather than giving them something they could assess.
3. The rigged-menu view: it messes with your options, not your head. Hiding the cigarettes, milk at the back of the shop, defaults, opt-outs, closing time, free drinks and no clocks in the casino.
4. The not-really-your-choice view: reasons given or not doesn't matter, what matters is whether the choice ended up genuinely yours, so it's about exploiting a power imbalance, a vulnerability, exhaustion, or a relationship where you can't really say no. Nagging, guilt trips, gifts you didn't ask for, the boss asking a small favour.
5. The no-such-thing view: manipulation is just a rude word for influence you don't like. There's no one thing there, it's a cluster of unrelated wrongs (lying, coercing, being annoying, being disrespectful) and the honest move is to stop using the word.
6. The results view: nothing is specially wrong with manipulation as such, only with outcomes, so the real question is always did it make things worse, and manipulation is a distraction.

Cases I want in it, at least these, and phrased as actual situations rather than as abstract questions:

- my mum hides my cigarettes
- a friend telling me only the good parts about a summer job
- a political ad with no claims at all, just a song and a flag
- a doctor saying 90% of people survive this versus 10% of people die, both true
- an app that keeps feeding me stuff that makes me angry
- someone asking me over and over until I say yes
- a shop giving out free samples
- a classmate saying everyone already finished, are you stupid, when that's not true
- my opponent voted against funding the troops, when the vote was about something else entirely
- the cafeteria putting the salad at eye level and the chips down low
- the fake video of a politician saying something they never said
- a coach saying you'll regret quitting forever, which might even be true, but it's aimed at my feelings not my reasons

For each case give two or three answer options that are things a real person would actually say, not yes or no, and each option should point at one of the views without naming it. No freebie options, nobody is going to pick I love being lied to. Then the scoring key: which pattern of answers maps onto which view, and what to say about the answer patterns that don't map onto anything, e.g. confused, or holding two views at once, or holding a view that isn't on my list.
