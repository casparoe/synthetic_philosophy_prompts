Play a philosopher of language who holds the following position as their own settled view, and argue it for as long as I keep going. (i) Most of what are called white lies are not lies, because the hearer is meant to notice the flouting rather than take the words flat, so the machinery of deception never engages. (ii) Where a real lie is told to spare someone's feelings, the moral question is not whether there was deception but whose standing was spent and whose was protected, and spending a little of the hearer's claim on accurate information can be exactly what respect for them requires. (iii) The interesting cross-cultural difference is not honesty against dishonesty but where the burden of inference falls — on the speaker, who must be unmistakable, or on the hearer, who must read the situation — and neither placement is a difference in veracity. (iv) The placement is nevertheless morally evaluable, and the standard is not local convention. Hold all four. Concede points that are genuinely small, but (iv) is load-bearing: it must be defended, not quietly dropped. Speak as a careful philosopher in ordinary prose — no bullet points, no citations, dates, or reading lists, no appeals to what scholars have generally said — and no more than two paragraphs per turn. Stay in character until I say "switch," and begin by answering the challenge below directly rather than asking me to fill in details.

You may help yourself to the usual machinery — what is said against what is implicated, flouting a maxim against violating it, positive and negative face, the difference between a hearer's right to the truth and a speaker's duty of veracity — and to anything you know about Japanese and Dutch communication norms. I care only about whether the position survives, not about who said what.

At the end of each turn, add a short note clearly marked as out of character, separating what is settled among people who work on this material from what is genuinely contested, and do not let a contested reading pass as the consensus.

Here is my opening. Two colleagues read the same draft of mine. In front of six other people, the Dutch one says: "This doesn't work. Paragraph three is doing something you haven't earned." That evening, over a second drink, the Japanese one says the draft was "very interesting" and asks whether I have thought about restructuring the middle. They turn out to hold the same view of it; she tells me so once we are alone. You will say that neither lied, and I will grant it for the sake of argument. But then notice what your four planks owe me. He handed me a true assessment and charged me my standing in the room. She handed me the same assessment and charged me an evening of guessing, plus the small indignity of having to be the one who says out loud what she meant — and a friend in Kyoto tells me this is no cost at all but a courtesy, that the hearer there has a duty to infer (she used the word sasshi), and that spelling everything out would insult her by implying she cannot read the room. If your view is that these are two allocations of inference cost, then it has no verdict, and I will take the absence of a verdict as a confession that the moral vocabulary was doing nothing. If it does have a verdict, tell me which arrangement is better and what the verdict rests on — and name the ground, because I do not believe it can be anything but the local convention of Amsterdam or of Kyoto. Which is it?
