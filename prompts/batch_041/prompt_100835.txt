Hey, so I'm a fourth-year PhD student in political theory, I'm running the week on Nozick in the reading group I co-organize, and instead of another lecture I want to hand people a decision tree. The point is to sort them by what they actually think about the Wilt Chamberlain argument rather than by whatever label they've adopted, so every node has to be a question someone can answer without already knowing which position it leads to.

Start everyone at a commitment I think basically the whole room shares: that Nozick's entitlement theory is at least the right *shape* of answer — holdings are just if they came about by just acquisition, just transfer, or rectification of past injustice, and there's no further requirement that the resulting distribution match any pattern. From there I want branches that split on the things that actually matter. E.g.: is the argument's target limited to patterns that leave liberty out, or does it also hit a pattern that's partly *defined* by voluntary transfer? Is the initial distribution stipulated to be just, and if it isn't, does the argument even get going? Is the claim being made that a pattern *must* be violated, that it *will* be if people keep transferring, or that maintaining it *requires continuous interference* — those are definately three different claims and I suspect alot of people slide between them. And does the force of the Chamberlain case depend on the transfer being voluntary in a sense that survives the fact that Chamberlain's talent and the audience's tastes are both unchosen?

Leaves I need in there: Nozick's own view; left-libertarianism (self-ownership yes, but the acquisition proviso does serious egalitarian work); the Cohen objection — I think from the 1977 paper collected in Self-Ownership, Freedom and Equality — that the argument doesn't touch patterned principles as such because liberty itself can be part of a pattern; the Rawlsian/basic-structure line that the argument is beside the point since the thing being justified is the background structure, not the post-transfer holdings; the liberal egalitarian who accepts the conclusion but denies it's a reductio, on the grounds that continuously maintaining a pattern is a cost we can just pay; and the question-begging reading, where the argument only works against people who already accept the baseline it quietly assumes. If there are positions that fit the space but nobody actually defends, say so and tell me whether they're empty because they're incoherent, because they're unattractive, or just because nobody's bothered — that's half the point of the exercise for me.

To stop it being a Wikipedia dump, build the tree around two variants of the case I've been kicking around: (a) the same transfer structure but where Chamberlain's talent is the upshot of a brute genetic lottery he did nothing to earn, and (b) a case where the pattern itself is "each according to the transfers they actually agreed to", so the pattern and the liberty are the same thing. Throw in a third where the transfers impose costs on people who never agreed to anything. Use those to test whether the branches actually do work — if two positions land in the same leaf on all three cases, flag them as ones the tree can't separate and don't fake a distinction.

Two practical things. First, you're a political philosopher who's taught Nozick a bunch of times, so think it through carefully before committing to a structure — sketch the logical space of answers first and then place the named positions in it, don't start from a list of names and reverse-engineer questions. Second, lead with the one sentence naming the single distinction that does the most work in the whole tree, then give the tree and the reasoning, no throat-clearing and don't restate what I asked for.
