I rewatched Dr. Strangelove last night for like the fifth time and now I can't stop thinking about it as a decision theory movie rather than just a farce, and I want to actually work through the reading instead of vibing about it. The doomsday machine is so clearly a precommitment device — the whole gag is that the Soviets built it to fire automatically and irreversibly so that the threat doesn't depend on any human deciding to push the button later. That's the acausal-threat thing as I understand it: the retaliation isn't responsive to anything you do in the moment, it just sits there already wired up, and it only changes your behaviour if you believe it. Except the movie then turns the screw, because the thing only deters anyone if they know about it, and in the film they hadn't announced it yet — so we get this gorgeous demonstration of a commitment that's perfectly real but doesn't actually bind anyone because the info never propagated. Is that the movie's real thesis, that credibility has a knowledge condition and not just an automation condition?

And then the part I'm less sure about: surrogate goals. The way I had it explained to me — if someone threatens you to make you do X, one move is to not just resist but to have already adopted a different goal, so that their threat lands on something it can't move. Like you precommit that if they do the bad thing you'll (uselessly, spitefully) do some other thing they hate, which makes threatening you pointless because you've changed what you're optimizing for rather than just gritting your teeth. So: does Strangelove leave any room for that, or is the doomsday machine precisely the case where a surrogate goal can't help, because the "threat" is already being executed by a machine that doesn't care what anyone wants? I want you to take the film seriously as a piece of decision theory and not flatten it into satire. Where does it get the logic of commitment and credibility right, where does it cheat or simplify because it's a comedy and needs the plot to end, and is there something it dramatizes that the people writing about precommitment and acausal threats keep missing? Point me at the specific scenes that carry the argument. And please be straight with me if the surrogate-goal frame just doesn't fit here and I'm forcing it — I'd rather know.
