I manage observability and reliability for a distributed-systems team, and I have no formal philosophy training, so I think about measurement in a particular vocabulary: channels and instrumentation, ground truth, calibration and drift, observer effects, silent failures, and error attribution across instrument, transduction, and reporting stages. Restate the philosophical debate about the reliability of introspection in that vocabulary as faithfully as you can — specifically the disputes over whether self-reports are accurate about the causes of one's behaviour, about the content of one's current mental states, and about their phenomenal character; over whether confabulation counts as error at all; and over whether the first-person report enjoys any evidential privilege. Tell me which of these land on problems my vocabulary already recognises, which survive only in distorted form, and what in the debate has no counterpart there at all — for instance, any claim that the report is constitutive of the state rather than evidence about it, or that there is a fact of the matter independent of every channel. Finally, say whether the residue marks a gap in how engineers think about measurement or a sign that the philosophical question is less well posed than the engineering one.
