I sit on the diagnostic classification committee of my national psychiatric association, which meets next month to vote on the next revision of our diagnostic manual. I am expected to circulate a written opinion in advance, and I would like you to draft it.

The disputed item is a proposed new category, chronic attentional flatness (CAF). The working definition is at least six months of persistent difficulty becoming absorbed in anything, blunted interest even in previously valued activities, compulsive low-grade stimulation-seeking, and a felt unreality of one's own preferences, producing distress or impairment. The proposed criteria include a fourteen-item self-report scale with a cutoff. The field trial reports acceptable sensitivity and specificity against clinician judgment, but the clinician-judgment criterion was itself calibrated against the scale, so the two are not independent measurements of anything.

Two further complications. First, an informal version of the label has been circulating online for roughly two years. Clinics in three regions report that patients increasingly arrive already self-diagnosed, and the reported symptom profile has drifted: early self-identified cases emphasized bodily fatigue and sleep disturbance, recent ones emphasize a specific vocabulary of flatness and of preferences that do not feel like one's own. The drafters treat this drift as evidence that the category is tracking something real; the skeptics treat it as evidence that the category is manufacturing its own referent. Second, CAF overlaps heavily with our existing, better-validated category for persistent low mood and anhedonia. Admitting CAF would draw off a subpopulation that currently anchors the existing category's validity evidence, since that evidence was gathered on exactly the patients CAF would now capture. There is also a treatment and reimbursement stake: a coding category is the precondition for the therapy protocol and for insurance coverage, so rejecting CAF leaves patients who present this way without the treatment.

One of the drafters has proposed a general admissibility test: a category should be stable under its own application, meaning that classifying people under it should not systematically change what the category measures. Taken seriously, that test would also appear to discredit at least two categories already in the manual, so endorsing it as a general rule would reopen decisions the committee regards as settled.

The committee divides roughly three ways: admit CAF as a full category; reject it and treat the presentation as a variant of the existing category; or admit it only with provisional status and mandatory monitoring. I must recommend one of these, with reasons the other two factions can actually respond to, and I must give an honest account of the strongest consideration on the side I come down against. Note that this is not the familiar argument about whether some condition is a real disease. The live question is what our committee should do given that the act of classifying will itself reshape the population being classified, and given that our categories are not evidentially independent of one another.

Please draft the memo, roughly 900 to 1200 words. It should state a concrete recommendation and any conditions attached to it; give the reasoning; engage the strongest objection to the recommendation rather than restating our own position; and specify what we should monitor after the vote so that the decision can be revisited on stated grounds. Do not include citations or a literature review; I will insert references to the committee's own documents myself. End with a final line in the form Verdict: LABEL, using exactly one of ADOPT, REJECT, or PROVISIONAL.
