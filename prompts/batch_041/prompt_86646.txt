A regulator runs a two-track regime for risky products. To bring a new drug or a novel biological agent to market, an applicant must submit a positive safety case and wait for the regulator to accept it; the regulator itself is not obliged to show that the product causes harm. Products already in wide use before the regime existed may be sold freely unless the regulator assembles enough evidence of harm to justify restricting them. Three features were added recently: a trigger under which credible evidence of a plausible mechanism of large-scale or irreversible harm shifts the burden onto the applicant before any harm has been observed; a substitution rule under which a riskier product may be refused if a safer alternative is available at comparable cost; and a sunset clause that returns every approval for reconsideration after a fixed term. Defenders describe the whole arrangement as an application of the precautionary principle. Critics call that principle incoherent, on the ground that refusing to approve a technology is itself an action with risks of its own, so that a consistent precautionary regulator would have to forbid the forbidding. I cannot reconcile the criticism with the regime I have just described, which seems entirely coherent.

Leave aside whether the regime is a good idea and reconstruct the philosophy it presupposes. What conception of the burden of proof, and what view of the moral status of the status quo, does the two-track structure require? What does it commit itself to about the distinction between acting and refraining, and about whether a mechanistically credible but unquantified risk can count as a reason for state action? Is the strong version of the principle that the critics attack actually implied by these features, or does the attack land only on a version this regime need not hold? Then separate what the regime strictly presupposes from what is explained by more mundane considerations such as reliance interests, retroactivity, and administrative cost; identify where it is underdetermined, in that incompatible theories of risk and authority would license the same rules; and say whether the grandfather clause, the substitution rule, and the trigger can be held together. Finally, describe what would change if the regime's implicit view were replaced by a single expected-value calculus applied indifferently to action and inaction.
