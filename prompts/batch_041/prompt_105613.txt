I've followed the gender-equality-paradox literature for a while, and I'd like to skip the standard nature-versus-culture framing and get a structured evaluation instead. Treat the cross-national finding as the explanandum — that wealthier, more gender-egalitarian societies often show larger measured sex differences in preferences, personality, and field choice — and tier the following candidate explanations by how much you'd trust each to account for the pattern. My list deliberately mixes the standard accounts with a few I've mainly seen floated in blog comments: (1) a measurement artifact, where richer countries simply produce cleaner or differently-scaled data; (2) sample selection, where egalitarian countries draw from more educated or more liberal subpopulations; (3) response-style differences, where gender-typical answering behavior varies with national culture; (4) relative-return or opportunity-cost sorting, where in rich economies STEM pays comparatively less, so freer women sort elsewhere; (5) developmental affordance, where material security lets temperament express itself more fully; (6) a costly-signaling account, where in affluent societies preferences become identity markers that people invest in and exaggerate; and (7) a second-order-norms account, where a culture's emphasis on autonomy itself selects for people who hold and report stronger intrinsic preferences.

Sort these into tiers — I'd expect three or four — and for each one give a single sentence of justification, the one observation or design that would most cleanly separate it from its nearest rival, and a note on which entries are genuinely competing rather than mutually compatible. Then give me a number between 0 and 1 for the fraction of the cross-country pattern you'd attribute to artifacts rather than to real differences in preference, and one more number for the fraction that a policy of pushing more women into STEM could plausibly move.
