I've been keeping a note on my phone of times ive said or refused to say that someone 'knows' something, and I'd like to know what rule I'm actually following. Go by what I'd really say, not what sounds respectable.

- My roommate glanced at the whiteboard by the door and told me the landlord's inspection is Tuesday. If I'm idly wondering, I'll say she knows. If I've already booked the day off and I'm rearranging my week around it, I'll say she doesn't know, she only glanced at the board — and I mean that as a claim about her, not just about how careful I'm being. Same woman, same glance, same whiteboard; what changed is me.

- My brother is deciding whether to quit his job over whether the office is closing; he heard it from a colleague who's usually right. I'm on the sofa with nothing riding on it and I'll cheerfully say he knows. I'll also tell him not to stake his career on it. Those two don't feel like they're fighting each other, but I gather they're supposed to.

- The reverse: his evidence is thin, he's about to quit, and I'm the nervous one because we share a lease. I still don't think I get to say he doesn't know just because I'm the one sweating. He's being reckless, which is different.

- Where the belief is rock solid — he knows his own mother's name, and he's betting the house on it — the stakes do nothing at all. High stakes only seem to eat into knowledge when the evidence is thin or the belief is shaky. If that's a threshold thing, fine, but say so out loud.

- Two conversations, one day. In the first I say my student knows the exam is Thursday (she checked the official page twice, I have no stake). In the second I say she doesn't know (same evidence — but I'm about to promise a visiting speaker the room is free). I don't think either of us is saying something false, and I also don't think 'knows' has quietly changed meaning between the two conversations. I'd rather that combination be explained than have one half of it dropped.

- I only learned months later that a casual belief of mine had mattered a lot; I'd have lost my deposit if I'd been wrong. It feels wrong that whether I knew should be settled retroactively, but I notice I now say I didn't really know, and I said I knew at the time.

- In a safety briefing or a courtroom the bar goes way up. That feels like a fact about the room, not about anybody's knowledge.

What principle, or small set of principles, gets most of this? Precise enough that I can see what it says about a case I haven't listed — say, someone with borderline evidence, high stakes, who is also the only person in the conversation who cares.
