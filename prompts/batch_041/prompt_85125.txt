I'm a second-year PhD student putting together an application for a one-semester internal research fellowship — the kind that buys teaching relief plus a modest stipend and expects a single substantial paper at the end (mine would be around 12,000 words). I'd like you to write the research proposal, roughly two pages. My half-formed idea is this. Everyone agrees there is something wrong with "bootstrapping": using a source's own deliverances to certify that source as reliable, and then using that certification to shore up the very beliefs you started from. The standard illustration is a person who glances at a gauge, comes to believe the tank is half full, notes that she came to believe it by glancing at the gauge, and concludes that the gauge is reliable — and the intuition is that she cannot then turn around and use that conclusion as support for her belief about the tank. I accept the intuition. What I doubt is the usual explanation of it. The usual explanations say the reasoning is circular, or that she doesn't independently know the premise, or that the premise's warrant depends on the conclusion's. But I can construct cases with the same structure that strike me as perfectly fine — a person who gets the same answer from a source three times over a morning, in a way that feels like corroboration rather than mere repetition — and I can construct cases with no circularity at all that strike me as just as bad. So I want to test a diagnosis of my own: that this debate has run together two questions, (a) a structural question about whether a belief can be justified in virtue of the deliverances of the very faculty or process that produced it, and (b) a dialectical question about whether a subject may legitimately answer a challenge to such a belief by citing that same faculty or process. My hunch is that the answer to (a) is yes — there is nothing wrong with self-supporting warrant, since warrant has to bottom out somewhere — while the answer to (b) is no, because you cannot discharge a challenge by presupposing the very thing challenged, and that most of the apparent disagreement in this area comes from sliding between the two. I also suspect there is a third thing going on in the cases I find benign, something closer to an inheritance of trust than to genuine additional evidence, and I can't tell whether that helps me or just relabels the problem.

The proposal should sharpen this into a question with a determinate answer, explain why the existing answers leave it open rather than settling it, and be candid about the two obvious alternatives I would be rejecting — that circularity itself is the defect, and that there is nothing wrong with these cases at all. It should also justify the method against those alternatives: why separating the structural from the dialectical question, and working through paired cases designed to pull them apart, is a better route than simply refining the notion of circularity or the notion of a defeater.

Concretely, I'd like the proposal to specify the method in stages rather than as a vague list — presumably constructing a small battery of paired cases engineered to isolate (a) from (b), giving a precise account of what makes a challenge to a belief legitimate, and then testing the diagnosis against those pairs, including at least one case where the verdicts come apart and I have to choose. Say what the deliverable looks like at the end (I'm imagining a paper with one central distinction, a set of cases, and a verdict on two or three of the hardest ones), say what would count as the project failing — including the possibility that the distinction collapses under pressure, or that the cases cannot be made to pull apart cleanly — and give a realistic scope for one semester, including what I should cut if I run out of time. Please don't include a bibliography or reading list, and don't lean on citations to do the argumentative work; refer to positions and arguments by description and I'll attach the references myself. And please don't simply tell me the topic is "the debate about easy knowledge" — the point is to hand a referee a question that looks both open and finishable.
