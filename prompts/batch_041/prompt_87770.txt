I've been roped into running next month's session of an interdisciplinary reading group — vets, a couple of animal-science people, one lawyer, one philosopher, we meet in the back of a pub and shout over the quiz machine — and the topic is Kant on animals. Seventy-five minutes, no prep on their end, and I'd rather they didn't leave thinking the man was either a monster or an embarrassment we can pat on the head. So plan the explanation for me, not the explanation itself: what has to be in place before the question even makes sense to them, in what order, and why that order — what depends on what. My hunch is the load-bearing step is the duties-to-oneself move (cruelty to a dog wrongs me, or rather my own humanity, not the dog), but that's exactly the bit I expect to lose them on, and I can't decide whether it should come before or after the famous business about cruelty blunting our sympathy for people. Which comes first matters, I think, because the second one sounds like an empirical prediction and the first doesn't, and I don't want them walking out convinced the whole position is hostage to how sociopaths turn out to behave.

I also need the carrying example, and I suspect the obvious one is a trap — dog-kicking lets them hear the view as "be nice to animals so you stay nice to people," which is precisely the misconception I most want to kill. Would vivisection serve better, or the farmer who treats his stock well because it pays, or the man who's scrupulous with animals on principle and cold to every person he meets? Whatever it is, tell me what it has to have and what it has to lack so it doesn't smuggle the wrong picture in. Then the misconceptions in the order they'll actually surface, with the point in the sequence where each gets headed off rather than saved for the end: that "indirect duty" means not really a duty at all; that Kant thought animals were just machines (I think that's Descartes, but they won't know the difference); that the argument is a broken utilitarian calculation in fancy dress; that animals can't reason, offered as a gotcha. The vet is going to raise the sociopath — tortures cats, lovely to his family, no detectable blunting of feeling — and I'd like the sequence to have made room for that before he does. And the lawyer keeps asking whether we can just retrofit the whole thing so animals come out as ends in themselves; I think she's been reading Korsgaard. Last five minutes, or leave it out and say so?

Finally: what do I leave out at this level and announce out loud that I'm leaving out, what one question do I put to the room at the end to see whether they've got the position as a position rather than as a punchline, and no reading list please, I've got the texts.
