I teach a grad seminar on democratic theory and two of my students have been going at each other since Thursday; both of them have now emailed me separately asking me to settle it, which I've said I won't do in class, so I'm working out my own view here instead. The fight started because we'd been reading the standard stuff on epistocracy — the idea that maybe the politically incompetent shouldn't get an equal say — and one of them, call her A, decided to make it concrete with a proposal of her own. She calls it "the ratchet": everyone votes, one person one vote, no tests, no weighting; but for a specific narrow class of decisions — the irreversible ones, her examples were selling off a public water utility or amending the state constitution — the result also has to clear a citizens' jury of a few hundred people drawn by lot, paid, briefed for a week with adversarial experts, deliberating, and able to veto. Her line was that this isn't epistocracy at all, because nobody is selected for being smarter or better informed, it's just sortition plus a check, and the whole point is to slow down exactly the decisions you can't take back. And that a democrat should love it, since sortition is the oldest democratic device there is, and no one's vote is being weighted — you're just requiring a second, more careful pass.

B wasn't having any of it. B said the "irreversibility" trigger is doing something sneaky: if the jury can veto, the jury's preference beats the majority's, and that's a transfer of power to a body that is neither elected nor removable, whatever its selection method — so the ratchet is epistocracy with the serial numbers filed off, or at best rule by the randomly chosen, which is its own problem. A said accountability isn't the only legitimation story going, and that criminal juries work exactly this way and nobody calls them oligarchs. B said criminal juries decide facts under rules made by elected legislatures, whereas this jury would be deciding the policy question itself, so the analogy collapses. A said fine, call it a fourth branch then. B said a fourth branch with a veto over constitutional amendments is a fairly enormous deal, and that "irreversibility" is either so vague it swallows everything — any decision is irreversible if you squint, roads get built, treaties get signed — or so narrow it's a gimmick, and either way A owes a non-arbitrary line. A's last move was that B's own position, plain majoritarianism, has to either accept that a bare majority can permanently alienate a public asset or smuggle in substantive limits of its own, in which case B needs the ratchet as much as A does.

Anyway, they want a verdict and I'd rather not just split the difference. Can you go through the exchange move by move — not just who wins overall — and say which specific claims are right, which are wrong, and what both of them are missing? I care especially about two things: whether the sortition move actually gets A out of the epistocracy charge or just relocates it somewhere less visible, and whether B's "vague or a gimmick" dilemma really lands against the irreversibility criterion or is a false dilemma. Their arguements have gotten a bit circular by now, so it'd help to have the actual crux named. And if you think there's a repaired version of the ratchet that survives B's objections, say what it would have to look like; if you think the whole idea should be dropped, say that instead and tell me what B is still missing even then.
