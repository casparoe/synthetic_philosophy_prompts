I'm a philosophy PhD student with a decent background in modal logic and probability and exactly one graduate course in game theory, and I'm trying to decide what to spend the next two or three years on. My working thesis idea is that the literature on the common prior assumption is really three arguments wearing one coat: a descriptive claim about why agents with shared learning histories ought to converge on the same priors; a modelling convention for representing incomplete information, which people adopt without argument whenever they write down a Bayesian game; and a substantive restriction on the set of admissible beliefs and equilibria that does real work whenever someone appeals to the agreement result or to the no-trade theorems. A friend in the economics department tells me the first reading is dead, the second is uninteresting, and the third is the only one anyone has ever cared about; I suspect that's too quick, because the things I'd like to build on — the reduction of incomplete information to imperfect information via a move by nature, the characterization of correlated equilibrium, the impossibility of agreeing to disagree — seem to depend on the assumption in different ways and to different degrees, and it isn't obvious to me that they stand or fall together. So what I want from you is an agenda rather than a survey. If I have roughly three years, working alone, with more logic than economics and no appetite for heavy econometrics, which open questions about the common prior assumption are most worth attacking, and in what order? Defend the ordering: which questions are upstream of which, which would leave large parts of the existing literature standing whichever way they came out, which are tractable for someone who is comfortable with epistemic logic but allergic to applied work, and which look central from inside the field but would not actually change much downstream.

Two suggestions I've heard and would like you to adjudicate along the way, since they seem to me to pull in opposite directions. The first is that the real question is not whether the common prior is true but whether the results that lean on it survive its replacement by something weaker — a common-support condition, or a mutual absolute continuity requirement, or a time-indexed assumption about common learning rather than a single prior over a type space — and that the field has been arguing about the wrong object for fifty years. The second is that the sharpest pressure on the assumption comes not from disagreement between agents, which the type-space apparatus can model perfectly well, but from unawareness, where it is genuinely unclear whether the standard partitional framework can even state the assumption before asking whether it holds. If one of these reframings is the right way in and the other is a distraction, say so and say why; if both are worth a chapter, tell me which chapter comes first. I'd also like your criteria made explicit — importance, tractability, neglectedness, how many other results depend on the answer — and your honest view of the trade-offs between them for someone in my position, plus what developments over the next couple of years (a counterexample, a representation theorem, a shift in how a neighbouring literature uses the assumption) would move a question up or down your list.

Please don't give me citations or a reading list; I can find the literature myself, and I'd rather have the argument about what matters. Do tell me how confident you are in the ranking and in the reasoning behind it, and single out the one claim in your answer you are least sure of — I would much rather have a flagged weak point than a smooth one, even if the weak point turns out to be the claim that the three readings are distinct in the first place.
