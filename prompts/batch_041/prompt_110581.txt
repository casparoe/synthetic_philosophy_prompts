Nine years ago this spring I was the incident commander for the Cass Fork volunteer rescue squad on the night the river came over the road at Sutter's Bend, and I am the one who sent the last boat to the four people on the roof of the feed store rather than to the man in the Subaru, who was still on the phone with the dispatcher when the water reached him. This spring the county is dedicating a memorial stone at the trailhead, and the squad has asked me to give the address. I have turned down every interview for nine years and I am not going to start now, but I owe the squad and that family something said out loud, and I have not been able to write it, because every draft I make turns into either an apology or a justification and I believe neither one. The audience will be about two hundred people standing in a field: the parents and two brothers of the man who drowned, who asked me privately, and I promised them, that nobody on the program would say it was his time, or that everything happens for a reason, or that four lives were bought with his; the four survivors, one of whom is now seventeen and has asked to speak after me and has told the squad she wants to say that she does not know whether she has any right to be alive; the volunteers, some of whom have never forgiven me and some of whom think I was obviously right and would like to hear me say so; and the county emergency management director, who has spent two years trying to get a written policy adopted that says, in effect, always go for the larger group first, and who will be listening to see whether I back it. I am not religious and most of that field is; I teach a seminar on the ethics of rescue at the community college, and I will be talking to people I have known for thirty years.

What I cannot stop thinking about, and what I want the address to be built on, is the argument in Taurek's "Should the Numbers Count?" that I teach every year and that has never once felt like a classroom exercise to me: that five deaths are not a greater misfortune than one, because there is no one who suffers the five; that the claim of any one of the four on that roof was no stronger than the claim of the man in the car, and that the four claims together did not amount to a claim four times as strong as his; and that if what you are trying to do is be fair, the fair procedure is to give each claimant an equal chance rather than to weigh lives against lives. I also want the address to know what that argument does not show: at most it shows that the four had no claim that made my choice obligatory, not that my choice was forbidden. The honest description of what I did that night is that I broke a tie, and counting heads is a way of breaking ties, not a reason for anything. So the address has to do something harder than defend me. It has to say something to a room in which the parents of the one and the four who lived are sitting in the same rows, without asserting the thing Taurek denies and without falling back on the platitude that all lives are equally precious and so it does not matter who was saved — which is precisely the sentence that would tell those parents their son's death did not matter. And it has to say something about the county's proposed rule, which I am inclined to support, without pretending that a rule of always counting heads is justified by a greater claim that the larger group does not have; I suspect it can only be defended as a convention we adopt because it is the one nobody can object to being bound by, and I would like the address to make that thought visible without making it sound like a technicality.

Please write the address: roughly nine hundred words, six or seven minutes read slowly, no names of philosophers, no jargon, no citations, no footnotes, nothing that requires a screen — it will be read outdoors into a bad microphone by a woman who will have to stop once in the middle. It should be neither a lecture nor a confession; the philosophy should do its work in the choices about what the address refuses to say. In particular it must not preempt the seventeen-year-old's speech or answer the question she is going to ask about whether she has a right to be alive, and it must not leave the parents of the drowned man with the impression that their son was the price of four tickets. End with something the four can carry and something the two parents can carry, which will not be the same thing. After the address, add a short set of notes for my own use — five or six bullets, clearly marked as not part of the speech — telling me which standard justification you refused to give and where you refused it, and what you had to give up in order to keep the parents and the four in the same room.
