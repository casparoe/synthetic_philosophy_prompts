For the last couple of years I've been running a small reading group that grew out of an argument in a blog comments section, and we keep circling back to the same tangle: if it is computations rather than carbon that carry moral weight, then which computations, and what makes a given computation the system's own rather than one we have merely chosen to ascribe to it? I want to turn our accumulated notes into a one-page handout, and the form that would help most is a decision tree rather than another prose essay. Build it from the logical space first and locate the named positions inside that space second, rather than starting from a list of famous theories: each branch node should be a question a member of the group could answer without already knowing which position it leads to, and each leaf a position, carrying its label (or 'unnamed' where the space is unoccupied) plus one sentence on its most awkward commitment. Fix the size at roughly six to eight branch nodes and no more than ten leaves, and pitch it at people who are comfortable with functionalism and with the consciousness literature but are not specialists.

The distinctions I care most about, and around which I would like the tree built, are these. First, whether anything is added by moving from a physical to a computational description at all, or whether talk of the computation a system runs is a redescription with no independent grip; I have been bothered for a while by the familiar point that under a permissive enough mapping almost any physical system can be read as implementing almost any finite-state machine, so that absent some constraint on what counts as implementing a computation the view slides toward saying that a rock, or a bucket of water, is doing morally relevant work. Second, given some such constraint, which feature of the computation is supposed to carry the weight: a global broadcast of information, recurrent integration of the kind that ties a state to the rest of the system, the presence of a self-model, or something closer to valence. Third, and this is what the group argues about most, what licenses attributing a computation to the system as its own rather than to us as interpreters; whether the computation must play a role in the system's ongoing self-maintenance, whether it must be available to the system in some way, or whether there is no fact of the matter and attribution is a pragmatic choice all seem to be live options, and the tree should make the choice explicit rather than smuggling it into a definition. Fourth, whether status of this kind is graded or all-or-nothing, since a graded answer changes what the tree can conclude at its leaves. If two of the leaves your tree produces turn out to be indistinguishable on the cases that matter, say so instead of pretending the tree separates them, and where some combination of answers is one that nobody seems to occupy, note whether you think that is because the combination is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored.

Finally, since I will be handing this out to people who enjoy pushing back: wherever you are drawing on something that is genuinely contested among people who work on this, mark it as contested and say briefly what the disagreement turns on, rather than writing as though there were a consensus, and keep visible the line between the parts where you take the answer not to be seriously in dispute and the parts where you are picking a side. I would much rather have a handout that admits where the field is split than one that looks more settled than it is.
