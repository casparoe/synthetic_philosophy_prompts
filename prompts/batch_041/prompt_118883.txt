I run a small reading group that meets in the entomology building, and last month we read the "Ant Fugue" dialogue in Hofstadter's Gödel, Escher, Bach — the stretch in which the ant colony Aunt Hillary is treated as a conversational partner rather than as a heap of insects. The discussion ended in a four-way standoff, and I have come to think the reason is that we were quietly answering different questions: two of us were asking whether anything in the colony is *about* the world, one was asking whether the colony could be *hurt*, and the biologists kept asking whether the colony is one organism at all. I want to turn that standoff into a one-page handout for newcomers to the group — first-year graduate students from philosophy and biology, so it has to be self-contained and free of reading lists. Please write a decision tree that begins from commitments nobody in the room denies and ends at a named position on whether an ant colony can have a mind. Build it from the logical space rather than from a list of authors: work out the questions and the combinations of answers they permit first, and only then place the positions that actually occupy those combinations. The very first fork should be the ambiguity itself — whether the reader means states that represent, experience, or agency of the kind that could be praised or blamed — since our whole quarrel seemed to dissolve once that distinction was made. Every branch node must be a concrete case or a plain-language claim that a reader can answer without already knowing which position it leads to; if a node can only be answered by invoking a technical label, rewrite it. Aim for eight to ten nodes and nine to twelve leaves.

Four cases have to appear as nodes, because they are the ones on which the group actually split. First, the pheromone-wipe: the colony's only memory is in its trails, so we erase the trails and leave every ant alive; the colony now behaves as though it has lost its past. Second, the split nest: the colony separates into two viable halves, each of which forages and builds much as the original did. Third, the gradual substitution: workers are replaced one at a time by silicon chips that reproduce their behaviour exactly, until no ant remains. Fourth, the hidden puppeteer: a single ant-sized device buried in the nest turns out to be dictating every worker's movements, so that the apparent colony-level coordination is its doing rather than the ants'. Use these to force the reader to settle, in order, whether the colony must have states that represent the world, whether such states must be realised in something with a locus (a place where they are), and whether that second question is a matter of fact or of what we want the word "mind" to do for us. Where a case can be answered two ways that lead to the same leaf, collapse it; where two leaves come apart only on a case I have not listed, add the case rather than papering over the gap.

At the leaves I need at least the following positions to appear, if the tree can reach them: that only individual ants have minds and the colony has none; that the colony is the organism and the workers are more like its cells; that the workers have minds and the colony is nothing over and above their interaction; that the colony has genuine higher-level intentional states but no experience of its own; that the colony has experience as well; that talk of a colony mind is licensed by how well it predicts and there is no further fact of the matter; that the mind is spread across ants, trails, and nest rather than located in the colony as a unit; and a deflationary leaf on which the question is not yet well-posed. If the tree cannot reach one of these, say so, and say what would have to change for it to. For each leaf, give one sentence naming its most awkward commitment. Flag any pair of leaves your tree cannot separate, with a note on whether the two views differ in what they say about the cases or only in their preferred vocabulary, and mark any combination of answers that no position occupies, diagnosing it briefly as incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. Two final constraints: the biologists in the room will object to "organism" being used loosely, so no branch may turn on a definition they would reject; and nothing in the tree may presuppose an answer to whether experience requires a nervous system. Close with a short paragraph for my own use, identifying which single node is the crux of each of the four disagreements I described.
