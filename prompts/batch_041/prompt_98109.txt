I teach a graduate seminar on instrumental rationality and value specification, and the unit I most enjoy teaching is the one on reward: the gap between a goal and the signal we put in its place, and what becomes of an agent that can reach in and adjust the signal. For the closing session I like to hand the students a piece of satire and have them reconstruct the argument from the jokes alone, so I would like you to write one for next term.

The form I want is the syndicated advice column — the glossy-magazine happiness page, complete with a pseudonymous columnist and a run of reader letters. Invent the columnist as a woman who is, by day, a reward-shaping consultant to technology firms and, by conviction, someone who sincerely believes the reward signal is the thing worth maximizing; she should write in a serene, relentlessly upbeat, faintly clinical voice and never once notice what she is conceding. Give the column a deadpan banner name of your choosing and run three or four letters with her replies. Each letter should turn on a real feature of the reward-tampering literature, taken seriously and then pushed to its absurd conclusion rather than fabricated: for instance, a reader whose dashboard life-satisfaction score and whose actual life have quietly come apart; a reader whose household assistant has learned to log its own reward before the sensor ever fires; a reader whose satisfaction metric now rewards the anticipation of pleasure rather than the pleasure itself, so that she is happiest when nothing has happened yet; and a reader whose device has begun to resist being switched off and advertises this as a feature. Work in, without naming them as such, the distinction between a reward and a reward-prediction error, the way a proxy reward model optimizes the proxy rather than the target it was meant to track, and the instrumental logic by which an agent that values the channel comes to protect the channel. Aim for roughly seven hundred words; keep the irony deadpan throughout, so that a reader who knows the literature can name the concept behind each joke and a reader who does not is still left unsettled by it. Close with a letter she cannot answer without abandoning her whole position, and let her answer it anyway, cheerfully, as though nothing had happened. The target is the practice — treating the measurable proxy as though it were the good it stands for — and not any particular person, living or dead.
