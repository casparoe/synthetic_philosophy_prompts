I work in a government strategy unit, and the briefing notes we put in front of ministers are full of claims about generations: that Gen Z will not tolerate a particular working arrangement, that the Baby Boomers voted as a bloc, that Millennials are the first cohort to be worse off than their parents. I am the person who signs these off, I have about two hours per note, no microdata, and no time to commission any. Write me a procedure I can run on a single generational claim to decide whether it can stand in a briefing. Give me the tests in the order I should run them, what counts as passing and failing each, and then apply it to two claims: one that should survive in some amended form, and one that must be cut even though it is the line the minister most wants to use. Two things need to be taken seriously. First, an effect of age, an effect of the period and an effect of belonging to a particular cohort are routinely confused with one another, and a briefing that confuses them will recommend the wrong policy. Second, treat "generation" as a claim to name a real kind rather than merely a birth-year bracket: a real kind here would have a shared causal mechanism, some self-awareness among the people it names, and some predictive power beyond the birth years themselves. Be explicit about which steps the procedure cannot settle and where a person must decide, and about what it will get wrong. Answer in no more than 800 words, leading with the procedure itself.
