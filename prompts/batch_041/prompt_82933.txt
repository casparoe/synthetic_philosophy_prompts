I'm a novelist, and I've been circling one story for two years without landing it. Its subject is translation under formal constraint: the situation Douglas Hofstadter built Le Ton beau de Marot around, where dozens of translators of a single small sixteenth-century French poem by Clément Marot — a note to a sick young woman — each keep something and surrender something else, and no version can hold all of it at once. You needn't know that book, and I don't want you writing about it or quoting it. I want the story itself, with the problem lodged in its bones rather than discussed by its characters.

Write me a short story, in American English, of roughly two thousand words. Here is what it has to do.

The spine is one act of translation. A working translator — not a writer, not a professor, someone with an ordinary job and an ordinary week — is rendering one short, tightly constrained poem into English under some pressure that matters: a deadline, a commission, a person for whom the rendering will mean something. The source poem must be your own invention. It may be in a real language or an invented one, but it must not be a real poem and must not be passed off as one; if a real poem comes to mind, invent a different one instead. State its formal skeleton plainly, once, so the reader can hold it: how many syllables to the line, the rhyme, the word that returns at fixed points, whatever else binds it. Then give a literal, graceless gloss of what it says, so the reader sees exactly what the translator is up against.

Inside the story, give me at least three distinct English renderings of that one poem. Each must hold a different subset of the constraints and lose the rest, and each must be a genuinely different poem, not three drafts of the same one. A reader who counted should be able to check them against the skeleton. Make it clear these are not ranked from bad to good: each is a different reading of what the poem is for.

Three things I specifically need to see, because they're the reason I can't finish the story myself:

One rendering should be the one the translator finds most beautiful in English and least faithful to the original. The story should leave the reader uneasy about it, and should not resolve that unease.

One rendering should satisfy every constraint perfectly and read as dead — proof that a form preserved without pressure is a shell.

Somewhere in the working, the translator must realize that one of the poem's constraints is not decoration. Its brevity, or its refrain, or its sing-song, is doing the emotional work, and dropping it changes what the poem claims rather than merely how it sounds.

Show the thinking the way a translator actually thinks — a word tried, syllables counted, a line discarded, another begun — rather than having a narrator explain the theory afterward. No epigraphs, no citations, no further reading, no character who exists to voice the theme, and no setting in a workshop, seminar, or publishing office.

Do not end with a correct translation or a solved problem. End with a choice, a refusal to choose, or the translator understanding that whatever they pick is a reading they will have to live with — and leave me, the reader, holding two versions rather than one. If you can manage it, give the story a small formal constraint of its own, some sentence or gesture that returns at intervals and means something different each time.
