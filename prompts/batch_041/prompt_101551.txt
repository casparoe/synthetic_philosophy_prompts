I'm a retired paleobiologist — thirty-odd years on benthic forams and a lot of it crouched over muddy core samples — and since I packed it in I've been working my way through philosophy books, mostly phil of biology and history of science, and I've gotten myself tangled up in something. I've been reading about the historians' fight over historical inevitability: whether the big outcomes were going to happen anyway, whether some battle or bad harvest or one stubborn individual actually altered the course of anything, and the whole cottage industry of "what if" history where people argue about whether counterfactual questions are even legitimate in history or just parlor games. There seem to be two families of views on the substantive question — one side saying the outcomes were overdetermined by structural forces like demography, geography and economics, the other saying they hung on contingency and particular decisions — and then a separate methodological fight about whether you can responsibly reason counterfactually at all, or whether the historian's job is just to explain what happened rather than what didn't. In my old field we argued about almost exactly this shape of thing: whether rewinding the tape and running evolution again would give you roughly the same animals or a completely different zoo, how much is selection pulling toward a few solutions versus drift plus one-off accidents of who happened to survive, and whether "convergence" means the outcome was inevitable or only that there are so many ways to make a living.

So what I'd like is this. Take the historians' positions — the inevitabilist one, the contingency one, and the two sides of the methodological quarrel about counterfactuals — and restate each of them as faithfully as you can in the vocabulary I actually think in: fitness landscapes, selection versus drift, contingency and convergence, developmental constraint, exaptation, path dependence, robustness across replays of a lineage. For each, tell me what goes across cleanly, what has to be stretched or fudged to fit, and what simply has no expression in my vocabulary at all. Then the part I really care about: is whatever's left over because my biological toolkit is too crude to say what the historians mean, or because their dispute is thinner than it looks once you press on what "inevitable" is even supposed to be a claim about? Don't give me a reading list — I'm out in the sticks and can't look anything up — just argue it out in your own words. I know the biology cold, so no need to explain drift to me, but don't assume I know the philosophers' jargon.

Last line: a single line beginning "Verdict:" giving me, in one short phrase, whether the residue is a defect of my framework or a sign the historians' argument is less substantive than it appears.
