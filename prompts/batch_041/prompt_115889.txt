I am assembling a small collection of philosophical jokes for a graduate seminar in decision theory, and I need one that will land with readers who have actually worked through the money-pump argument rather than merely heard its name. Please write that joke.

It should have a clear setup and a punchline, and be short enough to deliver aloud in under half a minute. It should be funny to someone who knows the argument: the humour should come from the structure of the problem rather than from the mere mention of it, and the joke should not need a footnote to be understood. Please give me the joke itself, with no explanation, framing, or commentary around it, and do not invent citations or attribute the argument to anyone.

So that the joke can be entirely self-contained, here is the machinery it may use. An agent's preferences run in a cycle: she ranks A above B, B above C, and C above A. She begins by holding A. Because she prefers C to A, she will pay a penny to exchange A for C; now holding C, she prefers B and pays a second penny; now holding B, she prefers A and pays a third. She is back where she began, three pennies poorer, having done at every step exactly what she preferred at that moment — and the same three trades can be offered again, so the drain has no natural stopping point. Two replies are standard. The first denies that a cycle can be a coherent preference structure for a single agent at a single time, and owes an account of what is supposed to be incoherent about it, since each of the three pairwise comparisons looks impeccable taken on its own. The second is a resolute policy: settle on a plan in advance and refuse a later trade that one would, at that moment, rather accept. This second reply avoids the pump only by having the agent act, at some point, against her contemporaneous ranking. Note, finally, that the pump need never actually run: an agent who merely foresees the sequence of offers must refuse the first trade, which she prefers, and so fails to get what she wants although no exchange takes place. Whatever has gone wrong is never located at any single moment; it exists only across time.

The punchline should turn on that structure — on the gap between a choice that is impeccable one at a time and a sequence that is a bad deal overall, or on what each escape from the pump costs the agent who takes it. It should not turn merely on her having lost a few pennies, or on the oddity of economists as a class.
