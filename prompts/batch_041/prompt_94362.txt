I retired recently from a career in physiology and have been sitting in on an undergraduate course in the philosophy of mind. I offered to help grade the second problem set, on pain and its disvalue, and before I hand one answer back I would like a second opinion on how to mark it. The question on the set was this:

Patients with pain asymbolia, typically after damage to particular cortical regions, report that they feel pain — they can localize it and rate its intensity — but they are not distressed by it, and they often fail to withdraw from the stimulus that produces it. What does this syndrome establish about (i) whether the sensory and affective dimensions of pain can come apart, and (ii) whether the badness of pain is intrinsic to the experience? Be explicit about what follows from the clinical findings and what does not, and say what further evidence would be needed to settle the matter.

The student wrote:

Pain asymbolia is the clearest human case of pain without suffering. These patients can still detect and localize noxious stimuli and rate their intensity, so the syndrome establishes that the sensory-discriminative dimension of pain and its affective-motivational dimension are genuinely distinct: one can be present while the other is missing. This is quite different from congenital insensitivity to pain, where the sensory channel itself is absent; the asymbolic patient has the sensation but not the reaction to it. Since cognition and motor control are otherwise intact in these patients, the deficit can be located specifically in the affective component.

The philosophical consequence is that the badness of pain cannot be intrinsic to the sensation. If pain were intrinsically bad, it would be impossible to feel it without being bothered by it, since the badness would be part of what the sensation is; the patients show that this is possible. The unpleasantness must therefore be contributed by something over and above the sensation — most plausibly, the subject's evaluation of the sensation as a threat or as something to be avoided. The sensory quality on its own is neutral and acquires its negative character from the attitude taken toward it. So the syndrome tells against any view on which pain is essentially aversive, and supports the view that the aversiveness is a separable add-on.

Because the patients can still report that something is wrong, the case does not support a thoroughgoing eliminativism about pain affect; it shows only that the affect can be detached from the sensation. I do not think further evidence is needed on this point, since the dissociation is about as clean as clinical material ever gets.

Please grade this out of 10, applying the standard you would use for a competent third-year student, and then give me the point-by-point comments that justify the grade: which claims are correct and well put, which are overstated or rest on an inference that does not go through, which distinctions the student blurs, and what the answer should have said about the third part of the question. I would rather have the reasoning behind each judgment than a general impression, since I will be handing the comments back to the student.
