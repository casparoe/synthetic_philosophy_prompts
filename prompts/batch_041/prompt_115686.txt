Émilie du Châtelet's writings on happiness are often read as holding that strong passions are indispensable to a life worth living, that illusions frequently sustain those passions, and that the love of study is the passion most capable of outlasting the others, consoling us even after our illusions and attachments have collapsed. Suppose we sharpen this constellation of claims into a single thesis, which we may call the surplus thesis: happiness is not a state that can be aimed at directly, but the felt surplus of an activity that already fully occupies us, so that any deliberate attempt to optimize for happiness tends to corrode the passions that produce it.

Please argue for the surplus thesis. In doing so, address three difficulties. First, if happiness cannot be aimed at, how is deliberation about how to live still possible, and what does the surplus thesis add beyond the familiar thought that happiness is a byproduct of absorbing activity? Second, how can the surplus thesis distinguish passions that produce happiness from passions that consume it, given that from the inside these are often indistinguishable until it is too late? Third, does the privileged place assigned to the love of study make the surplus thesis an elitist doctrine, available only to those with the temperament and leisure for intellectual work?

Your argument should be constructive rather than merely defensive: it should show how the surplus thesis can be made precise enough to guide a life, and it should be candid about the point at which it can no longer do so.
