I retired from the lab a few years ago and have been filling my time with philosophy, which is how I ended up reading about machine translation and whether it "understands" anything. I spent my working life reading spectra -- inferring a structure from a squiggle -- so a picture where you recover a hidden message from a signal has a certain pull for me, which is probably why I want it audited properly.

There's an analogy doing a lot of quiet work everywhere I look, in one form or another: translating is like cracking a code. You have the message in the foreign language, you have the key -- the dictionary, the grammar, the parallel texts -- and once you have the key the job is mechanical. The fields own word for what the machine does is "decoding", so it isn't only a metaphor. The version I read last week ran it to a cheerful conclusion: cryptanalysts never understood the messages they broke, so understanding was never the point, and the complaint that machine translation is shallow is just nostalgia for a human mystique. Someone else in the same book ran the same analogy the other way -- only a being who already knew roughly what the message was about could crack the code at all, so a machine that doesn't know can't.

Could you audit it? I want the mapping spelled out -- what corresponds to the ciphertext, the plaintext, the key, the crib, the codebook, the cryptanalyst, and the check that the deciphered text is right -- then where the mapping breaks, and which of the breaks actually matter for those two conclusions. If a break doesn't touch what the analogy is being asked to carry, say so; I suspect at least one side is getting its conclusion for free.

Two things to fold in, since they're what makes me doubt the picture. First, real decipherment seems to need a crib or a known target: the Rosetta stone had Greek on it, and the Linear B tablets gave way once someone guessed the underlying language was Greek -- whereas texts whose statistics look perfectly language-like have sat uncracked for a century (the Voynich thing, whatever it is). If the statistics of the ciphertext don't carry you to the plaintext, that cuts against the idea that a big enough key does the job. Second, and I think this is the real test: a text with no plaintext in it to recover. Nonsense verse, a poem where the sound is doing the work, a legal formula whose point is long forgotten. There's no message hidden in there, and yet people translate these, sometimes well. Where does the code-breaker picture go then?

Give me a verdict, not a menu. If the analogy holds for some kinds of translation and not others, draw the line and say which side of it the conclusions fall on. And if there's a better analogy, tell me what it is -- I keep wanting to say it's more like porting a program to a new machine, where what you preserve is behaviour and not bytes, but I can't tell whether that's an improvement or just more decoration. Be fairly commited: if you think the analogy carries one of those conclusions and not the other, say which, and if you think it carries neither and the argument is really resting on something else, say that. British English, please.
