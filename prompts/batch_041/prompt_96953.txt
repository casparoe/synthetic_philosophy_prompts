I am a novelist, and in the spring I am giving a forty-five-minute craft talk at a small writers' festival to an audience of working novelists and MFA students — people who have read no philosophy since university and who will be quietly suspicious that a lecture is being smuggled into their weekend. What I want to put in front of them, under the heading of the sympathetic villain, is the wrong kind of reasons problem: the puzzle about whether a consideration that gives you reason to want an attitude, or to bring it about, is thereby a reason for the attitude itself. My ulterior motive is that almost every technique by which we buy a reader's affection for a monstrous character — charm, narrative momentum, a small kindness in the opening chapter, the sheer fact of having been shut inside his head for four hundred pages — looks like a reason of the wrong kind, and I want them to see that this is a real distinction rather than a purist's quibble.

Do not write the talk. Plan the explanation. Give me the order in which the pieces must arrive, and why each depends on the one before it: what has to be in place before the phrase a reason to admire can even be heard as ambiguous, and where this audience's grip on it will first slip.

Nominate the single case that should carry the whole thing, and say what it must have and must lack — in particular, whether it needs the wrong-kind consideration to be undeniably a reason for something, so that the audience cannot simply deny it. Then tell me what to make of the case I have been using in my own manuscript: a woman who is told she will not be released until she feels genuine remorse, and who therefore sets about trying to feel it. Is that the same structure as the threat case, or does the fact that she is being asked to manufacture the attitude from the inside change the problem? Say plainly which of the two is the better carrier, and why.

Then the misconceptions, in the order this audience will form them, with the exact point in the sequence where each should be headed off. I expect they will hear wrong kind as immoral; that they will conclude wrong-kind reasons are not reasons at all; and that they will collapse the whole thing into the observation that you cannot choose your feelings. I also want to know whether the distinction survives the move from admiration to belief — whether a reason to believe something because believing it would be good for you belongs to the same family or a different one — and, if it is a different family, what that tells us about the original problem.

Finish with the one question I should ask at the end that would show whether they have the distinction rather than a slogan. And tell me which of the deeper stakes — why this is a problem for a whole way of analysing value, rather than a curiosity about the emotions — I should refuse to explain in forty-five minutes, and what I lose by refusing.
