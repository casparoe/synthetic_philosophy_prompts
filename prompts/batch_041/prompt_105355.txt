In a seminar on ritual and belief in later prehistory, a colleague has twice now closed discussion with the line that absence of evidence is not evidence of absence. He used it most recently against an inference of mine: that a settlement excavated to sterile soil over most of its area, yielding no figurines, no obviously non-utilitarian objects, and no deposits that look structured rather than dumped, was probably without anything we should call ritual practice. He did not claim that the site holds ritual remains we have missed; he claimed that my inference cannot be drawn at all.

I want a menu of the precise claims that sentence could be making, with a verdict on each, and an indication of which reading does the work he needs. Keep the readings genuinely apart. For each, state the thesis in a sentence, say whether it is trivially true, substantive and defensible, or false, and say whether it would in fact block my inference. At a minimum, separate:

- the humility reading: that my failure to find something is not by itself evidence about the world;
- the general claim that absence of evidence is never evidence of absence, taken at face value;
- the sensitivity reading, on which silence counts as evidence exactly in proportion to how likely the search was to have turned the thing up had it been there;
- the preservation reading, on which the silence is a fact about taphonomy and the loss of perishable materials rather than about the past;
- the recognition reading, on which the absence lies in our categories rather than in the past — including the awkward possibility that ritual serves as a residual category for finds we cannot otherwise explain, in which case the claim that a site yields no evidence of ritual comes close to analytic;
- the conversational reading, on which the sentence is not a claim about evidence at all but a device for moving the burden of proof.

Then answer the questions the menu raises. Which readings, if any, license his refusal to let the inference be drawn, and which license only caution? What would I need to establish about the site, the excavation, and the class of finds in question for the sensitivity reading to favor him rather than me? Is there a version of the maxim that is both true and useful to archaeologists, or is the only honest replacement something narrower, such as a claim about what a particular body of evidence can bear? Which of the readings is he most likely to intend, and how would I tell in a given case? Order the readings from weakest to strongest, and mark those that are platitudes dressed up as insights. Finally, if he is willing to combine the slogan with the thought that ritual is in any case archaeologically invisible, tell me plainly whether his position is then immune to excavation — and if it is, what that costs him as a claim about the past.
