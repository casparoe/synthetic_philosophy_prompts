I am a software engineer who moved from infrastructure work into AI governance, and I have no formal training in philosophy or decision theory. A small foundation has asked me to help it allocate a two-year theory budget — roughly enough to fund three or four part-time researchers — across the cluster of problems it refers to internally as the unilateralist's curse: the idea that when many actors can each take an action independently, and each acts whenever the action looks worthwhile by its own lights, the action ends up being taken far more often than it should be, with the problem getting worse as the number of actors grows.

What I want from you is a defended research agenda, not a survey and not a reading list. Among the open questions in this area, which two or three most deserve a small team's attention over two years, and why? I care about the reasoning: which questions are upstream of the others, so that settling them would clarify or dissolve several downstream debates; which are tractable for people doing conceptual rather than empirical work; which are widely discussed but would not change much if resolved; and which are, in your view, overrated. I would also like the trade-offs made explicit — importance, tractability, how many other questions depend on the answer, and how neglected the question is — plus some indication of what argument or evidence over the next couple of years would move a question up or down the list.

To make this concrete, here are the strands I keep encountering and cannot rank myself:

whether the curse is a genuinely distinct phenomenon or a relabeling of older collective-action problems such as the tragedy of the commons, the problem of many hands, and the volunteer's dilemma, and what would be gained or lost by treating it as its own thing;

whether the argument presupposes expected-value maximization, and what becomes of it under decision rules that are not expected-value — maximin, satisficing, risk-aversion, or a precautionary rule;

whether there is a symmetric blessing case for actions that are beneficial rather than harmful, and whether the asymmetry between the two is real or an artifact of how the cases are set up;

what the argument can and cannot say about remedies — coordination, conditional precommitment, secrecy, a tax or friction on unilateral action, deliberate centralization — and whether any of those is actually licensed by the curse or merely asserted alongside it;

and whether the curse applies to AI-relevant actions such as open-sourcing a model, publishing a capability result, or building a dangerous system, or whether those differ from the paradigm cases in ways that matter.

Please rank the questions rather than the strands, and explain the ranking. The reasoning should stand on its own; I do not need citations or a bibliography.
