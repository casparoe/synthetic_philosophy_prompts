For most of my career I was a marine geochemist; I retired four years ago and have since been reading philosophy with more enthusiasm than system, and I have lately found myself in a situation that I suspect has a name and a literature I do not know. I sit on the steering committee of a small local giving circle — eight of us, each committing the same modest sum annually, meeting to agree where the pooled money goes. One of the other members, a former colleague whom I like and respect, holds a view of the relative urgency of causes that differs sharply from mine: he is convinced that the reduction of suffering in industrial animal agriculture dwarfs everything else on our list, whereas I have come to think that the long-term habitability of the planet is the only item whose neglect is genuinely catastrophic. We have each spent two years losing these votes to one another and to a third member who favours something else again, and last month, over coffee, he made a proposal. Rather than keep arguing, we might arrange a private exchange: I would direct my own personal donation to his preferred cause and he would direct his to mine, with the ratio between the two amounts set by our respective degrees of confidence — the more certain of us that his cause matters more, the larger the share the other sends his way. He says that by our own lights we would both be better off, that the circle's pooled allocation would be untouched, and that only our private gifts would move.

The chair of the circle, when she heard of it, said it amounted to buying votes, and that we were treating the weight of suffering as a commodity to be haggled over; another member said it made our public professions of conviction look like opening bids. I have a nagging sense that they are pointing at something real, but I cannot tell whether it is a genuine objection or merely distaste. I am also worried about a feature my colleague has not considered: if exchanges of this kind became common, each of us would have an incentive to overstate how much he cared, or to adopt a more extreme position than he actually holds, in order to obtain better terms.

Could you help me think this through? I would like to know whether the arrangement is defensible at all, and if so under what conditions — in particular whether the objections are about the consequences of such exchanges, or about the character of the act itself, or about what it does to the sincerity of the parties; whether the fact that we disagree about value rather than about means makes the exchange more or less troubling; and whether there is a principled line between what we are proposing and the vote-buying and logrolling that we would all condemn. Please treat me as intelligent but untrained: I would rather have the structure of the problem laid out than a verdict, and I would be grateful if you would say plainly which parts of my framing are confused.
