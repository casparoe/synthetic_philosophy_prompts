I sit on the "responsible sourcing" committee at the regional grocery chain where I work — about 400 stores, and our public promise is that we don't sell anything from a supplier that fails our animal-welfare standard, except the standard is just a five-rung ladder the committee invented four years ago over two afternoons and nobody has really revisited since. Every year we've voted some category up a rung: cage-free eggs, crate-free pork, that slow-growth chicken certification. Same argument every time, and it's a good one — the next rung costs a few cents a unit and is plainly better for the animals, so we say yes and feel fine about it. I've been the one arguing hardest for the last two rungs, which is part of why this is eating at me. Last week the supplier walked us through the top rung for the chicken line, which means dropping a supplier who is something like 12% of our volume and taking a margin hit we genuinely can't absorb this year, and every one of us, me included, wanted to say no.

Then a colleague said the thing I can't get out of my head: if rung 4 beats rung 3, and 3 beats 2, then rung 5 beats rung 1, and we've been voting on exactly that reasoning for four years — so a no now isn't a judgment about chickens, it's the current quarter protecting itself. Unless the ladder was never a ladder, because 'better than' stops behaving like a real ordering somewhere along it and we've been pretending otherwise. I half remember this being what spectrum arguments are about, and that the two standard escapes are either that betterness comes in jumps, so a small improvement stops counting as an improvement at all past some level, or that two options can be 'on a par' — not better, not worse, not equal — which keeps transitivity by refusing to line the whole thing up into a single series. I can't tell whether either of those is a real position or just a fancy way of saying we don't feel like it.

The board meets on the 14th and I have to circulate the memo on Friday. So write it for me: a concrete recommendation on the chicken line — approve, decline, or approve on a timetable — and if we decline, what we actually say to the supplier, plus the general rule for where the ladder stops and why that rule isn't the status quo wearing a spreadsheet. And please take my colleague seriously rather than patting him on the head: if he's right that we're just being inconsistent, say so and tell me what we then owe the animals; if he's wrong, name the premise he's smuggling in. Write it for trustees who've read the charter and not much else — no reading list, just the arguement. One of them has a philosophy degree and will absolutely check.
