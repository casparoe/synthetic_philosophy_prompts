I'm a novelist and the token "arts person" on my city's Percent-for-Art panel — we dole out the 1% of capital budgets set aside for civic buildings, and I usually just vote with the architects. Next Thursday we pick one of three finalists for the new transit hub, and I'm writing the recommendation because I missed the last meeting and this is my pennance.

The mess: our ordinance says the money is for "the acquisition or commission of works of art of permanent value for public spaces," and nobody on the panel is sure what that phrase is meant to rule out.

A: a bronze monument to a local labour organizer, by a sculptor with a solid CV. Expensive, obviously "art," boring design, and the neighborhood association has already emailed us twice.

B: a collective wants to buy the derelict shop on the corner of the site, keep it running as a real shop with two paid staff for eight years, and call the whole arrangement the artwork. No object survives; the budget is mostly wages and stock. They say the piece is "the ongoing life of the shop." Half the panel loves it, half thinks we'd be laundering a small-business subsidy through the art budget, and the solicitor has quietly asked whether the money can legally pay salaries.

C: use an image model to generate a fresh mural for the hoarding every month for two years, credited to the collective that writes the prompts. They're articulate about it. I keep flipping on whether there's an artist in there at all or just a committee with a subscription.

Write me the memo that goes in the file before Thursday — the thing the solicitor and the council will read if someone challenges the award. Don't give me a lecture on the definition of art in the abstract; I know the argument doesn't settle, and what I need is a decision I can defend in a council meeting.

Sections, in this order: "Bottom line" (two sentences — who wins, what happens to the other two); "What the ordinance actually forces us to decide" (one paragraph, and say plainly if it forces nothing); "Best case for each" (three short paragraphs, one per finalist — give B its strongest form, I don't want a strawman); "What worries me about each"; "My recommendation, and the objection I can't answer"; "Thursday's motion" (exact wording, timeline, and what we tell the two we reject). Under two pages, I have to read it aloud.
