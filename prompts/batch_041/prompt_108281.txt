I sit on the research ethics committee of a mid-sized teaching hospital and its affiliated university, and I am the person who sees consent documents first — informed consent forms, recruitment scripts, and increasingly the click-through screens of app-based studies — before they reach the full board. We take up roughly fifteen new protocols a month plus amendments, most on a two-week clock, and the board largely defers to my write-up: when I say the consent process is sound the study usually proceeds, and when I flag it the study is held, redesigned, or killed. What I do not have is a procedure. I have instincts and the standard three-legged stool of information, voluntariness, and capacity, and the stool is silent precisely where I need it most — when the disclosure is complete, the signature genuine, and the consent still feels hollow. I need you to turn the philosophy of consent into something I can run at my desk in about twenty minutes with the documents in front of me and no philosopher in the room; if a step requires a specialist to execute, it is not a step. Fix the order of the questions, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops with a no, where it must hand the decision back to the board because the question is one of values rather than facts, and who has to sign off on what.

Several distinctions should be built into the machinery rather than bolted on, because they are where my instincts keep failing me. I want the procedure to separate consent as an act — a communicative performance that either succeeds or misfires — from consent as a standing state, a permission that can be void from the start, exhausted by time or by use, or silently invalidated when the study's material terms drift; and to say which of my questions are answered under which heading. I want it to distinguish a threat from a merely unattractive offer, while admitting that the baseline against which that line is drawn is itself a normative choice, so the procedure must declare the baseline it is using and identify who is entitled to set it. It should take a position on the disagreement between those who call a proposal coercive only when it wrongfully bypasses the will and those who call it coercive whenever it leaves the person no acceptable alternative, and say what changes if the other side is right. It needs to handle the case where the participant cannot be informed because the thing consented to does not yet exist — broad consent for unspecified future uses — and to say what, if anything, legitimately substitutes for information there, and why that substitute is adequate or is not. And it must be able to name what I have started calling consent-washing: a process that satisfies every box and transfers no real authority.

Then run the procedure, in full, on three cases: a pharmacokinetic study paying several thousand dollars to healthy volunteers with unstable housing for ten days of inpatient residence; a quality-improvement project that will later be published as research, where patients are told only that they are receiving standard care and the team seeks a waiver of consent with debriefing afterwards; and a biobank amendment adding whole-genome sequencing and open-ended future uses to a cohort that originally consented to a single disease study. At least one of the three should come out in a way I will not like, and I want you to say so plainly rather than softening it. Close by telling me where the procedure will misfire, which of its verdicts are judgment calls dressed up as procedure, and what it simply cannot settle — I do not need a bibliography; I need the thing itself.
