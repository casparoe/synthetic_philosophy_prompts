The question, verbatim: "Set out Dennett's three stances — physical, design, and intentional — and explain what orders them. Then explain what makes a pattern real on his account, and how that account is supposed to answer the objection that the intentional stance is a useful fiction. Then address three cases: (a) whether the design stance can be used to explain the behaviour of a system that had no designer; (b) whether every system whose behaviour can be predicted from the intentional stance thereby has beliefs; (c) whether a large language model that is a convincing conversational partner has beliefs, in Dennett's sense. Where your answer turns on a distinction, say what work the distinction is doing." The rubric is worth twenty points, four points per criterion: (i) accurate exposition of the three stances and of what orders them; (ii) an accurate account of what makes a pattern real, including the roles of compression, prediction, and counterfactuals, and the sense in which the reality of a pattern is not the same as its being interest-free; (iii) a treatment of the fiction objection that distinguishes being able to take a stance toward a system from the stance paying off, and that uses that distinction consistently when it comes to ascribing beliefs; (iv) the design stance and the no-designer case, with the evolutionary application handled correctly; (v) the language-model case, judged by whether the student is using Dennett's criterion for realness or a proxy for it. The professor's conversion is 18–20 A, 16–17 B+, 14–15 B, 12–13 C+, 10–11 C, and below 10 unsatisfactory.

Here is the answer I have to grade, transcribed exactly as written: "The three stances are ways of predicting what a system will do. From the physical stance you use what you know about the system's constitution and the laws governing it; from the design stance you assume the system was designed to behave in a certain way and that it is functioning as designed, and you predict accordingly; from the intentional stance you assume the system is a rational agent, attribute to it the beliefs and desires it ought to have given its situation and its purposes, and predict that it will do what those beliefs and desires make it rational to do. Dennett orders them: the physical stance is the most basic, the design stance builds on it, and the intentional stance is the least basic. The ordering is not a ranking of what is more real. His answer to the charge that this is fiction-mongering is that patterns are real when they earn their keep. If a short description of a system's behaviour predicts it better than chance and supports counterfactuals — if I can say what it would have done had things been otherwise — then there is a fact of the matter about the pattern, and the beliefs and desires are part of that pattern rather than decorations we add to it. The analogy is the centre of gravity: a body does not contain a little point labelled 'centre of gravity', but statements about where its centre of gravity is are true or false. I take the point about interests to be that the pattern is really there for anyone equipped to see it, but that which patterns we bother to pick out depends on what we want to explain, so realness is not the same thing as mind-independence. I think the objection can be answered, but that the answer has a cost. Any system whose behaviour can be predicted from the intentional stance is a system with beliefs and desires; if the predictions work, the beliefs are real. So the thermostat believes that the room is too cold, the chess program wants to win, and my neighbour believes it is going to rain. The cost is that this looks inflationary: if taking the stance is enough, then almost everything has beliefs. I think Dennett accepts this and says that beliefs come in degrees of richness. The place where his account strains is the design stance. When I predict that a system will behave in a certain way because it was designed to do that, I seem to be presupposing a designer. That is harmless for a thermostat or a chess engine, which really were designed, but it looks like trouble for the biological case, where Dennett wants to use the design stance to explain why an organism has a trait. If design explanations require a designer, then using the design stance about organisms commits him to creationism, which cannot be right, and he does not say enough about how to avoid it. For a large language model that is a convincing conversational partner, the answer follows from the above: if the best predictions of its replies come from attributing beliefs about the conversation to it, then it has beliefs in just the same sense that a person does. What differs is that its beliefs are less stable and that it cannot be held responsible for them."
