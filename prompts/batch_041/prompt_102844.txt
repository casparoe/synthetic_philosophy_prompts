I'm in a graduate reading group that has spent the term on welfare economics and its critics, and we keep discovering that we mean different things by "interpersonal comparison" without noticing it until someone says something that turns out to presuppose a whole position. I'd like to build a diagnostic instrument we can use at the start of next term: a decision tree that takes a participant from a minimal starting commitment to a named position on the comparability of utility, together with a grid of the underlying logical space so we can see which cells are actually occupied and which are empty.

Build it out of the logical space rather than out of a list of names. The two axes I want treated as independent are, first, measurability — whether a person's welfare is merely rankable, rankable with comparable differences or units, or carries a natural zero and comparable ratios — and second, comparability — whether we can rank one person's level against another's, whether we can compare units or differences across persons, both, or neither. The tree should make vivid that these come apart: the von Neumann–Morgenstern apparatus delivers cardinality within a person with no interpersonal comparability whatsoever, while the ability to rank A's level against B's gives comparability without cardinality, so no branch node may treat "cardinal" and "comparable" as the same thing. Add a third branch for what is being compared and on what ground — hedonic or mental states, informed or idealized preferences (including the extended-sympathy and veil-of-ignorance routes), objective lists or functionings, or a behavioural measure such as willingness to pay — and a fourth for whether the comparison is meant to report a fact, to be an idealized construction, or to function merely as an "as if" modelling device. Every node must be phrased as a question someone can answer without already knowing which position it leads to; no node may be "are you a utilitarian?".

The leaves must include at least the following: the ordinal, non-comparable framework within which social choice runs into impossibility; classical utilitarianism with a common unit and no common zero; aggregation from equiprobable positions behind a veil of ignorance; maximin on an index of primary goods; the worst-off-weighted view that needs only an ordinal ranking of levels; bargaining solutions that use cardinal utilities invariant under independent affine transformations; a capability or functionings view; and a common ratio scale of the sort health economists use for quality-adjusted life years. Where the tree genuinely cannot separate two leaves — I expect at least one pair of aggregation rules that differ in the formula but share the same comparability assumptions — flag it as such rather than papering over it with a node that pretends to distinguish them. Close with the grid: measurability on one axis, comparability on the other, each occupied cell naming its occupants, each empty cell diagnosed as incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. Assume graduate-level competence in both economics and philosophy, keep the tree to eight to twelve branch nodes, and give each leaf one sentence on its most awkward commitment.

Format: end with a single final line beginning "Verdict:" that names, in a short phrase, the one branch question which does the most sorting — the one that eliminates the most positions — followed by a one-clause reason.
