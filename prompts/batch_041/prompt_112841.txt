I'm a second-year PhD student in phil of science, and I'm sitting in on a confirmation-theory seminar where Goodman's grue keeps coming up. I get the basic setup — the predicate that applies to emeralds examined before some fixed future time T just in case they're green, and to ones examined after T just in case they're blue, with bleen as the mirror image — and I get that "all emeralds are green" and "all emeralds are grue" both fit everything we've observed so far equally well. What's bugging me is this. Grue is obviously a cheater predicate: the definition mentions a particular time, T. Green doesn't mention any particular time. So isn't the whole paradox just an artifact of letting these time-indexed, gerrymandered predicates into the language in the first place? If we laid down a rule that the predicates we're allowed to project have to be "position-independent" — no reference to particular times or places, nothing whose meaning flips at a stipulated moment — grue gets excluded by fiat and induction is fine again. A friend told me this is too quick, something about how green is itself definable in terms of grue and bleen so the time-reference can't be what's doing the work, but I couldn't get him to spell it out and I'm not sure if the reply is decisive or just a cute symmetry trick that doesn't really engage the objection.

Can you actually work through whether the ban-the-time-indexed-predicates move survives, or where it breaks? (2) if the difference between green and grue isn't the time-reference, then what actually is the difference that's supposed to make one projectible and the other not? and (3) how does entrenchment fit in — is it an independent answer or does it just rename the thing we were trying to explain?
