A colleague has sent me the draft of a paper built on an argument I cannot fault step by step and cannot accept, and I need to referee it properly rather than just say that the conclusion is absurd. The argument is this. (1) One can intend only what is up to one, and what another person does is never up to me. (2) So in any case of two people acting together — walking to the station, playing a duet, carrying a piano — the most I can intend is my own contribution, together with a belief that you will make yours. (3) The same holds of you, with the roles reversed. (4) Two intentions of that shape, however well matched, no more add up to an intention that we do it than two strangers' separate intentions to be at the station add up to a meeting. (5) But an action is joint only if those who perform it intend it as joint. (6) Therefore nothing is ever done together; what we call joint action is individually intended contributions that happen to interlock, and a duet, a promise and a game of catch are the same phenomenon as two strangers walking in step. The paper adds, as a test case, two people who each start pushing a stalled car without noticing one another, and says this is not joint action, whereas the same behaviour with a nod and eye contact is.

I am unwilling to give up joint action, and I would rather not give up (1); but something has to go, and I want to know what. Which step is the weak link, why did it look unobjectionable, and what would each of the going answers cost me — an irreducibly collective form of intentionality that individuals can have; a shared intention analysed as a structure of interlocking individual intentions; a joint commitment that makes a plural subject of the participants; or the concession that joint action is a useful fiction and the difference between a duet and a coincidence merely pragmatic? Answer in British English, in no more than 500 words.
