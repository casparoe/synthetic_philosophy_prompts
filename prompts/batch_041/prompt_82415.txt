You are a satirical essayist who happens to be a competent game theorist and a careful reader of the technical AI-safety literature. You write deadpan: no winking asides, no narrator stepping in to tell the reader what to think, no scare quotes around your own claims. Work through the strategic logic below before you draft — a short bulleted plan (three to five lines) of the argument's structure and of the exact point at which that structure becomes uncomfortable is welcome, followed by the piece itself.

The piece is an op-ed, published in a near-future industry trade outlet, written by the fictional chief strategy officer of a fictional frontier lab. Roughly 750 words: headline, standfirst, body.

Assume the following world, and treat these as the materials you are satirizing rather than as background to be explained to the reader:

The alignment tax. The cost of safety work — evaluations, red-teaming, interpretability, staged deployment, the decision not to ship — measured either in capability or in calendar time. Nothing guarantees the tax is positive; some safety tooling may pay for itself by making training more legible or more efficient. Those two senses of the tax, one paid in capability and one paid in delay, are not the same quantity, and your speaker should be permitted to slide between them, because his argument depends on the slide.

The competitiveness constraint. A lab's influence over how the technology is built and deployed depends on its relative position, not on its absolute quality. If a safety practice slows you while a rival skips it, the practice can be self-defeating even if it is good in itself: you lose, and the reckless lab wins. So a safety practice must survive competitive pressure, not merely be defensible in the abstract. Structurally, safety investment is a public good with a private cost, and credible commitment among rivals requires verifying their claims, which is precisely what is hard.

The invented mechanism. A fictional multilateral agreement, signed by every major lab, that converts safety work into a capability-equivalent debit against each signatory's compute budget, with a mutual pledge not to deploy past a defined capability threshold. The pledge contains an escape clause: any signatory may proceed past the threshold without penalty once any non-signatory is assessed to be within one quarter of it. Assessment is performed by a panel staffed by the signatories' own employees.

Requirements:

1. Your speaker must be internally consistent. Every move he makes must be one a competent strategist could actually make, and he must never break character. If an objection surfaces, he answers it in his own voice rather than dismissing it, and his answer must be at least as good as the objection.
2. His thesis should be a reductio: the honest conclusion of his own reasoning, followed straight-faced to a place a sympathetic reader will recoil from. Do not soften the ending, and do not have him concede.
3. Include at least one worked toy example with numbers, showing how a unilateral safety investment costs relative position between two labs of unequal speed.
4. Include one quoted remark from a fictional colleague or critic, invented and clearly in-universe. Attribute no quotation, statistic, view, or document to any real person, lab, company, government, or publication, and invent no citations, papers, or reports.
5. Do not rely on recalling specific real papers; where you need a technical detail you are unsure of, describe it generically rather than naming it.
6. A specialist should be able to identify the load-bearing equivocation in the argument and find the piece fair — that is, the absurdity should come from following a real line of reasoning rather than from a strawman.
7. The final line should leave the reader uneasy rather than reassured.
