Final exam, question 3 (20 marks)

Moral foundations theory is often summarised in the claim that political opponents are not morally worse than one another, only morally different.

(a) Set out the theory's central descriptive claims as Haidt and his collaborators develop them, identifying the foundations, the individualizing/binding distinction, and the pattern of differences the research reports among liberals, conservatives, and libertarians.

(b) Three claims are sometimes said to follow from the theory: (i) moral judgment is generally intuitive, with reasoning typically arriving after the verdict; (ii) the theory shows that no political morality is objectively correct; (iii) the theory gives us reason to regard one side's moral outlook as more adequate than the other's. For each claim, say whether it follows from the descriptive findings, and explain why or why not.

(c) What, if anything, does the theory contribute to the epistemology of moral belief — that is, to whether we should be confident in our own moral judgments?

Student's answer

Moral foundations theory (MFT) begins from a claim about how moral judgment is produced rather than about what is true. Haidt's social intuitionist model holds that judgment is fast and intuitive, with conscious reasoning arriving afterwards to justify a verdict that has already been reached; reasoning is in this sense epiphenomenal, and the reasons people offer are confabulated rather than causes of their judgments. The moral dumbfounding studies are the paradigm case: subjects who condemn a harmless act of incest are unable to say what is wrong with it.

The descriptive core of the theory is that moral judgment is organised by a small set of foundations, each with an evolutionary history and a characteristic set of concerns: care/harm, fairness/cheating, loyalty/betrayal, authority/subversion, and sanctity/degradation, with liberty/oppression added later as a sixth. MFT can be read as a refinement of Kohlberg's developmental theory, replacing his six stages with five foundations. It descends from Shweder's three ethics of autonomy, community, and divinity, and it treats the foundations as innate in the sense of being organised in advance of experience, while allowing that cultures elaborate them very differently.

Care and fairness are individualizing foundations, concerned with the protection of persons; loyalty, authority, and sanctity are binding foundations, concerned with the cohesion of groups. The research reports that liberals respond strongly to the individualizing pair and weakly to the binding three, that conservatives respond strongly to all of them, and that libertarians are distinctive in responding strongly to liberty/oppression and weakly to the rest. The suggestion that conservatives are simply less moral than liberals is therefore not supported; the more accurate picture is that conservatives have a wider moral palate and liberals a narrower one.

Of the three claims: (i) follows from social intuitionism. (ii) also follows, in the end. If the foundations are innate and every culture's morality is an elaboration of them, then there is no vantage point outside the foundations from which one morality could be ranked above another, and each is valid relative to the foundations that generate it. (iii) is more interesting than it looks. MFT does give us a reason to regard the conservative outlook as the more adequate one in one respect: because the binding foundations do real work in sustaining institutions, and a morality that engages all five is thicker than one that engages only two, the conservative palate is the more complete moral outlook — though this is not to say that conservative positions are correct on any particular issue.

As to (c), the theory's contribution to moral epistemology is mainly negative. If my political convictions are the output of foundations I did not choose and cannot step outside, then my confidence in them is unwarranted. MFT can explain why we disagree; it cannot tell us which of us is right.
