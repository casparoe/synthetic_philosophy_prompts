I'm a backend engineer with no formal training in philosophy, and I've been trying to make sense of a framework a colleague brought up during a long-running argument we keep having on Slack about whether the language models we build are really thinking. The framework, as he explained it, sorts minds into four ascending kinds: Darwinian creatures, whose behavior is fixed by natural selection; Skinnerian creatures, which learn from trial and error and reinforcement; Popperian creatures, which can run a hypothesis through their own heads before acting on it; and Gregorian creatures, which think with tools they inherit from culture rather than from their own genes. He laid these out as if they were rungs on a ladder of increasing mental sophistication, each rung naming a distinct sort of animal, and I've been trying ever since to work out where a modern large language model sits on it.

My working assumption is that an LLM has to be the most sophisticated case of all, since it deals in language and symbols, which feels like the top of any such ladder; so I'd guess it counts as a Gregorian creature and is therefore a higher and more intelligent kind of mind than a dog, a crow, or a chimpanzee. I'm also puzzled, incidentally, as to why the most advanced of the four categories would be named after a medieval pope. Could you tell me which of the four kinds a current language model actually belongs to, and whether being a Gregorian creature would make it a higher kind of mind than the animals on the lower rungs? I'm less interested in a ranking than in whether the scheme itself works the way I've been assuming.
