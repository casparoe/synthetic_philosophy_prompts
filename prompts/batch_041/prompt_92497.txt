I spent thirty-five years as an experimental molecular spectroscopist — lasers, cryogenics, and a great deal of plumbing — and retired two years ago into what I had always promised myself would be a serious reading of philosophy. I have been working through philosophy of physics with a small seminar group, and the topic that has me thoroughly stuck is Bell's theorem, or rather the relation between the theorem and the experiments said to have settled it. I can follow the derivation perfectly well; what I cannot get straight is what exactly the laboratory work has established about the world, as opposed to about theories. I should like you to take one specific episode and work it through: the loophole-free tests reported in 2015, in which the detection and communication loopholes were closed in a single run — one of these used entangled electron spins in diamond separated by more than a kilometre, with the analyser settings chosen by fast random generators, while others used photons and high-efficiency detectors — together with the later variants in which the settings were fixed by physical processes that had been under way long before the experiment began, such as light from distant astronomical sources. Treat that body of work as the case and Bell's notion of local causality as the lens.

I want the analysis to run in both directions. First, from the lens onto the case: state precisely which set of assumptions must hold for a Bell-type inequality to be derivable, and be careful to separate local causality from the mere absence of superluminal signalling, since I have one colleague who insists the experiments show that the world is non-local, and another who insists that nothing can travel faster than light and therefore that nothing has been shown at all. Then give me the mapping I keep failing to find in the secondary literature: for each of the serious surviving programmes — a deterministic hidden-variable theory of the de Broglie–Bohm kind, a dynamical-collapse theory, an Everettian or many-worlds reading, a retrocausal model, and a superdeterministic one — say which single assumption of the derivation it abandons, using the standard division between the assumption that one wing's outcome is independent of the distant setting and the assumption that it is independent of the distant outcome. I want that mapping exact rather than gestural, and I want you to say plainly what the experiments cannot do: identify the failing assumption, demonstrate any influence, or eliminate an interpretation.

Second, from the case back onto the theory. What did a competent experiment have to guarantee — spacelike separation of the setting choice from the distant outcome, sufficiently high detection efficiency, settings genuinely independent of the system being measured — and what would a null result have vindicated: would correlations coming in below the bound have restored the incompleteness argument of 1935, or only a piece of it? Then tell me where the lens goes silent: whether local causality is even well defined for a theory whose fundamental ontology is not a set of events with probabilities, and whether the theorem constrains an interpretation that buys itself out by adding structure, such as a preferred foliation, at the cost of Lorentz invariance. Finally, I would like your view on the philosophical oddity that started all this. An argument that reads to me as a priori — a constraint on the shape of any theory meeting certain conditions — has been converted into an experimental programme, and the 2022 Nobel Prize in Physics went to three of the principals. Is this a case of what one might call experimental metaphysics, and if so, what exactly was put to the test: a theorem about theories, or an assumption about the world? Continuous prose, roughly two thousand words, with a compact table for the mapping in the middle section; no reading list, since what I want is the reasoning.
