Okay, I've got a pile of gut reactions to a family of cases and I can't get them to line up under one rule, so I'm throwing them at you. In all of them I'm stranded on a desert highway and some guy in a truck is deciding whether to give me a lift into town. In all of them I stipulate that nobody will ever find out what I do afterward, I'll never see him again, and there's no way for him to collect anything or retaliate. So don't tell me it's about my reputation.

1. He says he'll only take me if I promise to mail him $200 once I'm home. I promise, and I mean it. Two weeks later, at my kitchen table, envelope in hand: I mail it. This one doesn't feel up for grabs at all.

2. Same guy, same truck, except this time he doesn't ask for a promise. He says he only picks up people who'd send the money even if they'd never promised anything. I get in. When I'm home I still feel like I owe him the $200 and should send it. Which bugs me, because now there's no promise to point at and I can't say what's generating the obligation.

3. Before he makes up his mind I can swallow a pill that will make it psycologically impossible for me to break any promise for the next month. The lift is worth maybe $50 to me and the promise is for $200. I take the pill, promise, and pay. And I want to say that taking the pill was the smart move — even though the entire function of the pill is to make my future self do something at the kitchen table that my future self would otherwise call a straight waste of $200.

4. Twist on 3 that I hate: the pill doesn't care about circumstances, so the future self it locks in would also pay if, by then, I'm broke and the $200 was going to be my kid's medicine. I still think swallowing the pill at the roadside is the right call. I'm not comfortable with that.

5. Halfway to town I find out what he's running from — he hurt someone, or the truck bed is full of stolen tools, take your pick. Do I still mail the $200? Gut: yes, I send it, and I don't like my own answer, because my stated reason for paying in 1 and 2 was that he did me a good turn and it'd be rotten to stiff him, and I'm not in the mood to do him favors anymore. Something else must be doing the work and I don't know what.

6. The one that actually breaks me: he says he'll take me only if I *beleive* right now — sincerely, not as a promise — that I'll pay him. He reads people well and pretending won't work. I know that by the time I'm home the belief will have evaporated. So, no ride. But I also think the right answer here is to be the kind of person who'd pay, and I don't think you can decide to be that kind of person on the spot, which seems like a bad feature for an ethics to have.

7. The one I keep flip-flopping on: instead of $200 to him, he asks me to promise to give the next stranded stranger a lift. I've never picked up a hitchhiker in my life and probably never will. Do I owe him a lift I'll never be asked to give? I genuinely don't know, and my answer changes with my mood.

My own guess at a principle, for you to kick around: do whatever the character you'd have chosen for yourself before you knew which of these situations you'd end up in would do. But it doesn't explain why 1 and 2 come out the same when only one of them has a promise in it, it gives a too-easy answer in 7, and I suspect it can't tell 3 from 5.

So: is there a principle, or a short list of candidates, that gets all seven — or six of them, with an honest note about which one has to go? What does it predict for a case I haven't listed, like the guy who wants the $200 in cash up front, before he starts the engine, so there's no promise and no credit at all? And is the answer different depending on whether we're asking what's rational or what's right? Because in 3 I keep reaching for "rational," in 1 and 2 I keep reaching for "should," and I can't tell if that's me being sloppy or the cases being two different things.
