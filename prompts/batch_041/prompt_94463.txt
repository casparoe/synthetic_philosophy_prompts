After thirty-five years as an experimental physicist, most of them spent trying to design instruments that could actually discriminate between hypotheses I knew perfectly well were underdetermined by the data I had, I have retired and talked my way into a graduate seminar in philosophy of science. The instructor has asked me to run a full session, and I have chosen to build it around Bongard problems: the puzzles in which a solver is shown a row of simple figures on the left and a row on the right, with no explanation of either, and must work out what separates them. I encountered them years ago in a pattern-recognition context and never forgot the particular discomfort of them. What I want the students to feel, rather than be told, comes in three stages: that the same handful of examples is consistent with an embarrassing number of different rules; that among those rules some feel natural and intended while others feel like cheats, even though the examples themselves do nothing to rank them; and that one well-chosen new example can annihilate a hypothesis that had come to seem inevitable, which is a loss people take personally.

Please design the session for me: ninety minutes, sixteen participants, a room with a projector and enough table space to seat them in groups of four. I want it built as a game rather than a demonstration, with teams, a scoring scheme that needs no prizes beyond bragging rights, a sequence of rounds, and at least one point where the ground shifts and a rule the room has confidently settled on turns out not to be the one that was intended. The materials should be things I can actually make the night before: index cards, printed sheets, a whiteboard. Nobody should hear the words underdetermination or grue until the debrief. Along with the rules and a minute-by-minute timeline, I need a debrief that maps specific moments in the game onto the philosophical points, showing where the underdetermination actually surfaced, where the pull of the natural rule came from, and why the disjunctive cheat rule was formally just as good as the elegant one. I also want an honest account of the predictable failures and what I should do when each occurs: the team that finds the intended rule in ninety seconds and disengages, the team that refuses to commit to any rule at all, and the student who objects that the exercise is rigged because somebody had to choose the figures in the first place.

Finally, tell me plainly what this game cannot do. My worry is that in a game the intended rule is guaranteed to exist, because I invented it, and that students will therefore leave thinking underdetermination is a small-scale annoyance that more data or a better experiment would cure, when the argument I actually care about is that no accumulation of data closes the gap by itself and that in real science there is no designer whose intention can serve as the tiebreaker. I would like you to say which parts of the idea the facilitator has to supply from the front of the room, since the activity will not deliver them. Write everything in American English.
