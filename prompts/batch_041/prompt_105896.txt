I sit on the research advisory committee of a small foundation that funds quantitative social science, and on Thursday we vote on a renewal that has become, awkwardly, two decisions at once: whether to keep funding a lab that has built a structural-demographic model of political instability in the cliodynamics tradition, and whether to let it publish the forward-looking half of the work. The model treats a polity as a dynamical system driven by three coupled pressures -- immiseration of the general population, overproduction of elites, and fiscal distress of the state -- fits them to several centuries of European and American history, and outputs what the team calls a political stress index at annual resolution. The retrospective work is done. The renewal adds a ten-year forecast of political stress for roughly twenty countries plus a public-facing dashboard, and because our grant agreement gives the committee a review right over public claims about identifiable countries, the release question lands on my desk rather than the communications office's. The committee is split. The historians among us think this is numerology with error bars and want a clean decline; the quantitative people answer that a model which was never told about the crises it flags is doing something real, and that refusing to fund a forecast because it might be misused is a failure of nerve dressed up as prudence. I have to draft the decision memo: a concrete recommendation -- fund as proposed, fund with conditions, or decline -- with the conditions and a timetable spelled out if conditions are the answer, and the losing side's case stated at its strongest rather than as a straw man.

Two things keep sliding off the table in our meetings, and I want the memo to take them seriously. First, whether this is a prediction at all or a scenario generator, and whether that distinction should change what we fund and how it is released. The team's own write-up concedes that its estimated relationships are not policy-invariant: if a finance ministry read the dashboard and tightened spending, it would be altering the system the model describes. A colleague of mine who used to work at a central bank says this is the Lucas critique in historical dress; I suspect he is half right, though I can't tell whether the half that's wrong matters for our decision. Second, whether publishing country-level risk scores does something to the countries scored. Such scores would be quotable by officials, creditors, and opposition movements, and might become premises in decisions rather than descriptions of them -- as likely to help avert a crisis as to help bring one on. I don't want the memo to treat either point as a reason to bury the work; I want them treated as design constraints. What should be published, at what resolution, with what language about uncertainty, and subject to what review, given that the object here is a reflexive social system rather than a weather system?

For what it's worth, I have read the retrospective validation chapter twice and still cannot tell whether the fit is genuinely impressive or the predictable residue of a team searching over enough specifications that something was bound to line up; if that judgment is doing most of the work in my decision, the memo should say so plainly rather than dress a statistical hunch as a philosophical one. And if I have mis-framed the task -- if our charter in fact lets us split the funding decision from the release decision cleanly, or if the live question here is something more mundane like ordinary peer review -- then tell me that instead of writing a careful memo to a badly posed question. Where you do take a position, mark how much of it is confident judgment and how much is a guess I should go check. No need for citations; I'll fill those in myself.
