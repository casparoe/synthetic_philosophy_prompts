Someone in my reading group produced the following argument, and I cannot decide whether it is a reductio of one of its premises or a genuine result, because it seems to dissolve not only weakness of will but a great deal besides.

(1) At any moment, an agent's ranking of future options can be represented by a discount function over their utility.
(2) For most agents that function is hyperbolic, so that a smaller-sooner reward can outrank a larger-later one while both are distant, and lose to it as the smaller-sooner approaches; the ranking reverses with no change in the agent's information or in the options available.
(3) If nothing but the passage of time changes between the earlier and the later ranking, then the later ranking is not a corruption of the earlier one but simply what the agent now judges best.
(4) Weakness of will requires acting against one's own better judgment at the time of action.
(5) At the time of action the agent's better judgment is whatever ranking it then holds, and by (2) and (3) that ranking favours the nearer reward.
(6) So the agent acts in accordance with its better judgment, and no such reversal is a case of weakness of will.
(7) Since any case can be described in this way, weakness of will is either impossible or collapses into ordinary preference change.

I would like to keep (1) and (2), since I think the empirical model is sound, and I am uneasy about (4), though I do not see how to dispense with it. Which step do you reject, why did it look compelling, and what does rejecting it cost? In particular, say what each of the following must concede: a view that identifies better judgment with the strongest current desire; a view that treats the agent as a succession of time-slices, each with its own preferences; and a view on which an agent can bind itself in advance to a plan it will later want to abandon. If the argument is valid, tell me which premise is cheapest to surrender and what the rest of the theory looks like afterwards.
