In our school's mock election we use a ballot copied from the real one, and two features of it puzzle me. The ballot lists only candidates who have already qualified, so a voter who finds none of them acceptable has nowhere to say so; a ballot left blank is recorded as invalid, as though the voter had made a mistake rather than a judgment, and such a voter is simply left to pick the least bad name on the list. Yet the same body of rules makes it a crime to pay a voter for their vote, on the ground that a vote is not a commodity. We are taught that voting is a right and a way of expressing what we want for the country, but a system that cannot register the expression "none of these" and treats silence as an error appears to presuppose a narrower theory of what a vote is. Please reconstruct that theory: is a vote a selection from a fixed menu, an act of expression, the discharge of a duty, or something else again? Say which parts of the practice genuinely require it and which would be licensed by any of several rival theories, and then tell me what would have to change if my club adopted the rule I have proposed, that a blank or spoiled ballot be counted and published as an explicit vote for "none of the above," which the winning candidate would have to beat.
