I'm writing a chapter on the family as a political model in Confucian role ethics, and I keep meeting the analogy my colleagues use to frame the tradition: the polity is a household writ large, the ruler stands to the people as a father to his children, and political obligation is therefore continuous with familial obligation rather than grounded in coercion or contract. It is invoked to license a further claim — that a well-ordered state need not lean on law and punishment, because the affection that moves a son to serve his father will move a subject to serve his ruler once the ruler's character is right. The Legalist counter usually associated with Han Feizi, that subjects are moved by profit and fear rather than by the love that binds parents to children, tends to be waved away in my field as cynicism, and I suspect that is too quick. I'd like an audit rather than a verdict. Give me the correspondence table explicitly: what plays the parent, the child, the household, the shared blood, the years of nurture, the unquestioned authority. Then mark where the mapping breaks, and distinguish the disanalogies that matter for the conclusion my colleagues want from those that do not. Finally, the passage in the Mencius where Tao Ying asks what Shun would do if his father committed murder and Gao Yao, as minister of justice, were bound to arrest him — Mencius has Shun surrender the throne and carry his father away to the coast. Does that concede that the familial and the political role cannot be held together in one person, and does it damage the analogy itself or only my colleagues' version of it? If the analogy ought to be replaced, what would carry the weight better?
