A friend and I spent most of last night arguing about the Pasadena game, and we ended up at a stalemate that I suspect is really a disagreement about something more general. The game, as I understand it, is this: a fair coin is tossed until it lands heads for the first time; if the first heads occurs on toss n, the payoff is (-1)^(n+1) times 2^n divided by n. So the payoffs run +2, -2, +8/3, -4, +32/5, and so on, with the nth outcome having probability 1/2^n. The expected value, if you sum in the natural order of n, is the alternating harmonic series 1 - 1/2 + 1/3 - 1/4 + ..., which converges to ln 2, about 0.69. But because that series is only conditionally convergent, rearranging the terms can produce any real number you like, or make the sum diverge. So the game's expected value is usually said to be undefined rather than infinite or finite. This is supposed to be worse than the St. Petersburg game, where the expectation is at least infinite and you can say something about it.

The question I want your credence on is this: Is there a determinate rational verdict about whether to play the Pasadena game for one cent? That is, is some one of the following correct—playing is rationally required, playing is rationally permitted, playing is rationally forbidden—rather than rational choice theory simply having no verdict because the game's expectation is undefined? I want your credence that there is a determinate verdict, not just your view about what the verdict is.

My own credence is about 0.3; my friend's is about 0.85. He thinks it is obvious that you should play for a penny, because the natural-order sum is positive and the undefinedness is a technicality about rearranging an infinite series that never arises in any finite play. I think the undefinedness is exactly the kind of thing that shows there is no fact of the matter here, and that any verdict would have to privilege one ordering of the outcomes over others without a principled reason.

Please give me your own number, and decompose it into the sub-questions you think it depends on. Some candidates, though you should add or replace as you see fit: (a) the probability that rational choice theory is in the business of delivering determinate verdicts for every well-defined prospect, including ones with undefined expected value; (b) the probability that the natural ordering of the outcomes is privileged in a way that makes the ln 2 sum the right one to use; (c) the probability that some extension or alternative to expected utility theory—say, taking limits of finite truncations, using dominance reasoning, or adopting a non-expected-utility decision rule—succeeds in delivering a determinate verdict; (d) the probability that the idealizations in the game (unbounded payoffs, infinitely many tosses, no discounting or risk aversion) are what generate the problem, so that a realistic version of the game would have a determinate verdict; (e) the probability that the game's undefined expectation is a defect of the theory rather than a genuine feature of the choice situation, so that a correct theory would have to say something determinate. For each sub-question, give a rough probability and a sentence of justification. Then show how you combine them into your final number, and tell me which sub-question your number is most sensitive to.

Finally, tell me where my friend and I most likely disagree: is it about whether rational verdicts must be determinate in undefined-expectation cases, about whether the natural ordering is privileged, about whether any extension of expected utility theory works, or about something else? And tell me what evidence or argument would move your number most—what would you have to learn about the Pasadena game, or about rational choice theory, to shift your credence substantially?
