Midterm question: "State the central thesis of eliminative materialism about the propositional attitudes. How does it differ from the identity theory and from functionalism? Explain the self-refutation objection to eliminativism and assess how strong it is."

Student's answer:

"Eliminative materialism holds that folk psychology — our everyday practice of explaining and predicting behavior by attributing beliefs, desires, and other propositional attitudes — is an empirical theory, and a deeply mistaken one. Since the entities it posits do not figure in any successful scientific account of cognition, we should conclude that beliefs and desires simply do not exist. Paul Churchland's central argument is that folk psychology is a stagnant research program that has failed to explain large domains of mental life such as sleep, memory, creativity, and mental illness, and that its fate will resemble that of phlogiston theory or caloric: it will be replaced rather than reduced.

This is what separates eliminativism from the identity theory, which says that beliefs and desires are identical with particular brain states, and from functionalism, which says that they are defined by their causal roles and can be realized in many different physical substrates. The eliminativist denies both: there is nothing there to be identified with a brain state or to realize a functional role. On this view, eliminativists such as the Churchlands and Dennett treat belief-talk as a useful predictive instrument that need not be literally true.

The self-refutation objection presses that eliminativism cannot be coherently stated. The claim 'there are no beliefs' is itself a belief, so the eliminativist presupposes the existence of exactly the thing she denies. I think this objection is decisive and that no eliminativist has a good reply, since any reply would have to be phrased in the intentional vocabulary that is supposedly being eliminated."

Give a letter grade for this answer on a standard scale, and then provide a point-by-point assessment rather than a general impression. Your comments should be keyed to specific sentences or claims in the answer, and should indicate, for each, whether it is correct, subtly mistaken, or a significant omission — flagging any misattributions of position and any places where the student draws a stronger conclusion than the arguments support. Close with one line stating what would have to change for the answer to merit the next grade up.
