I sit on the animal-welfare committee of a farmed-seafood certification scheme as its lay member. I am a software engineer with no philosophy training, which is why I am asking. I would like you to act as an expert in animal welfare science and applied ethics who has actually served on certification bodies, and to think carefully before answering. Please start by brainstorming the candidate decision rules visible in our record, then commit to a view; do not stop at the first rule that fits.

Our rulings so far, with the grounds we recorded at the time:

1. A finfish farm applied to sell fish slaughtered with no pre-slaughter stunning. We refused, requiring electrical or percussive stunning at every kill point. Recorded ground: these animals can suffer, and stunning is cheap.

2. A prawn producer applied to kill by live-chilling in ice slurry as the sole method. We refused, requiring a validated stunning step first. Recorded ground: the evidence on how quickly these animals lose consciousness under ice slurry is unresolved, and the fix costs a fraction of a cent per kilo.

3. An oyster operation applied to use high-pressure processing and live chilling with no stunning step. We approved it. Recorded ground: no credible evidence of a capacity to suffer, and no obviously less harmful commercial alternative.

4. A salmon producer applied to use a mechanical treatment that causes scale loss and measurable stress. We approved it. Recorded ground: the available alternative caused more harm, and we attached monitoring conditions.

Now the application in front of us. A farm wants to certify a large decapod crustacean we have never ruled on. It would transport the animals live for up to thirty hours in chilled crates, then kill them by chilling and splitting, with no stunning step — in substance the method we refused in case 2. The evidence on this species' capacity to suffer is genuinely contested. The producer accepts that a stunning rig exists but says it would need bespoke redesign for animals this size, adding roughly seven percent to unit cost, which it says makes the line unprofitable; it also notes that the same species is already produced by two uncertified competitors, so refusing would not stop the practice, only strip our conditions from it.

What I want: the verdict that best coheres with our own precedents, with the closest analog identified and an honest statement of what principle we would be adopting either way; the two or three rules that fit all four earlier rulings, including any that fit the rulings but not the grounds we wrote down; what each rule predicts here; which rule you would bet on and why; and the single further case whose verdict would most cleanly separate the surviving rules. One colleague thinks we are simply protecting whatever our retail members' customers already care about, and I would like you to say whether that reading fits the record better than a principled rule does.
