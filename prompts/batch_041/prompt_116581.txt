I teach a final-year undergraduate module on the foundations of quantum mechanics, and I have one ninety-minute slot that I want to spend on an activity rather than a lecture. The group is about sixteen students; they have all seen unitary evolution and can write down a superposition, and they have all heard the phrase many-worlds, but most of them have never had to think about why probability is supposed to be a problem for it. I want them to feel that problem in their hands rather than hear me state it. The problem, as I want them to run into it, is roughly this: if the wavefunction never collapses and every outcome of a measurement is realised in some branch, then nothing in the picture obviously singles out any one outcome as the one that will happen to me, and yet the theory attaches unequal weights — the amplitudes — to the branches, and it is those weights that the Born rule converts into probabilities. The tempting first thought is that if every branch is equally real then every branch should count equally, which gives equal odds; the uncomfortable second thought is that this cannot be right, because the amplitudes are plainly doing work, and it is not at all obvious where the squared modulus is meant to come from if all the branches simply occur. I would like the students to be drawn into committing to a betting policy under the branching picture and then to feel the floor give way beneath it.

Please approach this as an experienced teacher and designer of philosophy-of-physics activities, and design me a single runnable session. I want rules, materials, roles, a clear timeline for the ninety minutes, and a payoff scheme that does not require real money; the activity should arrange its incentives, roles or physical set-up so that the students themselves arrive at the tension — placing bets, splitting into branches, arguing about what they are owed — before anyone names it, so please keep the exposition out of the opening and hold the terminology back until the debrief. The debrief is the part I care most about: map the specific moments of the activity onto the parts of the problem, explain how the naive counting intuition was seduced and then punished, and be candid about the fact that no classroom game can actually demonstrate the Born rule or show that the world is amplitude-weighted, so tell me exactly which piece of the problem the activity cannot reproduce and that I will therefore have to supply myself. I also want you to predict what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it — the students who refuse to bet, the ones who notice the set-up is rigged, the ones who announce the answer immediately — and say how I should handle each.

Please start by brainstorming two or three possible mechanics before settling on one, then commit: give me a definite design with definite choices, take a clear position on why it is the best of the candidates, and give your reasons, rather than laying out options and leaving the selection to me. Write in British English.
