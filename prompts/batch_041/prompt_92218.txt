I'm drafting a position paper for my social and political philosophy course and I'd like a consistency audit before I commit to it. Here is where I've landed on Marx.

(1) Exploitation should be defined structurally and without moralizing: A exploits B when A appropriates a surplus produced by B's labor, where that appropriation rests on A's control of the means of production rather than on a contract term B could actually have refused. On this definition "exploitation" names a relation of production, not a wrong; any wrongness has to be located elsewhere.

(2) The labor theory of value is false as an account of how prices are actually determined, so I don't want my definition in (1) to depend on surplus value in Marx's technical sense.

(3) What makes capitalist wage labor bad is alienation: the worker is estranged from her activity, her product, and her species-being, and this estrangement is an objective condition of the labor rather than a feeling she may or may not have.

(4) Capitalism is unjust, and its injustice is exploitation — that is what the critique is fundamentally about.

(5) Alienation is not specific to capitalism: it follows from commodity production as such, so it would survive in a market economy of worker-owned firms.

(6) I think unpaid domestic labor is exploited, even though no one who controls a means of production appropriates a surplus from it in the way (1) requires.

I can't tell whether these hang together or whether I'm quietly helping myself to more than one notion of exploitation. Please tell me which commitments genuinely conflict, which pairs only look like they do, and which single commitment is doing the most damage. Rank the tensions by severity rather than treating them all as equally bad, and for each real one say what the cheapest revision would be and what it would cost me — in particular whether any of the fixes forces me to give up (4), since that is the one I most want to keep.
