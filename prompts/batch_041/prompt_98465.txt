Me and a friend got into this argument and now I can't stop chewing on it. I'm not a computer scientist — I did a bit of coding years ago and I read the odd blog post — but I've been thinking about whether the whole P vs NP thing is secretly a claim about creativity, or about math, or about what minds can do. My friend thinks it basically proves machines can never be truly creative, and he laid it out like this:

(1) Finding a proof of a hard theorem is really a search problem: you're hunting through the space of possible proofs for one that works, and checking a proof once you have it is easy and fast. (2) That's exactly the shape of the problems people say P vs NP is about — hard to solve, easy to check. (3) If P turned out to equal NP, then every problem whose answer is easy to check would also be easy to solve, i.e. there'd be a method that finds the answer about as fast as you can check it. (4) So if P = NP there'd be a method that finds proofs about as fast as we can check them, which means mathematical discovery would be, in principle, mechanical — you'd just run the method and wait. (5) But mathematical discovery is not mechanical; it takes insight, taste, genius, it's the most creative thing people do. (6) Therefore P ≠ NP, and the difficulty of discovery is baked into the structure of computation itself.

I sort of want to believe (6) because it's a beautiful thought, but I also smell a rat and I can't find it. Something about "in principle" and "about as fast" doing too much work? Or maybe the jump from (3) to (4) — I keep feeling that "there exists a fast method" isn't the same as "we could actually run it," but I can't say exactly why without hand-waving. Also I saw a video claiming there's some single search method that's supposedly within a constant factor of the best possible for basically any problem of this kind, which if true makes step (4) sound even more like it should be the end of the story — except apparently the constant is so huge and unknown that it's useless in practice, and I can't tell if that rescues the argument or kills it. So: which numbered step should I give up, why did it look so reasonable in the first place, and what do I have to pay to reject it? And if it turns out the argument is actually valid and I just have to pick which nice-sounding premise to sacrifice, tell me which one is cheapest — though I'd really like to keep (5) and (1) if I can.
