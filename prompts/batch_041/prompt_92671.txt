My friend and I spent most of last night arguing about Bernard Williams, and I came away with the uncomfortable sense that I was defending a position I could not state cleanly while he was attacking something he had flattened. He has read the long critique of utilitarianism that Williams wrote alongside Smart, and the essay on persons and character, and he set out the whole business for me with real confidence: "Williams's integrity objection is that utilitarianism forces you to treat your own deepest commitments as just one more pile of preferences to be weighed in an impersonal calculus. Jim, in the village with the twenty prisoners, is told he can save nineteen by shooting one; George is offered the chemical warfare job and told he must take it because otherwise a worse man will. The utilitarian verdict in each case is that you do the thing, and the cost is that a person with integrity cannot do it, because integrity means sticking to your principles and refusing to sell out, and the theory will not let you. The slogan about having one thought too many is the same point in miniature: two people are in equal peril and a man can save only one, and he saves his wife, and if he has to console himself that partiality to a spouse is morally permitted, he has had one thought too many; Williams's point is that no moral justification of the rescue can be given, because the moment you need one you have already wrecked the relationship. So this is really the demandingness objection in fancier dress, and since nobody seriously thinks you must maximize utility at the cost of everything you love, utilitarianism is refuted -- or it survives only by abandoning the impartial calculus that makes it a distinct view."

I suspect this is unfair somewhere, but I cannot locate the seam, and my friend is not a careless reader, which is part of what bothers me. Would you audit it claim by claim: what Williams would accept, what is a popularizer's simplification, and what is simply wrong? In particular I want to know whether the demandingness reading captures or dissolves the integrity point; whether the drowning-wife case is a second argument against utilitarianism or something with a different target and a different moral; and whether the concluding claim, that utilitarianism is refuted, follows from any of the parts that survive. Please also tell me what would change your verdict -- which consideration would move you to call the summary fair after all -- and where your own reasoning is most likely to be wrong.
