You are a philosopher of public memory and an expert on the ethics of commemoration. Please begin by brainstorming the nearest concepts in commemorative theory, then give a careful answer. I am a municipal historic-preservation officer, not a philosopher, and I keep encountering a pattern for which I have no name. A controversial statue is kept in place, but an official addition is installed beside it—a plaque or panel that records the arguments for and against the figure and treats the dispute itself as the thing now being commemorated. The same happens with a street sign: the old name stays, but a second sign below explains that the name is disputed and summarizes the objections. Officials then describe the site as honoring the conversation rather than the man, and both defenders and critics can claim the result vindicates them. I know about iconoclasm and damnatio memoriae, but those concern erasure or removal, and I know about simply adding neutral historical context. What I am after is the specific move of preserving the original object while making its contested status the official subject of commemoration. Is there an established term for this? If there is, define it and distinguish it from the nearest alternatives; if there is not, coin a term, define it precisely, and give boundary cases that show what would and would not count. A bare label will not help—I need the carving.
