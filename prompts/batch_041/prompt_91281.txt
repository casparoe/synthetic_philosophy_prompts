I'm in a seminar on evolutionary psychology and we keep fighting about which adaptationist stories are legit, and I've noticed I have pretty consistent gut verdicts on them but can't state the rule I'm using. Case by case:

Lactose tolerance in dairying populations — buy it, though I guess that's cheating, we have the actual gene and the ancient DNA.

Morning sickness as a defence against teratogens in the first trimester — buy this one too, and I can't point to a gene or a mechanism. It's just that the aversion lands on exactly the risky foods, in exactly the window when the embryo is doing its organ-building, and it isn't just "pregnant women feel gross in general."

Kids scared of snakes and spiders but not of cars or plug sockets — believe it, mostly because of the dissociation: the thing that actually kills modern kids is the thing they're not scared of.

Men preferring a waist-to-hip ratio of 0.7 — don't believe it, even though there's data. The number is arbitrary and the effect sizes bounce around.

Music evolving as a group-bonding signal — don't believe it. It's the kind of story you could tell about any human behaviour and it dosn't predict anything in particular.

Men being better at mental rotation because ancestral men did the hunting — don't believe, even though there's a sex difference and there's a mechanism. The difference is small and it moves around with training and with how the test is framed.

And one a friend in the lab told me: that the frequency band of a baby's cry sits right where adult hearing is most sensitive, so the cry is "tuned" to hurt us into responding. Don't believe that either — a cry is whatever an infant larynx can produce, so the overlap is a constraint, not a signal.

The one I can't call is the endowment effect, people wanting more to give up a mug than they'd pay for it. Half of me says it's an evolved reluctance to part with resources; half says it's an artefact of how the experiments are run, and that people with market experience stop doing it.

My rough rule is that I trust the ones where the hypothesis predicts the fine structure of the trait, not just that it would've been useful. But the baby cry case has fine structure and I still don't trust it, so maybe the rule is "trust it when the fine structure would be unlikely under some boring alternative" — which might mean I have to give up the morning sickness one, since "pregnancy makes you averse to strong smells" is a boring alternative that fits the same data. Is there a principle that gets these, what does it say about the endowment effect, and which of my verdicts do I have to drop?
