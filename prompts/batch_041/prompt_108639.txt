I'm on a small mechanistic interpretability team — we poke at open models, trace circuits, do activation patching, that kind of thing — and we keep slamming into the same meta-problem that isn't really about any one model. An "anomaly" for us is any behavior the current mechanistic story doesn't predict: a head doing something it shouldn't, a capability that shows up in evals with no obvious circuit behind it, a prompt that flips a behavior in a way our causal graph says is impossible. We file these as tickets, and then we have to decide, constantly, whether two tickets are the same mechanism or two different ones. Merge when they're actually distinct and we burn a month building one story that fits neither; keep them apart when they're the same and we reinvent the same explanation three times over. Our team lead has to sign off on any merge, which makes it worse, because she wants a reason and "they feel related" is not one. A colleague who did a phil-of-science masters keeps insisting the criterion is obvious — two anomalies are the same mechanism iff no intervention dissociates them: find any patch, ablation, or steering vector that moves one and not the other and they're different, otherwise same. I dont buy that it's that clean, and she admits it's rough, but we need something we can actually run on a Friday afternoon.

So here's what I want from you. Take the philosophy-of-science picture of what a mechanism even is — a set of components or parts, the activities those parts engage in, the way they're organized and wired together, the boundary that separates the thing from its surroundings, and the counterfactual profile (what happens to the output when you intervene on each part) — and turn it into a decision procedure my team can run on two anomaly tickets, in order, in under an hour, with zero philosophy background and nobody who wants to read a paper. I want the ordered questions, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure terminates with a verdict of same-mechanism or different-mechanism, and the specific points where it has to hand off to a human because the question is really about taste or about what we care about rather than about fact. Build it so it survives three cases I actually care about: (1) two behaviors that no intervention we've found dissociates, but that live in different layers and might simply always be co-activated; (2) a small sub-circuit that is plainly a piece of some bigger circuit — same mechanism or not; (3) two different implementations that seem to compute the same function — one mechanism or two. Then run your procedure on all three, including the one where it hands me an answer I won't like, and tell me plainly what the procedure cannot do at all — the residue it can't touch no matter how you tweak it. And don't hedge: when your procedure says same or different, commit to the verdict and give the reason, rather than laying out the options and leaving the call to me.
