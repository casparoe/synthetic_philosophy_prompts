I have no academic background in philosophy or neuroscience, but over the past couple of years I have read a fair amount of popular writing on animal minds and on variation in human cognition, and I have settled into a position that I would now like you to try to break rather than flatter. The position is this: brain size — and by that I mean any physical measure of the organ's magnitude, whether raw mass, the ratio of brain to body weight, or the sheer count of neurons — carries no moral weight of its own. Whenever we feel tempted to say that a creature with a larger or richer brain matters more, I think what is actually doing the moral work is some capacity or other — the capacity to suffer, to plan, to remember, to form attachments, to reflect on oneself — and the size of the brain enters only as fallible evidence about whether such capacities are present. Size is a proxy, never a ground. I call this the proxy view, and it strikes me as almost obviously correct, which is precisely why I distrust it and want to know what it is exposed to.

So treat the proxy view as the thing under examination. I would like a map of its vulnerabilities: which observations, arguments, intuitions, or formal results would count against it; which of those would be genuinely decisive rather than merely awkward; and what the view's own defenders would have to concede as counting against them, as opposed to what only its critics would say. Please rank the defeaters from fatal to tolerable. I am especially interested in whether the view risks anything at all — whether it makes any prediction its rivals do not — or whether it has been quietly insulated, and if so, whether that insulation is a defect or simply the price of a truism. And if you conclude that it has been qualified into something unfalsifiable, say so plainly.

A few candidate objections already half-occur to me, and I want to know which of them have teeth. First, a perfectionist rival: suppose what matters is not any item on a list of capacities but the depth or richness of experience itself, and suppose that richness supervenes on the sheer amount of processing going on — then the quantity we call size might be a better tracker of what matters than any capacity we can name, and the proxy view would be false in spirit even if true in letter. Second, a marginal worry: if capacities are implemented in tissue, then adding neurons that add a sliver of capacity would give size a derivative relevance at the margins, which seems to soften the claim that size never matters in itself. Third, a thought experiment the view seems committed to: if a mind with all the capacities of an adult human could be built into a device the size of a pea, the proxy view must say it matters exactly as much as a person, and I think that is right but I want to know whether it is a commitment the view can bear. Finally, the worry that most unsettles me: the word capacity is so plastic that any verdict about who or what matters could be redescribed as a verdict about capacities, which would make the proxy view true by construction and therefore worthless. Tell me which of these a defender must take seriously, which they can wave away, and which would move someone like me who is not yet committed either way.
