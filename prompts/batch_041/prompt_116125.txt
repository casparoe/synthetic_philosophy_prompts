I'm in a reading group that's been arguing about revealed preference for what feels like forever and nobody has moved an inch. I want a diagnosis of the disagreement, not a verdict on who's right.

Here's the shape of it. One camp says revealed preference theory makes a genuine empirical claim: that there's a tight enough link between a person's choices and their preferences that you can read the preferences off the choices, and that when choices violate the standard consistency conditions (the weak axiom, and the stronger stuff you need to build an actual utility function by integration) the theory has been falsified or at least embarrassed. They point to cases where people cycle, or where adding an irrelevant option flips a choice, or where framing changes what gets picked, and they treat those as data.

The other camp says none of that can be empirical, because 'preference' here isn't a separate thing that choice is evidence about -- it just is the pattern of choice, by stipulation. On that reading the consistency conditions can't be falsified, only violated, and when someone violates them we call it a mistake, which already assumes we know what they really wanted, i.e. it smuggles back in exactly the mental state the behaviorist program was supposed to replace.

And then there's a third thread that I suspect might be the real fight: the whole apparatus is also used to say what's good for people -- that if you choose it, it's better for you, or at least that we should take your choices as authoritative about your welfare. That's a normative claim, and it seems to come from nowhere in the theory itself. Meanwhile 'utility' keeps sliding between what gets maximized in a decision (the thing the model uses to predict choice), what a person actually experiences, and what counts as their interest.

My working hypothesis is that the impasse is mostly a matter of 'preference' and 'utility' each doing several jobs at once -- a predictive construct, a psychological state, and a welfare measure -- and that each side quietly grabs a different one and then talks past the other, with a normative premise hiding under the empiricist one. I could be wrong though, and it might definately be a real, substantive disagreement about the world or about what counts as evidence.

So: what kind of disagreement is this actually? Verbal, a hidden empirical question, a clash of unargued normative starting points, or two camps answering different questions under a single name? And given your diagnosis, is there anything that would count as progress -- a distinction, a formal result, a kind of experiment -- or should we expect it to keep going the way it has?
