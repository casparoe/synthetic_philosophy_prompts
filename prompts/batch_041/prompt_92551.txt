I am a novelist, some way into a book that stands with one foot in legend and one in history, and I have been invited to apply for a one-year fellowship at a humanities institute — the sort of fellowship that expects the project to be genuinely conceptual rather than a disguised book proposal. I should like you to draft the research proposal, roughly 1,500 words.

Here is my half-formed idea, which I would like you to take seriously and then either sharpen or, if it will not bear the weight, replace with something better. We habitually run together two questions: whether a story is true, and whether it has been admitted into the genre of history. I want to keep the second question separate and call it the question of admission. My suspicion is that admission is governed less by evidence than by what I have been calling a charter moment — the point at which some institution (a city, a church, a dynasty, a school syllabus, a tourist authority) begins to cite the story as a warrant for a present claim or entitlement. On this reading a legend becomes history not when it is proved but when it is put to work. I want to test that suspicion against three cases that live in different disciplines and appear to have crossed the line at different speeds and for different reasons: the Trojan War, Arthur, and the Exodus.

Please state the governing question precisely, so that it has a determinate answer: is the transition from legend to history best explained by a charter moment of this kind, and do these three cases yield a common account, or do they show that 'becoming history' is not one process but several? Then say why the existing positions leave it open — the evidentialist who holds that it is all a matter of proof, the constructivist who holds that it is all a matter of power, and the folklorist who holds that legend and history lie on a continuum and that there is no threshold to find. Describe the method: what I would actually do, month by month, over a single year, and why that approach rather than the obvious alternatives. Say what a result would look like, what would count as the project failing, and give an honest assessment of whether this is too much for a year or perhaps too little. I am a novelist, not a historian, so be candid about where that helps and where it is a liability; keep the proposal conceptual throughout, and do not let it drift into a synopsis of the novel. You may refer to positions and arguments, but do not supply a bibliography or reading list — I shall add the citations myself.

I would also like you to mark clearly, as you go, what is genuinely settled and what is contested among people who work on these cases, and not to present a contested view as though it were the consensus — for instance about whether the Exodus happened, or about what the excavated layers of Troy can bear, or about how much of Arthur is a later invention. Where you are uncertain, say so rather than smoothing it over. Write in British English.
