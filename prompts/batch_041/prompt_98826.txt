I'm in a small film-and-philosophy group — we watch something, then argue about it for two hours, and it's my turn to lead when we do Amadeus. I've seen it four or five times and I genuinely think it's great filmmaking, but this time it landed wrong, because the week before I'd fallen down a rabbit hole reading about the actual Salieri: the Viennese court composer, the man who taught Beethoven and Schubert, who spent his last years mentally unwell and in and out of institutions, and who for two centuries has mostly been known to the general public as a mediocre poisoner who killed Mozart out of envy. That image is not history, it's a story — the rumor is old, but the film is what made it the default picture in everyone's head. And that got me wondering whether something wrong has been done, and to whom, and how you'd even cash that out. He's been dead since 1825, he can't be embarrassed, he can't feel his reputation slide — so either the dead can't be wronged at all and this is a lot of fuss about nothing, or the wrong has to be located somewhere else: in his name being attached to a character who never existed, in the fact that anyone who wants to know the real man now has to fight through a story they already love, in some interest of the living (his students' legacy, the profession, the record itself), or in the sheer fact that the fiction has crowded out the truth so completely that the correction never reaches the people who absorbed the lie.

What I want to work through with the group is a reading of the film through exactly this lens: treat the movie as a case study in what we owe the real dead when we dramatize them, and take both sides seriously. My friend has a name for one half of it — he calls it name larceny, when a fictional character doesn't just borrow a real person's events but takes over their name so that the real life becomes a footnote to the invention — but I don't love the phrase and I'm not sure it's one thing or two. So I'd like you to carve it properly: separate putting a real person into invented events from replacing them in public memory, say whether the second is a distinct and worse wrong, and say what each one is a wrong *to*. Then push it harder. Does the film's own frame let it off the hook — the asylum, the confession to the priest, the fact that the whole thing is one unreliable old man's account, the last benediction he gives the other patients — or does that framing actually make it worse, because it dresses the slander up as a meditation on guilt and so makes it more credible and more memorable? Does it matter that the film is beautiful, or is a lovely lie just a lie with better retention? Does the wrong shrink if the defamed person was himself a bit of a snob, or grow because he can never answer? And notice it isn't only Salieri — Mozart is turned into a giggling man-child, Constanze into a giggling wife, and the Requiem commission into a death sentence, so I'd like you to say whether the film's treatment of its real subjects is one sin or several. Please argue it rather than pointing me at essays — I want a position I can bring to the group, and specific scenes I can use as evidence for it, including any scene where you think the film quietly knows it's lying.
