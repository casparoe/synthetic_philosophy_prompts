I am a novelist, and I have reached a scene I do not yet know how to write: one in which a character's account of her own experience has to be, at the same time, entirely authoritative and quietly beside the point. Behind that difficulty lies a disagreement I understand only partially — between the Cartesian theater, in which experiences are delivered to a single audience of one at a definite moment and in a definite order, and the multiple drafts model, which replaces the theater with a crowd of parallel processes and denies that there is any one moment or place at which an experience becomes conscious. I would like you to construct a thought experiment that forces these two pictures apart, to the following specifications.

The case must be drawn from ordinary life: no laboratory, no apparatus, no surgery, no brain damage, no drugs, no science-fictional devices. Write the scenario itself as a single paragraph of plain prose, under 250 words, and keep the philosophical vocabulary out of it — the scene has to survive as a scene in the novel.

Do not build it from the standard demonstrations. The phi phenomenon, metacontrast and backward masking, the cutaneous rabbit, priming, split-brain patients, and any case in which time is stipulated to freeze or stutter are all closed to you.

The character's account of the experience must be given as it happens, in the present tense, in real time — a commentator calling a play, a musician counting the beat aloud, an interpreter speaking over a voice. This is the specification I care about most. I want the theater theorist to be unable to say that the report is merely a later reconstruction, because there is no later; the simultaneity has to be built into the scenario rather than asserted at the end.

The phenomenology must be neutral from the inside. The character must not be able to tell, by introspection, which picture is true; the two pictures should diverge only in what they commit us to about what was happening behind the scenes. And the case must be one in which the theater picture has to pay a price: it must either place the ordering of experience before the performance begins or place the editing of it after the fact, and both moves should look unattractive once the case is stated plainly.

Finally, locate one further position from the contemporary literature on consciousness — a higher-order representation view, a global workspace view, or an illusionist view, named and described in a sentence or two rather than cited — and say whether the case separates it from both pictures, from one of them, or from neither. If it cannot be separated, say so instead of manufacturing a difference.

After the scenario: state the verdict each picture gives and the reasoning behind it; identify the single feature of the case that is doing the work; say what becomes of the case if that feature is removed; and give the strongest line of attack available to whichever picture comes out badly. Close by telling me honestly whether the specifications can be jointly satisfied, and if one of them has to be relaxed, which one and why.
