So I'm a postdoc in a cognitive science dept (philosophy by training, so this is a bit of a stretch for me) and in two weeks I have to present at our weekly "Minds and Machines" seminar. The paper is on the knowing-how vs. knowing-that distinction, and my line is that the distinction is real but almost everyone draws it in the wrong place. The standard framing — know-how is procedural/implicit/non-propositional, know-that is declarative/explicit — I think is mostly a red herring, because you can get cases that come apart in the opposite direction. My actual proposal is that what's distinctive about know-how is that it's indexical through and through: it's knowledge of how to do *this*, here, now, with *these* hands, and any propositional statement of it drops exactly the part that made it know-how in the first place. My central example is a bit odd — I use a close-up card magician who can do a sleight and a movement coach who can teach it in fine detail, and I argue the coach has a very rich know-that while the magician has a know-how no description captures; then I flip it with someone who can describe a movement perfectly and can't perform it at all. I'm worried the whole thing collapses under questions I won't see coming.

Here's the audience, and this is why I need help: it's about eight people. Three or four are computational cognitive scientists who build reinforcement-learning models of skill acquisition and treat "knowing how" as just the policy an agent ends up with; one of them is a roboticist who will flatly say an RL policy *is* the know-how, nothing left over. There's one philosopher who reads everything through Ryle and thinks any propositional gloss is a category mistake, and one committed intellectualist who thinks know-how is just know-that about ways of acting. And our chair is a phenomenologist who keeps circling back to what it's like to have the skill in the first place.

So what I want is a Q&A prep sheet: the objections I'll actually get from *these* people, in their own vocabulary, not the ones I'd get from a room full of epistemologists. Order them by how likely they are and how much damage they'd do to me, phrase each the way that particular person would put it, give me a two-minute reply to each, and tell me where the reply is soft. Give it to me as exactly five numbered items and nothing else — no intro, no closing summary — each item a couple of sentences covering the objection, the reply, and the weak spot. Make the fifth one the question I'm secretly hoping nobody asks.
