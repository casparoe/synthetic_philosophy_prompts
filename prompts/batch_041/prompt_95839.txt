I am a novelist, roughly two-thirds through a book whose central object is a machine — a device that observes, keeps records and issues reports, and whose reports a tribunal within the story must decide either to treat as testimony or to dismiss as mere printout — and I have reached the point at which I can no longer fudge the metaphysics. I have read enough of the dispute between Dennett and Searle to know the standard move: the machine's states have at best derived intentionality, borrowed from the people who built it, whereas ours is original; and I have read enough to suspect that this distinction is doing considerably less work than its popularity suggests. What I want from you is not a verdict on either man but the specification I should be working from — the list of requirements that any account of original versus derived intentionality would have to meet before I can let a character rest an argument on it in front of a judge. Give me the requirements, each with the case or consideration that puts it on the list: the cases the account must get right (Dennett's coin-operated two-bitser; a thermostat; a frog's visual system; a shopping list; the Chinese room; and the case I have invented for the book, a device of the two-bitser family that is shipped to another country and refitted, so that its inner states keep their old roles while its surroundings change — which I cannot decide whether a theory must classify as false belief or as damage); the distinctions it must not collapse (original versus derived; as-if versus genuine; having content versus having truth-conditions; a difference in degree versus a difference in kind; intrinsic versus observer-relative); and the constraints it cannot contradict (that we routinely and successfully ascribe beliefs to one another without any knowledge of mechanisms; that misrepresentation must be possible at all; that content is frequently indeterminate; that nothing may be smuggled in that physics could not in principle deliver). I have one candidate requirement of my own, which I would like you to test rather than assume: that the mark of the original article is the capacity to be not merely broken but wrong — a state that can misrepresent, rather than one that simply fails to do what it was made for. If that is a genuine requirement, say what follows for the derived side; if it is not, say what counterexample kills it.

Mark, for each requirement, whether it is non-negotiable for any account or merely a preference of one camp wearing the costume of a requirement, and tell me which single requirement does the most excluding work, in the sense that conceding it costs the most theories. I am chiefly interested in whether some subset of these requirements is jointly unsatisfiable — in particular whether a thoroughgoing anti-intrinsicalism about content (nothing has original intentionality; all of it is ascribed relative to our interests and purposes) can be held together with both the claim that there is a real difference between original and derived intentionality and the thought that interpretation is always interest-relative. If the three cannot be held together, say which is the weakest and what its defenders would have to surrender; if they can, sketch the position that holds all three, since that is the one my book needs and the one I have never seen stated plainly. I do not need a scorecard of the camps, though if a short paragraph on what each of the two familiar positions would have to abandon in order to satisfy the list comes easily, include it.

Write it as a specification rather than an essay, in prose I can read at a desk, and please give me no citations, references, reading list or bibliography — I shall supply those myself and would rather not be handed plausible-looking titles I cannot check. Refer to positions and arguments by description rather than by paper. Finally, be candid about your own footing: tell me which of your verdicts you hold most loosely, especially the claim about joint unsatisfiability, which consideration would lead you to demote a requirement from non-negotiable to a mere preference of a camp, and what would persuade you to abandon my criterion of the capacity to be wrong. Where is this reasoning most likely to come apart?
