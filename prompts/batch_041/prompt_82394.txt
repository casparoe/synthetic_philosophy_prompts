So I'm writing a long feature for a general-reader mag about whether John Dewey's whole take on democracy still holds up, and I keep getting stuck on the same question: what would he actually have made of the civic experiments people are running right now? I mean the binding citizens' assemblies picked by lottery, and now these AI "deliberation assistants" that chew through tens of thousands of public comments overnight and hand the assembly a short menu of policy options it then votes on. I don't want the usual "Dewey loved democracy, Dewey loved science" mush, and I definitely don't want a reading list or citations -- a model with no web access would just be guessing at those anyway. What I want is someone actually reasoning from his machinery.

So put yourself in his head and tell me, exactly, where he'd come down. Give me exactly five numbered predictions, one or two sentences each, and nothing else -- no intro, no wrap-up at the end. Across the five, make sure you're working from his more technical commitments and not just vibes: the spectator theory of knowledge he was always going after, inquiry as the business of turning an indeterminate situation into a determinate one, the means-and-ends continuum, and his idea that a public is constituted by the indirect consequences of transactions rather than by shared identity. Say for each one whether it would push him toward enthusiasm or alarm, and be specific about which piece of his view does the pushing. It's a seperate issue whether the thing would actually work -- I care more about what he'd predict. Also, don't be shy about the wierd cases: would he see the AI summarizer as continuous with his method of intelligence, or as a brand-new version of the spectator he spent his life trying to kill?
