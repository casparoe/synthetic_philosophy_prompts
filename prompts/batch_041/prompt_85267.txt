I am a novelist halfway through a book whose central figure is a translator, and I have reached the part of the manuscript that has stalled me for a month: the poems. She works on short poems in a language I invented for the book — an eight-line form, two quatrains, lines of eight syllables, internal rhyme in the third and seventh, and a final line that is the first line with one word changed. It is meant to be sung, and I print the tune. The invented grammar obliges me in ways I did not anticipate when I sketched it. Verbs carry no tense. Nouns carry no gender. Definiteness is unmarked. The second person carries an obligatory grade of address that distinguishes a child from a peer from a superior. English obliges differently: it makes me commit to a sex, a time, and an article whether or not the original does, and it gives me no grammatical way to convey the grade of address except by changing the words themselves.

There are a dozen of these poems in the book, and none is decoration. One is a lullaby sung aloud at a deathbed. One is carved on a monument and read out at a ceremony. One is the only thing two estranged characters ever say to each other. My editor reads no poetry in any language and will judge these by how they sound when read aloud; the originals will be printed facing the translations, so a reader who has learned my invented grammar can check me. I write before my day job and I get one morning per poem. There is no native speaker to consult, since the language is mine, and the only authority is the grammar I have already fixed.

I have been reading Douglas Hofstadter's Le Ton beau de Marot, which is built around dozens of English versions of a single short Renaissance French poem addressed to a sick young woman, and what has lodged in me is not any one version but the fact that no two of them preserve the same things. One keeps the rhyme and mangles the sense; one keeps the sense and becomes prose; one keeps the shape and loses the tenderness. Hofstadter treats each as defensible because each answers a different question about what the poem is. A reviewer of the book grumbled that the poem is a charming trifle whose charm cannot be separated from its elegant form, which seems to me exactly the kind of poem that will break any procedure — so the procedure had better say so in advance.

What I want from you is a procedure I can actually run, one poem at a time, in a single morning, and that I could also put in my character's mouth without embarrassment. It should be a sequence of steps in the order I should take them, with a statement of what counts as a sufficient answer at each step, and a clear indication of where the sequence stops. It should include a short set of tests I can apply to a draft, each with a pass and a fail condition. It should tell me how to decide which constraints are load-bearing rather than merely visible, and it should include a step that forces me to write one sentence about what the poem does and then re-derive the constraint list from that sentence. It should tell me what to do when the constraints cannot all be satisfied, including how to break the form on purpose rather than badly, and how to tell a deliberate break from a failure. It should handle the two directions of obligation: what the source cannot leave unsaid and English cannot say without ceasing to be a translation, and what English forces me to decide that the source leaves open — with a step for making those decisions deliberately instead of by accident.

Then run the procedure on three poems and show me the results, including the drafts it produces and the reasons for the choices: the lullaby sung at the deathbed, where singability is not negotiable; the poem whose last line is its first line with one word changed, where the change is the whole poem and English word order may push the changed word out of final position; and an elegy that never says who died and never says when. I expect at least one of these to yield an answer I will not like, so say plainly when no version satisfies the procedure, which loss I must accept, and which decisions are mine as the novelist rather than the translator's because they turn on what the book is about rather than on what the poem is.

No survey of translation theory, no reading list, no citations, no appeals to authorities I will not read. If a technical term is unavoidable, give it once, in plain words, and move on. Tell me which distinctions you had to drop to make the procedure runnable and what is lost by dropping them, where it will misfire, what it cannot do — including whether it can be run at all on a poem I have not understood — and where it merely launders a judgment call into a checklist.
