I am a novelist at work on a book set in a small academy, and two of my characters are carrying the old quarrel between Mencius and Xunzi about human nature. The first is a tutor who tells his pupils that the moral feelings are like sprouts: everyone is born with them, they need only soil and water and a little protection from frost, and the teacher's whole business is to keep the frost off. The second is a headmaster who answers him with a carpenter's image, human nature as warped wood, which no amount of growth will ever straighten and which only steam, the press-frame, and the plane can bring to a true line; from that image he draws the conclusion that governs his school, namely that a boy's spontaneous impulses can never be trusted and that discipline must break them rather than feed them. I have been reading around in both thinkers, and it strikes me that the received contrast between them is carried almost entirely by these two pictures, and that my headmaster in particular wins his case by the picture rather than by any argument I can find underneath it. I have a suspicion, which I would like you to confirm or kill, that the two images disagree far more sharply than the two philosophers do, and that part of this quarrel is really a quarrel between a gardener and a carpenter rather than between two accounts of the same thing. I am writing the scene in which the two of them argue over a boy who has been stealing from the school stores, and I need to know how much either image can actually settle. What I want from you is not a summary of Mencius and Xunzi but an examination of the pair of images as arguments.

Set out for me, in prose, what maps onto what: the seed, the soil, the gardener, the press-frame, the craftsman, the timber, the finished plank, the standard the carpenter works to. Be explicit about which features of each source image are being asked to do the real work, and then tell me where each image breaks. I am especially interested in the passivity the carpentry image assigns to the wood, in the preformation the horticultural image assigns to the seed, and in whether either thinker's own account can be squeezed into the mould his picture provides; whether nature, on Xunzi's understanding, is inert material waiting to be shaped or something with a pull of its own matters enormously here, since my headmaster's entire case turns on the answer, and I suspect he has quietly chosen the wrong material. Then tell me which of the two conclusions the images can actually support, my tutor's claim that a decent upbringing merely lets something already present come out, and my headmaster's claim that goodness must be imposed from outside by ritual and instruction, with the corollary he draws from it that the impulses are to be broken. Say which parts of each conclusion survive once the disanalogies are on the table, which parts were being carried by the picture all along, and which disanalogies are simply irrelevant to the use each character is putting his picture to. If a third image would do the work better than either, something that is neither a seed nor a plank, describe it and tell me exactly what it buys and what it costs. Please write this as continuous prose, the way you would write a long letter to a novelist you had never met: no headings, no numbered lists, no bullet points, no bold, and no tables. If you need to lay out a set of correspondences, do it in sentences.
