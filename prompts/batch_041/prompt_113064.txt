I am a novelist, and I have written myself into a corner that I would rather not resolve by taste alone. I want you to act as a philosopher of history and a methodologist of counterfactual reasoning — someone at home both in the older literature on historical inevitability and in the formal semantics of counterfactuals. Think carefully before answering, and begin by brainstorming: enumerate the candidate rules the evidence below will admit, test each candidate against every verdict, and tell me candidly where the evidence underdetermines the rule. I want reasoning, not a reading list; please give no citations or bibliography of any kind.

My protagonist is a historian of a particular cast of mind. She keeps a ledger of counterfactual verdicts, ruling on historical episodes one at a time and refusing to state her criterion in advance. The novel has already committed her, in scenes I cannot now rewrite, to four verdicts:

— the collapse of the Western Roman Empire: inevitable;
— the Industrial Revolution's beginning in Britain rather than in France or the Low Countries: not inevitable;
— the outcome of the Waterloo campaign: not inevitable;
— the extinction of the passenger pigeon: inevitable.

She has one mannerism I have already put in her mouth: when asked whether some upheaval was inevitable, she first demands to know which upheaval, under what description, her questioner has in mind.

The chapter I am writing now turns on a case she has not yet ruled on, and I need her verdict to be the one she would actually give. The case is the opening of the Berlin Wall's crossings on the night of 9 November 1989. The structural reading, which I want the novel to present fairly: the East German state could no longer buy legitimacy, its patron in Moscow had made plain he would not send tanks, a neighbouring state had already cut the wire, and the population had stopped believing the official account — so that some breach, some opening, was coming within months whatever any individual did. The other reading, which the novel will also present: what happened that night began with a press conference at which the regime's spokesman, asked when the new travel rules took effect, consulted his notes and answered that they took effect at once, a slip that sent crowds to the crossings; there the guards telephoned their superiors and found nobody willing to take responsibility for ordering the use of force; and at Bornholmer Strasse the officer in charge, still without orders, eventually raised the barrier himself. On that reading, a misread note and a handful of decisions that no one was willing to make decided the date, and perhaps more than the date.

So: what would my historian say about this case, given her ledger? Reconstruct the rule or rules she appears to be running; name the rival rules that fit her four verdicts just as well; state what each of those rules predicts about the Berlin case; give me your best guess as to her verdict, with the reasons for preferring it over the rivals; and name the further case that would best tell those rivals apart. I have been telling myself that she is simply a structural determinist, but I suspect that is only one of the rules that would fit her, and that a rule turning on decisive human agency, or on the fragility of the trigger, would fit her ledger just as well — I need to know which, and whether her four verdicts can distinguish them at all. Since she has to defend the verdict aloud, give me the shape of the argument she would actually make rather than a label. Where the ledger leaves the matter genuinely open, say so plainly instead of manufacturing a rule that fits it by accident.
