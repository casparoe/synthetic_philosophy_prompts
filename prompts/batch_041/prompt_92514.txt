I'm grading midterms for a second-year course on nationalism and I could use a second pair of eyes on one of them. The question I set was:

Question: In a recent national-day address, the president of Karistan called the nation three thousand years old, and that week inaugurated a rebuilt ancestral temple, a revived harvest festival described as immemorial, and an official national costume. In fact the costume was designed in 1974 by a state folklore institute, the festival was first staged in 1988, the temple was finished last year, the country was only unified as a single state in 1948, and its national language was standardized and then spread through state schools and a national radio service starting in the 1950s. Using Hobsbawm's account of the invention of tradition and Anderson's account of nations as imagined communities, assess the president's claim that Karistan is an ancient nation.

The awnser:

The president's claim is best read through both theorists, and they mostly agree. Hobsbawm's central point is that nations are imagined communities: the members of a nation feel bound to one another even though they will never meet most of them, and this bond is a cultural construction rather than a natural fact. Anderson, for his part, shows that the rituals and symbols of national belonging — flags, anthems, costumes, festivals — are invented traditions, practices that claim an ancient pedigree but were in fact stitched together recently, often by state institutions. So the two concepts really come to the same thing: to say a community is imagined is just to say that its traditions are invented.

Applied to Karistan, the picture is fairly clear. The costume was designed in 1974 and the festival staged in 1988, and both were given an ancient gloss by the state folklore institute, which is textbook invented tradition. The temple finished last year does the same job, since a monument is exactly the kind of thing that establishes continuity with a suitable past. And the standardization of the language and its diffusion through schools and radio is precisely the mechanism Anderson identifies: print and broadcast media let scattered people imagine themselves as members of one bounded community. The 1948 unification created the state, and the state then created the nation — nationalism precedes the nation, which is Hobsbawm's point.

It follows that the president's claim is simply false. If the costume is fifty years old, the festival thirty, and the temple brand new, then Karistan has no ancient nation; the identity is a fabrication and the three-thousand-years line is a lie told to manufacture loyalty. What both frameworks show is that nationalism is a kind of collective self-deception.

One qualification: Anderson insists that imagined does not mean false — all communities larger than the face-to-face village are imagined. But in Karistan's case the imagining was done from above, by the state, so it is less authentic than a nation that grew organically from below.
