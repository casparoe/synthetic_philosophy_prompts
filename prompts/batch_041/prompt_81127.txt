Me and a mate spent about two hours last night going round in circles on whether there can be a reason for something happening when nothing around has a reason in mind. His line was that "the bee flies to the flower in order to get nectar" is either a claim about what the bee wants or a bit of poetry, and I don't buy it, but I couldn't get him to see the difference. So I want a case built to order instead of another arguement.

Build me one. I want a case where a thing — an organism, a machine, a physical system, whatever you like — has a free-floating rationale in the strong sense: there's a real means-end structure in its behaviour, so "it does X in order to Y" isn't just a dressed-up way of saying "it does X". The "in order to" has to earn its keep, and for me that means the structure supports counterfactuals — if X stopped delivering Y, the thing would do something else that gets Y, and the something else would come from the thing rather than from the observer being generous. And here's the part I actually care about: no selection history, no design, no learning, no designer, and no agent anywhere that represents Y — not even some internal state that you're going to call a representation by courtesy. It has to be something a person could actually build or sit and watch happen, so no stipulated beings, no demons, no "imagine a rock that yearns to be warm". And don't build it out of a one-in-a-googol coincidence, because then the counterfactuals are luck rather than a pattern. One paragraph, plain English. There's alot riding on this bit so don't fudge it.

Then do the work. Give me the case first, with no preamble or throat-clearing, then start the analysis with your verdict in the first sentence of it, and then: (a) go constraint by constraint and show the case really meets them, and say where the "reason" is living, if it's anywhere at all; (b) tell me what someone who thinks reasons of this kind have to be grounded in selection or in proper function has to say about it — do they have to deny there's a rationale here, and if so is that a cost or a bullet they can bite?; (c) tell me what someone who thinks these rationales are just the interpreter's projection has to say, and whether the counterfactual robustness of my case makes that harder to hold; (d) name the single feature doing the heavy lifting and tell me what happens if I take it away — if I let the thing have a history, does the case just collapse into the boring uncontroversial version?; (e) how each side attacks the case as rigged.

And please don't just hand me the wasp with a coat of paint on it.
