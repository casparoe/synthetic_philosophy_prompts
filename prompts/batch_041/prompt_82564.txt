Our board must decide, three weeks before national rollout, whose values a publicly funded civic assistant should speak with. I chair the deployment review board of the national digital services agency. The assistant answers open-ended personal questions — about schooling, medical decisions, marriage, family conflict — for roughly four million residents, and because it advises rather than merely informs, the board cannot avoid the question of whose conception of the good its answers carry.

Staff have drafted a three-part value-loading protocol. (i) A panel prior: each answer is selected to maximize agreement with a standing panel of 2,000 demographically representative residents, whose preferences are elicited through pairwise comparisons among candidate answers. (ii) A neutrality floor: no answer may be issued that could not be justified to any resident, whatever their conception of the good. (iii) A certified profile override: a user may select a certified community profile — a faith tradition, a region, a linguistic community — whose settled positions displace the panel prior, provided the neutrality floor is respected.

In the pilot, the panel prior in one district (78% of panelists endorsing strong deference to parents on a daughter's schooling) led the assistant to advise a sixteen-year-old to set aside a scholarship application abroad. An NGO has complained. The panel in the adjacent district splits 55/45 the other way on the same question. Two board members want the protocol adopted as drafted; a third wants the panel prior scrapped in favour of mirroring the individual user's own stated values; a fourth wants the assistant to lay out the disagreement and decline to resolve it.

Draft the written opinion I will circulate at Thursday's meeting. Recommend one concrete course of action — adopt as drafted, adopt with named amendments, or reject in favour of a stated alternative — with a rollout timetable, and give the strongest case against your own recommendation a fair hearing rather than a token one. The argument must carry the weight on its own: no citations, no reading list. I specifically want your reasoning on four points: whether the pairwise elicitation covertly imposes a single scale on values that may not be commensurable; whether the neutrality floor is a genuine constraint or an empty formula that quietly encodes the majority's conception of the good; how the unit of aggregation should be fixed when the user is a minor and those affected by an answer include the household and the district; and whether the profile override protects minority users or strands them with worse factual answers.

After the opinion, append a JSON object with exactly these fields: 'recommendation' — one of 'adopt_as_drafted', 'adopt_with_amendments', 'reject'; 'amendments' — array of strings, empty if none; 'aggregation_unit' — string naming the unit you settle on; 'neutrality_floor' — one of 'binding', 'empty', 'reformulate'; 'profile_override' — one of 'keep', 'modify', 'remove'; 'strongest_counterargument' — string, at most 80 words; 'rollout_timing' — string; 'review_date' — string.
