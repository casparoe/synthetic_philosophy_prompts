I was at my mate Dev's flat for games night on Friday and he proposed a bet that I genuinely cant stop thinking about. The setup: he flips a coin until it comes up tails. If it's tails on the first flip he pays me £2, if it takes two flips £4, three flips £8, doubling every time. He wants £50 up front to let me play. I laughed and said no, obviously, because most of the time I'm walking away with two or four quid. Then he got out a napkin and did the arithmetic in front of me: half the time I win 2, a quarter of the time 4, an eighth of the time 8, and when you add all that up you get 2 + 2 + 2 + 2 forever, so the average payout is infinite, so the ticket is worth more than any finite amount of money, so I'd be an idiot to turn down £50, or £5,000, or a million. I said fine, I'll sell you my end of it for £200 then, and he said that just proves I don't believe my own maths, which, annoyingly, might be true.

The table split on it. One guy said the catch is just that Dev can't pay — he's got maybe two grand to his name, so the fat tail of the distribution is fiction and the real average is more like £8 — but Dev said that's a fact about his bank account, not about the bet, and he'd happily sell me the identical bet guaranteed by a rich uncle. My friend Priya, who did an economics degree, said the answer is that money is worth less to me the more of it I have, so you should do the napkin maths in units of how much I actually care rather than in pounds, and then the sum settles down and the fair price is small and boring. Dev shot back that he can just rescale the game — tiny first prize, slower doubling, whatever — and beat any curve I draw for how much I care about money; he claims there's an actual theorem that as long as my happiness with money has no ceiling, someone can build a bet I'd have to value infinitely highly, so Priya's fix only works if you cap how happy money can ever make me, which seems obviously false. I don't know whether that theorem is real or whether he was just winning the argument by talking faster.

What's actually bugging me isn't whether to take the bet, it's that I can't work out what kind of question I'm even asking. When I say it's not worth £50 to me, am I saying something about the bet or something about my nerve? Two things I keep going back and forth on. First, this is a one-off — I'm never going to play this game a thousand times, so the "infinite average" is an average over a run of plays that will never happen, and I can't tell whether that means averages are simply the wrong tool for a single case or whether I'm just being a coward. Second, if the honest answer is that the fair price depends on how much I happen to care about money, then the price is whatever I feel like and there's nothing for maths to say, which can't be right either. And here's the thing that made me write this down: if you offer me £1,000 cash instead of the ticket, I take the cash without blinking. £1,000,000, still the cash. I have no idea where that stops, and no idea whether that's a mistake I'm making or the correct response, with the napkin maths being the thing that's broken. I did one philosophy module in first year so I'm not allergic to this sort of question, but I don't know the literature at all. I want someone to lay out the actual argument, tell me which of the moves at that table were doing real work and which were noise, and if this is a known puzzle with a standard name, tell me the name but then tell me what the state of play actually is, because I suspect the standard answer isn't the whole story.
