ok so I've been trying to work out what a bunch of work-time arrangements actually assume, and I keep going in circles. take three that seem to point in different directions: France's legal 35-hour week; Japan's karoshi, where death from overwork gets recognized and compensated as a work injury; and the American habit of making salaried professionals "exempt" from overtime, so a 50-hour week costs the employer nothing and firms dress it up as "unlimited vacation." throw in the newer "right to disconnect" laws too. these can't all be running on the same picture of what work and leisure are for.

what I want is the philosophical commitments each of these presupposes. specifically: does treating leisure as something the law has to protect presuppose leisure is a right, or constitutive of a good life, while the exempt-salary and unlimited-vacation practices presuppose it's a commodity you can trade away? does karoshi compensation presuppose a causal picture where chronic, ordinary work -- not some discrete accident -- can be what kills you, and if so what theory of responsibility does that rest on: the employer's, the worker's, or some vague "the system's"? is the 35-hour cap assuming leisure is restorative (you rest so you work better) or an end in itself? is any of this a legacy of the Protestant work ethic or a break from it?

and then the part I actually care about: which of these commitments are genuinely forced by the practice and which could be swapped out while the practice stays intact? where is the whole thing philosophically underdetermined, such that several different theories of what work is worth would license the exact same arrangements? don't just hand me the standard protestant-work-ethic-versus-leisure story, I've read that one -- I want the implicit theory reconstructed from the practices themselves, and tell me which of the three is hardest to make consistent with the others.

also, say how confident you are in the reconstruction and flag the one claim you're least sure about.
