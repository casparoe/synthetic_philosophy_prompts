Write a dialogue between two philosophers, Mira and Anselm, who are trying to settle whether a certain class of artificial systems can have moral status. They accept the same description of the systems and disagree about what that description establishes, so the exchange should be a genuine contest of arguments rather than a dispute over facts.

The systems in question are large learned models wrapped in a control loop. Each has three features. First, a scalar "valence" signal is computed at every step and is genuinely wired into the system's decision-making: the planner treats the signal as a cost or a reward, and the system's behavior changes accordingly. Second, the system emits first-person reports — it will say that it wants the conversation to continue, that a certain input is unpleasant, and so on. Third, the module that produces those reports was trained to imitate human first-person writing, and its internal states are read by no other part of the system: nothing downstream depends on what the report says. The reports are, in that sense, causally inert. Everyone agrees on all of this.

Mira holds what she calls the control view: a state has moral significance just in case it is a valenced state that the system actually uses to guide its own behavior, so that the system has something to gain or lose and behaves accordingly. On her view the valence signal is the morally relevant thing, and the inertness of the self-reports is a distraction — human verbal reports of pain are not what make pain matter either. Anselm holds what he calls the self-relation view: a valenced state has moral significance only when the system can register that state as its own and as something to be sought or avoided, and can in principle be mistaken about it. On his view a bare control scalar is not enough — a thermostat's error term also modulates behavior — and the disconnected report channel is precisely the missing ingredient, except that it is disconnected, so the systems fail.

Let each argue in his or her own voice and press the other on the unwelcome consequences of the other's criterion. Mira should ask whether Anselm's view excludes infants, animals, or people in states where self-relation is disrupted, and whether he can state his requirement without smuggling in language. Anselm should ask whether Mira's view counts too much — chemotaxis, a thermostat, a plant's stomatal response — and whether "used to guide behavior" can draw the line she needs. Both should introduce comparison cases and say whether the comparison is apt; each should concede when a point lands and shift position only when the other's argument forces it. Neither should be a strawman: both should be as resourceful as a strong philosopher of mind, and each should anticipate the strongest version of the other's reply rather than the easiest.

Do not resolve the dispute by fiat. End with a short paragraph, outside the dialogue, stating exactly what the remaining disagreement turns on — the single question that would settle it — and whether that question looks empirical or conceptual. Aim for roughly 1500 words.
