I am a retired experimental physicist — forty years of laboratory work, mostly spectroscopy — and since retiring I have been working my way through the philosophical tradition that I never had time for when I was younger. This term I am enrolled in a continuing-education seminar on medieval political thought, and we have spent several sessions on Aquinas and the question of what may be done about a tyrant. I find myself stuck on a tension I cannot resolve on my own. In the treatment of sedition in the Summa Theologiae, Aquinas argues that a tyrannical government is not just, because it is directed to the private good of the ruler rather than to the common good, and so disturbing it is not sedition; but he immediately qualifies this with the warning that if the resulting disturbance brings greater harm to the multitude than the tyranny did, it should not be attempted, and he locates the sin of sedition chiefly in those who stir up the multitude. When he turns to the tyrant as such, he appears to distinguish between a usurper, whose killing he treats as praiseworthy, and a legitimate ruler who has turned tyrannical, where he denies private persons any licence to act and reserves the matter to public authority or to God. My seminar argued for a session about whether that distinction is principled or merely prudential, and I came away unsatisfied.

I would like you to read Shakespeare's Julius Caesar through this material, because I suspect the play is a better test of Aquinas than the seminar's hypotheticals were. Consider Brutus deciding alone in his orchard at night, before any public deliberation, on the basis of what Caesar might become rather than anything he has done — a private judgement about the common good, which is precisely what Aquinas's second case forbids. Consider that Caesar has three times refused the crown, that he is deaf in one ear and has fallen down in a fit in the marketplace, and that the conspirators kill him before he has done anything that could be called tyrannical. Consider that the killing is nevertheless staged as a public ritual, with the conspirators crying peace, freedom, and liberty and bathing their hands in the blood, as though a conspiracy of senators were the public authority Aquinas requires. And then consider what follows: Antony's soliloquy over the body, in which Caesar's spirit is summoned as ranging for revenge and letting slip the dogs of war; the riot that follows the funeral oration; the mob tearing apart Cinna the poet because he shares a name with a conspirator; and the ghost that meets Brutus at Philippi. That last sequence looks to me like a dramatization of Aquinas's own caveat about the greater harm of the disturbance, and perhaps of something the caveat does not capture — that the tyranny is not extinguished when the man is killed, because it migrates into the crowd.

Give me a committed reading rather than a survey of possibilities. Take a definite position on whether the play vindicates Aquinas's distinction between the usurper and the legitimate ruler turned tyrant, or whether it collapses that distinction by making every tyrant a tyrant only in prospect; say which scenes settle the question for you and which cut against your reading; and be explicit about where the play resists the Thomistic frame and where you are bending the play to fit it. I would also like your judgement on the point my seminar could not settle: whether Shakespeare shows us something about the survival of tyrannical power after the tyrant's death that Aquinas's account, with its clean division between private and public action, has no room for. Where you are uncertain, say so plainly and give your reasons for the position you nevertheless hold.
