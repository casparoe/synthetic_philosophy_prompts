I'm a novelist — historical fiction, mostly the 1830s and 40s in England with one foot stuck in the 1790s — and I've been circling a methodological knot for about two years that I'd like you to help me pull apart. Take the role of a philosopher of history with a good editor's instincts: I don't want a tour of what the various schools say, I want an audit of me. Before you answer, brainstorm a little on your own about how the commitments below might interact, and then give me a real verdict instead of a diplomatic it depends. For context, this started at a literary festival panel last month where somebody used presentism as an accusation and I realised I couldn't say what I actually thought, and my editor has been telling me for months that the third act of the new book needs to feel inevitable, which may or may not be related to all this.

Here's roughly where I've landed, and I suspect at least two of these are quietly at war, though I genuinely can't tell which: (1) the Whig interpretation of history — reading the past as a build-up to us and grading people by how much they contributed to what we happen to approve of now — is the cardinal sin of writing about the past, and I'd honestly rather my books be useless than flattering; (2) to render a past person properly you have to inhabit their sense of what was obvious and what was possible, and a failure of that sympathy ruins a novel faster than a wrong button; (3) novels aren't history — I compress, invent, omit, and I owe the reader a truth rather than the archive; (4) some things really did get better — the abolition of chattel slavery, married women's legal personhood, not dying at thirty of an infected tooth — I'm not a relativist about that, and I'd think less of a novelist who pretended to be; (5) nothing in history was inevitable, at every point there were live alternatives, and the people who lost weren't fools or dress rehearsals for us; (6) I don't want to write museum pieces, I want someone in 2025 to close the book feeling that the past is load-bearing and that the fights in it are still being decided, which is honestly why I write about it at all; and (7) I refuse to be neutral toward my villains — I won't stage a plantation as a debate between two equal sides, some of my characters convictions are simply bad and the prose should know it.

What I want from you: go through these pairwise, tell me which pairs are in genuine tension and which only look like it, rank the genuine ones by severity rather than treating them all as equally bad, and for each say what specifically would have to give — the doctrine or the craft habit — and what it would cost me to give it up. If there's a single revision that cleans up the most trouble, name that one at the end. And be concrete about craft where you can: if the trouble is formal, tell me what it would actually look like on the page, because write with more irony is not a fix I can use.
