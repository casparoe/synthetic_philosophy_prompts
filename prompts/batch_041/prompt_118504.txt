I have come to think that the Gibbard–Satterthwaite theorem settles a practical question and not merely a technical one. If every social choice rule that accepts unrestricted rankings over three or more alternatives and is not dictatorial can be profitably misreported by some voter at some profile, then the search for a manipulation-proof democratic rule should be abandoned, and reform should aim instead at rules whose manipulation is difficult, self-limiting, or harmless. What would count against this? Sort the escape routes by how much of the conclusion they genuinely salvage — restricting the preference domain to single-peaked or otherwise structured rankings, admitting cardinal or interpersonally comparable utility, randomization, and the gap between the theorem's existence claim and the observed frequency of strategic voting — and say which of them survive only by stretching the theorem's definitions. I would also like to know what my position predicts that its rivals do not, and whether, once I have conceded all of these qualifications, anything remains that could refute it.
