I am working through Machiavelli's chapter on fortune in The Prince and have run into a puzzle I cannot resolve. What follows is my own paraphrase of the argument, not a quotation, since I am working from memory and a rough translation.

Machiavelli grants that fortune governs half of our actions, but only half, leaving the rest, or nearly the rest, to us. He then offers two images. In the first, fortune is a violent river that floods the plains, uproots trees, and destroys buildings; nothing resists it, yet in calm seasons men can build dikes and embankments, so that when the water rises again it does either no harm or less. Italy, he says, is a country without such dikes, which is why its floods have been so ruinous. In the second image, fortune is a woman, and the man who wishes to master her must beat and coerce her; the impetuous and audacious succeed with her, the cautious do not. He adds that men prosper when their way of proceeding suits the times and fail when it does not, and that a man cannot readily change the temperament that is his.

What I want help with is three things. First, are these two images doing the same work, or are they at odds? The river suggests fortune is overwhelming but can be blunted by preparation made in advance; the woman suggests fortune is not merely blunted but conquered, and that audacity rather than preparation is what conquers her. If preparation is the answer, audacity looks like a different and possibly incompatible prescription. Second, and more troubling: building dikes takes foresight, and foresight about which flood is coming seems to be precisely what fortune controls. If a man's temperament is fixed and suits the times only by luck, then virtù — the capacity to prepare, to change course, to meet the occasion — appears to be something we have only when fortune has already been kind. Does Machiavelli have the resources to avoid this, or is the half he assigns to us a way of keeping free will and responsibility in the picture rather than a conclusion he argues for? Third, what does Cesare Borgia's failure do to the thesis? He is Machiavelli's model of virtù, yet he was undone when his father died and he himself fell ill at the same moment, a conjunction Machiavelli attributes to an extraordinary and extreme malignity of fortune. If the most virtuous man in the book is defeated by fortune, in what sense does fortune govern only half?

Please explain what the chapter is actually claiming and where I am misreading it. Do not give me the standard line that Machiavelli thinks both virtù and fortune are real; I want to know how the specific images are supposed to fit together, and whether the account is consistent or deliberately left unresolved.
