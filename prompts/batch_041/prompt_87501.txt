I am a third-year PhD student in cognitive science, and I have been asked to run a 75-minute session for a mixed methods-and-foundations workshop: roughly 14 participants, mostly graduate students in philosophy, psychology, and machine learning, none of whom will have been told anything in advance about what the session is really about. What I need from you is a complete, runnable activity — rules, materials, timing, and debrief — that makes the group feel the force of the problem of concept induction from examples: that a finite set of examples never pins down the concept, and that our sense of having "gotten it" is supplied by something other than the data. I want them to walk into that wall themselves rather than be told about it.

The core mechanic I have in mind, which you should develop or improve on, is a Bongard-style induction game. A problem is a card with two panels; each panel shows a small collection of simple figures, and the hidden rule separates the left panel from the right. Participants see several solved problems, propose the rule, and then commit to a prediction on a held-out test card before I reveal the answer. The pivot of the session should be a deliberately underdetermined problem: every figure in the training panels is consistent with at least two genuinely different rules, and those two rules disagree about the test card. I want the room split between two camps that are each fully consistent with all the evidence, a visible moment of commitment, and then a reveal that settles nothing about which rule is right — only which rule the designer happened to have in mind.

Please build the activity so that it does the following:

- It runs with no prior knowledge. Nobody needs to have heard of Bongard problems, Goodman's grue, or anything else; the vocabulary and the point should arrive after the experience, not before. If you want to gesture at the classic framing in the debrief, keep it optional and do not let the activity depend on participants recognizing it.
- The rules and incentives are arranged so that the discovery happens in what the group does. They should produce the competing rules themselves, commit to predictions under some mild stake (points, a small token, or simply the cost of being visibly wrong in front of peers — no real money), and only then see the discriminating case.
- There is a second phase in which participants design an underdetermined problem of their own for another group: they construct a training set where two different rules fit, and the other group must guess the intended rule. This is where I expect the real force of the point to land, since designing the trap forces them to see that the intended rule is just one of an open-ended family, and that a sufficiently inventive guesser can always find a rule that fits the data and predicts the opposite of what the designer meant.

Concretely, give me: a materials list (how many problems, what the figures should be, whether they are drawn, printed, or projected, and how to keep the hand-drawn design cheap and legible); the exact rules and scoring; a minute-by-minute timeline for the 75 minutes including the design round and the debrief; facilitator instructions, including what to say and, more importantly, what not to say at each stage; and a debrief that maps specific moments in the game onto the philosophical points — underdetermination by data, the need for a hypothesis space or a prior over "natural" properties, the difference between fitting the examples and latching onto the intended concept, and why a rule that fits everything so far can still be arbitrarily bad going forward. Do not let the debrief become a lecture that re-teaches the game; it should name what they already did.

I also want you to be honest with me about the seams:

- Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this and how to handle it. I am especially worried that the design round will stall, that one participant will dominate the induction, or that the group will treat the ambiguous problem as a trick or as my mistake rather than as the point.
- Tell me which feature of the underlying idea the activity cannot reproduce and that I will have to supply in words — for instance, that a handful of hand-built cases can only gesture at the general claim that any finite data set is consistent with endlessly many rules, and that the game cannot decide whether our preference for simple or natural rules is innate, learned, or merely conventional.
- If you think a different staging of the same idea would work better than the two-panel cards, say so and give me that alternative in the same detail; but the final plan you hand me must be single and runnable, not a menu.
