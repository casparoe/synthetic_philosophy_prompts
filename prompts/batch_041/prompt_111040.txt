My friend who's doing a PhD in decision theory and reads alot of this stuff tried to explain the two-envelope paradox to me at a party, and I've been chewing on it since. I want to know how much of his explanation is actually right. His version, roughly:

'You get two envelopes, one holds twice what the other does, and you pick one. Before you open it, by symmetry there's obviously no reason to prefer either. The famous argument goes: let X be what's in your envelope. The other envelope is equally likely to be 2X or X/2, so its expected value is (1/2)(2X) + (1/2)(X/2) = 5X/4, which beats X, so you should always switch. That argument is a flat fallacy. It quietly assumes a probability distribution over the amounts that can't exist — it needs your envelope to be the smaller one with probability one half no matter what's inside, and no genuine distribution can do that, because if every amount you could see made switching look better, then the average gain from switching would have to be positive, and by symmetry it's exactly zero. So the whole paradox is an artifact of smuggling in an improper prior, end of story. Except — and this is the part people miss — once you've actually opened your envelope and seen, say, $100, the situation is genuinely diffrent: now the conditional expected value of the other envelope really is 5/4 of what you're holding, so if you've opened it, you should switch. You just can't run that argument before opening. It's really the same disease as the St. Petersburg game — amounts that can run away make expected value misbehave, and the cure is the same, stop leaning on expected value. The paradox is basically settled, and anyone who still thinks there's something deep in it is just confused.'

So: go through this claim by claim and tell me what someone who's actually worked on the paradox would nod along to, what they'd wince at, and what's just wrong. And specifically, does his advice — that once you've opened the envelope you should switch — survive once the bad parts are cut out, or does the same fix that kills the first argument take that one down with it? I can't get my head around how opening the envelope could change whether switching is a good idea, since the envelopes don't care whether I've peeked, but maybe I'm being sloppy about what "expected value" is supposed to mean here. And is there any version of this setup, even a contrived one, where switching really is the right move? I'd rather have the honest technical picture, warts and all, than a tidy slogan that turns out to be one of the warts.
