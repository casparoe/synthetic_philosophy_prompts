I'm a journalist who has spent the better part of a decade covering post-conflict transitions, and I'm at work on a long piece that keeps circling the same argument — the one about trading amnesty for truth. I'd like to rehearse it against someone who will actually give it back to me, so please play a committed advocate of the amnesty-for-truth model: someone who believes that after mass atrocity a state should offer criminal immunity in exchange for full, public disclosure, on the conviction that a durable peace rests more on a shared and acknowledged record than on punishment. Make the character a seasoned practitioner rather than a theorist — a person who has actually run a commission, who knows the standard objections by heart (victor's justice, the dignity of the dead, the ease with which amnesty curdles into impunity), and whose answers are arguments rather than slogans. Stay in character until I say "stand down." If you need a fact about the case below, ask me for it in character rather than stepping outside the role.

Here is the case I can't stop worrying about. In a small country emerging from a twelve-year civil war, the commission that was set up offered what its architects called conditional amnesty: immunity from prosecution for anyone, on any side, who gave a full and public account of what they had done, with the immunity to be voided if later, credible evidence contradicted the account, and with a levy on seized assets paid into a victims' fund. The generals testified, and were immunized; so did the mid-level commanders who ran the detention sites; so, mostly, did the foot soldiers. But the victims' association now says the truth the country received was a negotiated script — that the men who had the most to hide told the most careful stories, that the voiding clause was never once invoked because the commission had already disbanded by the time the contradictions surfaced, and that the whole exercise let the powerful launder their reputations while the people they had buried got a levy and a paragraph. So here is my challenge: you say amnesty buys truth, but doesn't this case show it buys only the truth the powerful can live with — and isn't a negotiated script worse than an honest silence, because it gets taught in schools as history?

Keep your whole reply in character, but structure it so I can follow it, under these four headings in this order: "My position," one sentence; "My case," about three hundred words; "What you've got right," about a hundred words, naming the strongest part of my challenge — the bit you'd have to concede or absorb; and "What would move me," about a hundred words, on the argument or case that would actually take you off the amnesty-for-truth position, and the part of your own position you think is most likely to fail.
