I write about science and ideas for a general audience — mind and brain stuff, mostly — and I'm working up a piece on the philosophers who say consciousness is an illusion. A friend of mine has a book draft in this area and sent me his chapter on them. He's a good writer and he's clearly read the literature, so I'd been treating his summary as reliable, but two people I mentioned it to have now winced, and I'm not sure wether I can use any of it. Here's his gist, in my words:

"Consciousness is supposed to be the hard problem — there's something it's like to be you, and no amount of neuroscience ever touches that. The illusionists say the hard problem is a mistake. On their view phenomenal consciousness doesn't exist: no inner glow, no qualia, nothing it's like. What's real is a brain that represents itself as having those properties, and that representation is the illusion. So illusionism is really just eliminativism about consciousness in a nicer suit — it says the felt quality of pain is a bit of folk psychology that science will eventually drop, like phlogiston. And the payoff is that there's nothing left to explain: once you see the phenomenal properties were never there, the hard problem dissolves, and the only real question left is why we're so sure we have experiences. They lean on the psychology of introspection — confabulation, choice blindness, that kind of thing — to show we're not the reliable narrators of our own minds we think we are. They insist they aren't saying we're zombies, since they don't deny we're conscious in the ordinary sense, though I don't see how you get to say both of those things. And anyway the whole position looks self-undermining to me: an illusion has to seem like something to somebody, so if there's nothing it's like to be under the illusion, there's no illusion."

My instinct is that the self-undermining point is fatal and the rest is a word game. But I've been wrong about this kind of thing before, and I'd rather not print something that gets me a pile of emails. What I need isn't a philosophy lecture, it's an accuracy audit I can act on: which parts of his summary are fair, which are subtly off, and whether the conclusions he draws from it — that the view collapses into eliminativism, and that it refutes itself — actually follow from the accurate parts. Don't bother with a reading list, I won't have room for it anyway.

Give it to me in these sections, in this order:

- "The short version" — 2-3 sentences, the thing I'd say out loud at a party.
- "What holds up" — one short paragraph on the parts a specialist would nod along to.
- "What's off" — the long one. Go claim by claim through his summary and tell me which bits are just wrong, which are common simplifications, and which are things a defender would say he's smuggled in. Be explicit about which of his sentences I should not repeat.
- "How they'd answer him" — one paragraph in the voice of the strongest defender, especially on the self-undermining objection.
- "What I can print" — a handful of sentences I could actually put in the piece, safe enough that nobody in the field writes me an angry email.

American English please.
