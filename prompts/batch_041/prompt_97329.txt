I cover science and ideas for a magazine, and I'm putting together a long feature on the therapy/enhancement line -- you know, the distinction everyone in bioethics reaches for whenever a new drug or gene edit or brain implant shows up, the one that's supposed to tell you whether we're curing someone or upgrading them. I've been following two camps that have been sniping at each other for years and I'm tired of writing "the debate rages on." On one side are the people who insist the line is real and load-bearing: medicine is about restoring normal functioning, and going past that is a different kind of act with a different kind of justification, even if the edges are fuzzy. On the other are the continuum people who say the line is a fiction we tell ourselves -- that "normal" is either a statistical artifact or a social norm dressed up as biology, that the same pill is therapy for one person and enhancement for the next, and that policy should stop pretending it can draw the line at all. What I want from you is the joint statement the two camps could actually both put there names to if they locked themselves in a room for two days. Not a summary of who's winning -- a document both would sign.

So: a numbered list of claims they agree on (about what "normal functioning" even means, about why the distinction keeps getting used anyway, about what would count as a decisive case either way). Then the disagreements stated precisely, each phrased the way that side would want its own view put, not a strawman of it. Then the cruxes -- the sub-questions where settling things would definately move somebody -- and I'd bet at least one of them is whether there's any baseline that isn't either circular or just the population average, and another is whether a fuzzy line can still be doing honest work in medicine even if you can't state it cleanly. For each crux give me a concrete case both sides would agree bears on it, and write down what each side predicts about that case in advance. Some cases I keep coming back to and want you to fold in: a memory drug that brings someone with mild age-related decline back to average versus the same drug given to a sharp twenty-year-old; growth hormone for a kid who's short but not "diseased"; a prosthetic that replaces a lost limb but outperforms the original; and an antidepressant that lifts a clinically depressed person to normal versus one that lifts a normal person to unusually cheerful. Oh, and one more that ties me in knots -- if enhancement is relative to your own baseline rather than the population's, then "restoring" a person who was always above average starts to look like enhancement, so can the distinction even be stated without first picking a baseline, and does the choice of baseline beg the question? Keep it tight enough that a smart reader who's never opened a bioethics journal could still follow it.
