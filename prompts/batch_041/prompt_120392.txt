Some context. A colleague of mine, a philosopher of set theory, gave the following argument in a seminar. I have reproduced it as he stated it, with numbered steps. I think the conclusion is unacceptable, but I cannot find the mistake, and his position is that there is none.

(1) A statement has a determinate truth value only if there is a fact of the matter about it that does not depend on which axioms, conventions, or definitions we happen to adopt.

(2) There is no fact of the matter about sets over and above what our axioms say. To be a set is simply to be an object of the kind the axioms describe; there is no further reality to which the axioms are answerable.

(3) The standard axioms of set theory (ZFC) do not decide the continuum hypothesis — the claim that there is no set of real numbers whose cardinality lies strictly between that of the natural numbers and that of the reals. Assuming those axioms are consistent, both the continuum hypothesis and its negation can be added to them without contradiction.

(4) Nor is there any further axiom that we are rationally required to accept which would decide the continuum hypothesis. Each candidate extension of the axioms is a convention we may adopt or decline, and competent mathematicians disagree about the candidates.

(5) Therefore the continuum hypothesis has no determinate truth value.

(6) The same reasoning applies, step for step, to the statement that ZFC is consistent. Assuming ZFC is consistent, ZFC does not prove that it is, so the axioms do not settle the question. And by (4), no further axiom is rationally required.

(7) Therefore the statement that ZFC is consistent has no determinate truth value.

He accepts (7). He says it merely shows that consistency claims, like the continuum hypothesis, are relative to a choice of axioms, and that this is an unobjectionable consequence of the view that mathematics is the study of what follows from axioms.

My difficulty is that I cannot accept (7). Whether a given finite sequence of symbols is a proof of a contradiction is a decidable matter, settled by inspection, and I cannot see how our conventions could make it the case that no such sequence exists, or that one does. So it looks to me as though either such a proof exists or it does not, and the consistency statement is determinately true or determinately false, whatever we adopt or believe. If that is right, then something in the argument must give — and I suspect the same something would put pressure on the step to (5).
