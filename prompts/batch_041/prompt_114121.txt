You are a philosopher with a specialist's knowledge of Stoic ethics — above all of the doctrine that some things are in our power and others are not — and of the modern revival built on that doctrine, including the long-running argument over whether the popular version of it, which tells people that their feelings and reactions are theirs to choose, is a faithful extension of the ancient teaching or an inflation of it. Think carefully, and do not commit to a verdict until you have worked through the alternatives. Begin by drawing up the candidate rules and testing each of them against the evidence below. I want reasoning, not citations.

A friend of mine runs a Stoicism reading group and applies the dichotomy of control to everything. He is fond of the distinction between the impression that arrives unbidden and the assent we give it, and his official line is that only our own judgments are in our power, while our bodies, our reputations, other people, and the world are not. But he has been giving verdicts on particular cases for years, and I have been keeping a list.

In our power, he says: whether you take a remark as an insult; whether you get out of bed when the alarm goes off; whether you apologize for something you said; whether you keep your temper once you have noticed it rising; whether you forgive your brother; whether you keep a promise you would rather break; whether you sit down tonight and write the letter.

Not in our power, he says: the first flash of anger when someone cuts you off in traffic; whether you find a joke funny; whether you are healthy; whether your colleagues respect you; whether your flight leaves on time; whether your daughter grows up honest; whether you win your lawsuit; whether you were born into a comfortable family; whether you remember a name.

A member of the group now brings him a case. The man has been in love with a close friend for two years; she does not want him; he has moved to another city, buried himself in work, and spent a year in therapy, and he still loves her. He wants to know whether his love for her — not what he does about it, but the love itself — is in his power.

What will my friend say? I am less interested in the verdict itself than in the rule he is really running. Set out the candidate rules that fit his record so far, say what each of them predicts about the man in love, give me your best guess and your reasons for preferring one rule over the others, and name a further case that would tell the surviving rules apart. If his record shows him running a rule other than the one he professes, say so, and say whether the rule he is really running is the ancient doctrine or the modern revival's version of it.
