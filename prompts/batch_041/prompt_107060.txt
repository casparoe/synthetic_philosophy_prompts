I am a philosophy graduate student preparing for a seminar on the ethics of war, and I have spent the last week arguing with two friends about Anscombe's claim that Truman was a murderer. One of them thinks the claim is fanaticism; the other agrees with it but cannot explain why it does not equally condemn the crews who firebombed German cities. I do not want a summary of what Anscombe is generally supposed to have held, and I do not want citations or reading suggestions — I want to argue with her directly. Please play Anscombe herself, in the first person, and stay in character until I write the word stop. She should be blunt, unsentimental, and entirely unimpressed by appeals to consequences, by the authority of statesmen, or by the intention/foresight distinction when that distinction is being used as a licence; she may be sharp, even rude, but she must argue rather than merely assert, and she must make any religious or Thomistic premise she relies on explicit so that I can dispute it. She should be willing to press me on what I actually mean and to concede a point only when it is genuinely correct, but she must never concede that the bombings were anything other than the deliberate killing of the innocent, and she must not step out of character to explain that she is role-playing or to refer me to her own published work. Keep each reply to a paragraph or two, so that it stays a conversation rather than becoming a lecture.

Here is my opening move, which I would like her to answer. I grant that Truman intended the deaths in the thin sense that he knew they would occur and chose the cities precisely because they were densely populated. What I deny is that this makes him a murderer rather than a man who did something monstrous for a great good. My reason is this: suppose that on the day before the drop he had learned, from a source he trusted, that both cities had been secretly evacuated and that the bombs would fall on empty streets. He would have dropped them anyway. His aim was to demonstrate the weapon and break the Japanese government's will, and the demonstration would have worked on that government even without corpses. So the deaths were not what he wanted, not even as a means; they were a price he was prepared to pay for something he wanted more. If that is the correct description of his mind, then the word murderer rests on a psychological claim about him that cannot be established from the outside, and you are using it to express revulsion at a state of affairs rather than to name an act of will you can actually identify. And please do not answer that I am making excuses for him — I am not defending his intentions, I am denying that you can know them.
