Ok, so — I've been lurking on rationalist blogs and forums for something like a decade, and the thing I keep bumping into is that Reflection principle (van Fraassen's, I'm pretty sure). The gist as I understand it: a rational agent should defer to their future self. If you're certain that tomorrow you'll be 90% confident in some claim, you should be 90% confident right now — because your future self has all your current evidence plus whatever else they've picked up. It's a coherence constraint on your credences across time. I know it's officially about credences and not about How To Live, and that stretching it into life advice is itself kind of the joke. But that's what I want.

Write me an advice-column installment, the Dear-Abby / Slate kind, deadpan, maybe 700-800 words, where the columnist is a sincere Reflection maximalist and every bit of the humor comes from the arguments being *right*. No winking, no "of course, I'm being ironic", no meta-jokes. A reader who knows the literature should recognize that whoever wrote it knows what they're satirizing.

The letters should each squeeze a different real joint of the view (tweak them if you want, but keep the pressure points):

1. A writer who is confident — with good reason — that their future self will have been convinced by a cult, or will have spent a year reading one very bad blog, and will therefore hold a belief the writer currently thinks is nuts. Reflection says defer. Have the columnist deal with the standard escape hatch — you only defer insofar as you take your future self to be reliable, and you can have defeaters about your future self's competence — without letting the principle collapse into "defer unless you don't feel like it."

2. A Ulysses case: the writer is about to be offered something (a bet, a drug, a terrible romance, pick one) and wants to precommit to refusing, because they know their future self will cave. But precommitment is precisely refusing to defer to the future self who'll have more information. Let the tension be real. If the honest answer is "then just cave now", let it land rather than getting waved away.

3. Someone asking why the deference only runs one direction. Their past self believed all sorts of things they now think are false; why is no respect owed backward? The asymmetry has an actual reason (evidence accumulates, so more information later and less earlier), and the columnist should give it.

4. A writer who asks whether it extends from credences to preferences — plans, tastes, who they want to become. Their future self will want things they currently find contemptible. Does Reflection say they should want those things now? Let the columnist squirm honestly instead of dodging.

Keep the columnist internally consistent the whole way through. No slipping into "well, in a sense we're all different people" or into compatibilist mush. If they have to concede something, they concede it the way that columnist would concede it.

Then, after the column, add a short note — a few sentences or bullets — saying where the piece leans on contested readings versus where it's just the standard statement of the view, and flag anything a philosopher would call a stretch. Not "some disagree" — say which parts, specifically. E.g. is Reflection even a requirement of rationality, how much work is that reliability proviso doing, does it really conflict with precommitment, is the preference extension even a version of the same principle or a different one.

No citations, no reading lists, no dates, and don't put words in any real author's mouth with quotation marks — neither of us can check those, so just argue it out in plain language.

Last line, machine readable, exactly like this:

Verdict: <one sentence on what the column ends up claiming>

Also, seperate paragraphs for each letter and each reply, please.
