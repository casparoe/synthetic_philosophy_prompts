I'm in a reading group on early modern philosophy of space and we're doing the Scholium near the start of the Principia — the stretch right after the definitions of quantity of matter and quantity of motion, where Newton sets out his distinctions between absolute and relative time, space, place, and motion, and then argues for the absolute ones. I can follow the definitions fine. It's the argument he builds after them that I keep losing, and the secondary stuff I've read either compresses it into a paragraph or assumes I already see why it works. I'm not looking for a reading list, I just want the thing itself untangled.

Here's the argument as I've reconstructed it, in my own words (so don't treat my wording as the text's). He starts with a bucket hanging from a cord: you twist the cord up, let go, and watch. First stage: the vessel spins but the water hasn't caught up yet, the surface stays flat, and the water is in relative motion with respect to the vessel. Second stage: the water gradually takes up the spin until it's moving together with the vessel, so there's no relative motion between water and vessel anymore — yet now the surface is concave, climbing the sides, which he takes to show that the parts of the water are endeavouring to recede from the axis. Third stage: the vessel slows but the water keeps going, so relative motion reappears, now in the opposite direction, while the surface stays concave. His point seems to be that the effect which actually tells you about the water's motion — the curvature, the endeavour to recede — is at zero in the first stage when relative motion is greatest, and at its maximum in the second stage when relative motion is zero. So relative motion is neither the measure nor the cause of the effect. He then says true motion is not changed except by forces impressed on the body itself, while relative motion can be changed by forces acting on other things, and that the effects by which true motion is distinguished from relative are the forces of receding from the axis of circular motion.

Then there's the bit I find harder: two globes joined by a cord, spinning about their common centre in otherwise empty space. The tension in the cord tells you the quantity of their circular motion, and he says this quantity doesn't depend on any relation to surrounding bodies — take all other bodies away and the tension is still there.

My question is what exactly this establishes. Does it get him space as a thing in its own right, or only the weaker claim that there's a privileged state of rotation that can't be cashed out as motion relative to other bodies? I keep running into the claim that the bucket only refutes the crudest relationism — rotation relative to the vessel, or to nearby bodies — and leaves a smarter relationism untouched: the Mach-flavoured line that the relevant reference is the whole mass of the universe, so the globes are still rotating relative to something, just not to nearby stuff. Is that fair, or does his insistence that the quantity of true motion isn't defined by relation to other bodies (presumably including the fixed stars) actually shut that door? And if I grant him everything about motion, how much does he get about space? I suspect the globes do most of the work and the bucket is more of a vivid illustration, but I want to know whether the globes case really lands you in substantivalism or only in a non-relational notion of rotation that a relationist could still swallow by making the relation hold to the universe as a whole, or to some structure rather than to individual bodies.

So: walk me through the argument step by step, tell me which steps are load-bearing and which are decoration, say what a careful relationist (not the strawman) replies at each step, and tell me whether the conclusion about space survives if the conclusion about motion doesn't. Also, where is he inferring to the best explanation and where is he just stipulating what true motion shall mean? And is there a standard name for the objection I'm groping toward, or am I inventing a worry that isn't really one? Also flag anything I've got wrong in my paraphrase — I'm fairly sure I'm mangling at least one step, probably the third stage.
