I'm TAing a decision theory unit and I've got to get a problem sheet back to the students, but there's one answer I keep going back and forth on and I'd rather have a second pair of eyes on it than guess. The question was:

"(i) Counterfactual mugging. Omega, a perfect predictor, flips a fair coin. If it comes up heads, Omega pays you £10,000, but only if it predicted that you would pay £100 if it had come up tails. It comes up tails, Omega tells you so, and asks you for £100. What does causal decision theory tell you to do, and what does evidential decision theory tell you to do? Explain the reasoning behind each answer.

(ii) XOR blackmail. Omega sends you a message if and only if your house is infested with termites xor you pay Omega £1,000. (Termites would cost £1,000,000 to fix; Omega is a perfect predictor of your behaviour and is perfectly honest about all this.) You recieve the message. What do the two theories tell you to do here?

(iii) Do (i) and (ii) present the same kind of challenge to causal decision theory, or two seperate kinds? Justify your answer."

And here's what the student wrote:

"(i) In the mugging, CDT says refuse. The toss is over and Omega's prediction is already fixed, so handing over £100 cannot make the coin have come up heads or change what Omega did. Paying just loses you £100, so a causal decision theorist keeps the money. EDT says pay. Handing over the £100 is a sign that you're the sort of agent Omega predicts will pay, and Omega only hands over the £10,000 in the heads case when it made exactly that prediction. So paying raises the probability that the toss came up heads and that the £10,000 is yours, which is worth far more than the £100 it costs, so EDT pays. Everyone agrees that if you could pre-commit before the toss you should commit to paying; the interesting question is what to do when you're actually asked.

(ii) CDT says refuse here too. Whether you pay has no causal effect on whether your house is infested, since handing £1,000 to Omega doesn't kill any termites, so the causal reasoner keeps the money and takes their chances on the termites. EDT says pay. Since Omega sent the message, exactly one of the two disjuncts holds: either the house is infested and you refuse, or the house is clean and you pay. So paying is evidence that Omega sent the message because it predicted your payment rather than because of an infestation, which means the house is clean. £1,000 to dodge a £1,000,000 repair bill is a bargain, so EDT pays.

(iii) As I see it, the two cases pose the same kind of challenge to CDT. In both, the causal decision theorist refuses to pay and ends up worse off, while the evidentialist hands over the money and comes out ahead. Both are therefore counterexamples to CDT of the same shape: the theory insists that only causal influence counts, and that leads it to turn down a bet that is clearly worth taking. The only difference between the cases is the mechanism of the prediction, a coin toss in (i) and termites in (ii), not the kind of problem they raise for the causal theory."
