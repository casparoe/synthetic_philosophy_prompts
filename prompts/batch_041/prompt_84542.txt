I'm a junior in high school, and my civics teacher has assigned an essay in which we are to read a film through a philosophical lens, choosing our own film and our own idea; I would like your help thinking it through. I have chosen the 2008 film Swing Vote, directed by Joshua Michael Stern, in which a presidential election deadlocks and then comes down to New Mexico's five electoral votes, and, because of a malfunctioning voting machine, to the single ballot of Bud Johnson, a disengaged and heavy-drinking single father living in a trailer in Texico, who is given ten days to cast the deciding vote while both campaigns descend on him with flattery and bribes and his young daughter Molly tries to shame him into taking the choice seriously. What interests me is that the film's premise seems to be a deliberate answer to an argument that my friends and I have been arguing about for weeks, the one my teacher calls the inefficacy objection: that since no single vote, no single flight, and no single hamburger ever changes any outcome, an individual cannot be doing anything wrong by voting, flying, or eating meat, and cannot fairly be blamed for the results that only vast numbers of people together produce. The film imagines the one situation in which that reasoning collapses, and I want you to treat it as a thought experiment rather than as a comedy. My friend Priya says the film actually confirms the objection, since the only way one vote can matter is if everything else is exactly tied, which is a coincidence rather than a reason to vote; my friend Daniel says the film shows that being decisive was never the point, and that Bud's ballot matters because of what casting it says about who he is and whom he stands with. I suspect Priya is right but would be glad to be argued out of it.

My teacher handed out a page making a distinction she wants us to use, and I would like you to use it and also to stress-test it rather than merely illustrating it: the difference between being decisive, in the sense that the outcome would have been different had you acted otherwise, and being a member of the set of people whose combined actions produce the outcome, even though no member's action is decisive on its own. She says the interesting question is whether obligations and blame track the first or the second, and I would like the film to be made to press on that question. Please address, at least: whether the film stacks the deck by staging the exception rather than the rule, and whether that staging leaves the ordinary voter's situation untouched or instead reveals something the simple objection misses; what the film implies about where the value of a vote lies, given that Bud is ignorant, indifferent, and manipulated for most of the running time and yet is the one whose vote counts; whether the film's resolution, in which Bud stops drinking, reads the letters sent to him by strangers, and studies the issues, offers a genuine answer to the objection or merely substitutes sentiment for argument; whether the same analysis carries over, briefly and without straining the comparison, to individual contributions to climate change and to eating meat; and where the film resists the reading altogether, since much of it is a satire of campaign pandering and a story about a father and daughter rather than a story about individual responsibility, and I would rather my essay say so than force the philosophy onto scenes that will not bear it.

You need not quote the film or cite any sources, and I would prefer that you not, since I cannot check quotations and do not need a bibliography. What I want is a reading that is faithful in both directions, one that neither reduces the film to an illustration of a doctrine nor distorts the doctrine to fit the film, and that ends by saying what the film contributes to the debate over individual contributions to collective harms that the arguments alone do not. If the honest conclusion is that a two-hour comedy can dramatize the problem but not solve it, I would rather have that conclusion than a tidy one.
