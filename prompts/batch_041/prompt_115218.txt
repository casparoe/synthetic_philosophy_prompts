I'm a third-year PhD student writing a chapter on the aggregation problem in Scanlon-style contractualism — the difficulty that arises once you test principles by asking whether anyone could reasonably reject them, and then have to decide what to do with cases in which a principle imposes a modest burden on a very large number of people and a severe burden on a very small number, or the reverse. The standard repertoire is familiar to me: the thought that each person's complaint must be weighed on its own rather than summed with others'; the appeal to the strength of a single complaint; the suggestion that the reasonableness of a complaint, rather than any separate aggregative principle, is what does the work; and the worry that the whole apparatus quietly inherits a prioritarianism it never admits to. What I keep finding is that the same three or four cases get re-litigated while nobody asks which of the underlying questions is actually upstream of the others. My own working hunch — which I want you to attack, refine, or replace rather than simply endorse — is that almost all of the disagreement is downstream of two things: how a complaint is individuated (one person's total burden across a life, one burden at a time, one interest, or one claim about what one is owed under a fair scheme), and whether the anti-aggregation commitment is a constraint on the content of principles that can pass the test or instead a constraint on the kinds of reasons admissible inside the test. If that hunch is right, the individuation question is the live one and the rest is commentary; if it is wrong, I want to know which question is really doing the load-bearing work.

So please give me the open questions in this area that matter most, ordered by how much else depends on them — a dependency-aware agenda, not a survey. Rank by the number and importance of other questions that an answer would settle or dissolve, then by how tractable the question is for someone working at the level of theory alone (no empirical work, no formal modelling beyond what fits on a blackboard), and then by whether an answer would actually change what contractualists say about concrete cases. For each entry I want the reason it sits where it does, not just a description of the debate, and — where you can manage it in the space — a note on what development over the next two or three years would move it up or down the list. Please don't restrict yourself to the questions I have already named; if I am too close to the debate to see something (about whether the aggregation problem is even a problem for a theory whose verdicts concern wrongness rather than what to do, or about harms distributed across time and across people who do not yet exist), put it on the list and tell me why I have been missing it. I am not asking for citations, a reading list, or who said what — I know the cast of characters, and I would rather have the structure of the problem argued from first principles.

Give me the response as a numbered list of exactly five items, one or two dense sentences each, in my ranking order, with no introduction or conclusion.
