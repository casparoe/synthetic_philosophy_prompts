I'm a fourth-year PhD student in philosophy of mind — dissertation on self-models, and I'm already sick of it — and for the last eight months I've been the token philosopher on the ethics advisory board of a small startup that makes what the industry calls "digital legacy companions." You hand over years of someone's texts, emails, voicenotes, and you get a chatbot that talks in their voice. The board meets once a month for an hour and we re-litigate the same thing from scratch every time: last meeting we spent forty minutes on whether a dead seventeen-year-old's parents can license their son's messages to us, ran out of time, and the engineers shipped anyway. The other members are two lawyers, a palliative-care nurse, our CEO, and an investor who keeps saying "it's just a product." None of them have read any philosophy and I'm the only person in the room who knows what a strange loop is, so whatever I write has to be usable by them without a lecture first.

What I want is a decision procedure we can actually run in that hour on a single proposed product or deployment, and that I can hand to the nurse and the lawyers. Please build it on Hofstadter's picture of the self: the "I" as a self-referential pattern rather than a thing inside one skull, souls as coming in degrees rather than all-or-nothing, and the claim in I Am a Strange Loop that a person is partly instantiated in every brain that has modelled them — the reason he could say that after his wife died a substantial piece of her was still around, spread out across the people who had loved her. Don't worry about getting his exact wording or page numbers right, I'm not checking, and if you're unsure about a detail of the view just say what you're assuming and move on. What matters to me is that the procedure keeps the distinctions that do the real work — pattern vs. substrate, "how much of her is here" vs. "is this her," a self-modelling loop that can care about things and revise itself vs. a frozen trace of someone's output, the person persisting in the mourners vs. in the artifact — and quietly drops the ones that only matter to us in the seminar room.

So: the questions in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, which answers end the process with a no (and whether anything ends it with a clear yes), and which questions the procedure should hand back to a person as a values call rather than pretend to settle. Then run it on three cases: the dead teenager's parents; a hospice program where patients record a few hours of themselves while still lucid so their young kids can "ask dad things" afterwards; and a publisher who wants to use a model to keep putting out novels under a dead author's name. At least one of the three should come out the way I won't like — I have enormous sympathy for those parents and I suspect the procedure is going to make me say something cold to them, so I want to see exactly where that happens and whether it's the procedure's fault or mine. Finish with what the procedure can't do, where it will misfire, and the seperate places where it looks like a procedure but is really just me asserting Hofstadter and hoping nobody notices.
