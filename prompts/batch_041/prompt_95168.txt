I'm a second-year PhD student on a project that builds long-run forecasts for states and empires, and I've hit a conceptual snag that has come to feel more important than the modelling work I'm actually paid to do. The project runs on the outside view: to forecast whether a given polity will suffer a fiscal or territorial collapse over the next several decades, you assemble the reference class of comparable polities and take the base rate of collapse. My trouble is that for empires I cannot write down a reference class I believe in. Every membership rule I try — great-power standing, an extractive fiscal apparatus, military expenditure as a share of revenue, a certain rank in a hierarchy of tribute — either encodes the very outcome I am trying to forecast, or is so broad that the resulting base rate is a fact about my sampling choices rather than about empires. The distinction I have been using privately to keep this straight is between a class's membership rule (something I could apply to a case before its outcome is known) and its outcome label (what later befell its members); my suspicion is that for empires these two always coincide, so that what gets reported as a base rate is a hindsight summary wearing the costume of a forecast. I have taken to calling a class of that sort outcome-infected.

Please argue for the strong version of my suspicion: that the outside view is simply unavailable for imperial decline, because no reference class of empires is at once well defined ex ante and non-trivial, and that forecasters working on this subject should abandon base rates altogether rather than patch them up with multiple classes, sensitivity ranges, or broader denominators. I put this to a reading group and was told I was mistaking a practical difficulty for an impossibility, so I want the case made properly and without hedging — but I also want to be told, in the same breath, where the argument is weakest and what a defender of the outside view would say to break it.

Give me your answer as a numbered list of exactly five items, each one or two sentences long, with no introduction or conclusion.
