My two roomates have been going at each other for two days over a quiz from a psych class, and they've drafted me as referee. The quiz had two questions, and the class was told that afterwards one of your two answers would be drawn at random and actually played for money.

Question 1: (A) $2,500 for sure, or (B) an 80% chance of $3,200 and a 20% chance of nothing.
Question 2: (C) a 25% chance of $2,500 and a 75% chance of nothing, or (D) a 20% chance of $3,200 and an 80% chance of nothing.

Sam answered A and D. Ravi says her sheet is incoherent. Here's roughly how it's gone.

(1) Ravi: Question 2 is question 1 with every probability cut to a quarter and the leftover three quarters handed to nothing in both options. If A beats B, then a quarter-sized chance at A has to beat a quarter-sized chance at B. A quarter-sized chance at A is exactly C, and a quarter-sized chance at B is exactly D. She took the worse of the two gambles both times. That isn't a preference, it's a contradiction.

(2) Sam: The only thing that makes my answers conflict is the principle that scaling every probability in a pair by the same factor can't flip which option is better. That's a substantive claim about lotteries, not a rule of logic, and it's the claim in dispute. And the questions don't feel like the same question. In the first, A is a floor — I walk out with money whatever happens. In the second, nothing is the baseline and both options are just tickets, so the only thing to care about is which ticket. A principle that says I have to answer them the same way is a principle that tells me to ignore the one feature I actually care about.

(3) Ravi: Forget the axiom, then. Take a two-stage version. First I flip a coin that ends the game with 75% probability and pays nothing; with 25% probability we go to a second stage where you answer question 1 for real. The version where you'd pick A at stage two is the same thing as C; the version where you'd pick B is the same thing as D — same prizes, same chances, and the coin is out of your hands. So don't tell me the sure thing in A is doing the work: in that game you don't get the floor unless the coin lets you through and you then pick A. If you'd still pick A at stage two, you should have picked C. If you'd switch to B at stage two, then your A-over-B answer was the mistake. Either way one of the two answers on your sheet has to go.

(4) Sam: I'd pick A at stage two, obviously — if the coin lets me through, why on earth would I gamble? But I don't accept that the two-stage game with A in it is the same option as C. In the two-stage game there's no moment where I'm holding the $2,500 and hand it back. C is a ticket and nothing else. If you identify them, you're assuming that all that matters is the final distribution over prizes, which is the very thing you're supposed to be arguing for.

(5) Ravi: If you care about the path, then the same physical lottery is better or worse depending on the order I flip the coins in, and that's something I can charge you for. Set the coins up one way and you'll pay to have them rearranged; run a long enough sequence of these and you end up behind no matter how the coins land. And there's no reason to care about the order except that the description is tripping you up — the coins don't care who flips them.

(6) Sam: A plan I fix in advance doesn't get pumped. I decide once, before any coin moves, what I'll do at every stage, and then you have nothing to sell me. And second: the quiz itself assumes the thing you say I'm not allowed to deny. My sheet is a compound lottery, and one answer drawn at random and played only means anything if you can reduce a compound lottery to its chances over prizes.
