Bit of an odd request, but here goes. I'm a second-year PhD student in what my department calls "historical social science" (don't ask), and I've spent the past year slowly talking myself into a position about non-human causes that I keep failing to state cleanly, so let me state it flatly and you can tell me how it breaks. The claim: pathogens, climate regimes, and volcanic events can be actors in a historical explanation in the same sense that a merchant guild or a warlord coalition is -- not because they intend anything or reason or bear responsibility, but because the best explanation of an outcome has to cite what they did, and cite it in the same slot where we'd otherwise put a human agent's doings. The default alternatives in my field both seem bad to me: either you park the volcano in the "background conditions" box, where it can't explain anything on its own, or you call it a shock or a pressure, which is just environmental determinism wearing a lab coat. Two things I'll concede up front so you don't waste time on them: I don't think this commits me to saying any given collapse was inevitable or even probable in advance -- what the volcano did is the issue, not what it made likely -- and I don't think calling something an actor smuggles in intentions.

What I actually want is the case against it, and not the anthropomorphism complaints, which I've heard alot. Specifically: is there a shape of historical or archaeological evidence that would show the actor-talk is doing no explanatory work -- where we've pinned the non-human cause down completely and it still drops out of the best explanation? I've been using a rough test on myself and I'd like you to try to wreck it: if you can rewrite the explanation without mentioning the pathogen or the monsoon or the tephra layer and lose nothing that a working historian would care about, then it wasn't an actor. Where does that test give the wrong answer? What would it take to show the position quietly collapses back into determinism, or into something vacuous, where "the volcano acted" adds nothing over "the volcano happened, then things changed"? And what does my view predict that a boring multicausal account doesn't -- because if the honest answer is "nothing," I'd rather hear it now.

I do suspect it might be unfalsifiable, and I'd like to know whether that's fatal for a claim of this kind or just the cost of doing business with a framing claim. So: rank the objections by how much they should actually move me, tell me which ones a sympathetic defender would simply dig in on, and be straight about whether any of them is decisive rather than merely awkward. No need for citations or a reading list, I'm after the shape of the objections, not the litrature.
