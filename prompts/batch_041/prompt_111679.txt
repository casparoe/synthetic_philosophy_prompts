I teach the graduate Bayesian epistemology seminar in our department (philosophy, though a couple of the students drift in from stats) and I'm putting together this year's take-home paper. I want one long question that forces them to actually turn the crank instead of reciting the usual posterior-is-proportional-to-prior-times-likelihood line, which they can all do half-asleep. The setup I've landed on: an agent's credences over two propositions A and B start at P(A and B) = 0.2, P(A and not-B) = 0.1, P(not-A and B) = 0.3, P(not-A and not-B) = 0.4. She then recieves uncertain evidence bearing on A that moves her credence in A to 0.9, and later receives uncertain evidence bearing on B that moves her credence in B to 0.9. Both bits of evidence are to be processed by Jeffrey conditionalisation (probability kinematics), not by strict conditionalisation, since in neither case does she become certain of anything.

What I want the question to make them do is work out what the agent's final credences come to under the two possible orderings — A's evidence first then B's, and B's evidence first then A's — and then sit with the awkward discovery that the two orderings don't agree. I've checked the numbers myself so I know there's a real discrepancy and not just rounding noise, and I'd like them to report the final joint distributions side by side so the disagreement is staring them in the face. The crux I'm really after is *why* the order matters and what that shows. My own view is that the tidy move of just doing a single Jeffrey update on the four-cell common refinement, specifying the two marginals, doesn't actually pin anything down, because those two marginal constraints leave the joint underdetermined, and that some hidden rigidity or independence assumption is quietly doing the work whenever people think they've got a unique answer. I'd like them to say whether this is a genuine defect in the rule or whether a defender can shrug it off, and to contrast it with the strict-conditionalisation case, where the order of updating on certainties obviously makes no difference. Whether the discrepancy should push us to give up on updating on uncertain evidence altogether, or just make us more careful about what the later evidence means once the earlier evidence has been absorbed, is the sort of thing I'd want a strong answer to take a view on.

So please write me the exam question itself (graduate level, roughly 45 minutes of the paper), a model answer of about 600-700 words, and a marking scheme worth 20 marks that names the specific moves a first-class answer has to make and the specific mistakes that should cost marks — and for each must-hit point, a line on how students typically get it wrong. Spelling and idiom in British English please, since that's what the cohort writes in. No need to cite any papers or textbooks; it's closed-book and I don't want them thinking a bibliography is what earns the marks.
