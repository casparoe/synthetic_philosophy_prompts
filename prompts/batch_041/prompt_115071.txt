I'm a third-year PhD student in philosophy, working on aesthetic testimony — the question whether a verdict about a work's aesthetic character can transmit justification from speaker to hearer, or whether, as the pessimists have it, aesthetic belief must be grounded in first-hand acquaintance with the object. I'm broadly sympathetic to the pessimist side, but a friend in my epistemology reading group has constructed an argument that the pessimism is not a substantive thesis at all, just a hangover from an over-moralised picture of taste, and I can't find the exit. I'd like you to diagnose it.

His chain runs as follows. (1) For any subject matter F, if a hearer is justified in believing that a speaker is reliable about F, and the speaker asserts of a particular F that it is G, then the hearer is thereby justified in believing that that F is G; this is just what testimony is, and nobody restricts the principle to history or medicine. (2) Some speakers are reliable about the aesthetic character of works: a critic with decades of exposure and a strong track record gets the merits of works in her area right far more often than a novice does, so critical expertise is a real and measurable competence. (3) Therefore a hearer who justifiably believes such a critic to be reliable is justified in believing what the critic asserts about a work's aesthetic character, without having experienced the work. (4) Hence there is no epistemic asymmetry between the aesthetic and the non-aesthetic: aesthetic knowledge is as transmissible as any other. (5) If (4) holds, a person could assemble a complete and justified aesthetic outlook — correct verdicts about every work — without ever encountering a single one, simply by reading the right sources; he calls this figure the Blind Curator, and insists it is an ideal of rationality, not a reductio. (6) It also follows that in cases of aesthetic disagreement the rational move is to defer to whoever has the better record, exactly as with conflicting medical testimony, and that a novice who trusts her own eyes against the critical consensus is simply unjustified.

I want to keep (1) and (2) if I possibly can — they look like nothing more than the general theory of testimony plus an obvious empirical fact about critics — but I can't stomach (5) and (6), and I don't think that's just squeamishness. My hunch is that the trouble is somewhere around (2), though every way I try to break the chain there seems to cost me the very idea of critical expertise, which I'm not willing to give up either. So: which step should I reject, why did it look innocent, and what do I pay? I'd especially like to know what the alternatives cost — what happens to critical practice, to the possibility of aesthetic education, and to the case of a work destroyed before anyone judged it, whose merits, on the pessimist line, would seem to die with its last beholder. If the argument is in fact sound, tell me that too, and tell me what the rest of us have been getting wrong.
