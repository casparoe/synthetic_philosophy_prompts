Here is a reading of Nāgārjuna's tetralemma that I would like you to defend: its four lemmas are not four answers to a question, nor four truth-values, but four ways a question can fail because of what its subject-term is doing. The first lemma grants the question's presupposition and answers in the affirmative; the second grants it and answers in the negative; the third exposes the subject-term as equivocal, so that the question was really two questions with different answers; the fourth exposes the subject-term as failing to refer, so that there was nothing for the predicate or its negation to attach to. Argue that this reading is correct, and that it entails that the tetralemma cannot be faithfully rendered in a many-valued logic that assigns the fourth lemma a truth-value of its own. Then state what the rival reading, on which the tetralemma simply exhausts the logical space of predication, explains better than yours, and argue that the overall balance nonetheless favors your reading.
