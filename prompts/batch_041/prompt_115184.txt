I am an undergraduate in a comparative moral psychology seminar, and I am writing my term paper on a contrast the course has been running with all term. The professor's working picture is that two folk moralities are in play in the modern West, which he calls form-morality and voice-morality (his coinage; he insists the labels are heuristic and that neither belongs to any one country). Form-morality locates the moral demand in the shared rule: the rule is what makes a common world possible, so the bare fact that a rule exists is itself a reason to keep it, and the serious fault is to treat a rule as negotiable whenever it looks pointless or inconvenient. Voice-morality locates the demand in the individual's own judgment: the serious fault is to act against one's conscience, or to go along with something merely because it is the done thing, and the person who obeys a rule he privately rejects is not conscientious but hollow. The course illustrates form-morality mainly with German material (order, duty, the thought that everyone observing the same forms is what keeps daily life bearable) and voice-morality mainly with American material (question authority, be true to yourself, distrust of mere conformity). I find the contrast illuminating, but I suspect it is doing too much work, and I do not want to write a paper that merely repeats national stereotypes back at the professor. I have therefore written eight cases. I have deliberately left the nationalities out of them, so that what is being rated is the moralities and not the countries.

A. Quiet hours. A tenant throws a loud party well past the building's stated quiet hours, having never troubled to learn what they were. The next morning he apologizes warmly, says he will try to be more considerate, and offers to buy the neighbors dinner. They are not much bothered by the noise; what bothers them is that he treats the quiet hours as a matter to be renegotiated between reasonable people rather than as simply the rule of the house.

B. The form. An employee is asked to sign off on a compliance form she believes serves no purpose, and declines, saying she will not put her name to something she does not believe in. Her manager cannot see what her beliefs have to do with it: the form exists, the signature certifies only that it was filled in, and a person who refuses to sign because she finds the form silly is simply not doing her job. He would have had no objection at all if she had signed and then complained.

C. The leak. An engineer discovers that his employer is dumping waste illegally. One colleague wants to go to a journalist that week: the dumping is happening now, and his conscience is not a matter of procedure. The other wants to raise it internally and wait for the regulator, going to the press only if that fails, even if it takes a year and the dumping continues meanwhile; he says that if he bypasses the process, the next person will too, and then there will be no process left to protect anyone.

D. The light. At three in the morning, on an empty street with no traffic in sight, one pedestrian waits for the light and another crosses. The one who waits says the rule is not a calculation about cars: if everyone crossed whenever the street looked clear, the street would stop being a place where anyone could predict anyone. The one who crosses says a rule that has stopped serving its purpose has no claim on him, and that obeying it anyway is a superstition.

E. The twelve-page paper. A student submits twelve pages where fifteen were required, believing the twelve are better than anything he could pad to fifteen. His professor fails the paper without reading it, on the ground that the length was part of the assignment, and that a student who decides for himself which parts of an assignment apply to him has not done the assignment. The student thinks this is formalism preferred to the work itself.

F. The refusal. An employee at a hospital declines, on grounds of conscience, to take part in a procedure that is legal and that the hospital performs routinely. The hospital rearranges the rota without much fuss, and her colleagues treat the refusal as respectable even where they disagree with it — the same colleagues who would be scandalized if she skipped a form because she found it pointless.

G. The fence. A homeowners' association fines a family for a fence a few inches taller than the covenant allows. The neighbors like the family and support the fine: they say the covenant is what keeps the neighborhood from becoming a place where each person does as he pleases, and that a rule not enforced against the pleasant family will mean nothing against the unpleasant one. The family thinks this is petty tyranny.

H. The apology. A woman apologizes to a colleague for a mistake she did not make, because the meeting was going badly and an apology was the quickest way to end it. A colleague from another office is disturbed by this: if she did not do it, he says, then saying she did is a lie, and a lie told to make a room comfortable is still a lie. She thinks he has confused what the words mean with what they are for, and that he is the self-indulgent one — he gets to feel honest while she does the work of keeping the room workable.

What I would like from you:

(a) For each case, two numbers from 0 to 5 — how much approval form-morality would extend to the conduct at issue, and how much approval voice-morality would extend to it. Where a case contains more than one act, say which act you took as the one being rated, so that I can see whether a gap comes from the case or from your reading of it.

(b) A ranking of the eight cases by the size of the gap between the two ratings, largest first, with the signed gap for each, positive meaning that form-morality is the more approving of the two.

(c) For the three largest gaps, a short paragraph each: is this a genuine disagreement about what matters morally, a disagreement about which matters count as moral at all (the moral/conventional line our textbook draws), or a difference of emphasis that would evaporate under pressure?

(d) For the two smallest gaps, a sentence each on why the case fails to separate the two moralities, and whether that is because it is really a case about something else — politeness, institutional role, self-interest, the limits of what an institution may demand.

(e) A closing paragraph on the cases with the largest gaps: does either morality come off better, or is each a defensible response to a real danger (form-morality to the danger of everyone becoming a law unto himself, voice-morality to the danger of the rule becoming an idol)? My paper has to take a position rather than adjudicate, so please say which way you would come out and why.

Give the ratings and the ranking as a compact table, then the prose, and end with a final line of exactly this form: \answer{...}, containing the case letters in order from largest gap to smallest, each with its signed gap in parentheses.
