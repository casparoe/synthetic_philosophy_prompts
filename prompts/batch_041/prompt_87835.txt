I teach a third-year metaphysics course and I'm setting the final. I need one essay question on the identity of indiscernibles -- the PII -- that a student can't pass just by reciting "Black's two spheres show two distinct things can share all their properties." They all trot out the same example: an otherwise empty universe with two iron spheres that are perfect duplicates, a mile apart. I want the question to force them past that.

Can you build it around a wrinkle I've been playing with? Take the usual two-sphere world. Now suppose a rival says the very same world can be described instead as containing just one sphere that is wholly present at two places at once -- call that bilocation -- in which case there is only one object there and the PII never gets threatened at all. I want students to have to decide whether bilocation is a genuine alternative description of that world, a distinct possible world, or simply incoherent, and what their answer implies for whether the PII is a necessary truth, a contingent one, or just false.

So: write the essay question (one question, doable in about 50 minutes); then a model answer of roughly 600 words that actually comes down on a side rather than just surveying the field; then a marking scheme out of 20 that tells me what separates an A from a B. The question should make them (a) state the PII and distinguish numerical from qualitative identity, (b) work out what a bundle theorist, a substratum / bare-particular theorist, and a haecceitist each must say about the two-sphere case, and (c) deal with the bilocation wrinkle. In the marking scheme, name the specific moves a top answer has to make, give marks for each, and note the common mistakes that should cost marks -- e.g. treating the PII as a claim about qualitative identity by definition, or thinking "they differ because one is sphere A and the other is sphere B" settles anything without argument.

Dont bother with readings or citations, the students don't need them.
