For a while now I have been trying to make sense of a way of thinking that a friend keeps pressing on me: that the only thing making a belief good or bad as a belief is how accurate it is, how close it sits to the truth, and that nothing else — whether it comforts you, whether it helps you get what you want, whether it merely feels well justified — counts as a properly epistemic merit. What he uses to make this vivid is betting. He says my being seventy percent sure it will rain tomorrow is nothing over and above the odds at which I would take a bet on rain, and that if my various odds were inconsistent with one another, a cleverer person could stitch together a package of bets that empties my pockets whatever the weather, which he calls a Dutch book, so my confidence simply has to behave like a probability. I like the picture, but I suspect the betting is doing more of the work than he lets on. My worry is that the whole reason my odds must hang together is that inconsistency costs me money, a thoroughly practical matter, whereas he insists the requirement is purely about truth. He tells me that the modern version of his view reaches the same result, that your credences ought to be probabilities, without any wagering at all, just from the thought that a set of credences that does not behave like a probability can always be shown to be less accurate than some rival set no matter how the world turns out, so holding it would be irrational. I cannot tell whether that is genuinely a separate argument or the betting story dressed up.

Can you lay this out for me? I would like the correspondence spelled out first — in the betting picture, what plays the role of my beliefs, of the truth, of the payoff, of the other gambler, and of the reason I should care — and then an honest account of where the comparison breaks: what a bet has that a belief does not, what a belief has that a bet does not, and which of those differences actually bear on the conclusion my friend wants. In particular I want to know whether the betting analogy quietly imports something — a chooser, a stake, a common currency of value — that the purely accuracy-based story is meant to do without, and, if it does, whether the conclusion that credences must be probabilities can still stand on the accuracy story alone or whether it was leaning on the wager all along. If you think a better analogy would do the job, say what it is. Please keep the whole reply under seven hundred words.
