I recently joined a worker cooperative—we bought out the small design firm we used to work for—and I volunteered for the onboarding committee. The whole point of the buyout was to run the place without managers, so we wrote a charter in which every member is reviewed by the people they work with, and every reviewer is in turn reviewed by the people they review. It looked elegant on the whiteboard. In practice, we spend every meeting arguing about who gets to break a tie when two people give each other failing reviews, whether the person who schedules the meetings has authority over the agenda, and why the bylaws themselves seem to be the only thing nobody is allowed to revise. I keep thinking about M.C. Escher's Drawing Hands and about the idea of an inviolate level—the outside rule or medium that a tangled hierarchy has to stand on if it is not going to eat its own tail. I would like a parody that puts this logic to work.

Please write an employee onboarding handbook, about 800–1,000 words, for a fictional self-managing cooperative called the Drawing Hands Collective. The handbook should be deadpan and in-universe, written by the cooperative's own Ministry of Recursive Accountability, and it should take the form of numbered sections, a short description of the org chart, a FAQ, and one sidebar from a concerned outside consultant, including a direct quotation from that consultant. It should never mention Escher, philosophy, or the word paradox; it should read like an ordinary HR document that believes it is being practical. The comedy should come from the actual structure of tangled hierarchies: mutual review loops, the regress of who evaluates the evaluator, the difference between a loop that is grounded in some unrevised rule and one that is not, and the way an inviolate level gets smuggled back in even when everyone has agreed there is no boss. Let the handbook insist that the collective has solved the problem while its own examples quietly show that it has not—perhaps the bylaws are unamendable, or the scheduling software's tie-breaker is treated as neutral, or the founder's original email has become a kind of scripture. Do not have anyone step outside the fiction to explain the joke; let the procedures themselves do the work. The target is the fantasy of a hierarchy with no outside, not any real cooperative or person. A reader who knows the Escher image and the inviolate level should recognize that the logic is being handled correctly; a reader who does not should still find it funny and follow what is happening. Do not cite real books, articles, or theorists, and do not include a reading list; if the document needs an authority, invent one inside the fiction. Please write in American English.
