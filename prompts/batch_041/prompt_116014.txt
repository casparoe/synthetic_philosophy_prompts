Last night a friend of mine — a cognitive science graduate student, so not someone I can wave off — spent two hours insisting that eliminative materialism is the most misunderstood position in philosophy of mind, and then offered me the following summary of it, which I suspect is neither fair nor wholly wrong. As best I can reconstruct him: 'Eliminative materialists hold that science will eventually establish that beliefs and desires do not exist. The view is really a form of behaviorism: since nobody can point to a belief in the brain, the vocabulary of "believing" and "wanting" is just a convenient shorthand for patterns of behavior, which a mature neuroscience will discard. It is also a reductivist program — beliefs really are just neurons firing, and the wiring diagram will eventually tell us which belief is which. The supporting argument is that folk psychology is a failed research program, millennia old and making no progress, so it will go the way of phlogiston. The fatal problem is that the view refutes itself: to assert that there are no beliefs is itself to express a belief, so eliminative materialism cannot be true. And nobody could actually live this way — you cannot help attributing beliefs to the driver who cuts you off — so it can never be more than a philosophical parlour game.' I do not know where he went wrong, or whether he did; I am inclined to think the self-refutation point is too quick, but I cannot say why, and the rest I simply cannot assess. Please walk through the summary piece by piece and tell me what an eliminativist would accept, what they would call a caricature, which of these are standard objections rather than refutations, and whether his closing verdict follows from anything true in the rest of what he said. Be explicit about which parts of this are settled and which are genuinely contested among people who work on the topic, rather than presenting a contested reading as the consensus.
