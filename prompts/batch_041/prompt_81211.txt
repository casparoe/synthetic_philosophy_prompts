I'm a novelist, and the book I'm writing turns on an idea I borrow from Hofstadter: that a self is not a thing but a pattern — a strange loop — which can run in more than one brain, so that when a person dies, what those who knew them carry inside their heads is not merely a memory of the dead but a partial continuation of them, with the amount that survives scaling to how deeply the person was modeled. I want the novel's metaphysics to hold up to a philosopher's scrutiny, so write me an essay on whether a self can genuinely be distributed across several brains in this way. The crux, as I see it, is whether what persists in another mind is the person or only a copy of the person — and whether that distinction even survives once we grant that the person was always a pattern rather than a thing. Argue both sides honestly, then come down on one: give me a verdict, not a hedge. Close by telling me how confident you are in that verdict and naming the single point in your reasoning you are least sure of. Write it as continuous prose — no headings, no bullet points, no bold text, no tables — the way you would set out your thinking in a long letter.
