I run compliance for a mid-sized NGO working in about a dozen countries, and what actually eats my week isn't fraud, it's gifts: envelopes, hampers, lunches, facilitation payments, a case of whisky for someone's brother's wedding. Staff ask me weekly whether something counts as a bribe and Ive been winging it. Our regional director keeps saying the whole framing is parochial -- that in a lot of these places a transfer to an official isn't payment for a decision but how a relationship gets acknowledged, and that reading it as a bribe assumes a public/private split plenty of places don't organise themselves around; he adds that corruption talk locally is usually an accusation aimed at whoever's ahead, not a neutral description. I don't want to wave that off, and I don't want to pretend HQ's standard is neutral either. So give me a procedure I can run in about twenty minutes on a request: the questions in order, what counts as enough of an answer to each, where it stops with a clean no, and where it hands the call to a named person because the question has stopped being factual (which conception of corruption we're using, and who eats the cost). It has to survive a country office that resents it and a board that wants a defensible file. Then run it on three: the customary gift to the village chief at every project launch -- he has no say over our contracts, but he does decide whose families get onto our beneficiary list; a small cash payment to a clerk to move a permit we're legally entitled to; and our chair's complaint that the real rot is that every hire now runs through two families, with no envelope anywhere. Make sure one of them comes out telling us to stop something we've done for years, and tell me what the procedure can't decide.
