I'm setting the final for an upper-level philosophy of science course on models, idealization and unrealistic assumptions, and I want a single 50-minute essay question built around a case of my own rather than the stock textbook examples. Here is the case.

A fisheries model used to set catch quotas assumes (a) that the stock is large enough that no single boat's catch measurably affects it, so that the fishing effort of the whole fleet can be aggregated into one variable; (b) that fishers know, when they choose their effort, what recruitment into the stock will be five years later; and (c) that the fleet's behaviour can be represented by a single representative fisher whose preferences are the average of the actual fishers' preferences. Each of the three is false in the applications the model is used for, and the model nonetheless tracks recorded landings closely over the period on which it was fitted. The stock has since been fished down, and the same model is now being used to advise on quotas.

Please write the essay question, a model answer of about 700 words, and a marking scheme worth 20 marks.

The question must not be answerable by stating Friedman's thesis (sometimes called the F-twist) that the realism of a theory's assumptions is irrelevant to assessing it so long as its predictions are accurate, and then reciting the familiar objections to that thesis. It should instead require candidates to sort (a), (b) and (c) by the kind of failure each represents, to say for each what Friedman's criterion does and does not license, and to state what a criterion of assumption-assessment would have to add in order to make those distinctions at all. It should leave room for the view that one of the three is not, strictly speaking, the sort of claim that can be true or false, and should make candidates say what follows for the assessment of the model if that view is right.

The marking scheme should name the specific moves a top answer must make, give the marks for each (20 in total), and add one line per move on how candidates typically get it wrong. No citations, reading lists or attributions of any kind: the model answer should stand on its own argument, and the exam is closed-book.
