My friend Rosa has a theory of money that I can't decide is profound or just nuts. Her line: money isn't a thing at all, it's a permission — a social token that lets you lay claim to other people's labor and stuff — and the only reason we're so fixated on gold is habit, plus the fact that its hard to counterfeit. She thinks a society could run entirely on bookkeeping, no coins, no bullion, and lose nothing that's actually valuable, because real wealth is just the power to command work.

Anyway, turns out this is roughly what Berkeley was on about back in the 1730s. He kept asking whether the true idea of money, as such, isn't just that of a "ticket or counter," whether gold, silver, and paper are all just tickets or counters for reckoning, recording, and transferring claims, and whether a nation could keep its denominations and its prices even with the bullion gone. He also insisted that real wealth comes from the elements and human labor, and asked whether a man who could have everything he wished would even value money.

So here's what I want from you: compare Rosa's account with Berkeley's. Are they the same view in different clothes, or do they come apart somewhere that matters? I think the interesting fault line is where the ticket gets its authority in the first place — Berkeley seems to anchor it in labor and real goods, while Rosa anchors it in whoever issues the permission (the state, or whoever controls the resources). Which of the two does a better job explaining (a) why money is valued at all, and (b) why people seem to want money for its own sake and not just for what it buys? Dont just lay out both sides and shrug — argue it and end with a verdict, even if the verdict is a messy "each one wins on a different question." No need to cite anything, just reason it out.
