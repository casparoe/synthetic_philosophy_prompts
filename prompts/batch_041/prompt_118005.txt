I'm a novelist, about halfway through a book about a woman who inherits her father's market garden and his small pest-control business, and I keep hitting a philosophical wall that I think is actually the point of the book but I can't get my head around it. Here's what started it. Where I live, if you trap a squirrel in your attic you're legally obliged to do it in a way that's certified humane -- there are rules, there's paperwork, in theory a vet could be involved -- but the same person can drench a greenhouse in soap and suffocate tens of thousands of aphids over a weekend and nobody raises an eyebrow. Animal welfare law basically starts at vertebrates. Meanwhile my brother in law is in a farm co-op that does integrated pest management, which means they only spray when the pest count crosses a threshold where the expected crop loss outweighs the cost of spraying -- so, if you think about it, the whole system has a price tag on insect lives, and it isn't zero, but it's very very small, and it's set by the crop rather than by the insect. And now the co-op has started publishing something they call a kill ledger, where members report the mass of invertebrates killed, in grams, instead of counting individuals, because one of them argued that counting individuals is unmeasurable and morally meaningless at this scale. I have no idea where that came from -- it might be invented, it's in my notes either way.

What I want from you: what philosophical commitments does this whole package presuppose? The squirrel/aphid asymmetry, the economic threshold, and the gram-of-insect thing. Is there one coherent theory underneath it -- something like: moral standing tracks having a certain kind of nervous system, once you're below that line you're just biomass, and policy should then be governed by aggregate measurable quantities rather than by individuals? Or do the three parts secretly presuppose different and incompatible things -- the threshold rule looking like plain anthropocentric consequentialism about crops, the kill ledger looking like it's reaching for a utilitarian view of insects but has quietly given up on individuals as the unit that matters? Reconstruct the implicit theory as charitably as you can, then tell me which parts the practice strictly needs versus which are just window dressing. Could a Kantian, a virtue ethicist, and someone who thinks insects have no moral status whatsoever all sign off on exactly this set of rules? Where is the practice philosophically underdetermined, such that several quite different theories would license precisely this arrangement?

And then, since it's a novel and I need to know whether this is a plot problem or just a paperwork problem: if you swapped the underlying view for one where insect suffering actually counts for something, what would concretely have to change -- in the law, in the co-op's spray thresholds, in the ledger itself -- and how much of that would be a real change in what people do versus a change in how they describe what they're already doing?
