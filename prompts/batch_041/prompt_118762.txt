I'm a graduate student writing a seminar paper on future contingents, and I keep running into a knot I can't untie on my own. Take the Ockhamist's central move: statements about future contingent events — that there will be a sea battle tomorrow, that I will order the fish — are true or false right now, and not merely true or false relative to a "settledness" operator or to some particular history. I'm attracted to this because it preserves bivalence and gives a specific reason why the fatalist's argument from present truth to inevitability fails: it treats a fact about the past that is about the past only in virtue of what happens later (that it was true yesterday that there will be a sea battle) as though it were a fact genuinely about the past, over which I have no power. But I've been told — and I think I see why — that keeping determinate truth values for future contingents requires positing something stronger: that among all the branches of possible history running through the present moment, one branch is privileged as the one that will in fact be realized, so that "true about the future" has a truthmaker behind it and not just a semantic stipulation. My friend thinks that once you buy the privileged branch you've given up the openness of the future you were trying to defend, and that Ockhamism is therefore unstable; I suspect you can have determinate truth without a distinguished actual future, and I'd put my own credence at roughly 0.55.

What I want from you is your own number for the proposition that Ockhamism can be made coherent without a privileged actual future, but more importantly the decomposition. Give me a rough probability, with one line of justification each, for at least these sub-questions: (a) that bivalence holds for future contingent statements at all, as opposed to their truth values being relative to a history or to a context of assessment; (b) that, given bivalence, some privileged branch must exist, because truth about the future requires a truthmaker of the right kind rather than being readable off a deflationary or disquotational account of truth; (c) that the distinction between facts genuinely about the past and facts about the past only in virtue of later events can be drawn sharply enough to block the fatalist inference, rather than being an ad hoc device for getting the result one wanted; and (d) whatever else you think belongs, including the possibility that the whole problem is an artifact of regimenting English into a tensed metalanguage — for instance, if "will" is better analyzed as a modal, or if settledness is the only notion we ever really needed and truth-at-a-moment is a philosopher's fiction.

Then combine the terms into a single number, and tell me which term I should be most suspicious of and what argument or consideration would move your overall number the most. If you think the framing itself rests on a mistake, say so plainly and say what the mistake is — I would rather be told my question is malformed than get a tidy answer to a question I should not have asked.
