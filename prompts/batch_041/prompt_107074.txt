Mathematical practice enforces a norm at one level and suspends it at another, and I want to know what that combination presupposes. At the object level, a derivation counts as establishing its conclusion only if every step is licensed by an accepted rule of inference; a gap, however small, voids the proof, and we refuse to accept a theorem on the strength of an argument that is merely persuasive. At the meta-level, the rules are never proved. A proof assistant rests the whole edifice on a small trusted kernel whose inference rules are simply stipulated, and in ordinary mathematics the basic rules of inference are not derived from anything more basic — they are the machinery, not the results. So the practice insists that every step be justified while leaving the justifiers themselves unjustified, and treats this not as a scandal but as the correct and unremarkable state of affairs.

Carroll's tortoise is the standing reminder that this cannot be repaired by supplying the missing justification as one more premise: whatever conditional you hand over, the tortoise will accept it as a premise and still ask for the rule that licenses detaching the consequent, so no finite stock of premises can compel the inference. My question concerns what the practice's calm response to the tortoise commits it to.

Reconstruct the implicit theory of justification that the practice presupposes. Does it require that the basic rules are self-justifying — a foundationalism about logic — or only that justification terminates somewhere other than in further propositions, in a practice, a disposition to infer, or a form of life that is not itself the sort of thing that can be true or false? Or is it a coherentist or pragmatist commitment, on which the rules earn their place by belonging to a system that works and by the fact that inquiry cannot get off the ground without them? Which rival accounts of the justification of deduction does the practice quietly rule out, and which does it merely sit comfortably with? Be careful to separate what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with: it may be that the practice is underdetermined here and that several incompatible theories of logical justification would each license exactly the same behavior.

Then press on the apparent inconsistency. The same practice that will not let a single step pass unlicensed lets the entire rule-set pass unlicensed, and in the verification setting it treats the choice of rules as a matter of stipulation rather than of truth. Is that split stable, or does it merely relocate the tortoise's regress to the meta-level — since the kernel's rules are justified, if at all, by soundness proofs that themselves proceed by inference, and those proofs are carried out in a stronger theory whose own rules are again stipulated? Consider also what the practice assumes about the relation between following a rule and being entitled to the conclusion: does it presuppose that inferring is a genuine act of rule-following, or only that a disposition to move from premises to conclusion can be relied on?

Finally, tell me what would have to change if the presupposed view were replaced. If we adopted a theory on which the basic rules themselves stand in need of justification, would proof become impossible in the way the tortoise suggests, or would the demand simply be relocated rather than discharged? And if we adopted a view on which a proof is a social or rhetorical achievement rather than a warrant-conferring derivation, which parts of mathematical and verification practice would have to be abandoned, and which would survive untouched?
