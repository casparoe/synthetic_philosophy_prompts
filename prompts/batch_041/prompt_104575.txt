For three years I have co-taught a graduate seminar jointly listed in philosophy and in our behavioral-economics group, and the unit on weakness of will reliably produces the same impasse. The philosophers work with the familiar picture in which an agent judges, all things considered, that one course of action is best, and then fails to act on that judgment, with the failure located in the will itself — in the fragility of a resolution, in the exercise of self-control, in what the agent identifies with. The economists treat the same phenomena as time-inconsistent preferences: discounting that is steeper over short horizons than over long ones, present bias, a demand for commitment devices, and reversals of preference as the earlier option approaches. Both sides accept the same laboratory findings — the reversals, the commitment-seeking, the failures to follow through — and yet neither has moved in three years. The philosophers say the discounting framework has no room for the agent's own evaluation of her options and therefore cannot be describing the phenomenon at all; the economists reply that until the richer picture predicts something the flat model does not, it is a story rather than a competing explanation. My own hypothesis, which I would like you to test rather than assume, is that this is not a disagreement about the facts of the cases but about what a theory of action is for — one side wanting an account of what makes an action intelligible as the agent's own, the other wanting a model that constrains choice behavior — and that the two sides are answering different questions under the single heading of weakness of will.

What I want from you is a diagnosis rather than a verdict: is this a verbal dispute, a substantive one, or two enterprises sharing a name; is a hidden empirical question doing the work, such as whether the discounting is genuinely hyperbolic or whether the alleged reversals are reversals of preference rather than changes in what the agent values; are the sides disagreeing about which cases count as data, or about starting intuitions that neither can argue for; and, on your diagnosis, what would actually count as progress — a distinction, a formal result, an experiment, a change of question — or whether nothing should be expected to move it. Engage the positions as they are actually held rather than as caricatures, and be honest if my hypothesis is wrong.

Give me exactly five numbered items, each a sentence or two, with no introduction and no conclusion: first the diagnosis itself; second the evidence from each side that supports it; third the hidden question, if any, that is doing the real work; fourth what would count as progress given the diagnosis; and fifth your verdict on my hypothesis about the two questions being run together.
