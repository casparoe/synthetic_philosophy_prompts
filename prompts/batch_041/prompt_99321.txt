I am a high school student working on a long-form essay for my school's philosophy club, and I would like to understand the most important implications of the identity of indiscernibles by way of the familiar thought experiment involving two exactly similar spheres in an otherwise empty universe. I am not interested in a survey of the literature. Instead, suppose we sharpen the case in the following way: the two spheres are indistinguishable in all intrinsic properties and in every relational property that can be specified without already presupposing a distinction between them, and there is no external observer, coordinate system, or causal history that could serve to individuate them. The only fact that distinguishes them is that there are two of them rather than one. Please describe the most important implications of this sharpened scenario. I am especially interested in what it implies for (1) whether the identity of indiscernibles should be accepted, rejected, or reformulated; (2) whether we need a notion of bare particularity or haecceity to make sense of numerical distinctness; (3) how the scenario bears on the interpretation of permutation symmetry and indistinguishable particles in quantum mechanics; and (4) whether it has any consequences for personal identity or for the identity of ordinary objects over time. Please rank these implications by their importance, explain which alleged implications are overrated or simply do not follow, and make clear which assumptions each implication depends on.
