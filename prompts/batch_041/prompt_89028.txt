I did a physics degree and now write software, and for the past couple of years I have been reading philosophy of mind on my own, mostly on the problem of consciousness. In that reading I keep running into a shape of argument that I do not have a name for, and I would like to know whether it already has one or, failing that, what the right carving of it is. The shape is this: a certain kind of fact is declared to be real but causally idle — nothing about it makes any difference to what happens — and then, in the same breath, our reliability about that kind of fact is declared to be no problem at all. A philosopher tells me that the felt quality of an experience, the ache of the ache, never enters into the explanation of why I say that my tooth aches; the neurons do all the causing. Fine. But he also holds that my report is usually correct about the ache, and when I ask how that could be, he says the ache comes along with the neural facts, so wherever the neurons are arranged the right way the ache is there too, and my report, being caused by the neurons, lands on it. A friend in a semantics seminar makes what looks to me like the identical move about meaning: the meaning of a sentence does no work in the causal story of how someone responds to it, only the marks and the wiring do, but she still says we reliably recover what sentences mean, because the meaning is carried along by the marks. And the case that started me thinking about this was an argument that moral facts never cause anything, paired with the claim that our moral beliefs track them anyway.

What strikes me is not the general idea that some facts are idle — I understand that there are respectable positions of that sort, and I am not asking you to adjudicate whether consciousness does any causal work. It is the pairing, and the fact that the second half always seems to be discharged with one of a small number of moves: the idle fact is guaranteed to accompany the causally active facts, so getting it right comes for free; or the judgments about it do not need to track it, they only need to be useful; or the demand for reliability is quietly withdrawn, on the grounds that being wrong about these facts would not matter anyway. My suspicion is that the second half is doing all the work, and that the same three or four escape hatches keep reappearing across debates that otherwise have nothing to do with each other. So: is there an established name for either the pairing or the family of escape hatches? Please do not give me a bare label. I want to know what the nearest existing concepts are, how each of them differs from what I am describing — I suspect one of them is the standard objection to the view that experience is causally idle, and I suspect that one is narrower than what I am after — and whether the thing I am describing is a single phenomenon or several that I am lumping together. If there is no name, coin one and define it, and tell me where its edges are: what has to be true for a case to count as an instance, what looks like an instance but is not, and what would have to be shown for one of those escape hatches to actually work rather than merely postpone the difficulty.

Please finish with a JSON object, with nothing after it, containing exactly these fields: "term" (string — the established name if there is one, otherwise the name you are coining, kept as short as the job allows); "status" (one of "established", "established but narrower than what I described", "no established term"); "nearest_concepts" (array of objects, each with "name", "what_it_covers", "where_it_diverges" — how it differs from the pattern I described — and "closeness", a number from 1 to 5, where 5 is closest); "carving" (object with "necessary_conditions", an array of strings, and "what_it_is_not", an array of strings); "escape_hatches" (array of objects, each with "label", "the_move", one sentence stating the move, and "what_would_make_it_work", one sentence); "boundary_cases" (array of objects, each with "case", one sentence, "verdict", one of "instance", "not an instance", "borderline", and "why", one sentence); "verdict_on_unity" (one of "one phenomenon", "a family", "several distinct phenomena I conflated", with one sentence of justification); and "open_question" (one sentence naming the thing that would have to be settled to know whether I am describing one phenomenon or several).
