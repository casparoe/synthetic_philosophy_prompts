I run the monthly philosophy discussion group at my local public library — the audience is adults with no formal training in the subject, mostly retired schoolteachers, two nurses, and one engineer who will not let a loose formulation pass — and next month I'm the one leading the session on Nozick's tracking theory of knowledge and its treatment of closure. I have sixty minutes and a whiteboard, and I do not want the theory explained to me; I can do that part. What I want is a teaching plan: the order in which the pieces have to go so that each is motivated by the one before it rather than merely adjacent to it, the single case from ordinary adult life that should carry the whole hour and stand up to being re-entered three or four times without wearing out, the misconceptions this particular audience will arrive with or develop halfway through and the precise point in the sequence at which each has to be headed off, the one closing question that will tell me whether the main idea has landed or whether they have only nodded at it, and an explicit list of what I should refuse to cover at this level and say so out loud. The dependency structure matters to me more than coverage — I would rather they leave with one thing genuinely understood than five things gestured at — and the sequence has to make room for why closure fails on this account and why that failure is not a defect they should quietly patch.

The reason I care so much about the ordering is that I want to end on something of my own, and they can only reach it if the machinery is already in place. The tracking conditions are standardly put forward as conditions on a belief at a moment, but most of what any of us believes we hold over time, and I have come to think the counterfactuals come apart in a way I have not seen properly separated: a belief can be formed sensitively and then maintained insensitively, since I would go on holding it even if it had long since become false, because nothing in my ongoing situation is set up to dislodge it. I have been calling this the upkeep gap, and I want the final fifteen minutes to land it as a felt problem before I name it, so that by the time I say the phrase they can state the gap themselves. If that is really just the familiar point about forgetting, or about a justified belief being defeated by later evidence, tell me so and tell me how to re-cut it so that it is not, because I would rather find that out now than in front of the group. And please keep the stock illustrations out of it — barns and brains in vats, stopped clocks, thermometers, the lottery: several of them have met those in other sessions, and they now function as trivia rather than as tools. I need a case that is mine to use and that can bear the whole load.

My own guesses about where they will go wrong are that they will conflate tracking with causation — the fact caused the belief, so the belief tracks it — and that they will hear the sensitivity condition as a claim about how reliable the believer's method is rather than as a counterfactual profile of the belief itself. Correct me where I am wrong about what they will get wrong, and give me the misconceptions in the order they will actually surface rather than the order that would be tidiest to present. No reading list, incidentally; this group does not do homework. End with a single last line beginning "Load-bearing move:" naming the one step in the plan that everything else depends on — the step that, if it fails, takes the hour down with it.
