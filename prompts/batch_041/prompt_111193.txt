I am a secular philosopher, and I am giving the farewell toast at a dinner for Mara, a close colleague who is leaving a tenured professorship in philosophy to become a chaplain in a women's prison. The room will hold three constituencies: academic philosophers who know Kierkegaard's reading of Abraham and will be skeptical of anything that sounds like a religious calling; Mara's family, who are religious, proud of her, and frightened that she is throwing away a secure life; and a handful of her students, who will be inspired and will want the toast to be inspiring. I want the toast to honor her without pretending that her choice is justified by the ordinary ethical reasons we share.

Please write the toast (about five minutes spoken). It should be warm and personal, not a lecture, but the philosophical idea should do real structural work in it. The idea is the teleological suspension of the ethical: Abraham's relation to God suspends the ethical order rather than fulfilling it. I want you to use Kierkegaard's Abraham, and specifically the contrast between the tragic hero and Abraham. The tragic hero sacrifices one ethical duty for a higher ethical duty, remains within the universal, and can be understood; Abraham acts on a private calling that cannot be translated into universal terms, so he cannot speak and cannot be understood. The toast must not resolve whether Mara is a tragic hero, a knight of faith, or a woman making a terrible mistake. It must not say she is a hero, because that would collapse the paradox into a familiar story of noble sacrifice; and it must not say she is irrational or selfish, because that would be cheap. It should make the room feel the strangeness of a calling that cannot be justified in the terms we share.

To keep the toast from turning her into a martyr of resignation, you may use another Kierkegaardian distinction: the knight of resignation gives up the finite for the infinite and is forever separated from it; the knight of faith, by virtue of the absurd, believes he will receive the finite back. Abraham did not simply resign himself to losing Isaac. The toast can gesture at that difference without pretending to know which one Mara is.

The speech should land for the philosophers without losing the family, and it should end in a way that is more like a silence than a verdict. A toast, not a thesis. Do not include citations, readings, or references.

After the toast, add a brief note to me—not part of the speech—saying how confident you are in the reading of Kierkegaard that the toast relies on, and single out the point you are least sure of.
