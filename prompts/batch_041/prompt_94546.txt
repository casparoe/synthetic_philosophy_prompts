In a seminar I am taking on material constitution, one phrase is being made to do all the work, and I have come to suspect it is doing several incompatible jobs at once. The phrase is: the statue is nothing over and above the clay. My classmate uses it, first, to dissolve the puzzle of coincident objects — he says there is only the clay there, and the statue is that clay under a description, so there is no question of two things in one place. He uses it, second, to make a claim he treats as substantive: the clay grounds the statue, and the statue-facts are fixed once the clay-facts are fixed. And he uses it, third, to reassure me that the statue exists perfectly well — of course it does, since it is nothing over and above the clay. The case we keep returning to is a lump of clay that was kneaded into a figure: the clay existed before the statue, would survive being squashed back into a ball, and the statue would not.

I would like a menu rather than an adjudication on his behalf. Set out the distinct precise claims that sentence could be making, ordered from the thinnest to the most substantive. At a minimum, separate a strict identity claim; a claim about composition or shared parts; a mereological-essentialist claim about what can survive what; a supervenience claim; a grounding or dependence claim; a truthmaking claim; a claim about ontological commitment or quantifier innocence; and a claim about conceptual or explanatory priority — and add any further readings the idiom can bear that I have not thought of. For each reading, say exactly what it asserts, whether it is a platitude, a substantive but defensible thesis, or simply false, and what it implies for this case in particular, especially whether it permits two numerically distinct objects to coincide there or rules coincidence out.

Then tell me which readings would actually sustain each of the three uses above, and whether any single reading sustains all three — or whether he must quietly change readings between sentences, and where the change occurs. The phrase also comes in at least two grammatical forms, "is nothing over and above" and "just is"; say whether they can be read as one claim.

Two further points, because they are what the seminar keeps sliding over. First, one other person objects that the grounding talk gives the game away: only distinct things can stand in a relation of dependence, so my classmate has already conceded that there are two objects and the rest is decoration. My classmate replies that this is a verbal dispute. I want to know whether it is, and what turns on the answer. Second, say whether the whole-and-parts variant — the whole is nothing over and above its parts — is a special case of the statue-and-clay claim or a different claim altogether, since I have heard both asserted and never argued. Finally, tell me which reading the people who repeat the slogan most often probably have in mind, and which reading the argument in front of me actually needs.
