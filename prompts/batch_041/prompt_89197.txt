I am drafting a chapter that extends the Lewis-style analysis of convention to populations of agents who are computationally bounded and only roughly informed about one another, and before I defend any particular account I need the target clearly in view. Write out the desiderata for a theory of convention and of the evolution of norms: the phenomena it must explain, the cases it must get right, the distinctions it must not collapse, and the background results it must be consistent with — each with the consideration that motivates it. Keep the weight on the requirements themselves rather than on scoring named theories; the criteria are where the work is, and I will do the scoring myself.

The list should take an explicit position on at least the following, which are the points on which I keep stalling:

1. Non-circularity. The account may not help itself to semantic or normative materials — agreement, promise, meaning, obligation — that it is supposed to explain, since the paradigm case is linguistic convention and the account is meant to be available to someone who holds that meaning is explained by convention rather than the reverse.

2. Common knowledge. Is it a genuine requirement or an idealization? Conventions are followed by people who cannot articulate them and who hold no explicit beliefs about others' beliefs; the account must either show common knowledge to be attainable in such cases or replace it with something weaker without losing the capacity to distinguish a convention from a coincidence.

3. The vanishing population. The last speaker of a dying language goes on following what look like the conventions of that language. Say whether the account classifies this as convention, as convention only degenerately so, or as something else, and what that verdict reveals about the analysis.

4. Arbitrariness against near-inevitability. Does the theory require a genuinely available alternative regularity for every convention? Cases in which the regularity is nearly forced — by an asymmetry between the parties, or by one option being overwhelmingly salient — look like conventions but may fail a strong arbitrariness condition.

5. Asymmetric and exploitative conventions. Ordinary speech calls regularities conventions even when they benefit one party and are sustained by the other's lack of alternatives, and no one would describe those as coordination equilibria held up by a mutual preference for conformity. Decide whether the account is to cover such cases or to revise ordinary usage, and what follows either way.

6. Normativity. Whether the account yields a reason to conform for an agent who does not prefer conformity and is unmoved by others' expectations, and how any such reason relates to moral and legal obligation.

7. Emergence without a coordinator. What the account must say about how the first instance arises — in particular whether shared salience or analogy is required — and what it says about a bad convention that everyone would be better off abandoning.

8. Stability and change. The account must explain both why conventions resist small perturbations and how they occasionally flip abruptly, through mass simultaneous change, generational turnover, or external shock, and it must keep a convention's stability distinct from its efficiency.

Then identify which requirements cannot be jointly satisfied, and say so explicitly: I am most interested in whether non-circularity and normativity can be had together, and whether arbitrariness and the demand for an account of emergence can. Close with a single sentence naming the requirement that does the most to exclude otherwise attractive accounts.
