After 35 years in a chemistry department I've somehow ended up chairing our town's commemorative works committee, which has turned out to be a lot more philosophy than I bargined for. Last month we voted to take the bronze of a 19th-century industrialist out of the square because of how he treated his workers, and then voted, in the same meeting, to keep his name on the auditorium, on the grounds that a name is just a label while a statue is an honor. We left the plinth. An empty plinth says more than the statue did and I'm not sure what it says.

What does the practice of commemorating people with statues actually presuppose? A few things I can't untangle. Nobody erects a bronze of an industry, they erect one of a man, so is the medium committed to a great-man theory of history, or can it be read as just a convenient way of pointing at a collective achievement? And we only ever take these things down for moral objections, never because the person was wrong about the science -- so the statue seems to be making a claim about character rather than contribution, but then what's the rule that lets us keep the statues of everyone from before 1900 who held views we'd now find appalling? Is it whether the view was contested in their own time? If so, doesn't that presuppose a fairly strong position about how much of their era's consensus a person is answerable for? Then there's the label-versus-honor distinction the committee leaned on, and the question of whether the plinth is the honor and the figure is decoration, and the fact that we rename streets without any ceremony at all.

Tell me which of these the practice strictly requires, which are just things we happen to have bundled together, and which are underdetermined, so that several theories would license the same behaviour. And what would a town have to believe to do all of this without contradicting itself? Skip the reading list, I won't get to it; I just want the argument.
