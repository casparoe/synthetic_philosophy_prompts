I spent thirty-five years as a behavioural ecologist — optimal foraging stuff on shorebirds, then a long detour into state-dependent models — and since I retired I've been reading my way into philosophy and, lately, into the AI alignment literature, which honestly feels like re-reading my own field with the serial numbers filed off. A younger colleague who works on inverse reinforcement learning asked me to write down what I actually believe about inferring preferences from behaviour, because he thinks ecologists and RL people are circling the same question and neither side reads the other. So here's my list, roughly as I'd defend it in a seminar:

1. Preference is latent. All you ever observe is choice, so any claim about what an agent really wants has to be earned from behaviour and nothing else.
2. The clean move — the one that made my career possible — is to assume the agent is approximately maximising expected utility (or fitness, in my old game) and then find the objective function under which the observed choices come out optimal. That's the whole trick.
3. But animals aren't optimal. They use crude rules of thumb, they're inconsistent across contexts, they carry state-dependent biases. So a reward function that rationalises the behaviour is almost certainly not the real preference.
4. What we should actually align an AI to is not what a person does but what they'd endorse on reflection — what they'd choose if they were better informed and thinking clearly.
5. Reward is only identified up to positive affine transformation, and you can bolt on arbitrary potential-shaping terms without changing the optimal policy, so the reward function is partly an artefact of how you parameterised it.
6. If the demonstrator is an expert at the task, defer to them; if they're a novice, we should correct toward what they'd want if they knew better.
7. Preferences are over outcomes. Anything that looks like caring about the process — how a result was reached — can be folded into the description of the outcome, so we don't need a seperate notion of procedural value.

I'd like you to tell me whether this hangs together. I have a nagging feeling that two or three of these pull against each other and I can't see which, because I keep switching between my old ecological hat (where the optimality assumption was a modelling convenience I never believed literally) and my new normative hat (where I actually think reflection should win). Rank the tensions by how bad they are, tell me for each what would have to give, what the cheapest repair is, and what that repair costs me, then tell me which single revision would clean up the most. And please flag any pair that only looks like a conflict but isn't — I'd rather not throw out something I dont have to.
