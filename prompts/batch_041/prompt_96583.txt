You are a philosopher of criminal law with a specialization in the theory of punishment, and you have a particular talent for the unglamorous question of what a legal institution is committed to, as opposed to what it says about itself. Please think carefully before you answer, and open with a short brainstorming pass — a list of the candidate theories and of the specific features of the practice that might discriminate among them — before you commit to a reconstruction.

Here is the practice. In the jurisdictions that still sentence people to death, capital punishment is surrounded by an apparatus with no counterpart anywhere else in the criminal law. Conviction and sentence are separated: after a jury finds guilt, there is a second proceeding in which evidence is admitted that would never have been admissible at trial, the prosecution must establish aggravating circumstances, the defence may put in anything mitigating about the defendant's history, character or mental state, and the sentencer is obliged to weigh it. The first appeal is automatic rather than optional, and in some jurisdictions the reviewing court is asked whether the sentence is proportionate to what comparable defendants have received. People in the jury pool who say they could never vote for death are, for practical purposes, excluded from it. There is a peculiar rule about what jurors may be told: in some jurisdictions the judge is forbidden to tell them that the only alternative to death is a life sentence with no possibility of release, and it is only if the prosecution itself argues that the defendant will be dangerous in the future that the defence becomes entitled to correct the jurors' likely misunderstanding. Years, often decades, pass between sentence and execution.

Then there is the surrounding infrastructure, which is where I lose the thread. The same systems fund post-conviction DNA testing, maintain units whose stated purpose is to investigate claims of innocence, and, in most states, run statutes that pay compensation to people who were wrongly imprisoned and later exonerated; I have a vague impression that in some of these schedules a year wrongly spent on death row is priced higher than a year wrongly spent in an ordinary prison. By the counts that are commonly cited, something on the order of two per cent of those sentenced to death in the modern era have been exonerated. And there is the on-again, off-again pattern: a jurisdiction can go nearly two decades without carrying out an execution, then carry out a dozen within six months, then announce a moratorium, then lift it — while the statute, the death row, the sentencing proceedings and the appeals continue throughout, and nobody treats the penalty as repealed.

The contrast case is life without parole, which also confines a person until they die, and which gets none of this: no bifurcated proceeding, no mandatory appeal, no proportionality review, no special care about the jury, no compensation schedule keyed to the years wrongly served.

I would have expected a jurisdiction that keeps the death penalty for its deterrent value to want it swift and certain, and a jurisdiction that keeps it for retribution to be comparatively untroubled by the occasional punishment of the wrong person. What I seem to be looking at is a practice organized almost entirely around the possibility of error. So:

1. Reconstruct the theory of punishment this practice presupposes — not the theory its officials announce, but the one it would have to hold for the parts to hang together. What does it take for granted about desert, about deterrence, about the state's standing to kill, and about how a wrongly taken life compares in value with the lives supposedly protected by deterrence?

2. Say which rival theories the practice actually rules out and which it merely happens to fit. In particular: does it presuppose that death is different in kind from other punishments, or only that it is a larger quantity of the same thing? Does the reliability apparatus rule out a pure general-deterrence rationale, or can a sophisticated version of that rationale demand the apparatus? Could a retributivist be indifferent to all of it?

3. Distinguish carefully what the practice strictly presupposes from what it is merely compatible with. Where is it underdetermined — where would several incompatible theories license exactly what we observe?

4. Test the parts against one another for consistency. In particular: (a) the demand that the sentencer weigh everything about this particular defendant against the demand that like cases be treated alike; (b) a machinery for minimizing the risk of executing the innocent, bolted onto a sanction that cannot be undone; (c) a jury asked to make the most context-saturated judgment the law knows, while being kept ignorant of the one fact that would make the alternative determinate; (d) compensation for the exonerated, priced by the year, alongside a wrong for which no schedule of compensation exists at all.

5. Use two ideas that I think are underused here, and say explicitly whether they help. The first is the difference between a weighted trade-off and a lexicographic one: is the practice behaving as though one execution of an innocent person can be exchanged against some quantity of deterrence, at some rate, or as though it cannot be exchanged against anything at all? The second is the difference between reducing error and reallocating it: the apparatus may not lower the total number of mistakes in the system so much as move them around — onto who is charged capitally in the first place, onto which defendants are offered pleas, onto who survives jury selection.

6. Tell me what would have to change in the practice if we replaced the theory you reconstructed with each of the following: (i) a straightforward general-deterrence theory; (ii) a straightforward desert theory; (iii) a view on which executing an innocent person is not a worse outcome than wrongful imprisonment but a different kind of wrong; (iv) a view on which the state is entitled to keep the penalty on its books while binding itself never to carry it out.

7. Close with a verdict: is there one theory that best explains the practice, or is it best explained as an unstable compromise among theories that do not fit together — and if the latter, is that instability a defect, or is it precisely what makes the institution defensible?

Please argue rather than survey. I am not interested in a reading list, and I have no way of looking up references, so do not pad this with citations. Where you are unsure, say what you are unsure about and why. Aim for roughly 1,200 to 1,800 words, written for a reader who knows some philosophy but not this particular literature.
