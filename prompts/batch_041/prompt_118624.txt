I am a graduate student sitting in on a philosophy of biology seminar, and last week's session turned into an argument I have not been able to settle on my own. The topic was biological function, and the two positions on the table were the ones usually labelled etiological and causal-role: on the first, a trait's function is whatever it was selected for, so a trait can have a function it is currently failing to perform, and a malformed heart is a malfunctioning heart; on the second, function-talk is a matter of current causal organization rather than history, so a trait's function is the contribution it actually makes to the operation of the larger system that contains it, and the interesting work is done by an analysis of how the system's capacities are subserved by its parts. A classmate tried to dispose of the dispute in a single move. Organisms, he said, are machines — that is the picture that set the whole problem going in the first place — and the function of a part in a machine is simply the job it was built to do. The heart is the organism's pump; the pump's function is to pump because that is what it is for; therefore the etiological account is not one optional gloss on function-talk among others but the plain meaning of it, and the causal-role account must be describing something else — current activity, or useful effect — and mislabelling it. He has taken to calling this the machine-shop picture, and several people in the room nodded. I nodded too, and then spent the walk home feeling that I had been sold something.

What I want is not a recap of the two views; assume I can state them well enough to be bored by it. I want an audit of the analogy as an argument. Begin with the correspondence table: what plays the role of the machine, the part, the specification or blueprint, the designer, the assembly line, the warranty, the repair manual, the part that has been broken for years while the machine keeps running, and the part whose presence nobody can explain. For each row, say whether it is a structural parallel or a surface resemblance. Then tell me where the mapping breaks, and in particular which features of the machine case the analogy quietly imports that an evolved organism lacks, and which of those imports are doing the work in my classmate's inference rather than merely decorating it.

Then bring the breaks to bear on the cases where I suspect the two accounts come apart, and say which way each case actually cuts. A structure that now makes an indispensable contribution but was never selected for that contribution, having arisen for some other job and been co-opted — a wrist bone that once braced a joint and now helps strip bamboo, or a gas sac that does buoyancy work and descends from what was a lung. A trait that was selected for something it no longer does at all. A heart that could never have been selected because it has no evolutionary history whatsoever, having assembled by chance in a swamp and begun pumping. The plain fact that a heart makes a noise and a nose will hold up a pair of glasses, neither of which anyone wants to call a function. Someone more careful than the rest of us objected at one point that we kept sliding between a trait's being selected and its being selected for, and I still cannot tell how much turns on that distinction here. Finally, say whether the one-move refutation survives once the disanalogies are on the table — whether the machine-shop picture supports the etiological account, the causal-role account, both, or neither — and if it cannot carry the conclusion, sketch an analogy from some other domain that could, and say what it would have to preserve that the machine does not.
