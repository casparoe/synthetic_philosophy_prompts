I am an undergraduate enrolled in a course on the ethics and governance of advanced AI, and I came to it after four years of competitive policy debate, so nearly all of my instincts about argument are in that vocabulary: resolutions and plans, the affirmative and the negative, topicality, disadvantages, counterplans, kritiks, presumption and fiat, the flow, speaker points, judge paradigms, and the ordinary craft of judge adaptation — modeling the person in the back of the room and building a case that wins with that person in particular.

The proposal we have been studying is the one that treats debate as a mechanism of scalable oversight: train two systems to argue a question before a judge, with the judge — a human, or a weaker model — awarding the win, on the hope that it is easier to detect a bad answer than to produce a good one, so that a judge who could not solve a problem alone can still reliably select the correct answer when it is defended adversarially. Two features of the scheme are supposed to do the heavy lifting: the judge is weaker than the advocates by design, and the apparatus has to keep working as the advocates get smarter.

Please do two things. First, restate that proposal as faithfully as you can in the vocabulary I actually have, the tournament vocabulary above, and as a translation rather than an analogy: the same claims and the same structure, with the roles of judge, advocate, resolution, and win condition mapped onto their nearest counterparts in a debate round, and the central worry — that the advocates will find ways to win without being right — stated in the terms a debater would use. Second, give me the residue report: what translates cleanly, what has to be distorted to fit, and what has no expression at all in tournament debate. I am especially interested in three points. Does anything in the oversight proposal correspond to the resolution — is there a fact of the matter about what the debate is about, or does the proposal quietly leave that to the judge? Is the assumption that the honest advocate wins even expressible in a game whose scoring evolved to reward dropped arguments, technical concessions, and speaker points, or does that assumption require a judge paradigm that no competitive circuit actually trains for? And is there anything the tournament community learned about judges — paradigm disclosure, mutual preference judging, judge adaptation as a recognized strategic skill — that the oversight proposal has no way to state but that would matter to it if it could?
