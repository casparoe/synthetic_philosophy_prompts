I am a novelist, and the book I am writing has acquired a character who will not stop arguing. He is a scholar in a city that has lately been sacked, he has been reading al-Ghazālī, and this is the case he puts to anyone who will listen:

(1) Watch fire touch cotton and the cotton burn: you see the burning follow the touching, and follow it every time. You never see the connection between the two, and there is nothing there to see.
(2) Nothing follows from a constant succession except that it has been constant. So we never observe in the fire any power to burn.
(3) Therefore no created thing produces anything. God produces each event immediately, and the fire is only the occasion on which He burns the cotton.
(4) It makes no difference whether we call an event natural or miraculous; both come from the same hand with the same immediacy, and only habit distinguishes them.
(5) It is with my mind as with the cotton: my reasons do not produce my conclusions. God produces my assent, and the argument I take myself to be following is only the occasion of it.

He says the last step is not an embarrassment to him but a consequence of the rest, and that a man who believes he has reasoned his way to it has merely been caused to believe so. He is the cleverest person in the book, so I cannot answer him by making him a fool. I need him to be wrong, because I have four hundred pages in which people act on reasons and are ruined by it, and if nothing anyone does produces anything, I have written a chronicle and not a plot. Tell me which step to put the novel's weight against, why that step looks innocent until it is pressed, and what I give up elsewhere in the argument by refusing it — or, if no step can be refused, say so plainly and tell me what I am left with.
