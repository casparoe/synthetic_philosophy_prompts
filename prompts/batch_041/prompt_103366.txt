I am a novelist, and I am writing a book set in an invented country — call it the Republic of Vessary — that has just emerged from a long authoritarian period. My protagonist is a young magistrate on a newly constituted bench. The old regime's statutes remain on the books, and the new constitution provides that prior law continues in force until amended or repealed. Nearly every chapter turns on a ruling she must make, and I need those rulings to be consistent with one another and to read as the output of a legal system rather than as the author's convenience. I have been reading about the long dispute between Hart and Fuller over whether a morally iniquitous rule can count as law, and I want to convert that dispute into something my magistrate — who has no training in jurisprudence, works under time pressure, and must produce a short written order that a military overseer will countersign — could actually run.

Please give me a decision procedure, not an essay. I want the questions she asks in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, the point at which the procedure stops with a finding that the rule is not law at all, the point at which it stops with a finding that it is law and must be applied however distasteful, and the points at which it must hand the matter to someone else — the legislature, the appellate bench, the overseer — because the question is political rather than legal. It should be usable in an afternoon by someone who has never heard of either theorist, and it should be explicit about which distinctions from the debate had to survive the simplification and which I can let go. Tell me where the procedure will misfire and what it simply cannot do; I would rather know its edges than have it pretend to be complete.

Then run it, step by step, on three cases from the book. First, a man prosecuted under an old decree that criminalizes the publication of writing liable to spread despondency among the population; he has published a poem. Second, a former informer who denounced neighbours under a statute that was, by every procedural test, validly enacted at the time, and who now pleads that his conduct was lawful when he did it. Third, a detention order issued under a secret directive that was never published and that contradicts a standing statute on the same subject. One of these should come out in a way I will not like — I am fond of the poet, and I would prefer the law to release him. Do not bend the procedure to spare him; if it convicts, let it convict, and show me exactly which step does it.

Finally, tell me where the procedure's output is a judgment call wearing the costume of a rule. Those are the places where my characters can disagree in good faith, and I need to know where they are before I write the scene.
