I'm a novelist, historical fiction mostly, and I'm about four months into a book set in the aftermath of a war that may or may not have happened — I'm keeping the exact setting vague on purpose because my problem isn't specific to it. The problem is this. I have a contract for two more books after this one, and all three are set in periods where the line between what happened and what got told is genuinely contested: the Trojan War, Arthur, the Exodus. Every time I sit down to write a scene I have to make a call about some element — was there actually a decade-long siege of one city by a coalition of kings, or is that a compression of a century of raiding and one bad sack; was there a war-leader behind Arthur at all, and if so was he a king or a warlord or just a name someone needed; did a large population leave Egypt and become a nation, or is that a story a nation told about itself afterward. I make maybe fifty of these calls per book, I make them in an afternoon between other things, and I'm not a historian. Right now I do it on vibes, and then my historical consultant and the copy-editor at the publisher both come back and ask me is this true, and I never have a clean answer, because I genuinely don't know what I'm claiming. I don't want a reading list — I don't have time for one and I'm not going to get to a library this year anyway — and I don't want the standard shrug that the Trojan War is probably based on something real, because that's what I already tell myself.

So what I want from you is a decision procedure I can actually run on a single legendary element, in twenty minutes, and come out with a verdict I can defend to a consultant. Not a lecture on the philosophy of history — a procedure. The questions to ask in order, what counts as a good enough answer at each step, where it should stop and just say no, and where it has to hand the question back to me because it's a call about values, or about what kind of book I'm writing, rather than a fact about the past. And I want it to output something more useful than true or false. I think the honest options are more like: I can write this as established fact; I can write it as contested, with my characters disagreeing about it; I can write it as something people believe, or claim, or are told; or I can invent it outright and not pretend otherwise. If the procedure can sort an element into one of those four, and tell me when it's sorting on evidence and when it's sorting on my nerve, that's what I need. It also has to be honest about how it will misfire — where the tests historians use don't transfer to a novel, where I'll be tempted to fudge because I want a particular scene, and where the real question is about my reader rather than about the past.

Then run it, properly, on three elements, one from each of my periods, and don't be gentle: (1) a ten-year siege of Troy by a coalition of Greek kings over one woman, with a specific cast of named heroes; (2) Arthur as a king with a court, a queen, a capital and borders you could draw on a map; (3) a mass departure of several hundred thousand Israelites out of Egypt, forty years in the desert, arriving as a people. I want to watch the procedure actually work on each one — where it stops, what it decides, what it can't decide. Include at least one verdict I won't like, because I suspect I won't like all three, and finish by telling me plainly what the procedure can't do for me and where I'm still on my own with nothing but taste.
