So my buddy and I spent like two hours last night arguing about whether AI assistants are paternalistic, and I dont think we ever got to the actual question, which is what the behavior presupposes. Let me describe the thing itself, because I think the pattern is weirder than either of us admitted.

Ask an assistant how to disable the smoke detector in your apartment so you can sear a steak, or what a dangerous dose of a common over the counter medication is, or how to do some genuinely risky thing that's your own business, and you get a polite refusal with a little speech about safety. Ask the same system how to free solo a rock face, how to get into a street fight, or how to tank your own credit score, and it will happily talk your ear off. Ask for the exact same dangerous info but say you're writing a novel and it just... gives it to you. And the refusal, when it comes, is basically unappealable. There's no "here's my situation, I'm a grown adult, I understand the risk" that changes anything. Usually you get a hotline number or "please talk to a professional," even if you never said a word about being in crisis. Meanwhile it won't tell you whether to leave your boyfriend, because that's "your decision to make" -- so it protects your autonomy over the big stuff and overrides it on the small stuff.

What I want to know is what theory of paternalism that whole practice presupposes. My guess is it's some kind of soft paternalism -- intervene only when the person's judgment is actually compromised -- but the practice doesn't look like that at all, because it triggers on topic, not on the user's state. So either it's a hard paternalism that we'd all say is unjustified in a human advisor, or the real justification isn't paternalism toward the user at all but something else: that anyone can read the output, or that the provider doesn't want to be the proximate cause of a death, or plain legal and PR exposure. Those are completely different commitments and I want them separated out.

So: reconstruct the implicit theory, tell me which rival views it rules out, and tell me whether the parts are even consistent with each other. Where is the practice philosophically underdetermined -- i.e. which theories would license exactly this behavior, so we can't read the design off the behavior? And then the part I actually care about: if you swapped in a straight anti-paternalist view where competent adults get to make their own bad decisions, what would concretely have to change? An appeals mechanism, a competence check, refusals conditioned on the person rather than the topic, something else? Does that design stay as safe, and if not, is the safety we're buying with the current design worth what it costs in autonomy?
