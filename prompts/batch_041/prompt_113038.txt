Last month I sat in on a workshop on governance and development, and at the dinner afterward two of the participants — call them Dana and Alex — got into an argument that I've been chewing on ever since, partly because I honestly couldn't tell who had the better of it. Dana has spent years working on public-sector reform; Alex is an anthropologist who did long fieldwork in a region where the state is thinly staffed and civil servants are badly paid. The case they were fighting over was this: in a provincial permits office, applicants routinely hand the desk officer a payment — sometimes framed as a gift, sometimes as a thank-you for speed — to get a routine permit processed, and the officer is expected to pass part of it upward to his superiors and outward to his extended family and home village. Dana said this is corruption, full stop: a public official is converting a public power into private gain, and relabeling the payment a gift is just a euphemism; the fact that it's customary doesn't make it legitimate, any more than a customary practice of paying judges would be legitimate. Alex said that's the reflex of someone who has only ever known one kind of state. The payment, she argued, is embedded in a logic of reciprocity and mutual obligation that predates the formal bureaucracy and runs alongside it; it creates relationships of accountability where the impersonal office creates none; and the word corruption smuggles in a specifically Northern ideal of the disinterested official and then grades everyone else against it. She added that the same critics never blink at campaign contributions, paid lobbying, and the revolving door, which accomplish similar things by other, legalized means.

Dana came back at her: by that reasoning nothing is corruption anywhere — if being embedded in reciprocity exonerates the permit officer, it exonerates the minister who loots the treasury to fund his party's patronage machine. Alex said he was sliding: she wasn't claiming the arrangement was beyond criticism, only that the criticism had to be made in local terms — does it produce accountability and welfare, or does it merely extract? The universalist frame, she said, can't even ask that question, because it begins and ends with the verdict corruption. Dana took that up: fine, let's ask it — who actually benefits? The applicants who can afford the payment and the officer's own network; everyone outside that network waits, or goes without. That's a distributive injustice, and calling it gift-exchange doesn't change who is shut out. Alex replied that the impersonal alternative has its own insiders and outsiders; it just hides the queue behind forms. She also pointed to a distinction he was flattening — speed money for a permit you're legally owed is not the same as a bribe for a permit you're not — and said that collapsing the two is exactly the leveling she was objecting to. Dana said the distinction rescues nothing: in both cases you're paying an official to decide on grounds other than the ones he's supposed to use. And there they stalled.

I'd like you to referee. Go through their exchange move by move — rate each move from clearly mistaken through clearly right, and justify each rating briefly — and then tell me who has the better overall position and what each of them is missing. I'm less interested in a verdict on the fictional officer than in where the arguments themselves succeed and fail.
