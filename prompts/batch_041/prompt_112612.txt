For a graduate seminar on moral formation I am planning, a colleague has proposed a unit that pairs selections from Plutarch's paired Lives with a range of saints' lives, on the premise that the two are settings of a single pedagogical device: an edifying narrative that puts an admirable figure before a reader and lets admiration do the moral work. She distinguishes two settings. In the first, which she calls lateral exemplarity, the figure is close enough to the reader that the reader's own virtue could plausibly approach his, and Plutarch is her model. In the second, which she calls transcendent exemplarity, the figure stands above the reader, who can only revere rather than emulate, and the hagiographers are her models. She adds that in both genres fidelity to fact is subordinate to formative use, so the difference between them is one of altitude rather than of kind, and she wants the seminar to consider whether the second setting simply outperforms the first. My problem is that this looks exactly backwards. If the mechanism is one and the same, the loftier exemplar ought to be the more effective one, and the saints have everything on their side: virtue beyond dispute, suffering borne without complaint, and a reward promised to anyone who follows. Yet readers of the saints' lives seem to come away with devotion rather than emulation, while readers of Plutarch have claimed for centuries to have been made better by him. What am I failing to see? Is this a matter of degree, in which case my colleague's single-device picture stands and I need a better account of why the loftier setting fails to move people? Or is it a difference in kind, in which case I want to know precisely where the picture breaks, what each genre asks of its reader that the other does not, and whether altitude is even the right axis along which to place them? Please answer in American English.
