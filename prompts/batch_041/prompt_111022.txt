I teach an upper-level bioethics seminar and I'm writing the final. I want one essay question on the brain-death-versus-circulatory-death thing that a student cannot answer by parroting "the whole brain, including the brainstem" back at me, and I want the question, a model answer of around 700-800 words, and a marking scheme that says exactly which moves a top answer has to make and which mistakes should cost points.

Here's the setup I've been teaching, so the question should live inside it. When we withdraw life support in a controlled donation-after-circulatory-death case, the protocol waits somewhere around two to five minutes of no heartbeat and no breathing, and then declares death. The usual defense of that is that the loss of circulation is 'permanent' rather than 'irreversible' -- permanent meaning it won't come back by itself and nobody intends to restart it, irreversible meaning it couldn't be restarted even if someone tried. Those two come apart at exactly the moment that matters: a couple minutes in, chest compressions or ECMO could plausibly get that heart going again, so the patient isnt irreversible in the strict sense, only permanent because everyone has agreed to stand down. And the dead donor rule says you have to be dead before we take your organs. So I want students to confront whether the weaker notion is doing any real work or whether its a fudge, and then whether the same slippage is hiding in the brain-death criterion, where a body on a ventilator can keep a heartbeat, digest, heal, and even grow while we call it dead. Also throw in the higher-brain versus whole-brain fight, because some students will want to say a person in a persistent vegetative state is dead, and I want the question to force them to own that rather than mumble it.

Make the question answerable in 90 minutes and worth 25 points, and put the point breakdown in the marking scheme so a B answer and an A answer are visibly different -- a B recites the two legal criteria and the permanence move, an A actually takes a position on whether permanent-but-not-irreversible satisfies the dead donor rule and anticipates the strongest objection to its own position. Dont ask for readings or citations, we've already done those. Write everything in American English.
