I am a high school student preparing a short presentation on Buddhist views of the self, and I have been working from a recorded lecture series by a professor whose explanations I generally find clear. His summary of the doctrine of no-self goes roughly like this:

"The central insight of Buddhism is that the self is an illusion. The Buddha taught that what we call a person is nothing but a bundle of five constantly changing processes -- the body, feelings, perceptions, impulses, and consciousness -- and because none of these is permanent, there is no self. This is the doctrine of anatta, and it means that personal identity is a fiction we invent for convenience. The teaching was so radical that later Buddhist thinkers, especially Nagarjuna, extended it into the view that everything is empty. Since there is no self, Buddhism denies that any enduring person could be reborn; the cycle of rebirth is really just the continuation of impersonal causal processes, and moral responsibility is in the same way a useful fiction. Modern neuroscience has largely vindicated all of this, since imaging studies have not found any place in the brain where a self is located."

I suspect that most of this is broadly right, but that a few of the steps are doing more work than they should. Would you go through the passage claim by claim and tell me, for each one, whether a scholar of Buddhist thought would accept it as stated, would treat it as a defensible simplification, or would reject it outright? I am particularly unsure about the move from the absence of a self to conclusions about rebirth and moral responsibility, and about the appeal to neuroscience at the end: does the conclusion actually follow from whatever is true in the earlier claims, or is it being smuggled in? And if the characterization of anatta in the middle of the passage turns out to be off, does the rest of the argument still stand on its own? I am not asking for a reading list; I mainly want to know what I can safely say aloud and what I should phrase much more carefully.
