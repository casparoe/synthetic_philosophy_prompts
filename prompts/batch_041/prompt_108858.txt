I'm in a second-year philosophy of science seminar that's drifted off into economics methodology, and I've been arguing with a freind for three weeks about Friedman's F-twist — the view that a theory's assumptions don't have to be realistic at all, only its predictions have to come out right, and that the more wildly unrealistic the assumptions, the more powerful the theory. He thinks this is obviously correct and that the critics are just being prissy about description. Meanwhile I reread the original Foundation trilogy (Foundation, Foundation and Empire, Second Foundation — I'd rather you stayed with those three and didn't pull in the later sequels or prequels) and I can't shake the feeling that psychohistory is the F-twist in narrative form. It only works on populations of trillions, it treats people as so many gas molecules, it explicitly cannot say anything about any single individual — and that's precisely the unrealism Friedman tells us to stop fretting about, because the predictions (the fall of the Empire, thirty thousand years of chaos cut down to a thousand) come out right. Then the Mule shows up, one individual who shouldn't have mattered, and the whole thing comes apart, down to the pre-recorded Seldon message in the Time Vault turning out to be about a crisis that isn't the one happening. Then we find out there's a Second Foundation whose entire job is nudging deviations back onto the Plan, which I think is either the best thing in the books or a total cheat depending on the day.

What I want is a proper reading of the trilogy through the F-twist debate, not just an aha-psychohistory-is-Friedman take. Somewhere to start: does the Mule falsify the theory, so the assumptions were doing real work after all and Friedman is wrong, or does he show that the population assumption was really a scope condition — psychohistory never claimed to cover individuals, so nothing was refuted and only the description of its domain was too loose? A friend in the seminar keeps insisting you have to sort assumptions into at least three kinds: the ones that are false but harmless because the neglected factor is negligible, the ones that quietly draw the boundary of the theory's domain, and the ones that are pure scaffolding to get the mathematics moving. I'd like the reading to say which kind psychohistory's population assumption is, and whether the trilogy itself can tell the difference or just muddles the three together. The bit I care about most: in a novel the predictions are guaranteed, because Asimov is the one writing them, so my suspicion is that the pleasure of watching the Plan come true is exactly the pleasure Friedman's criterion invites us to take in a model — we mistake the author's control for predictive power. If that's right, say so; if it's a cheap trick the books actually defuse, say that instead. And please don't bend the novels to fit the philosophy: tell me where they push back, or where a scene I've read as confirming the point actually cuts the other way.

Start with your verdict on the Friedman question in two or three sentences, then the reading. Hard cap 900 words.
