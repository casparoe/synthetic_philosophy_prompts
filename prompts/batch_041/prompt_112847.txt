I teach a 300-level seminar on decision theory and philosophy of probability — 14 students, 75 minutes, one session. They can all do expected value and conditional probability; most have never seen the Sleeping Beauty problem, and the two who have think it's just a shouting match about whether the answer is 1/2 or 1/3. Design an in-class game that makes them feel the pull of the puzzle in their bodies before anyone names it.

What I want them to run into: that one answer looks *mandatory* once money is on the table, and then, the moment I change what the bet is, the same answer starts to look like it's exploiting a bookkeeping trick. Both of those have to happen in the room, not in my summary.

The mechanic I have in mind — change it if you've got something better — is a betting floor. Split them into Sleepers, Bankers, and Auditors. Bankers must post a single two-sided price on whether the hidden outcome came up heads: one number, and they have to take either side of it at that number, no spread, capped stakes, and the price has to be written down before any result is revealed. Sleepers fill out a bet slip at each awakening. Auditors don't bet; they tally, on a sheet nobody else can see until later, how many slips got filled in under each outcome.

Two things I want built into the rules. First, use a randomiser with a lopsided, non-obvious bias — say heads comes up about one time in four, via a bag of coloured chips — so nobody can coast on the remembered 1/2-vs-1/3 answer and they have to derive a price from scratch. Second, run it in two configurations: in one, slips are filled at every awakening; in the other, the bet can be placed at most once per experiment. Same randomiser, same payouts. Let the tally sheet and the Bankers' books do the talking.

They'll recieve tokens or candy, not money, and there should be no lecture up front — no mention of Beauty, halfers, thirders, or self-locating belief until the debrief, and ideally not even then until they've already argued themselves into positions. I'd rather they spend the last twenty minutes trying to say what the two configurations were actually testing.

Give me: the full rules (roles, materials, payout scheme, how a round is settled, how the amnesia is faked physically — identical booths, blindfolds, a helper who can't say which round it is), a minute-by-minute timeline for the 75, and the debrief. The debrief should walk back through specific moments — the tally reveal, the moment a Banker's price gets bought out, the moment someone says the once-per-experiment version feels like a seperate problem — and map each onto the actual dispute, including the halfer's strongest point, which is about evidence and not about being a bad bettor. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle it: I'm guessing at least one student declares the whole thing incoherent because no drug works like that, and at least one Banker refuses to post a number and tries to hedge or widen a spread, and someone tries to retroactively make their bet a once-per-experiment one after seeing the tally.

Finally, be honest with me about what the activity can't do. I already suspect it can't reproduce the first-person thing at all: my students always know which round they're in, and the Auditors' tally is exactly the god's-eye information that makes the puzzle bite, so I'll have to supply that part myself. If there's more the activity can't reach, say so plainly rather than pretending the game covers it.
