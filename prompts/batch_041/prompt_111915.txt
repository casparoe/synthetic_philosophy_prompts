I'm a novelist — four books, the last two written in a close third person that I've come to think of as my whole method — and for the past eight months I've been at work on a novel whose central character is a composer of instrumental music, which has had the side effect of sending me to bed with books on aesthetics. My closest friend from university is a philosopher, and over dinner a few weeks ago he insisted that Schopenhauer's chapter on music, the one in the third book of The World as Will and Representation, is the most serious thing anyone has ever written about the art, and that I could not write a composer honestly without coming to terms with it. I've now read that chapter twice, along with a couple of summaries, and I can follow the shape of the thing well enough: that music is not, like painting or poetry, a copy of the Ideas, but something much closer to a copy of the will itself; that the arrangement of voices from the bass upward tracks the grades of the will's objectification, with melody standing for the life of human striving — its satisfactions and its immediate relapse into fresh desire; that music consequently does not express this or that particular joy or sorrow but joy and sorrow as such; and that this is why he thought the words of an opera should never claim first place. What I cannot do is hold any of it in my own head, because the only vocabulary I possess for art is the vocabulary of my trade, and it keeps refusing the material.

So here is what I want from you. Restate that position, as faithfully as you can, in the language I actually work in: scene and summary, showing and telling, point of view, free indirect discourse, voice, focalization, subtext, dramatic irony, the gap between what the narrator knows and what the character knows, the difference between a novel that renders and a novel that explains, the rule that you cannot write the word grief — you can only write the particular gesture a particular person makes. Take his claims one at a time rather than giving me a general essay: the copy-of-the-will claim, the hierarchy of voices, the melody claim, the universality claim, the point about opera, and anything else you consider load-bearing. Then the part I actually care about. Tell me which of these survive the translation intact, which survive only with a distortion you have to flag (and say precisely what gets bent), and which have no counterpart in my vocabulary at all — where I would simply have to fall silent. For each leftover, give me your judgment as to its source: is my vocabulary thinner than his thought, is his claim vaguer than it looks so that the leftover is really fog, or is there a genuine disagreement between us that only becomes visible once both are stated in the same terms? I have a suspicion of my own — that what he calls the will in music is simply the tension and deferral I know as narrative momentum, and that a novelist should therefore be less impressed than my friend is — but I would rather you told me than have me tell you.

Two warnings, since I know how these exercises go. Don't let the translation quietly import my assumptions; in particular, do not assume that anything expressive must have a narrator, a tense, or a world, because that is the prejudice of my medium and I want to see it exposed rather than flattered. And don't hand me a tidy metaphor where the honest answer is that the term is missing. Assume I know the craft vocabulary cold and the philosophical vocabulary not at all, and don't send me a reading list — I want the translation and the leftover, not the sources. If music turns out to be the one art my vocabulary cannot describe, say so plainly, and tell me what that says about the vocabulary, or about him.
