Ok so heres something that bugs me. A famous painter can run a whole studio of assistants who do most of the actual brushwork, and everyone still calls the painting his — the assistants get a line in the catalogue, he gets the show. But when someone writes a long fiddly prompt for an image model and it spits out something gorgeous, most people say that person isn't the artist at all, they're just a "prompter." Same with the copyright offices that won't register a picture a machine made by itself. We do X in one place and Y in the other, and I can't tell if thats a principled line or just habit.

What view of creativity does that split presuppose? Does it need a theory about who causes the work, or who expresses the intention, or who does the mental work while someone else does the handwork — or is it really a social practice of attributing authorship that got dressed up as a theory? Reconstruct the implicit view as well as you can, tell me what the practice strictly requires versus what it just happens to fit comfortably, and say where the two halves might actually contradict each other. (Photography got the "machine did it" treatment for decades too, before everyone gave up and called it art.)

Then push on the edges. Someone who hits generate 300 times and keeps the one good one. Someone who curates ten thousand model outputs into a series. A model that generates and picks on its own with nobody in the loop. And the photographer who just points a machine at the world. Which of these does the implicit view handle, which does it break on, and if we swapped in a rival — creativity as selection, as social attribution, or as whatever a community decides to reward — what would actually have to change in how galleries, prizes, and the little labels on the wall work?
