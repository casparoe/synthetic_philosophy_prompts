I am a high school junior and a member of our school’s philosophy of science club. Last week our faculty advisor handed out a one-page summary of something she called the Presumption of Independence, which she defined roughly as follows: when you are reasoning about whether two variables A and B are related, and you have no evidence that they are dependent, you should proceed as if they are independent, using that assumption in your predictions and in any causal model you build. She stressed that this is not a metaphysical claim that A and B really are independent, only a default heuristic that keeps you from seeing patterns in noise. A friend in the club, who likes to build arguments with numbered steps, says the handout commits our advisor to something absurd, and I cannot find the exit. His argument runs: (1) The Presumption of Independence says that if you do not already know that A and B are dependent, you should treat them as independent. (2) To come to know that A and B are dependent, you must at some point gather or recognize evidence that they are dependent. (3) But gathering or recognizing evidence that they are dependent requires you to treat them as candidates for dependence, that is, not to treat them as independent. (4) So the Presumption forbids the only stance from which you could ever learn that A and B are dependent. (5) Therefore, if the Presumption is correct, no one can ever learn that any two variables are dependent. (6) But we obviously do learn such things; a student can learn that studying and grades are correlated, or that a certain medicine and a side effect are related. (7) Therefore the Presumption is false.

I suspect something slipped, but I cannot name it. Which step do you reject, and what does rejecting it cost? If I want to keep the Presumption as a working heuristic, what do I have to give up or restate, and how would you formalize it so that the same argument cannot be rebuilt against the formal version? If instead I keep the argument, what does that cost me in the way I do science? I would rather keep the Presumption than accept (5), but I want to know what I am paying.
