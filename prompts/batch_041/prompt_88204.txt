So I've been in this reading group on teleosemantics for about a year now — we started with Millikan and Dretske and the general idea that a mental or neural state gets its content from the biological function of whatever produces or consumes it, and honestly we've been having the exact same argument since like month two and nobody has moved an inch. The setup, as I understand it: the teleosemantic side says a state represents what it's supposed to represent because of what it was selected to do — the frog's tongue-snap represents a fly, or frog-food, because the mechanism was selected to track that, and the content is fixed by the proper function of the consumer, not by whatever happens to trigger it. The critics keep coming back with basically three clubs. First, the disjunction problem: if the mechanism reliably fires on both flies and small dark moving pellets, why does it represent fly rather than fly-or-pellet, and doesn't any answer just let us gerrymander the function to fit whatever content we already wanted? Second, the swampman-type case: a molecule-for-molecule duplicate that pops into existence with no selection history at all still seems to mean things, which would make the history irrelevant to content. Third, distality: is the state about the distal cause or the proximal stimulus, and does the theory even have the resources to say?

Here's my working hypothesis, and I'd like you to tell me if it's right, half right, or just wrong: I think the two sides aren't actually disagreeing about the same thing. The naturalizers are trying to answer what makes a state a representation at all, such that we can slot it into a naturalistic story, and they're fine with content being somewhat indeterminate or relative to the interests of the theorist — indeterminacy is a feature, not a bug. The critics are implicitly demanding a theory that pins down one determinate content per state, the way a formal semantics does, and then complaining that teleosemantics doesn't deliver it. If that's the real split, then the disjunction and distality objections are only lethal against a stronger claim than the teleosemanticists are actually making, and the swampman point is doing something else again — maybe testing whether the theory is offering a constitutive account or just a sufficient condition. But maybe I'm wrong and there's a genuine substantive dissagreement underneath about whether representation is a natural kind with an essence or a folk category we're just regimenting, or about whether the selection story is even empirically true of brains.

What I want is a diagnosis, not a survey: which of these is the load-bearing disagreement, whether it's verbal or substantive or two questions hiding under one name, and — given the diagnosis — what would actually count as progress here. A distinction? A formal result about how much indeterminacy is tolerable? A change of question? Or is this one of those debates where we should just admit the question is under-specified and stop expecting closure? Also, one of the people in the group is a computational neuroscientist who keeps insisting the whole thing is an empirical question about whether real brains even have the selection-shaped consumers the theory needs, and I genuinely can't tell if that's a profound point or a category error, so say something about that too.
