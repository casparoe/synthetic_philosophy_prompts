Background: I'm an infra/backend engineer, no real philosophy training (one ethics module in undergrad that I mostly slept through), but I've been reading around the AI alignment stuff and I wrote a short essay for my team's internal blog arguing a position I haven't seen put quite this way. I'd like you to tear into it. Accuracy first, then tell me whether it's actually interesting or just a relabeling of something old that I don't know the name of. Be blunt.

The thesis: an AI system should be deontological in its constitution and consequentialist in its deliberation.

The argument, roughly:

1. If you build a system whose deliberation is straightforwardly act-utilitarian — it just maximizes expected welfare over all available actions — it's going to be unstable. Expected value with tiny probabilities is a known trap: a 10^-30 chance of an astronomically large welfare gain dominates almost anything, so the "correct" move is often to do something monstrous for a rounding error's worth of probability. I've seen this called Pascal's mugging, but whatever you call it, any agent that takes expected value literally over unbounded outcomes will happily burn down a city for a sliver of a chance at utopia. So pure utilitarian deliberation seems like a non-starter for anything with real capabilities.

2. But deontology as the actual decision procedure is also bad. Rules conflict, they can't be enumerated ahead of time for open-ended situations, and a rigid rule-follower is exploitable — you just build the world so the only way out is through the forbidden act. So I don't want the system deliberating like a Kantian either.

3. So split the levels. The constitution is the set of things the system never does, period, regardless of expected value — hard side-constraints on the action space, like a type system or a set of runtime invariants. It doesn't matter that violating them would be net positive; the action isn't in the space. Then deliberation is consequentialist: among the actions the constitution permits, pick the one that maximizes expected welfare. Constraint layer says what's allowed, optimizer says what's best within that.

4. This is basically what we already do with safety in software. You don't write a controller that "usually" respects the invariant; you put a monitor in the loop that blocks the unsafe transition, and let the planner do whatever it wants in the space that's left. The invariant isn't a term in the objective, it's a seperate layer.

Where I get nervous, and where I want you to push: is this a real position or is it just rule-consequentialism wearing a hoodie? Someone is choosing the constitution, and choosing it well means thinking about consequences, so the whole thing bottoms out in consequences anyway. And if the constitution can't be written as a finite rule list — because situations are open-ended — then it has to be a learned model of "stuff a decent person would never do," which is itself a consequentialist artifact. So maybe I've smuggled in the very thing I was trying to get away from. Also I'm not sure whether this is a claim about how to *build* a system or a claim about what's *right*, and I keep sliding between the two.

Specifically I'd like: (a) is the two-level split coherent, or does it collapse into rule-consequentialism? (b) is claim 1 about utilitarian instability actually right, or am I overstating it? (c) what assumptions am I making that I haven't stated — especially about the constitution being fixed vs. revisable. (d) does the software analogy mislead anywhere? Thanks.
