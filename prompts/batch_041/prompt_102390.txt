I have been reading about Quine's gavagai example alongside a friend's description of a machine-learning system that is trained on large amounts of paired video and speech and learns to attach labels to what it sees. My friend's view is that with enough data such a system could in principle settle every ambiguity the field linguist faces: gavagai would eventually be pinned down to 'rabbit' rather than 'undetached rabbit parts' or 'rabbit-stage', because the statistics would decide it. As I understand Quine, his point is that the evidence available to the linguist always underdetermines the choice, so she can never be certain which translation is correct. But doesn't that simply mean she needs more evidence, of the kind the model has? And would not a bilingual who grew up speaking both languages simply know which translation is right, without having to infer it from behavior at all? If so, is the indeterminacy of translation not just an epistemic limitation — a gap between our evidence and a fact that is there anyway — one that better data or native competence could in principle close?
