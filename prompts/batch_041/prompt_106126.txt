I have just come back from a long argument with a friend who works on quantum foundations, and before I see him again I want to write down where I actually stand. The argument was about probability in the Everettian many-worlds interpretation — the cluster of problems people call the Born rule problem — and I found myself defending a package that I suspect may not hang together. Here it is. (1) The Everettian picture is correct: the wavefunction evolves unitarily, there is no collapse, and all branches are equally real. (2) There is no objective probability in fundamental physics; probability talk in this setting is talk about an agent’s self-locating uncertainty among branches, not about chances that single out some branches as more likely to occur. (3) Nonetheless, the rational credence for an agent to assign to finding herself on a branch is given by the Born rule — amplitude squared — and not by counting branches equally or by any other measure. (4) The right way to justify (3) is decision-theoretic: an agent’s preferences over rewards should be representable as expected utility with Born-weighted branches, and that is what makes probability talk in Everettian quantum mechanics meaningful rather than merely decorative. (5) I accept the epistemic separability principle: the value or credence an agent assigns to what happens on one branch should not depend on what happens on other branches that are causally disconnected from it. (6) I do not think personal identity across branches is what matters; what matters is that there are successors who inherit my preferences and projects. (7) I accept a no-double-counting principle: when I evaluate a gamble, a branch should contribute once, through its amplitude weight, and not again through the number of descendant branches or the number of copies of me on it.

My friend pressed me on the incoherence problem: if every outcome is realized by some future version of me, each future version should be certain of their own outcome, so where does self-locating uncertainty come from? I said it comes from the first-person perspective before branching, but he said that is just smuggling in a probability. I want an audit, not a lecture. Which of these commitments are genuinely in tension, which only appear to be, and how severe is each tension? Please order the genuine tensions by severity. For each, say exactly which commitment has to give or be weakened, what the cheapest revision would be, and what that revision costs elsewhere in the package. I am especially interested in whether (2) and (7) can survive (3) and (4) without my having already assumed the very notion of probability I claim not to believe in, whether (5) is doing real work or is just a restatement of (2), and whether (6) dissolves or deepens the worry about first-person uncertainty. I do not know which commitment I would abandon, so do not assume I am secretly attached to any of them. Finally, if one single revision would clean up the most, tell me which one and what I would be left with.
