I teach a graduate seminar in normative ethics, and this term we are working through the ethics of care — the tradition that treats persons as relational and dependent rather than as self-sufficient contractors, and that locates the moral point of view in the concrete practices of attending to, taking responsibility for, and responding to particular others rather than in impartial rules or in the adjudication of competing claims. I want a case study that will force the seminar to see both what the framework can do and where it strains, and the case I have settled on is the Chicago heat wave of July 1995. Over roughly a week that month, the city's death toll climbed into the many hundreds, and the dead were overwhelmingly the elderly, the poor, and the socially isolated; the deaths were not spread evenly, either, but clustered in certain neighborhoods while demographically similar ones — equally poor, but with more people on the stoops, in the shops, and in one another's company — were spared. A much-discussed sociological study argued that what killed people was less the heat itself than the failure of the ordinary social ties and public spaces through which neighbors ordinarily look in on one another. I do not want a summary of the disaster or of that study; I want the episode read through the ethics of care. Who, on that framework, are the carers and who the cared-for, and does that dyadic vocabulary survive translation to a city and a municipal government? Taken as a sequence of moments — noticing a need, assuming responsibility for it, being competent to meet it, and registering how the one cared for responds — which moment failed first, and does the answer change if we locate the failure in individual neighbors, in the institutions, or in the political order that distributes who must care for whom? And what does the episode do to the framework in return? I have in mind, in particular, the care-theoretical claim that privilege consists partly in the ability to hand one's caring obligations off to others and to remain untouched by the costs of doing so — the city's better-off residents could retreat to air-conditioned rooms and cars, while the institutions that should have assumed responsibility for those who could not were conspicuously absent — but I want you to tell me whether that idea is doing genuine explanatory work here or is merely a dignified label for what any theory would already condemn.

So: run the analysis in both directions, as I have asked the seminar to do with other cases — say what care ethics illuminates that a justice- or rights-based account would miss, and, honestly, what the rights account handles better; then say what the case exposes about the limits of the ethics of care, especially its difficulty in distributing responsibility across institutions and in scaling a relational ethic to the political level. Take a definite position rather than surveying the options: tell me which framework gives the better account of this episode, where you come down on whether the care framework needs the political extension its defenders have proposed or whether that extension costs it the distinctive insight that made it worth having, and which features of the case you judge it simply cannot reach. Be candid about where your reading of the episode is an interpretation rather than a fact, and flag any place where you are reconstructing the particulars from a general impression rather than from anything you can stand behind.
