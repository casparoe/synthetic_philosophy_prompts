I am a retired behavioural ecologist — thirty-five years of field work on insect signalling and orientation, most of it in the tropics — and I have spent the first years of my retirement sitting in on a graduate seminar in philosophy of biology, which has been more humbling than the fieldwork ever was. I am now applying to a small interdisciplinary institute for a six-month fellowship to write up an idea that the seminar has half-convinced me is a real paper rather than the confusion of an amateur; the application asks for a research proposal of about a page, and I would like to use the same text as the prospectus my seminar requires, so it has to be legible both to philosophers and to biologists who have never read a page of metaphysics. I do not need a bibliography, a literature review, or citations of any kind — I know roughly who holds which line and will put the references in myself once the argument has a shape. What I need from you is the shape.

The subject is the notion — a phrase of the seminar's, which I have adopted — of a "free-floating rationale": the idea that natural selection can be said to respond to reasons without anything ever having done the reasoning. A trait is there because of what it did for the lineages that had it, so there is a sense in which it exists for a purpose and a sense in which it can be judged to have gone wrong, even though no ancestor deliberated and nothing was intended. My half-formed suspicion is that the word "rationale" is carrying two quite different loads in these discussions. On one reading it explains why a trait is present at all; on the other it supplies a standard that the trait can meet or fail to meet; and these two come apart precisely where I spent my career looking. An insect that pours its reproductive effort into an object that merely resembles a mate is perfectly explicable — the explanation of its behaviour has not changed in the slightest — yet it is hard to say what that behaviour is "for" any longer. A signal that was reliable when it evolved and is now routinely exploited by a parasite raises the same difficulty. I have heard the standard lines in the seminar — that function talk is only shorthand for a history of selection, that it is irreducibly normative and cannot be reduced, that it ought to be replaced by mechanism — and none of them seems to me to touch the distinction I am after, which concerns how far a reason can float free of a reasoner before it stops being a reason at all, and whether the answer turns on something like whether the organism represents anything to itself.

Please turn this into a proposal I could send. State the question so that it has a determinate answer rather than being a theme; say why the positions currently on offer leave it open; describe the method, and why it is better than the obvious alternative of simply collecting more cases; say what a good result would look like and what would count as the project failing; and be honest about whether six months of part-time work by someone with no philosophical training is enough, or whether the question should be narrowed. Give me exactly five numbered items, one or two sentences each, with no introduction and no closing paragraph: the first the question, the second why it is open, the third the approach, the fourth success versus failure, the fifth scope.
