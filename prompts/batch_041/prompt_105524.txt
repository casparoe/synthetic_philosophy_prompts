I coordinate a reading group on decision theory that meets alongside a summer workshop; the members are graduate students in philosophy and economics together with a couple of practising statisticians, all comfortable with expected-utility talk but none of them specialists in the foundations of rational choice. Next month I have to run the session on superrationality, and I would like you to plan the teaching rather than deliver the explanation itself. My own sense is that the whole thing stands or falls on the order in which the pieces are introduced, and I keep getting it wrong when I improvise, so what I want is the dependency structure laid out: what must already be in place before the central claim can even be stated, which of the audience's existing habits of thought have to be dismantled first (I suspect the reflex of reading "rational" as "maximises expected utility given the other player's fixed behaviour" is the main obstacle), and at which point in the sequence each piece has to land for the later pieces to feel forced rather than stipulated.

Build the session around a single carrying example, and be explicit about what that example must contain and what it must be purged of. My instinct is a one-shot Prisoner's Dilemma between two agents who cannot communicate, will never meet again, and cannot causally influence one another — but I want you to say whether that example can carry the weight or whether it needs to be something else, and in either case to explain why the iterated Prisoner's Dilemma, the reputation story, and the tit-for-tat anecdote have to be kept out of the carrier; in my experience, the moment repetition enters, the audience stops seeing the actual problem and starts seeing a long-run self-interest argument. Then I want the misconception map and a check. The predictable errors I would like you to anticipate include: that superrationality is just the Golden Rule or Kant's universalizability test in decision-theoretic dress; that it secretly requires the two players to be the same person, or to be in telepathic or causal contact; that it is a claim about what people in fact do rather than about what it would be rational to do; that it collapses into ordinary evidential reasoning about what one's own action is evidence of; and that it works only for exactly identical agents, so a partner who is merely almost like you breaks it. For each, tell me where in the sequence the misconception forms and precisely how to head it off there rather than at the end.

I also want to fold in two ideas that I think are absent from the popular treatments and that should do real work in the lesson: first, the framing on which two agents cooperate because each can inspect the other's source code and can prove what the other will do, so that "I cooperate" becomes derivable from the other's decision procedure rather than from any expectation about how things will turn out; second, the related and stranger thought that two agents who merely run the same deterministic decision procedure, with no inspection and no communication at all, might each be able to establish that the other's cooperation follows from their own — I want the audience to feel why this is not the same as assuming the other will do whatever you do, and to see exactly which step does the work. Please tell me which of these two to introduce first, and what to leave out entirely at this level and to say so openly, since I would rather cut the formalism than lose the thread. Finally, give me one question I can put to the room at the very end that would distinguish someone who has grasped why superrationality is a claim about reasoning rather than about outcomes from someone who has merely learned to say "cooperate."
