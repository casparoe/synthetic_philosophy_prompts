I'm writing a comment for a graduate seminar on the metaphysics of kinds, and I need to sort out a slogan a presenter leaned on heavily last week. The line was 'a white horse is not a horse', offered as something the School of Names had supposedly established, and it was doing real work in his argument. He wanted to show that in 'the white horse is a horse' the 'is' cannot be the 'is' of identity, and, more strongly, that 'horse' cannot be doing the same job inside 'white horse' as it does standing alone — so that kind terms come out context-sensitive rather than straightforwardly extensional. When I pressed him he gestured at the dialogue: one speaker says that 'horse' is what designates shape and 'white' is what designates color, and that what designates color is not what designates shape; there is also the business about seeking a horse, where a yellow or black horse will do, versus seeking a white horse, where neither will. He treats all of this as settling the matter.

What I want from you is a menu, not a verdict on his reading. Lay out the distinct precise claims 'a white horse is not a horse' could be making, ordered from the trivial to the radical, and for each say what it is a claim about — the terms themselves, the concepts or universals involved, the extensions, the things — and whether it is trivially true, substantive and defensible, or simply false. Include at least: the reading on which it says only that the phrase 'white horse' is not synonymous with the phrase 'horse'; the reading on which it says the two terms have different extensions, so that the white horses are not the horses; the reading on which the negative is doing something other than denying category membership, e.g. 'other than' or 'not the same as'; the reading on which 'horse' and 'white' name separable features — a shape and a color — so that the compound names something over and above either; and the reading on which the sentence is a claim about kinds or universals rather than about animals at all. Then tell me which of these readings, if any, would actually give my presenter what he wanted — that the 'is' is not identity, and that 'horse' shifts its contribution across contexts — and which of them leave him with something far weaker than he thinks.

He also has a habit of treating the dialogue as a single settled doctrine, which is part of why I want the distinctions rather than a summary. So please be explicit about which parts of what I have reported are uncontroversial and which are matters on which people who work on this text genuinely disagree — including whether the two speakers are even arguing about the same thing, and whether the text is best read as a proof, a reductio, or a puzzle about a word. Do not present any contested reading as though it were the consensus. Finally, flag anything in your answer that turns on a particular translation of the received text, since I gather it is partly dislocated and translators sometimes rearrange it.
