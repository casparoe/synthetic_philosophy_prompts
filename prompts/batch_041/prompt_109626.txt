Hey — I host a small podcast on the history of ideas, mostly mid-century science and philosophy, and next month's guest is a historian of science whose whole project is Turing's sideline in parapsychology. Her thesis, which I half agree with and want to make her earn, is that the stretch near the end of the 1950 paper where he starts going on about telepathy and the Duke card-guessing results isn't a throwaway or a bit of period flavour, and that it actually exposes what the imitation game is and isn't doing. She's got a line she falls back on whenever someone calls that section an embarrassment — something like "it's not a lapse, it's the load-bearing wall" — and I don't want her just repeating it ten times. Audience is mostly CS people and philosophers who know the Turing test the standard textbook way, so the questions should be concrete and a little technical, and I need a follow-up for each one keyed to the answer she's most likely to give.

1. Start on the mechanics. The verdict in the game is supposed to be evidence about the machine alone. If telepathy is real, does it wreck the test by letting the machine get help from outside, or by letting the interrogator get information the game was supposed to withhold, or both? Make her commit to which side the threat actually falls on. Follow-up if she says both: then doesn't it roughly cancel out — and if it cancels out, why did Turing think the setup needed rethinking at all?

2. Ask her to state exactly what Turing does about it. My impression is he sketches a version of the game where the telepathy route would be closed off, but I've also seen people say he just concedes the experiment would be inconclusive rather than proposing a fix. Whichever it is, is the fix real, or does it just relocate the problem — the interrogator's own ESP, the human confederate's, the machine being run by a person in the next room? Follow-up: if the fix needs the interrogator to be ignorant of something, doesn't the test now rest on a stipulation about ignorance rather than on behaviour?

3. On evidential standards. He wouldn't wave away the statistical case for telepathy, but he also wouldn't engage the objection that a machine would have to actually feel what it's talking about, treating that whole line as a non-starter. Are those the same standard of evidence applied twice, or two different standards? Follow-up if she says consistent: fine, then what would count as statistical evidence that a machine thinks, as opposed to statistical evidence that it mimics?

4. On continuity with his other escape hatches. He also floats that the brain might not be a discrete-state machine — that it might do something a digital computer can't. Is the telepathy concession the same kind of move, naming a capacity the machine lacks, or a different kind, naming a channel the test fails to control? Follow-up if she says same kind: then why was telepathy a problem for the test but the continuous-brain suggestion wasn't a problem for computability?

5. What the section actually reveals about the logic of the game. Is the imitation game a test or a definition? If an empirical discovery — telepathy turns out to be real — can make its verdict unreliable, then he's treating it as an experiment with confounds, i.e. as evidence rather than as a criterion. Follow-up: if it's a criterion, how can any empirical discovery undermine it, and if it's a test, what was the sample size and who was the control?

6. Did he ever suggest running the thing the other way round — testing whether a machine could be telepathic, or using a machine as the receiver in an ESP experiment? I've seen that claimed and I genuinely can't tell if it's in the text or read into it. Follow-up: if it is in the text, does that make the section a research proposal rather than a concession?

7. The biographical thing. There's a story that his interest in all this goes back to a death at school and that he sat in on séances in the thirties — is that actually documented or is it biographers being novelistic? And does it matter? Follow-up if she says it's documented and it matters: isn't that a genetic fallacy, and if she says it's documented and doesn't matter, then why does the section read so personally?

8. Does the theme show up again in the later pieces — the BBC talks, the machine-intelligence writing, the Ratio Club conversations — or is it one page and then gone? Follow-up if it's a one-off: then how is she not over-reading a single page, and what would it take to show it's a one-off?

9. The modern analogue I actually care about. A model with web access, or one trained on the test itself, is getting help from outside the game in a way that feels structurally like the telepathy worry. Is that the true descendant of the worry, or is it a different problem — contamination versus channel? Follow-up: if it is the descendant, was Turing the first person to worry about test contamination, and if it isn't, what exactly is different?

10. Last one, and I want this on the record: what would make her drop the thesis? What's the strongest case that this is just a fad he absorbed from the period, and what evidence would she accept against her reading? Then: if she's right, what should a textbook say about the imitation game that it currently definately gets wrong?
