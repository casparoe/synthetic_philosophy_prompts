I am a high school junior and the outgoing president of my school's Foundations Club, a group of about fifteen students who meet on Thursday afternoons to argue about mathematics and philosophy, and who hold a formal banquet in the library at the end of every year. This year's banquet doubles as a retirement dinner for Mr. Alvarez, who has taught calculus here for thirty-one years and who has run the club for most of that time, feeding it the strange ideas it argues about; a line he repeats whenever we press him about the foundations of set theory is that the trouble with sets is that you can never see all of them at once. I have been asked to give the closing speech, roughly seven minutes. About half the room will be mathematics teachers who know perfectly well what the downward Löwenheim–Skolem theorem says; the other half will be my parents, two English teachers, a guidance counselor, and Mr. Alvarez's family, none of whom have heard of it. I would like the speech to be built around Skolem's paradox, and to be built only on the account of it I give below, so that nobody has to stop and teach formal logic mid-banquet: any consistent theory written in first-order logic that has an infinite model has a countable model; so there is a model of the standard axioms of set theory whose elements can in principle be listed off one, two, three, and yet which satisfies the sentence "there exist uncountable sets." Inside that model, the set playing the role of the real numbers admits no bijection with the natural numbers, because no such bijection is among the model's own elements; from outside, the whole model is countable, so a bijection exists but is not one of the model's sets. Skolem himself drew a relativist conclusion from this, holding that notions like set and uncountable have no absolute meaning and make sense only relative to a model; a number of philosophers have replied that the paradox is an artifact, since a countable model's membership relation is a stand-in for the genuine one, and a model whose sets are not the real sets tells us nothing about what is or is not uncountable.

I want the speech to take a definite side in that dispute rather than laying out both options and leaving the room to choose: pick whichever reading you judge stronger, commit to it openly, and give the reasons in a form a guidance counselor can follow without the mathematics teachers feeling that the subject has been quietly changed. It matters to me that the structure of the paradox, not merely its subject matter, does the real work of the speech — the way something can look one way from inside a framework and another way from outside it — and that the analogy it carries, whatever you make it, lands on Mr. Alvarez and on the seniors leaving, without flattering him in any way he would have noticed and disliked. Please do not cite books or papers, name philosophers, or gesture at a reading list; the speech should stand on the paradox itself and on him. Finally, append a short note to me, clearly marked as not part of the speech, telling me which parts of the picture above are genuinely contested among philosophers and where you simplified or took a side for rhetorical convenience, so that I know what I am saying when I say it.
