I am writing a novel whose central figure is the editor of a small historical review, and I have given him a line I am no longer sure I can defend on his behalf. A manuscript has come in from a mathematician: a book-length argument that the events we assign to classical Rome, Greece and Egypt actually took place in the Middle Ages, and that the chronology of antiquity was largely the invention of seventeenth-century scholarship — a tradition, I gather, that goes back to a scholar who held most of the classical texts to have been forged by monks. At dinner afterwards my editor disposes of the whole business with a sentence I have heard in a hundred other mouths: 'Extraordinary claims require extraordinary evidence.' I want to know precisely what he has said, because the weight of the scene depends on whether that sentence settles anything or merely sounds as though it does.

So lay out for me the distinct claims the sentence could be making — a menu, not a ruling on which one is correct. I am after the difference between a platitude and a principle. Is the requirement about the quantity of evidence or about its kind? Is it a rule governing the burden of proof or the standard of proof, and are those the same thing? Does 'extraordinary' describe the claim in itself, or the claim's relation to everything else we currently accept — in which case the demand is really about the cost of revising a whole interlocking body of theory rather than one proposition? Has the slogan been quietly promoted from an evidential rule into a test of what counts as a science, and does that promotion hold? And is the standard symmetric: if the manuscript's claim is extraordinary, is the counter-claim — that several centuries of documents are forgeries, or that a whole civilisation left no trace — any less so?

Then give me your verdicts. For each reading, say whether it is trivial, defensible or false, and which of them would genuinely license my editor's dismissal. I should add that the author has an answer ready: our failure to find something, he says, is not proof that it was never there, and the demand for extraordinary evidence is simply how a discipline protects its consensus from disturbance. Tell me what precise principle that counter-move amounts to, and whether it is as empty as I suspect. He needs the strongest version of his own position; I need to know which version is the weak one, so that a less sympathetic character can be made to reach for it. No reading list, please — I want the arguments, not the literature.
