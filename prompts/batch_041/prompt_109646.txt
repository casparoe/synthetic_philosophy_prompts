I am inclined to think Sen's liberal paradox is an artifact of a modeling choice rather than a real clash between liberty and efficiency. The result assumes that a right is unconditional decisiveness over a pair of alternatives — the protected individual's ranking of that pair fixes the social ranking no matter what — and then lets everyone's preferences range over everyone else's protected sphere, which is precisely the setup that manufactures the cycle. Model a right instead as a waivable default (I am decisive over my pair unless I choose to defer, and the deferral is itself just another preference), and I suspect the impossibility dissolves, taking with it the claim that liberalism and the Pareto principle are genuinely in tension.

Tell me what would count against this. Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, and give me the ones a committed deflationist would have to concede rather than dismiss: which formal results about waivability, which cases, and which distinctions — say, between rights as constraints on outcomes and rights as features of the decision procedure, or between meddlesome and non-meddlesome preferences — ought to move me. Say what the deflationary reading predicts that its rivals do not, and whether it can be hedged until nothing could count against it; if it survives every case only by adding epicycles, say so, and say whether that insulation is a defect or simply the price of dissolving a paradox formally. Write your answer in American English.
