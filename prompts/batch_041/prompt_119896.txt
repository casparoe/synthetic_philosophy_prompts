I have been thinking about the surprise exam paradox on my own, and every way out of it I have come across seems to buy its escape with something I am not willing to pay. Before I settle on one, I want a full specification of what any solution to it has to deliver.

The setup, so that we are working with the same thing: a teacher tells her class that there will be an exam next week, on a day they cannot know in advance; the class reasons backward from Friday, on the grounds that if Thursday night arrives with no exam then Friday is the only day left and the exam would not be a surprise, then the same step rules out Thursday, and so on down to Monday, leaving no exam at all; the exam is then given on Wednesday and genuinely surprises them. Two variations I would like the requirements to cover, because each seems to stall the reasoning in a different place: one in which the teacher says the exam will be a surprise or else the class all gets top marks, after which the backward step no longer seems to get started at Friday; and one in which the class is split, half of them trusting the announcement and half dismissing it, where I want to know which half is surprised and whether the answer just tracks who believed her.

Set out the requirements one at a time, each with the case that forces it and why it is a requirement rather than a preference of one camp: the cases a solution has to get right, the distinctions it must not collapse, and the things it must not have to deny (a solution that declares surprise exams impossible seems dead on arrival). Say which requirements are non-negotiable and which are loyalties to a particular picture of knowledge, which single requirement rules out the most candidates, and whether some pair cannot be met together — if so, which pair, and which one you would give up. A brief note on how the usual families of answer fare against the list is welcome at the end; the list is the part I am after.
