I've been circling this one for years and I've never seen anyone actually do it properly. Background, so you know where I'm coming from: in the corner of the internet I hang out in, the neolithic transition is the ur-example of a Moloch trap -- nobody chose agriculture, it just outcompeted everything else, and we've been stuck with the consequences ever since. Two takes float around as the standard moves. One is Sahlins's original affluent society: foragers had few wants and abundant means, worked a famously short week, and were rich by their own lights and probably by ours too. The other is the Diamond/Harari line that agriculture was the worst deal in human history -- shorter, sicker, more crowded, more unequal, and we only took it because farming populations outbred foraging ones. I've read Sapiens, I've read the Scott stuff about grain, tax and legibility, I know about the skeletons. Skip the 101.

What I want is a real comparison of those two as theories, not as vibes. They get presented as rivals but I suspect they're answering different questions and that half the fight is verbal. Two things I'd like you to bring in, because I think they're where the actual action is and I've never seen them integrated:

First, distinguish want-side affluence from means-side affluence, and then tell me whether "better off" survives once wants are endogenous. Sahlins seems to be running both at once -- plenty of food, and cheap satisfactions. If you bolt on the hedonic treadmill / set-point stuff and the idea of adapted preferences, does his claim get stronger (they really were fine) or does it collapse into unfalsifiability (any equilibrium feels normal)? Which of the two readings does the empirical record actually support, and does the answer change if the thing that got lost wasn't calories or leisure but exit -- the ability to walk away from a bad band, which peasants couldn't do from a bad lord?

Second, "mistake" seems to need a subject, and here there isn't one. If the spread of farming was selection rather than choice, can it be a mistake at all, or only a bad outcome? And how much of the debate is the counterfactual doing the work -- compared to what, for whom, over what horizon? Watch for the selection effect too: the people who might have kept the good version of forager life are exactly the ones who got absorbed.

No citations, no reading list, no bibliography -- obviously you can't look anything up, so dont pretend to. Just argue. And dont tell me it's complicated and leave it there; I want you to pick a side, even if it's a narrow one.
