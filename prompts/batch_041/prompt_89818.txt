At dinner last week two friends of mine, Rosa and Dev, argued for an hour about whether either of them could have come from a different sperm and egg. Rosa was defending the view usually called origin essentialism. I couldn't referee it, so I'm handing the transcript to you.

Rosa: If the other sperm had reached the egg first, the child born would have had a different genome, a different face, a different temperament — a stranger, not me in a new body.

Dev: That's a list of differences, not yet an argument. I could have had a different face too; an accident at six would have seen to that, and I'd still be me. So you owe us a criterion for which differences are identity-destroying, and originating from a particular cell is one candidate on that list rather than the thing that settles it.

Rosa: Origin isn't a change in something already there; it's what makes the thing that can be changed. A scar modifies you. Your origin doesn't modify you — it is you.

Dev: Then take an embryo that splits into identical twins: two people, one fertilization. Either they share an origin, in which case origin doesn't individuate anyone, or one of them isn't a person. And notice that the sperm that made you can't be pointed to in advance; it is your sperm because it made you. So "you couldn't have come from a different sperm" shrinks to "you couldn't have come from a sperm that didn't make you."

Rosa: Not a tautology, once the cells exist. Freeze the zygote for ten years, or let the same sperm and egg meet in a dish rather than a fallopian tube, and it's still me. So it's the particular pair of cells doing the work, not the timing or the place.

Dev: Then pick one. If it's the numerically same two cells, your claim is true but empty, since nothing else could have come from those very cells. If it's the genome they carried, the claim isn't about the cells at all, and a clone with my exact genome still wouldn't be me, so the genome isn't enough. If it's the event of their fusing, you've already conceded that the time and place don't matter.

Rosa: The cells, identified by their history rather than by any property they carry. If that is hard to state without circularity, that's a limitation on how we state essences, not a defect in them.

Please rate each of the seven moves from clearly mistaken to clearly right, justifying each rating, say who is right about what, and identify the question the two of them keep circling without ever asking it.
