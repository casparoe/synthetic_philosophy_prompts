I am a sixth-form student putting together a handout for my school's philosophy society, and I would like you to write its centrepiece: a decision tree that takes a reader from a commitment they will almost certainly already hold to a named position about perceptual experience. The session is on perception, and most of the room will have met the argument from illusion before, so I want the tree to earn its conclusions rather than announce them.

The starting commitment is this: an experience in which something appears other than it is, or in which nothing is there at all, can be subjectively indistinguishable from a veridical perception. Please make the first branch the question of what a reader takes to follow from that indistinguishability, and do not let any later branch presuppose the answer.

Each node should be phrased as a question a reader can answer without already knowing which position it leads to, and each leaf should carry the name of a position plus one sentence on its most awkward commitment. I need the following leaves to appear, and I would be grateful if you would gloss each in a few words so that a reader who has never met the label can still see what is at stake: sense-datum theory; the view that experience is not a relation to an object but a way of sensing (sometimes called adverbialism); the representational or intentional view, on which experience has content rather than an object; and disjunctivism, on which veridical perception and hallucination are not experiences of a single kind. If there is a position that accepts the argument from illusion but denies that experience has a common kind, include it; if there is not, say so and explain why not.

Please build the tree from the logical space rather than from a list of names: first the questions and the combinations of answers they permit, then the positions located within that space, and then a short note on any combinations that no one occupies, diagnosing whether each is empty because the view is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored.

Let these cases carry the tree, and use each of them at least once: a straight stick that looks bent in water; a white wall that looks grey in dim light; a drug-induced hallucination indistinguishable from a genuine sighting; a hallucination of a cat that happens to be sitting exactly where the hallucinator is looking; and blindsight, in which a subject responds to a stimulus they cannot consciously see.

Two further constraints. First, keep separate the question of what experience is like in itself from the question of what the subject can know or be justified in believing; I have noticed that these are run together, and I want a branch that divides them, with the corresponding positions named on both sides. Second, flag any two leaves the tree cannot really separate rather than papering over the gap.

The handout has room for six or seven leaves and no more than six branch questions. Please write in British English. Finally, I would like you to be explicit about which parts of this are settled among people who work on perception and which are genuinely contested, rather than presenting a contested view as the consensus; where a distinction I have asked for is drawn differently by different writers, please say so plainly.
