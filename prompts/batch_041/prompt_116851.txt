I recently moved from a city where almost nothing was enforced to one where small infractions — jaywalking, a raised voice on the train, an unanswered greeting — draw immediate and real social sanction, and I have been unable to settle whether I have landed among people who take one another seriously or among people who have mistaken order for virtue. I do not want a verdict on either city; I want the specification. Write the desiderata that any adequate theory of how much rule-following a society may demand — call tightness the strength of a society's norms together with its intolerance of deviation, and looseness their opposite — must satisfy. Give me the cases the theory has to get right, and what each one forces on it: a community that enforces norms no outsider would defend and whose members would not choose to leave; a looseness that is affordable only because some other community absorbs its costs; a tightness that falls hardest on those who did not write the rules; a norm whose enforcement is invisible to those who never breach it. Mark the distinctions the theory may not collapse — between a norm's content and the intensity of its enforcement, between how tight a society is and how tight it ought to be, between coordination and mere conformity, between what a norm is worth to insiders and what it costs outsiders — and the findings it must accommodate, including that people's preferences about tightness are themselves shaped by the tightness they were raised under. Then separate the requirements you consider non-negotiable from those that are the prejudices of a particular camp, and say whether any pair of them is jointly unsatisfiable; that is what I most want, since I suspect the demand that a society govern itself and the demand that it protect its dissenters cannot both be met. If one requirement does most of the work of excluding candidates, name it.
