I edit the 'what if' slot at a history magazine — one long counterfactual piece a month, maybe a dozen pitches to get through, and I have to triage them myself in about an hour. My fact-checker can verify facts but she can't tell me whether a what-if is a coherent what-if, and the editor-in-chief signs off on whatever I greenlight. I want a screening procedure: the questions to ask in order, what counts as a good enough answer to each, where it stops with a flat no, and where the call is mine because it's taste and not logic.

I've got a rule of thumb I'd like you to either formalize or kill. Only what-ifs about events are well-formed — a battle going the other way, a decision, a death, an accident. What-ifs about trends ('what if industrialization had started in China') arent, because there's no single minimal change that would have got you there; and once you've made your one change, everything downstream has to come from ordinary causes, not from the author quietly rearranging the world a second and third time to reach the ending they like. Related: I want the procedure to keep apart a what-if that shows the outcome wasn't inevitable from one that only shows it was unlikely. I dont think those are the same thing and I keep seeing pitches slide between them.

Then run it on three pitches and show your work: (1) what if the Armada had landed in 1588, (2) what if Princip's shot had missed in Sarajevo, (3) what if the library at Alexandria had never burned. Number three is my favorite and I suspect you're going to tell me it's junk — be straight with me about that, and about which parts of the procedure are doing real work versus which are just me dressing up a hunch.
