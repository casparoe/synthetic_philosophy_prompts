My friends and I got into an arguement the other night about asylum, after someone shared a story about a woman who fled a violent ex-husband in Central America and got turned down, while a guy from the same country who was beaten up for his politics got in, even though honestly what the woman went through sounded worse. I've been chewing on this since. Here's what I think I understand about how these systems actually work, mostly from a friend who volunteers at a legal aid clinic, so forgive me if I've got some of it wrong: to count as a refugee you have to show a well-founded fear of persecution, and the persecution has to be tied to one of a short list of grounds — race, religion, nationality, political opinion, or belonging to a particular social group — and usually the government has to be either doing the persecuting or failing to stop it. If the harm is just generalized — a civil war, a gang that runs the whole neighborhood, a hurricane — that's usually not enough, and if the reason you're targeted doesn't fit one of those boxes, you're out too. There's also this extra test I don't fully get, where a social group has to be defined by something you can't change and that the wider society can actually see as a distinct group, which is why victims of domestic violence and people targeted by gangs keep having to argue that they qualify at all.

What I want to know is what view of persecution, and of who deserves protection, this whole setup is quietly assuming. Does the grounds list mean the system thinks persecution is only really persecution when it's aimed at you because of some group you belong to — that the wrong is fundamentally about discrimination, or about the political order, and not about the suffering itself? Or is the list just a practical filter for sorting real refugees from economic migrants, with some other theory doing the real work underneath? I notice the system cares a lot about whether the state is complicit versus merely absent, which makes me wonder whether the hidden idea is that the real problem is a broken relationship between citizen and state rather than the harm as such — in which case someone fleeing a total collapse of order isn't a victim of persecution, just unlucky. Lay out the commitments this practice actually presupposes, be honest about which ones are strictly required versus which are just things the system happens to do, and tell me where it's underdetermined — where two or three different theories would all fit the same rules.

Also, my friend keeps pushing this reform idea at their clinic: scrap the grounds list and replace it with a single test, roughly whether the person's own state failed to provide them basic protection from serious harm, plus maybe a special category for harms that come from how a whole society is structured rather than from anything the person did. If you swapped the current rules for that, what would have to change in the theory the system is running on — and is that genuinely a different theory, or the same one wearing a different hat?

Write it in American English, and please lead with your bottom line in the first sentence and then give the reasoning — no throat-clearing, and don't restate my question back to me.
