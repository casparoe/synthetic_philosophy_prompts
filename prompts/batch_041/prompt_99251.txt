I'm a postdoc in a cognitive science department that has just won a small internal grant to host one visiting fellow for a year on foundational rather than empirical work -- no imaging, no modeling, just conceptual and formal scholarship. The theme we've settled on is computationalism: the thesis that the brain is, in some nontrivial sense, a computer, and that cognition just is computation of the right kind. As we draft the call for applicants, my colleagues and I keep gravitating toward the same handful of set pieces -- Searle's Chinese room, multiple realizability, the question of whether neurons implement a Turing machine -- and I have a nagging sense that we're circling the most familiar landmarks rather than the ones that actually bear weight. I'd like you to help me decide where to aim the fellowship, because a year is a real constraint and I don't want it spent re-litigating what everyone already has opinions about.

Give me a defended agenda: which open questions or lines of work in computationalism matter most, in what order, and why. I care much less about the list than about the reasoning behind it. For each candidate, explain what makes it upstream of the others -- how many adjacent disputes would be settled, dissolved, or reframed by an answer -- how tractable it is for someone with a background in logic and the philosophy of mind but no lab access and no appetite for empirical heavy lifting, and whether it is neglected relative to how much it matters. Then tell me what would move a question up or down this ranking over the next couple of years, and be explicit about the criteria you're applying and the trade-offs among them. I'm particularly interested in whether the highest-value work lies in the metaphysics of computation itself (what it is for a physical system to implement a computation at all, and whether the standard triviality results about arbitrary physical systems realizing arbitrary computations can be answered rather than merely dodged), in the question of what kind of computation the brain is doing (classical, connectionist, dynamical, or something with no clean analogy), or in whether the computational framing earns its explanatory keep at all when it comes to consciousness and intentionality. If you conclude that the standard debates I named really are where the value is, say so and defend it -- don't manufacture novelty for its own sake. Assume the fellow can read technical material but is not a specialist in any single subfield, and that what you write will become a memo I circulate to the search committee.
