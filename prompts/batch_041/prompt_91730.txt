Colloquium next month — joint stats/philosophy seminar, about thirty people — and I need a Q&A prep sheet, not a defense of the paper.

Here's what I'm arguing. Bertrand's chord problem doesn't refute the principle of indifference; it refutes indifference applied to a bare partition of a parameter space. The three answers (random endpoints gives 1/3, random point on a radius gives 1/2, random midpoint gives 1/4) come from three different ways of producing the chord, and the problem never says which one is in play. My claim is that a symmetry licenses indifference only when it's a symmetry of the procedure that generates the value, not a symmetry of some description of the value. So 'pick a point uniformly in the unit square' licenses nothing until you say what's doing the picking, whereas a dart thrown at a board does license indifference over regions, because the throwing setup has the relevant translational and rotational symmetries. I use that to explain why invariance arguments are gold in statistical mechanics and (I think) junk in Bertrand, though I'm less sure of that half than I let on in the abstract. Known soft spot: on my view I can't license indifference over the proportion of red balls in an urn I'm told nothing else about, which is the one place everyone actually wants it.

Who's in the room: a hardline frequentist who thinks probability is a long-run frequency and will want to know how any of this is about probability at all; two objective Bayesians who use invariance and reference priors and will either say I'm doing Jaynes badly or that I've sabotaged the one tool they have; a guy who works on imprecise probability and thinks the real lesson of Bertrand is to stop claiming uniqueness and just carry a set of priors — he'll press me on what my view adds over his, and he'll bring up cases where learning something makes your interval wider, which he treats as a reductio and which I dont have any comeback for; a philosopher of physics who'll attack 'symmetry of the procedure' as a notion with no independent grip, since I get to call whatever symmetry I need a symmetry of the setup; and a statistician who'll ask about stopping rules and whether I've made probability depend on the experimenter's intentions in a way the likelihood principle forbids. Plus at least one grad student who's noticed that diameters get double-counted in the radial-point method and thinks that's the whole problem.

Give me the objections in the order I'll actually get them, phrased the way each of these people would phrase it, ordered by how much damage they do, each with a two-minute reply and a note on where the reply is soft. Tell me what to concede in the first five minutes so I'm not defending indefensible ground. And include the question I'm hoping nobody asks — I have a suspicion what it is and I'd rather see it written down.
