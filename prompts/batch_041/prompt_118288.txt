I'm in the second year of a philosophy of science MA and the causation seminar im in has basically collapsed into a two-horse race: counterfactual theories, where c causes e if e wouldn't have happened without c, versus interventionist theories, where a causal claim is a claim about what would change under some possible intervention on c. The annoying thing is that whenever the discussion needs an example, everyone reaches for John Snow and the Broad Street pump, says "and the intervention settled it," and moves on. I want to actually do the mapping properly instead of waving at it, and I dont want the textbook version — I want the version where the case bites back.

Here's the episode as I have it, and please correct me if I've garbled something, because I'll be repeating this to people: Soho, 1854, a cholera outbreak around Broad Street kills more than four hundred people, miasma theory is the orthodoxy, and Snow thinks the disease is waterborne. He interviews locals, persuades the parish to take the handle off the pump, and the outbreak ends — that's the story everyone tells. Two things complicate it. The handle came off in early September, more than a week into the outbreak, by which point the epidemic may already have been in rapid decline by itself, so the famous gesture may have changed nothing that the numbers can see. And Snow's real evidence wasn't the pump at all, it was a natural experiment: two south London water companies served intermingled streets, one of them had moved its intake upstream a few years earlier to a stretch of the Thames above London's sewage outfalls, the other was still drawing from a stretch below them, and the customers of the first died of cholera at far lower rates. Nobody intervened on anything; the companies were chasing cleaner water for their own reasons.

What I want is the case run through both theories at once, and at both levels. For the counterfactual account: what counterfactual does Snow need for the type claim, that water carries cholera, versus the token claim that this particular pump killed this particular person on this particular morning — and does the natural experiment buy him the first without the second? For the interventionist account: does pulling the handle count as an intervention in the technical sense (it has to be a cause of the water being drunk, and not a cause of cholera by any route that bypasses the water — I think it passes, but tell me), and does it matter that the intervention came too late to change the outcome, or is the theory only ever committed to what some possible intervention would do? Then the part I actually care about: what the water companies do to both views. It looks like a case where a causal claim got made good with no intervention anywhere in sight, which should be awkward for the strong interventionist and fine for the counterfactualist — but I suspect the interventionist has a reply about possible interventions and invariance, and I want to know whether that reply is a save or a concession that the theory was doing epistemology all along and metaphysics only by courtesy. And at the end, your honest read: does the episode favor one of them, or is the dispute really about what we want causal claims for — explanation on one side, control on the other — in which case Snow is a bad test case precisely because he wanted both.

Format, please: five sections, in this order, with these names and roughly these lengths — "The case as I've told it" (about 100 words, flagging anything I've got wrong or anything historians actually dispute, since I'll be repeating it); "Counterfactual reading" (about 300 words); "Interventionist reading" (about 300 words); "What the water companies do to both" (about 250 words); "Verdict" (about 250 words). And in each of them say how confident you are and name the one thing you're least sure of, rather than writing the whole thing in the same confident register. My friend who does epidemiology says philosophers of causation are just reinventing confounding, so feel free to tell me if she's right.
