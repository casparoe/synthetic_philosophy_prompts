I'm running a 100-minute session for a phil of mind reading group, about 14 people, a mix of grad students and a few advanced undergrads, one or two of whom have read a bit about panpsychism and the rest are comming in cold. What I want is for them to feel the combination problem rather than hear about it, and specifically to feel that it's not one problem but a little family of them: that a bunch of subjects, however intimatley arranged, don't seem to add up to one subject (the summing worry); that even if you did get a single subject out of them, nothing in the parts seems to fix what it would be like to be it, so the resulting quality looks arbitrary; and that the relational structure of the parts -- this quality next to that one, brighter than that one -- has to show up somewhere in the unified whole and there's no obvious place for it to go. No lecture up front; they should hit the walls by trying to build the thing. I'd also like the activity to leave them with a live sense of the escape routes, so that when we debrief, "it just emerges" and "start with one cosmic subject and get the many out of it" both feel like moves a person might actually make, with a price attached, rather than things I mention at the end.

Here's a rough shape I had in mind, though build it out properly or replace it if you've got something better. Everyone gets a card with a single micro-quality on it (something like a faint ache, the smell of rain on hot pavement, a low hum) plus a note that there's something it is like to be them, and a couple of relational tags (this quality is next to / brighter than / comes just before that one). Any two or more of them can fuse, but only by a ritual in which one of them becomes the resulting subject and the others are extinguished -- card torn up, out of the game. So the only two things on offer are a heap (lots of subjects sitting side by side, no subject that is all of them) and a replacement (one subject, everyone else gone). When a group does fuse, the quality of the new subject gets decided by majority vote among the qualities that went in, and the new subject can hold at most two qualities and no relations at all. One card in the deck is a cosmic subject that starts out holding everything, and if someone plays it the others have to try to get the many back out of it. Give me rules, materials (I can print cards, so tell me what goes on them), timings, what the facilitator is doing during each phase, and a debrief that maps each mechanic onto the bit of the problem it's meant to make vivid.

Then the practical stuff. What will predictably go wrong the first time -- my money's on people flatly refusing to tear up their cards, and on the one guy in every group who fuses everything by fiat and announces the problem is solved -- and what to do when it does. And be honest at the end about what the activity can't reproduce: I assume it can't show that the many-to-one step is impossible, only that we couldn't do it in 100 minutes, and I'll have to supply the actual arguments myself, so tell me exactly which pieces I have to say out loud and where the activity will mislead them if I dont. We meet in a room with a giant whiteboard and terrible chairs, if that's any use.
