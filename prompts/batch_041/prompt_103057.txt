I belong to a small reading group on philosophy of mind, and one member has been pressing an analogy that I cannot decide whether to accept: an ant colony is to its ants as a brain is to its neurons. He gives the imagined result a name — Aunt Hillary, which he says he borrowed from somewhere he can no longer remember — and he uses the comparison to argue for something strong: that a mature colony literally has beliefs, preferences, and something like a point of view, so that when a colony is poisoned or ploughed under, what is destroyed is not merely a great many insects but a subject whose interests count. His case runs in two steps. First, the ants are individually mindless: an ant is a small bundle of neurons running reflexes, it does not know where the food is and does not want the colony to grow, yet the colony as a whole behaves as though it knows and wants. Second, no single neuron in a human brain knows or wants anything either, so the "but each ant is stupid" objection proves nothing unless one is prepared to say that one has no beliefs. I find the parallel genuinely illuminating, and I also suspect that it is quietly doing work it cannot do.

What I would like from you is an audit of the analogy rather than a verdict on whether colonies have minds. Set out the correspondence explicitly — what is supposed to map onto what (ant, neuron, pheromone trail, synapse, queen, and whatever else he needs) and at what level the mind is supposed to live — and then say where the mapping breaks down and which of the breaks matter for the conclusion he wants. In particular, I want to know which features of the brain side he is importing without argument: centralized control, a private internal medium, a single body whose interests the mind serves, a locus in which the mental states are stored. Does the colony have anything playing those roles, or does it have structural features with no counterpart in the brain at all — for instance a memory and a signalling medium that lie outside any individual, laid down in the nest and in the pheromone field, so that every message is also a public alteration of the environment that any passing ant may read? He also slides without noticing between calling the colony one mind and calling it one organism, and I am not sure whether those are the same claim or whether they can both be true of the same object; I would like that sorted out as well.

Finally, tell me whether the moral conclusion survives once the disanalogies are on the table. If the colony is not a mind in the way a brain is, is it still the kind of thing whose destruction can be described as the death of a subject, or was the analogy carrying the whole conclusion from the start? And if the comparison can be repaired — either by swapping in a better source analogy or by keeping this one and removing the parts that will not bear weight — say what the repaired version is and what it would and would not license.
