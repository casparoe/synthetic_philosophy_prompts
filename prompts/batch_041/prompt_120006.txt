My friend keeps waving Good's theorem at me -- you know, the one that says the expected value of information is never negative for a Bayesian, because whatever you learn you can always just ignore it and do what you would have done anyway -- as if it settled whether it can ever be rational to prefer not knowing. I think it's basicly a free-disposal observation wearing a proof as a costume. Read carefully it says: if (i) you're an expected-utility maximizer, (ii) your utility function doesnt shift when you learn the thing, and (iii) you can costlessly act as though you never learned it, then getting the information can't hurt you in expectation. My claim is that this makes the theorem nearly useless for the practical question, because in the everyday cases where "should I actually find out?" feels like a live question, at least one of (i)-(iii) is usually false. I'm at about 0.7 on that; he's at about 0.3.

Give me your credence in the same claim, and decompose it:

- P(that the theorem really is just the free-disposal observation -- that it has no content which would surprise someone who had thought hard about expected utility for five minutes);
- P(that preference-shift cases, where learning the thing changes what you want rather than merely what you believe, are a common class rather than an exotic corner);
- P(that the "just ignore it" move fails in ordinary cases -- updating is involuntary, or you have preferences about your own beliefs (you'd rather believe your partner is faithful), or the information is public and other people act on it too, so you can't reproduce the old outcome by ignoring it);
- P(that non-expected-utility preference structures (ambiguity aversion, regret, and friends) give information genuinely negative expected value for otherwise-rational agents, often enough to matter);
- and the one cutting the other way: P(that my "deflationary" reading is itself confused -- that I'm sliding from "the proof is trivial" to "the conclusion is trivial," when the conclusion does rule out something people sincerely believe, namely that you can be better off refusing free information you could have acted on).

For each, a number and one line of why.
