I'm a graduate student working on the foundations of decision and game theory, and I've been trying to write down, as sharply as I can, the solution concept I actually believe in for finite games — one that gets by without any equilibrium assumption at all. Here is where I've landed, and I defend it more aggressively than I probably should. The canonical reduction of a finite game is the iterated elimination of strictly dominated strategies, where "dominated" is read to include domination by a mixed strategy; and I think this is the right notion, for three reasons. First, it asks nothing of the players except that they not choose an option that is worse than another option — or a lottery over options — no matter what anyone else does, so it never presupposes that anybody's conjectures about anybody else are correct, unlike Nash equilibrium and every refinement of it. Second, it is order-independent: eliminating dominated strategies in any order leaves the same survivor set, so the reduction looks like a property of the game rather than an artifact of the sequence in which I happened to apply it. Third, I take the deepest justification to be epistemic — the survivors are exactly the strategies that can be played when the players' rationality is common knowledge — and I treat that characterization as what makes the concept more than a piece of algebra. Two things about my own position unsettle me. The mixed-strategy clause is doing quiet work: it is what aligns this reduction with the never-a-best-response notion, and if I restrict domination to pure strategies the two come apart, so the clause is not cosmetic. And there is a tension I can't yet resolve — the first pillar advertises the concept as epistemically undemanding, while the third makes it rest on a common-knowledge condition that may be very demanding indeed, with the familiar suspicion that in games having a unique backward-induction outcome, common knowledge of rationality is not merely demanding but impossible, which would render the characterization vacuous exactly where it is most needed.

What I want from you is a map of this position's exposure. Take it seriously as a candidate for the right solution concept, and tell me what would count against it — ranked from the defeaters that should be close to fatal down to the ones that are merely awkward, and with the ones a defender of this position would themselves concede kept separate from the ones only a critic would press. I'd like to know what the position predicts that its rivals don't, and where it risks nothing at all.

Three things interest me most. (a) The mixed-strategy clause: is there any principled reason to permit domination by a mixture, or does doing so quietly import an assumption — that players can and will randomize in the specific way the mixture prescribes — that the rest of the position was designed to avoid? And is there a cost to the pure-domination reading beyond the bare fact that it yields a different reduction? The case I keep returning to is a one-player problem with two contingencies and three options whose payoffs are (1, 0), (0, 1), and (0.4, 0.4): the third is strictly worse than the fifty-fifty mixture of the first two against both contingencies, yet strictly better than either of them against some contingency, so no pure option dominates it. (b) Which of the three pillars is load-bearing, and which could be surrendered without loss? (c) Insulation: I suspect the position may be so permissive that almost nothing tells against it — it eliminates nothing at all in a game like matching pennies, and it selects no unique outcome when a game has several equilibria — so I want you to say plainly whether that is a virtue (it stakes few claims, so it risks little) or a vice (it predicts nothing, so nothing could refute it), and whether dominance-solvability being the exception rather than the rule is a problem for treating this as *the* solution concept rather than merely a lower bound on rational play. What would move me most is an argument that the mixed-strategy clause smuggles in exactly the kind of assumption the position exists to avoid; second to that, anything showing that the epistemic characterization is not a characterization of this reduction at all. Please don't simply run through the standard catalogue of equilibrium refinements — I want an assessment of what this particular bundle of commitments risks.
