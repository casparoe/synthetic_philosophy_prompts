I have a cluster of intuitions about the timing of experience — about when a subject genuinely has an experience, and about when the experience they seem to have had actually took place — and I would like to know whether a single principle can capture them, or whether some of the verdicts must be surrendered.

The background is the family of phenomena in which what a subject experiences is fixed in part by stimulation arriving after the moment the experience is felt to occupy. The clearest is the colour-phi phenomenon: two dots are flashed in quick succession at different positions, the first red, the second green. Subjects report a single dot that travels from the first position to the second and changes colour about halfway along its path. The colour they report at the midpoint is the second dot's green, even though the second dot had not yet been presented at the instant at which the change is reported as occurring. Philosophers describe two readings of such cases. On the Orwellian reading the subject's experience is veridical in real time, and only the memory of it is subsequently rewritten. On the Stalinesque reading the experience itself is assembled after the fact, so there is no earlier veridical experience for the memory to be false to. I mention the labels only to set them aside; I want a criterion, not a choice between those two stories.

Here are my verdicts.

(a) Colour phi. The subject genuinely undergoes an experience as of a moving dot that changes colour, and it is not a confabulation. But I do not think the experience occurs when it seems to; it is put together after the second dot appears, and is merely felt to have occurred earlier. Genuine experience, backdated felt time.

(b) Backward masking. A word is flashed briefly and immediately followed by a pattern mask. The subject reports seeing no word, only a flicker, yet in a two-alternative forced choice can identify the word above chance. I say the subject has no experience of the word at any time, and does not later forget one. Whatever the above-chance performance shows, it is not an experience.

(c) Post-hoc suggestion. A subject views a brief scene and is later asked a leading question that presupposes an object that was not present; the subject comes sincerely to report having seen it. I say no experience of the object ever occurred. The later report is a false memory, not the memory of an experience.

(d) Ordinary delayed report. A red light comes on and the subject says red a second later. I say the experience occurred when the light came on; the delay is in the reporting, not in the experience, and the felt timing is trustworthy here.

(e) Phoneme restoration. A phoneme in a spoken sentence is replaced by a cough; the subject reports hearing the sentence intact, cough and all, and reports the missing phoneme as having been spoken. I say the subject genuinely experiences the phoneme, and experiences it as occurring at its natural place in the sentence, even though it was never uttered.

The pairs that trouble me: (a) and (c) both involve later material shaping what the subject takes themselves to have undergone, yet I call one a real experience and the other a mere false memory. (a) and (b) both involve information that was physically presented, processed well enough to steer a report, and yet, on my verdicts, not experienced as such — unless I am wrong about (b). And (a) and (e) both give a real experience a felt time it did not literally occupy, while (d) does not.

I am tempted by a principle along these lines: an experience is real just when the information that fixes its content is used to construct a perceptual representation that the subject's report is, at the time of reporting, answerable to; and such an experience is backdated just when the construction draws on events later than the time the experience is felt to occupy. But this threatens to count the masked word in (b) as an experience, since the masked word likewise fixes the forced-choice report. Either the principle needs repair or one of my verdicts must go.

Please tell me what principle, or small set of candidate principles, best captures my verdicts, stating each clause precisely and naming the case that motivates it; what those principles imply for at least two further cases I have not listed, of your own construction; and which of my verdicts, if any, the best principle cannot accommodate, and why.
