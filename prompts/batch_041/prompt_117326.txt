I'm a philosopher of mathematics, and in three weeks' time I have the opening slot at the fortnightly colloquium that the mathematics department runs jointly with ours. I'll be defending a position I've been building for a couple of years — I've been calling it thin realism, though the name is my own coinage and I'm not wedded to it. Roughly: that mathematical platonism is better understood not as a thesis about a realm of objects but as a thesis about the norms of ontological commitment, so that when we say numbers exist we are declining to paraphrase rather than describing a domain; and that the real work in the realism debate is done by the theory of reference and by the epistemology of rule-following, not by ontology. I try to earn this in two moves. First, an argument that the indeterminacy afflicting reference to mathematical objects is not a defect to be patched but the symptom that shows object-talk is doing something other than describing. Second, an argument that the platonist's most persuasive piece of rhetoric — the set theorist's way of talking about the true universe of sets as against a mere model of the axioms — is marking authority within a practice, and doing sociological rather than ontological work. I'll speak for fifty minutes and then take questions for an hour.

The audience is the problem. It is mostly working mathematicians — algebraists, number theorists, a couple of analysts — who are unreflective platonists in the seminar room and embarrassed formalists in the pub, and who will not have read a word of the literature I'm engaging with. Sitting among them are two set theorists who are serious, articulate platonists about the cumulative hierarchy and who will hear my second move as an attack on their life's work; a constructivist logician who thinks the entire realism debate is a symptom of my not having taken intuitionistic logic seriously; and a visiting philosopher of mathematics of a Quinean temper who will run the indispensability argument and will press me on what my view says about the physics to which mathematics is supposedly indispensable. There is also an emeritus who asks whether the whole thing isn't a verbal dispute, every single time, and he will ask it again.

Draft me a Q&A prep sheet for that hour. I want the objections I will actually get from this room — not the ones I'd get from a room of metaphysicians — ordered by how much damage they would do me rather than by how likely they are, each phrased the way the person who will raise it would actually phrase it, with a reply short enough to deliver in about two minutes and an honest note on where that reply is thin. Tell me which two or three I should simply concede and walk past, which one I must not concede even at the cost of the room, and where my own position is weakest, so that I can prepare to defend it rather than hope nobody notices. Finish with the question I am dreading, and the single reply that has to land for the position to survive the hour. No references or reading lists, please — I know the literature, and what I need is the shape of the room.
