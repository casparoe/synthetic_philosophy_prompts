Here is the proposition I want your credence on. Take a group of 101 forecasters answering a binary question drawn from a realistic forecasting distribution — not a designed test bank. Suppose each forecaster's marginal accuracy is 0.6, and suppose their errors are positively correlated through shared sources, at a pairwise error correlation of roughly 0.3. The claim is that, for such a group, majority vote is reliably more accurate than the average individual forecaster, and that the Condorcet jury theorem — suitably relaxed — is still the correct explanation of why.

Give me your credence that this claim is true, and then give me the decomposition rather than the bare number. I expect the number to depend on at least the following sub-questions, though you should add, drop, or split terms as you see fit:

1. Independence. Is the independence assumption doing real work, or is it a mathematical convenience the theorem survives without — given that the errors here are explicitly correlated?

2. Competence. Does each forecaster really stay above 0.5 on questions drawn from a realistic distribution, or are the interesting questions precisely the ones where competence sits at or below 0.5 — that is, is competence endogenous to question difficulty and to the group's shared framing?

3. Binarity. Is the binary framing a harmless simplification, or do real forecasting questions take many values in a way that voids the theorem's guarantee?

4. The aggregation rule. Is majority vote actually what such a group does, or does it aggregate through discussion, deference, and reputation, which need not preserve the theorem's properties?

5. The target. Does "accuracy" here mean the frequency of being right, calibration, or something else, and does the conclusion survive swapping one for another?

For each, give a rough probability and one line of justification, then show how they combine into your final number. After that, tell me which term you are least confident in, which single term your number is most sensitive to, and what evidence or argument would move that term most — ideally something I could actually observe rather than a thought experiment.
