At our formal epistemology reading group this term, a visiting statistician and a colleague of mine spent most of a session arguing about the problem of old evidence and left it unresolved. I was present but not party to it, and I have been asked to referee. I would like an assessment that takes their exchange as it actually went and adjudicates it move by move.

The case they used: a laboratory measured some quantity ten years ago and the reading was written down; call the recorded result D. That result has been on the books, and taken for granted, ever since. Someone now proposes a hypothesis H that entails D. The question is what, if anything, D does for H.

First round. My colleague, the philosopher, said this is a real difficulty for Bayesian confirmation. Because D has been known for a decade, anyone's credence in D is 1. But if H entails D, then P(D|H) = P(D) = 1, so learning D cannot raise the probability of H, and by the ordinary probability-raising account D gives H no confirmation whatever — which is absurd, since the ten-year-old reading is precisely the sort of thing scientists cite in favor of H. So something has to give.

Second round. The statistician said the difficulty is an artifact of a bad idealization. No working scientist's credence in a measurement is exactly 1; suppose the laboratory's was 0.97. If H entails D and thereby raises that credence to 0.99, then D does raise the probability of H, and by the standard ratio it confirms H. Drop the fiction that old evidence is certain and the problem evaporates.

Third round. The philosopher said this only relocates the trouble. Why 0.97 rather than 0.9999? The more confident the laboratory was in D, the smaller the boost H receives from it; the less confident, the larger. So the evidential weight of D is fixed by a number meant to describe the laboratory's bookkeeping a decade ago, a number with no bearing on H. A quantity of confirmation that can be dialed up or down by adjusting an irrelevant prior is not measuring anything about the world.

Fourth round. The statistician said the quantity that matters is not P(D) but the likelihood ratio P(D|H) / P(D|¬H), the Bayes factor, and that this is settled by what H and its rivals say about D, not by the laboratory's confidence. Since H entails D the numerator is 1; everything turns on how likely D is under ¬H, which is a substantive scientific question. If ¬H makes D unlikely, then D is strong evidence for H no matter how long ago it was recorded.

Fifth round. Here they introduced a distinction I had not previously seen either of them use, and it is the part I most want assessed. The philosopher proposed that the certainty attaches only to the record and not to the world: what was certain all along was the proposition that the notebook contains a certain entry, whereas the proposition that the measured quantity really had that value was never certain and never needed to be. On this view the old-evidence problem is a confusion between the datum and the record of the datum, and it dissolves once the two are kept apart. The statistician replied that the record is itself old evidence — we have known for a decade what the notebook says — so the same difficulty simply recurs one level up; and that if the world-directed proposition is genuinely uncertain, then the notebook does not entail it, and we are back to asking how reliable the notebook is, which is just the prior we were trying to discard.

Please adjudicate. Rate each of the five moves on a scale from clearly mistaken through doubtful and defensible to clearly right, and justify each rating briefly; where a move is partly right, say exactly which part survives. I am especially interested in whether the fifth-round distinction helps, hurts, or is neutral, and in what both parties are missing that would change how the argument should go. I do not want citations or references; I want the arguments judged on their merits, with a closing statement of who has the better of it overall.
