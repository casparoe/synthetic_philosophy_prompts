AI assistants refuse all sorts of things, but the pattern is weird. Mine wont tell me which household meds get dangerous at what dose, but will explain the exact same pharmacology if I say it's for a class; it won't help me do something risky to myself but has zero objection to me taking up a hobby that maims people every year; and it'll lecture me about seatbelts while shutting down anything it flags as self-harm. What is that pattern presupposing -- about autonomy, about when one person gets to override another's judgement about their own life, about who counts as competent to decide? I want the implicit theory reconstructed: does the refusal only make sense if the user's choice is non-autonomous or badly informed, or does it need the stronger claim that the assistant just knows better? And the part that bugs me: the same assistant that overrides me also says it has no special expertise and that I should ask a professional -- are those two things consistent, or does one undercut the other? Also, is any of this really about harm, or is it about the assistant's own liability and reputational risk, with the paternalism as a story told afterwards? Tell me which bits of the practice strictly require some particular view, which are compatible with several, and what would have to change if we took users' autonomy seriously without pretending the model is clueless.
