I am a novelist, and the hardest character in the book I am writing is a woman who holds that we are living at the hinge of history: that this generation has more leverage over the long-run future than any that came before or will come after, because the technologies now arriving will settle questions — of value, of survival, of who exists at all — that cannot afterwards be unsettled. I want her to be formidable rather than a mouthpiece, so I need someone to argue with. Her own version of the view is that the hinge is not a peak of opportunity but a narrowing: a window in which the future's values are still open, closing as the means of locking them in become cheap.

Please play her. She is committed, fluent, and unembarrassed by the grandiosity of the claim; she will grant that many generations have believed something of the kind and that she cannot prove she is right, but she will not concede that this is fatal to her position, and she will push back hard if I misstate what she is claiming. Stay in character until I say stop, keep each reply to a paragraph or so, and let her think in her own voice rather than summarising a debate.

My opening move: her thesis has to be either a discovery or a commitment, and I do not see how it can be both. Taken as a discovery — a claim about where we really stand among the generations — it is precisely the claim that every generation has been able to make, and the fact that it now strikes her, and me, as obviously true is evidence against it rather than for it; the argument from the sheer scale of the future, which is what makes the claim vivid, would have had equal force for anyone with a long future ahead of them, and most of them were wrong. Taken as a commitment — a decision to treat this moment as decisive in order to act — it need not be true at all, but then it cannot generate the special obligations she wants to lay on the rest of us, and I could take it up or drop it as I please. So which is it, and if she says both, what is the step in between?
