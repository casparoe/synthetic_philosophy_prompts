I lecture on truthfulness and deception in professional life — the module sits between applied ethics and the philosophy of language — and something that happened to me a few weeks ago has forced me to rewrite the final paper.

I rang my home insurer to ask whether making a small claim would push my payments up. I was told, accurately, that making a claim would not put my premium up. What I was not told, and what the person on the line cannot have supposed I would think to ask, is that claiming forfeits my no-claims discount, so the money comes off me by another route. Nothing I was told was false. I have spent rather a lot of time since then wondering why I felt lied to, and whether the feeling survives scrutiny.

My old exam question now strikes me as the reason my students kept missing this. They could earn a good mark by reciting the familiar contrast — the liar says what they believe to be false, the misleader merely gets the hearer to infer it — and then applying it. That is a summary, not an argument, and it says nothing about why the contrast should matter morally.

Please set me a new question, with a model answer and a marking scheme. It is the final paper for the module: one essay question, forty-five minutes, twenty marks.

The question must turn on two scenarios, which I would like you to use as I give them, so that no candidate can have prepared an answer in advance. The first is the insurance call. The second is a press conference at which the spokesperson for a firm under investigation states that the firm has never broken the law; everyone present, the spokesperson included, knows this to be untrue, and knows that the denial is understood by all as a ritual formality rather than a claim. Nobody in the room is deceived. The discomfort I want the candidates to feel is that the first case contains no false statement and yet, I think, deceives, while the second contains a false statement and yet deceives no one.

The question should require candidates to say what, if anything, was done wrong in each case, to state a general criterion for when misleading someone is as serious as lying to them, and to test that criterion against a case in which misleading comes out worse than lying. It should not be answerable by naming authors or reciting the standard distinction; no part of it should reward a reading list, and candidates should be able to answer it from the concepts alone.

The model answer should be about seven hundred words, written as the strongest candidate in the room would write it. The marking scheme should list the moves a first-class answer must make, the marks each is worth out of twenty, and, for each, the mistake candidates characteristically make at that point — the kind of error that turns an A into a B. I would also like a sentence or two on why this is the crux of the topic, and what a candidate who has merely memorised it will find themselves unable to do. Please write everything in British English.
