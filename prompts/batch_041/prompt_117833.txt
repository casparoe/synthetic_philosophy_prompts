I've been reading s-risk-adjacent work and the surrounding rationalist discussion for the better part of a decade, and my impression is that the bottleneck is no longer motivation or even funding but conceptual organization: there is a growing pile of proposed concerns — AI systems that optimize for suffering, suffering that arises incidentally from processes indifferent to it, value lock-in, wild-animal suffering, futures organized around producing disvalue — and remarkably little agreement about which of the underlying questions are load-bearing. I'm advising a grantmaker who can fund roughly five researchers for three years on conceptual work in this area (axiology, decision theory, philosophy of mind, forecasting methodology; not empirical ML), and I need a defended agenda rather than a survey. I am not looking for a reading list.

Please prioritize the open conceptual questions in s-risk research and make your reasoning explicit. Treat the following as candidate framings; reject, merge, or reframe them as you see fit.

1. Mechanism: should the field be organized around the difference between suffering that is deliberately optimized for — targeted as an end, or as a convergent instrument — and suffering that is incidental to processes indifferent to it? Which is likelier, which is more tractable to reason about, and which is more neglected?

2. Episodes versus structure: is the right object of concern discrete suffering events, or the stable features of a future — institutions, reward structures, selection pressures — that make suffering self-reproducing? Can the structural formulation be stated without collapsing into a redescription of the episodic one?

3. Aggregation: how should a tiny probability of an enormous disvalue be weighed against a larger probability of a moderate one, and is the decision theory implicit in most s-risk work stable under its own assumptions, or does it collapse into paralysis or into fanaticism?

4. Measurement: is any comparative measurement of suffering possible across different kinds of minds and different value systems? If not, what follows for prioritization?

5. Interaction with x-risk: does reducing extinction risk raise s-risk, and vice versa? Should that trade-off reorganize how effort is divided between the two?

For each, say whether it is upstream or downstream of the others, how tractable it is for philosophers and decision theorists rather than ML researchers, roughly what share of the field's effort it deserves, and what concrete development in the next two or three years would move it up or down your ranking. Then give the actual ordering, name the two or three questions you would cut or defer even though people find them interesting, and tell me whether there is a question I have not listed that is upstream of all of these. If the ordering turns on contested assumptions — how bad the worst outcomes are, how much moral weight non-human minds carry, whether the relevant probability distributions are heavy-tailed — say explicitly which way it cuts.
