I'm assembling materials for an upper-level seminar that compares Western and East Asian accounts of the relation between person and community, and I want a decision tree rather than a reading list — something that sorts a student by what they are actually committed to, not by the label they would choose for themselves.

Begin the tree from a single starting commitment that nearly everyone in the debate accepts: that a person's roles and relationships do more than influence them — they partly determine who the person is. Every branch node after that must be phrased as a question a student could answer without already knowing which position it leads to. I want the nodes to keep four questions apart, because the literature tends to run them together: (1) whether the constitutive role of relationships is a claim about what a self is, a claim about what we must cite to explain and predict a person, or a claim about where value lies; (2) whether a relation-constituted self can still press claims — rights, grounds for exit or dissent — against the community that constitutes it; (3) whether groups are themselves agents with intentions and claims, or only individuals whose selves are group-shaped; and (4) whether the alleged East–West difference is a difference in deep metaphysics, in the relative weight assigned to values, in which features of a situation people notice, or an artifact of how the comparison is drawn.

Each leaf should carry the name of a position where a name exists, and be marked "unnamed" where none does, with one sentence on that position's most awkward commitment. I expect at least these to appear somewhere on the tree: atomistic individualism; methodological individualism; a view on which groups are genuine agents; a role- or relation-based ethics in which the self is constituted by its roles; a communitarian position pairing a social conception of the self with a value priority to community; a liberal position that grants the social conception of the self while retaining individual priority; and a deflationary view on which the cultural contrast is a matter of situation and salience rather than metaphysics. Draw at least these cases into the branch material: a grown child who wants to emigrate while a parent expects to be cared for; a person asked to shield a relative from the law; a village or firm making a decision that falls hard on one member; and a ritual duty — mourning, naming, an annual obligation — that costs the individual something real.

Flag any leaves the tree cannot separate from one another, and note any combinations of answers that no one seems to occupy, with a one-line diagnosis of whether the combination is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. Keep the whole instrument under 1000 words.
