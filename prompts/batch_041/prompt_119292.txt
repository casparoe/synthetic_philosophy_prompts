I spent most of a long dinner last week arguing with a close friend — a historian who is finishing a book on the early Cold War — about who started it, and we broke off without agreement, mainly because I could not work out what rule she was actually applying. Her verdicts, as nearly as I can reconstruct them: the imposition of communist governments across Eastern Europe between 1945 and 1948 was Moscow's doing, and she treats it as the original act from which everything else descends; the Marshall Plan, and later NATO, were Washington's doing, and although she cheerfully grants that the intentions behind them were defensive rather than conquering, she still says they began the institutional division of Europe, because they were the first moves that placed the continent's arrangements beyond the other side's reach; the Berlin Blockade she lays at Moscow's door, conceding that the Western currency reform was the proximate trigger but maintaining that a currency reform is an administrative act while a blockade is the first move designed to reverse an outcome by force; Suez in 1956 she blames on London, Paris, and Tel Aviv rather than on Nasser, even though Nasser nationalized the Canal first, on the ground that nationalization was an exercise of sovereignty over one's own territory whereas the invasion was the first act of coercion; the Guatemalan coup of 1954 she blames on Washington even though Arbenz's land reform came first, for the same reason; and on the Cuban Missile Crisis of 1962 she refuses to name a starter at all, insisting that once two powers are each making defensive moves that look offensive from the other side, the question simply has no answer — which I took to be an evasion and she took to be a finding.

The case we could not settle is the one she has to lecture on next month: the collapse of détente and the renewed confrontation of the late 1970s and early 1980s — the Soviet SS-20 deployments, NATO's dual-track decision, the neutron bomb affair, Angola and Ethiopia, Afghanistan, Poland. I say she has to blame Moscow, since the SS-20s came first and the rest is response. She says that if her earlier verdicts mean anything they mean the opposite, since the whole point of her Marshall Plan verdict was that a first mover's defensive intentions do not exempt it. We both suspect that the honest answer for that period is that the question is malformed, but neither of us can say whether that follows from her rules or is an escape from them.

She keeps insisting on a distinction between three things she says people conflate — the first move, the move that locked the outcome in, and the move whose aim was to overturn someone else's arrangements — and I cannot tell which of the three her verdicts actually track. So what I want is this: the candidate rules that fit all six of her verdicts, including at least two that fit them equally well; what each of those rules predicts about the late 1970s and early 1980s; your best guess at what she would say, and what she should say if she wants to stay consistent with herself; and the further case that would tell the rules apart. I would also like your judgement on the larger matter, which is what our argument was really about: whether a track record of this kind commits her to the view that who started it is the wrong question, or leaves her entitled to keep asking it — and if the question is malformed in some episodes and well formed in others, what marks the difference.
