I've been poking at the whole architecture we've built around retirement saving and habit-breaking — auto-enrollment that puts you in a plan unless you opt out, payroll deductions you never actually see, those schemes that bump your contribution rate every time you get a raise, early-withdrawal penalties, apps where you pledge money and a friend takes it if you don't hit the gym, casinos that let you sign a self-exclusion list you can't get off for a year. It's an enormous amount of machinery aimed at the fact that we apparently can't trust ourselves.

What I want to know is what picture of the will all of this is quietly assuming. My hunch is it needs there to be two of me — a present me and a future me — and it needs the future one to be the real one, or at least the one whose preferances get enforced. But is that actually the assumption? Does the practice require a genuine gap between what someone wants now and what they want for themselves overall, i.e. weakness of will in the plain sense? Or could the whole thing be running on something thinner — just that our preferences change over time, the economists' story where we discount the near future much more steeply than the far one, and the later preference isn't any more "us" than the earlier one? Those are very different views of the person and I honestly can't tell which one the practice is committed to, or whether it just hums along fine under either.

And there's a second thing nagging me. The penalties and the lock-ins only make sense if the early self can bind the later self and the binding is supposed to stick. But we don't let someone sell themselves into servitude or enforce a promise to work for one employer forever. So where's the line, and what does our willingness to enforce some self-bindings but not others tell us about the theory underneath?

So: reconstruct the implicit view of agency and will that this whole apparatus presupposes. Be clear about what it strictly needs versus what it merely fits nicely with, and where it's underdetermined so that several rival theories would license the same practice. Then tell me what would have to change about the practice if you swapped in the rival view — say, if you really took seriously that the person at time two is a different agent from the person at time one, or if you denied that weakness of will is even a coherent description of what's happening. And I'd like a verdict on whether the assumption that the long-run preference is the authoritative one is doing any real work, or whether it's just a convenient story we tell.
