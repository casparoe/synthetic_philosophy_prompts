I have translated fiction and narrative nonfiction for eleven years, and since this spring my publisher has been sending me a machine translation of each book as a first pass, with my part in the schedule re-described as post-editing. I can see that the engine is missing something and I keep failing to say what it is, so I have gone back to Douglas Hofstadter's long-running case against machine translation — that it turns out fluent sentences with no grasp of what they mean — to see whether his account is right and whether it is complete. As a test of it I rewatched Arrival, which stages the problem with unusual seriousness, and I would like a reading of the film through that argument rather than a general essay on linguistic relativity. I want to be able to say something precise to my editor about what I am still for.

Two things in the film should be treated as evidence rather than scenery. First, the message that nearly starts the war: the heptapods' statement comes through as an offer of a weapon, and the reading that triggers the standoff is shaped by the frame of the team that produces it, since they have been communicating through a competitive game. What is in dispute is not the vocabulary but the intent, and the correct reading, once Louise has it, is that the gift was the language itself. Second, the way she gets it: she understands only by learning the language to the point where it reorders her perception of time, so her competence arrives with a change in her rather than with a decoding procedure. Both episodes look like exhibits for Hofstadter, and I want to know whether the film is really on his side.

Argue it in both directions and then decide. Where the film supports him: translation fails not for want of vocabulary but for want of a shared form of life, and it fails in exactly the places a statistical system cannot help but fail. Where it turns against him: the understanding it rewards is a rewiring of the knower rather than the accumulated experience of a culture, which is a more mechanical picture of understanding than his; and the film needs a strong version of the claim that a language shapes its speakers' thought, which is contested at best, and which, if true, would make the heptapods' language untranslatable in principle rather than merely shallowly translatable by machines. There is also the uncomfortable possibility that the film cheats, handing Louise an understanding no translator has ever had.

Give me a definite verdict rather than a balanced survey: which side the film comes down on, where it cheats, and what it shows about the limits of statistical translation that Hofstadter's argument alone does not. If the honest verdict is that the film is incoherent on this point — that it needs both the strong relativity premise and Hofstadter's humanism and cannot have both — say so and defend it. Do not hedge by saying that it depends on how the film is read. Maximum 900 words.
