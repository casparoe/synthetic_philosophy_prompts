I retired a few years back after 35 years in physical chemistry and, like alot of scientists I guess, spent most of my career assuming philosophy was mostly hot air. Then I actually started reading it, and now I'm a bit obsessed — especially with consciousness. The view that keeps pulling me in is the illusionist one (Frankish, Dennett): that phenomenal consciousness as such — qualia, the 'what it's like' — doesn't really exist, and that introspection systematically fools us into thinking it does.

My problem is I can't tell whether I actually believe this or just like how hard-nosed it sounds, and I can't tell whether it's even the kind of thing that could be wrong. So here's what I want: what would count against illusionism? I'm not after a reading list or a survey — I cant easily get to a library and I don't want citations. I want the actual defeaters — arguments, intuitions, findings — ranked by how badly they'd hurt, with a note on which ones illusionists would themselves admit count against them versus which they'd just brush off.

Three things nag at me in particular:

1. I spent my life on things that aren't fundamental but are perfectly real — temperature, phase transitions, the whole zoo of collective phenomena. Nothing in my old field was fundamental except a few fields, and yet I never once doubted a phase transition was really happening. So I keep suspecting illusionism slides between 'phenomenal properties aren't fundamental' and 'phenomenal properties aren't real,' and those are very different claims. Which one is it actually making? If it's the deflationary one, what's really at stake? If it's the other, where does it draw the line and why?

2. In spectroscopy there are 'forbidden' transitions — lines the selection rules say should have zero intensity but which show up weakly anyway through higher-order processes. I half-wonder whether the felt quality of experience is like that: something a simple theory predicts away but that keeps leaking through. Is that a useful analogy or just me being a chemist? And relatedly — is there any conceivable result that would make an illusionist change their mind, or is the view built so that nothing could?

3. The self-defeat objection — that you can't deny phenomenal consciousness without relying on it. I've seen it waved off as a cheap trick but never actually answered to my satisfaction. Real defeater, or confusion? And what would a genuinely good reply look like?

And if you think the whole framing is off — that 'what would count against it' is the wrong question to ask about a position like this — say so and tell me why.
