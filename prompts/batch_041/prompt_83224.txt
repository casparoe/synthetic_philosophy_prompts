I'm preparing a handout for a graduate seminar on rational choice, and I want the session organised around one decision tree rather than a survey of the literature. The topic is the backward induction paradox and the centipede game. What I need is a flowchart that starts from commitments a student might already hold and lands them on a named position about what the paradox actually shows.

Build the tree from the logical space first, then locate positions within it. Every branch node should be a question a student can answer without knowing which position the answer leads to, so nothing may be phrased as 'are you a subgame perfectionist?' or the like. I'd like the nodes to turn on at least: whether the argument's base case is sound; whether common knowledge of rationality is a coherent assumption to make about the players in question; whether 'rational' is being used to constrain beliefs or only choices; whether the payoffs in the tree are final or stand in for something else; and whether the solution concept is intended as a prediction of behaviour or as a standard of choice-worthiness. Then use the following variants as probes, and make sure the tree can route a student through each: (a) an ordinary finite centipede; (b) one whose final two nodes pay the last mover identically, so the induction has no base case; (c) one in which the player at each node is an earlier or later counterpart of a single person, so the game is diachronic; (d) one in which a costly binding commitment is available at the first node; (e) one in which each node is occupied by a fresh copy of the relevant player.

Each leaf should carry a name for the position. Descriptive coinages are fine, and I'd rather you didn't attribute positions to named authors: I'm not asking for citations, and I don't want to risk misattribution. Give each leaf one sentence on its most awkward commitment, flag any leaves your tree cannot separate, and where a combination of answers goes unoccupied, say briefly whether that is because the view is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. Six to nine leaves is about the right size.

If the request rests on a false assumption — if these are not positions on a single question, or if my variants collapse into one another or are ill-formed — say so plainly rather than producing a tree anyway. Where you are improvising rather than drawing on a settled debate, mark it as such. Please write the answer in British English.
