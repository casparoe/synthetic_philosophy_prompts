I'm in my final year of secondary school and I run the weekly sessions of our school's philosophy club. We have just finished a unit on the difference between knowing how to do something and knowing that something is the case — between an ability and a fact that could be written down — and I have been given the job of writing the end-of-unit paper for the fifteen members who took the unit. My problem is that every question I draft can be answered by restating the distinction, so I have built the paper around two cases that I hope the distinction cannot digest, and I would like you to write the paper for me.

The first case: a fourteen-year-old skateboarder can land a particular trick nine times out of ten. Asked to teach it to a friend, she gives instructions that are confidently and completely wrong — slow-motion footage shows her doing the opposite of what she describes — and she is genuinely surprised when she sees the footage. Yet she can tell at a glance when her friend's attempt is off, even though she still cannot say what is missing, and left to herself she fixes it by physically guiding her friend's foot through the motion.

The second case: a student has learned to solve a cube from a written method. He can recite the method perfectly and can spot which step another solver got wrong, but he has never solved a cube unaided; with the written method in front of him he can work through a solve, and the moment the paper is taken away he stalls, even though he has done it twenty times with the paper there.

Please write (i) the essay question, which should be answerable in about an hour and should not be answerable by reciting the distinction; (ii) a model answer of roughly 700 words; and (iii) a marking scheme built around five things a top answer must do, four marks each, twenty in total, with one line for each on the mistake that most often costs students those marks — real mistakes, not "unclear writing." The difficulty should come from the crux rather than from obscurity: the question should not depend on knowing who wrote what.

Begin your reply with the essay question itself, with no preamble or commentary before it. In the model answer, put its bottom line in the first sentence and then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question.
