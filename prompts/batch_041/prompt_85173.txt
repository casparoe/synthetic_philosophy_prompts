Write a dialogue of roughly 900 to 1400 words between two philosophers who are both illusionists about phenomenal consciousness. Both accept that experiences do not possess the intrinsic, subjectively felt character we ordinarily attribute to them, and that our confidence to the contrary is a systematic error rather than a report of an inner fact. Treat both interlocutors as fully competent: each should state the strongest version of their position, anticipate the other's objections, and concede a point where honesty requires it. Neither may cite a published author or work by name; they must argue from first principles and from the case described below.

They agree on the negative claim and disagree about where the illusion is generated.

The first holds the late-generation view. On this view, nothing is misrepresented in a creature's ordinary perceptual and affective processing; the illusion arises only when a creature represents its own representations as possessing a phenomenal character, that is, when it introspects, reports, or otherwise takes up an attitude toward its own states. The illusion is therefore a second-order, meta-representational error, and its existence depends on the creature's capacity for self-directed representation.

The second holds the early-generation view. On this view, the illusion is already in place at the first-order level: perceptual and affective states are presented to the creature as being 'for it' in a way that no amount of first-order processing can be correct about, and introspection merely renders explicit what was already there. The illusion is a feature of how the world is presented, not an error about one's own representations.

They have agreed to test their disagreement against a single case: the shallow organism, a hypothetical creature that is behaviorally and computationally sophisticated, discriminating finely, learning, planning, and negotiating a complex social world, but for which the best available evidence indicates that it never directs attention onto its own mental states and possesses neither a concept nor a word for experience. Assume the empirical description of the case is fixed and not itself in dispute; the question is what follows from it.

The dialogue should proceed through genuine exchange, with each party responding to the other's actual argument rather than restating their own position. In particular, let the early-generation proponent press the charge that the late view drains illusionism of its content: if the illusion exists only once a creature misrepresents its own representations, then what precisely is misrepresented, and in what sense is anything illusory rather than merely a confused way of talking? The late-generation proponent should answer this charge directly, and should in turn press the early proponent on whether 'presented as for me' can be specified without covertly reintroducing the very phenomenal character both of them deny. Let them also disagree explicitly about whether their dispute is empirical, settleable by evidence about the shallow organism, or conceptual and hence not settleable by any such evidence, and about what would count as evidence either way.

Do not resolve the dialogue into agreement or into a verdict from the narrator. It should end with the disagreement sharpened rather than dissolved: either with both positions still standing and their respective costs made explicit, or with a specific concession that narrows the dispute without ending it.
