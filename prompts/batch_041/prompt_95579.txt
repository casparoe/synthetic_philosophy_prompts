I am preparing a graduate seminar on Kantian ethics, and I have grown tired of the stock examples—false promises, suicide, lying to a murderer—that students can recite before they have understood what the universalizability tests are doing. A colleague insists that the Formula of Humanity is doing all the real work and that the Formula of Universal Law is little more than a heuristic; I am not so sure, and I want a case that tests the issue. Please construct a thought experiment in which the Formula of Universal Law and the Formula of Humanity come apart, so that one permits an action and the other forbids it, or at least gives a different verdict, while the action is neither obviously villainous nor obviously saintly. The case should be realistic enough to occur in an ordinary workplace, hospital, or academic department; it should fit in one paragraph; and it must not turn on a false promise, a lie, a killing, or any other example that has become a placeholder in the literature. No trolleys, no ticking bombs, no science fiction, and no appeals to exotic technology. The maxim at issue should be one a thoughtful person might actually adopt, not a cartoon maxim like I will steal whenever it profits me. I am especially interested in a maxim that contains a qualification or exception clause—something like I will do X only when Y, and only for people who have done Z—so that the universalizability verdict depends on how narrowly or broadly the maxim is described. Please make that description-dependence explicit rather than hiding it, and keep everything else fixed: same agent, same institution, same consequences.

After presenting the scenario, do the following. State the maxim in the form I will ... in order to ... and give its universalized counterpart. Then work through the Formula of Universal Law twice: once checking for a contradiction in conception and once for a contradiction in will. Then apply the Formula of Humanity and say whether it agrees or disagrees. If the two formulations diverge, identify exactly which feature of the case—the exception clause, the end, the means, the description of the universalized world, or something else—is doing the work, and say what happens if that feature is removed. Finally, tell me how a sophisticated Kantian would try to block the case: would they say the maxim has been misdescribed, that the contradiction is merely pragmatic, that the humanity formula has been misapplied, or something else? The case should generate the divergence from the formulas rather than stipulate it. I do not need citations, a bibliography, or a reading list; I need the case and the analysis.
