The case, roughly: a lab here published Paper A in 2019 on a dataset they'd been building for years — call it D, about 12k coded records. Then over four years they "cleaned and expanded" it. They dropped a couple thousand rows they decided were bad, fixed a coding bug that had flipped one variable, re-coded a big chunk of the survivors under new guidelines, and collected ~6k new records with a slightly changed instrument (one survey item reworded, coders trained differently). Same DOI the whole time, updates just pushed live; someone who downloaded D in 2020 and someone who downloads it today get pretty different objects under one landing page, one name, one citation. This year they published Paper B, which calls it "the same dataset, now larger," and the abstract says the new study "replicates" the 2019 result. A grad student in the lab has complained that this is laundering a failed replication — run Paper A's analysis on the current D and you don't get the old result, and you can't even check, because the old version is no longer downloadable.

So the committee has to decide something, and I honestly don't know what the question is. Some of us think "same dataset" is a fact you can look up (row overlap? provenance? the statistics?). Others think there's no fact of the matter and we should just stipulate whatever's most useful for science and then enforce it. One member keeps saying a dataset is a historical individual, so identity = causal continuity of the file lineage, the way you stay you through cell turnover. Another says no, a dataset is more like an instrument or a kind, so what matters is whether it still measures the same thing — and if the reworded item and the new coding change what's measured, it's a different dataset however much lineage it has. Then there's the thing nobody wants to answer: even after we pick a criterion, how much turnover is too much? 10%? 50%? All of it? It feels arbitrary, and I can't tell whether that means the criterion is bad or just that these things are definately vague.

What I want: the written opinion — the thing we can actually vote on.
