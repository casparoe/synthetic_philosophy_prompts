For two decades the Anthropocene has been the site of a dispute in which the two camps talk past each other. One treats it as a problem in stratigraphy: either it is a formal unit of the geological time scale, defined by a single synchronous physical marker in a reference section, or it is not a geological period at all — so the recent rejection of the mid-twentieth-century proposal, plutonium spike and all, settles the date but not the concept. The other treats it as a period in the historian's or Earth-system scientist's sense: an interval defined by a change in how the planet works, whose onset should track the cause — the spread of agriculture, the Columbian exchange, the Industrial Revolution, the Great Acceleration — rather than any one deposit.

Draft the joint statement these two camps could sign after a genuine adversarial collaboration. I want the claims both can accept, including about what the evidence does and does not establish regarding when human activity became a dominant Earth-system force; the residual disagreements, each stated in wording its own side would accept; the cruxes — among them the authority of the stratigraphic code over the term, the choice between a boundary fixed by a physical signal and one fixed by a cause, whether the Anthropocene is better modelled as an epoch or as a diffuse, ongoing geological event, and who is entitled to decide — and, for each crux, the consideration both sides agree bears on it, with each side's prediction recorded in advance. Where the dispute turns out to be verbal — about the word "period" rather than the world — say which part and why, and close by marking the points at which agreement is impossible in principle rather than merely unmet. No bibliography is needed; argue from the considerations themselves. Format it as a numbered agreed-claims section followed by a disagreements table.
