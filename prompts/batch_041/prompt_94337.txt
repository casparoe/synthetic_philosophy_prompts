I cover biology for a science desk and for the last few months I've been sitting in on the taxonomy board at a natural history museum — the group that signs off on whether some population gets written up as its own species for the collection and for a couple of journals. I'm trying to get ahead of next month's docket and I keep guessing wrong about these people, so I want your read.

Here's how they've been calling things lately. Two frog populations that meet in a narrow hybrid zone, stay obviously distinct, and breed in different ponds — split, two species. Two fish populations that look pretty different but share the same waters and melt into one big hybrid swarm wherever they overlap — kept as a single species. A parthenogenetic snail line that doesn't breed with anything else at all — recognized as a species. Two mouse populations on opposite sides of a mountain range that never meet, are near-identical geneticly, but live in completely different habitats — split, two species.

Now the case in front of them. A little crustacean: two populations that share ponds and never interbreed, not even a little, but they're so similar you need a sequencer to tell them apart, and they make their living in exactly the same way in exactly the same water.

Are they going to split it or keep it whole? I care less about the answer than about the rule they seem to be running, the other rules that would fit all four of their earlier calls just as well, which of those the crustacean actually pulls apart, and which rule you'd put money on. And tell me what would change your mind — what bit of evidence or argument would flip your bet — because I've been burned by this board before and I'd rather know where you're shaky.
