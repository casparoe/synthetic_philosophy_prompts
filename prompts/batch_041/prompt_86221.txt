I'm a paramedic by trade, which is how I ended up in a fortnightly reading group that a friend runs out of her kitchen — two engineers, a retired judge, a lapsed theologian, me. Last month we did Moore's hands thing and I had a small crisis: I've been deploying "ah but you can't prove the room you're in even exists" as a joke against my brother-in-law for years, and I realised I could not say what Moore's argument is supposed to have actually done. In the paper he lays out what he takes to be the requirements for a proof — the premises have to be different from the conclusion, they have to be known, and the conclusion has to follow — and then he just admits he can't prove the premises and says that's alright because he knows them anyway. Meanwhile the whole reception is a pile of charges that sound the same and aren't: that the argument begs the question, that it can't transmit warrant from premise to conclusion, that it's valid but can't move anybody who doubts it, that the conclusion isn't even the proposition the idealist or the Kantian was asking about, that saying "I know I have a hand" in that setting has been yanked out of the language-game where it does any work, that the sceptic's premise is simply less certain than the thing it's meant to undercut. I keep smooshing all of those into one complaint and I want them prised apart.

So: could you build me a decision tree for the group. A flowchart, one page, that starts from something as unglamorous as "yes I'm pretty sure I can tell my left hand from my right" and funnels people to a named position on what Moore's proof achieves. Rules for it: every branch node has to be a question a non-philosopher can answer without already knowing which leaf it leads to, so phrase them as cases or commitments rather than labels — nothing like "are you a dogmatist?". I want the very first split to be over whether the two-hands conclusion is even the proposition the sceptic denies, because that's what we shouted about for an hour; then a node on whether the premise about your hands can be known while the vat scenario is still, for you, an open question; then a node on whether a proof has to be able to talk a genuine doubter round; then a node on what you give up when the claim to know about the hands and the admission that you can't rule out the vat can't both stand. Eight to ten leaves at the most. Each leaf needs the position's name (or "unnamed" if nobody defends it), one sentence on what it says Moore's proof achieves, and one sentence on its most awkward commitment. After the tree, list the answer-combinations the tree permits that no actual position occupies, and for each say whether that's because it's incoherent, unattractive, or just unexplored — and flag any two leaves your questions as written can't seperate, with the extra question that would do it. Don't cite anything, no author-date stuff, the group will definately switch off if it looks like a bibliography; and don't assume I know the standard names, so give each leaf a half-line gloss.
