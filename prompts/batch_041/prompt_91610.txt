I've come to hold the all-affected principle in a strong form: the demos for a decision is the set of people whose basic interests are at stake in it, not the set picked out by jurisdiction or nationality, and the entitlement is scalar -- those more deeply affected get more weight, those touched only marginally get less -- so that resident non-citizens count fully and distant outsiders with a vital stake count for something, with the boundary of the demos floating from issue to issue. What would count against this? Give me the defeaters, ranked by how decisive they actually are, and separate the ones a defender must simply concede -- the unboundedness of "affected," the lack of any non-arbitrary metric for "stake," the sacrifice of one-person-one-vote, the question of who assigns the weights and by what authority -- from the ones that miss the position as I've stated it. Tell me what my view predicts that its main rival, the subjection-to-coercion or jurisdictional view, does not, and what it would look like for that rival to turn out right. And be candid about whether the hedges (only basic interests, only significant effects, a scaling rule) have turned the principle into something nothing could refute; if so, say whether that is a defect in a view of this kind or simply the price of its being a principle of legitimacy rather than an empirical hypothesis.
