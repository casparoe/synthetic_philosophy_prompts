I write a weekly column on science and ideas, and I've been circling a piece about vagueness ever since a labelling fight here got me interested — is a pea-protein patty "meat," is a guy with 40,000 hairs "bald," that kind of thing. I had a philosopher on my podcast last month to walk me through the whole area, and he gave me the two-minute version of the epistemicism vs. supervaluationism split. I keep replaying the recording, partly because it was so crisp and partly because something in it smells off and I can't put my finger on what. Here's more or less what he said:

"Right, so there are two main camps. The supervaluationists think a vague word like 'bald' or 'meat' doesn't have one exact meaning — instead there's a whole range of equally legitimate ways of drawing the line, and a sentence is true when it comes out true on every one of those drawings and false when it comes out false on every one. When it splits — true on some drawings, false on others — it's both true and false. That's the sense in which vagueness is real indeterminacy in the world, not just in our heads. The price is you lose excluded middle: 'either he's bald or he isn't' stops being true, which is why classical logicians hate it. Then you've got the epistemicists, Williamson and that crowd, who say there's actually one perfectly sharp cutoff, we just don't know where it is — and the fun part is that with a good enough instrument we could in principle find it. But here's what I tell my students: these two views are the same view. Take all the supervaluationist's acceptable line-drawings, and epistemicism just adds that one of them is the real one. So the debate is basically verbal, two sets of bookkeeping for one fact. Which is also why I don't lose sleep over it — the higher-order vagueness objection killed supervaluationism years ago anyway."

I'm definately not trying to grade him — he's the expert, I'm the guy with a microphone — but I want to know what I can safely put in print and where I'd be embarassing myself by just repeating him. So go sentence by sentence: what would someone who actually works on vagueness nod along to, what would make them wince, and what's just flat wrong? I'm especially shaky on the logicy bits (I took one semester of symbolic logic in college and mostly remember truth tables), and I can't tell whether his "it's all just verbal" punchline actually follows from the sameness he claims, or whether it's the kind of line that sounds deep at a dinner party and falls apart under inspection. And if his pea-protein example is doing any real work in the argument, tell me whether it's a well-chosen example for the point he's making or whether a different one would serve him better.
