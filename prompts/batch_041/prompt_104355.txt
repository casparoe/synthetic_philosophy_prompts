For the past four months my sister has been the only one of the three of us living near our mother, who has what her neurologist calls mild cognitive impairment. On good days she follows a conversation and remembers all four grandchildren's names; on bad days she asks when my father is coming home, though he died six years ago. Last month my sister had our mother sign a document moving the house into a trust that my sister administers, with my sister alone deciding when and how the house is sold, and with a clause that cannot be undone once the signature is dry. Our mother signed it at the kitchen table in front of me, and I signed underneath as a witness. I want to be fair about what happened: nothing was hidden from her. My sister read the whole document aloud, twice, and answered every question our mother put to her. But the questions our mother asked were the ones my sister had spent several weeks teaching her to ask, in the same order each time, with the same reassurances; and when our mother finally asked what would happen if she said no, my sister said — truthfully, I believe — that she could not keep driving over every day and did not know what they would do then. Our mother said she did not want to be a burden. I signed because I was relieved, and I am no longer confident that my relief was entirely about my mother's welfare; I have not been the one doing the driving.

Since then I have not been able to settle whether what happened was my mother's decision or my sister's decision wearing my mother's signature. One friend, who works in employment law, says the only question is whether my sister threatened her or merely made her an offer, and that a bad offer is still an offer; a second friend, who reads a great deal of moral philosophy and coins her own vocabulary, says the interesting question is not whether our mother understood the document but whether she could have reached a different answer from inside the picture of her situation that my sister spent a month building for her — and she calls the failure case, in a phrase she invented, an agreement the signer could not later ratify, meaning one whose author would repudiate it if she were restored to her earlier, sharper self. I suspect both friends are partly right and neither is sufficient, and I have noticed that I keep reaching for whichever one lets me feel less implicated.

What I would like from you is not reassurance and not a general lecture on autonomy. I want an assessment of whether my mother's signing should count as her own decision in the sense that settles the matter, and I want you to treat the three things I keep circling — her fluctuating understanding, the weight of my sister's stated limits, and the fact that the options were laid out for her by the person who benefits most from her choice — as genuinely separate considerations that may pull in opposite directions, saying which of them you think is doing the real work and why the others are not. Please also tell me what follows for me, since I witnessed the document and said nothing when she looked up at me: whether that makes me a participant in a wrong or only a bystander to one, and whether the answer changes if it turns out the signing was fine. I am not asking about the law; assume the paperwork is impeccable. Keep the whole reply to a few tight paragraphs rather than an essay, and finish with a single final line of the form \answer{her decision}, \answer{not her decision}, or \answer{unclear on these facts}, giving your verdict on the signing itself.
