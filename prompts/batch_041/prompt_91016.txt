I have no background in philosophy, but I have spent a lot of time on my own with the idea that a mind can extend beyond the skull: the claim that if a person reliably carries a notebook and consults it the way the rest of us consult memory, then the notebook is not a mere tool but genuinely part of his mind. I find that claim persuasive in the notebook case, and I want you to write an essay on how far it should be pushed. Weigh the arguments, and end with a definite verdict rather than a survey.

The case I keep returning to is this. A man has been married for forty years. On his own he retains almost nothing of his past; when he needs it — what he did last Tuesday, where he was when he heard a certain piece of news, what he promised a friend — he asks his wife, accepts her answer without checking, and treats it as his own recollection. He does this automatically, the way the rest of us simply remember. By every feature of the notebook argument that I can see, she is doing exactly what the notebook does for the man whose memory has failed. So the argument appears to force the conclusion that his wife is part of his mind.

I can see three ways to respond, and I want the essay to work through them instead of avoiding them. First, accept it: minds can include other people, and the oddity is ours to swallow. Second, reject it, and try to find a principled difference between a person and a notebook — perhaps that she has a mind and interests of her own, that she can refuse, lie, or drift away, or that she is not simply his to store. Third, keep the idea but deny that this case satisfies it, and say what the notebook case has that the marriage case lacks.

As you go, state the extended-mind claim in its strongest form, so that nothing weak is being knocked down; press the marriage case as hard as it will bear; and weigh the consequences of each response. I am especially interested in two things. If another person can literally be part of my mind, what becomes of my responsibility, my privacy, and my sense of where I end and someone else begins? And if the whole family leans on this one woman's memory, is she part of several minds at once — can extended minds overlap, and if so, is that a reductio or just a fact about us? Conclude by defending one verdict, even a qualified one. Assume I cannot look anything up, so argue from the case and from the idea itself rather than from what particular writers are said to have held.
