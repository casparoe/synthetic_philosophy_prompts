I chair the investment committee at a mid-sized engineering firm, and Thursday we have to vote on whether to sign a two-year co-development deal with a parter company that's been structured in a strange way, so I need a written opinion to circulate beforehand.

Here's the structure, stripped down. There are eight milestones. At each milestone, whichever side is on the clock can either stay in or pull out. If you stay in, the shared pot (the value of the joint IP) roughly doubles by the next milestone. If you pull out when it's your turn, the whole thing ends right there and you take the larger share of whatever the pot is at that moment — pulling out pays more than the current equal split, but obviously less than the equal split of the much bigger pot you'd both get by staying in all the way to milestone eight, where you split evenly and its the best outcome for both of us. Our outside counsel says the analysis is trivial: work backwards. At milestone eight the side on the clock pulls out, because taking the bigger share of the current pot beats splitting a pot that can't grow anymore. Knowing that, at milestone seven the other side pulls out, and so on all the way back, so at milestone one whoever is on the clock pulls out and we never get past the first round. Counsel's conclusion is that signing is irrational and we should walk away, or if we sign, we should plan to pull out at our first turn.

That's fine as far as it goes, but nobody actually behaves like that, and I don't just mean naive people — the whole reason this setup has a name is that it's a paradox, the backwards reasoning looks airtight and yet it predicts an outcome basically nobody produces. I also don't think counsel's story is as clean as they think, because the whole regress only goes through if it's common knowledge that both sides are rational in exactly this narrow sense — I have to know you'll pull out at eight, and know that you know I'll pull out at seven, and so on — and that infinite tower of mutual knowledge is doing all the work, and I don't think we're entitled to just assume it.

What I want from you is a memo I can circulate to the committee. Recommend a concrete course of action — sign or don't sign, and if we sign, what we should do at our first milestone, plus what if anything we should try to get written into the contract ahead of time (can we bind ourselves, or penalize ourselves, for pulling out early, and does that actually help or is it just wishful thinking). Give me a time frame. And engage the strongest version of counsel's position seriously instead of waving it off as unrealistic — if backward induction really does apply here, say what the deal-breaker is, and if it doesn't, say exactly where the reasoning breaks and what has to be true about our counterparty for staying in to be the right call.

One more thing I want you to actually take a position on: there's a version of this where what makes cooperation work is just that each side puts some chance on the other being the type who won't pull out — a stubborn or principled partner. If that's what's really going on, then us being visibly the stubborn type might be worth more than any contract clause, and I want to know whether that changes your recommendation.

Keep it under a page and a half, no jargon for its own sake — the CFO sits on this committee and hates game theory words.
