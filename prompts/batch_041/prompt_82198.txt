Write a dialogue between two equally competent proponents of opposing answers to the question of how much rule-following a society should demand. Stipulate, and let the whole exchange turn on, the following: tightness is not one dial but a profile of four separable features — the number of rules, the severity of sanctions, the degree to which rules admit of context-sensitive exception, and the predictability with which they are enforced. A regime with few rules enforced capriciously is not a loose culture but an erratic one; let both speakers grant that erratic is worse than any of the other three, and let their dispute be about how to rank, and how to steer towards, dense-but-mild, sparse-but-strict, and dense-but-overridable regimes — given that a society can be steered only by the very rules under dispute.

Give them a concrete case to argue about: a port town in which a long-standing norm forbids households to sell their fishing quotas to outsiders. The norm has kept the fleet viable, but any household that defects takes an immediate windfall while the cost is spread across everyone, and the norm bears hardest on the poorest households, which have the least cushion against it and the strongest incentive to break it.

Each speaker must engage the other's strongest point rather than a convenient weaker one, and at least one of them must shift position in some small, defensible way by the end — or explain why this is a disagreement they cannot move in. Neither may appeal to named authors or to empirical studies; both must argue from the case and from principle. About a thousand words.
