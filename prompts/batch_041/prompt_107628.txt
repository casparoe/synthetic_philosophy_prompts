Write me a short story that runs on Moore's paradox, but not the philosophy-101 version of it — the bit I care about is how local and fragile the strangeness is. Two shapes: the omissive one, where you assert that p and then say you don't believe it (the raining example everyone uses), and the commissive one, where you say you believe p and then assert not-p. Neither is a contradiction. Both halves can be true at once, you can simply be out of touch with yourself, and neither sentence is false — that's what makes it a puzzle and not a mistake. And the absurdity evaporates fast: shift it to the second or third person and it's fine; put the belief in the past tense and it's fine ('back then I thought the bridge would hold, and it didn't' is an ordinary sentence); some people think there's a milder version of the same weirdness in the future tense, where you say it will rain and that you'll believe it didn't. Put the sentence in the mouth of a parrot, a tape recorder, or anything that never takes a stand on how things are, and it's not just fine, it's inert — there's nothing there to be absurd. There's also a live split about where the trouble lives: some hold that asserting it is broken while believing it is perfectly possible, others that believing it would be just as broken as saying it. And one family of views says 'I believe p' isn't a report on the inside of your head at all, it's the act of putting your weight behind p, so pairing it with not-p is nodding and shaking your head in one breath.

Don't explain any of that. Make it the engine. 1000–1500 words, someone ends up inside one of these sentences, and the story turns on the fact that the sentence is only unbearable in the first person and the present tense. Somewhere in it the same sentence has to occure in a mouth or a tense where it costs nothing at all, and the character has to notice that it costs nothing there and be unable to say why it costs everything here. No character may utter the words Moore's paradox or lecture about assertion, belief, norms, or contradiction. No twist where someone discovers the sentence is secretly contradictory — it definately isn't, that's the point. Skip courtrooms and interrogation rooms, they're obvious; find your own setting. And don't resolve it at the end: leave open whether the character is being irrational or whether the rule they keep breaking is just a house rule of assertion that nobody can give a reason for.
