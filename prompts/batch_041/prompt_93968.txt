ok so i'm a novelist, and i've been playing a ton of games while putting off my next book, and i've got this half-baked theory i want you to argue for properly. here it is: whether a video game character matters morally has basically nothing to do with sentience or an inner life (obviously they don't have one). it's about how much of the character is actually built out of your choices in the moment — lets call it how "thick" a character is. a fully scripted npc who repeats the same three lines forever is thin. a character whose whole personality gets assembled out of thousands of little decisions you make — how you talk to them, whether you save them, what you do when you know they're watching — is thick. the claim is that the thicker a character is, the more they matter morally, and that the moral weight comes from something like shared authorship between player and designer, not from anything happening inside the character.

please argue for that position. and don't be lazy with it — take on the hard case head-on, which is that it flips the usual intuition upside down: the more freedom a game hands the player, the *more* moral patienthood the character ends up with, so a big open sandbox generates more moral weight than a tightly authored linear story, even though the linear one is definately better written and the characters in it feel more real. as a novelist this bugs me because i'd always assumed my characters matter more than some procedurally assembled guy wandering around a map, and i want you to convince me i'm wrong.

also deal with the obvious objection that we're just talking about the player's feelings, not the character, and say what any of this implies for actual obligations — like, is there anything wrong with reloading a save to undo a character's death, or is that more like tearing up a draft than like killing someone? give me a real argument, not a list of pros and cons.
