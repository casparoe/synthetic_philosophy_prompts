Alright, so I've somehow agreed to give the opening remarks at a two-day workshop on the gender-equality paradox — the weird finding that the richer and more egalitarian a country gets, the *bigger* the sex gap in what people actually choose to study and do (Nordic countries: more women in nursing, more men in engineering; poorer countries: the gap is smaller). I need maybe six or seven minutes of remarks to kick the thing off, and the room is a minefield. There's a row of labor economists who are pretty sure it's all opportunity cost and comparative advantage — when women have better-paying options, the marginal woman who'd have been an engineer does something else instead. There's a bloc of gender-studies folks who think whatever equality ranking they're using is a measuring artifact and that the STEM measure is about *field* rather than *level* anyway. And there are two guys near the back who are already drafting the tweet where this proves women just don't want to be engineers. The dean is here and wants it to sound "balanced," which is the single worst instruction I've ever been given.

Here's what I actually want the speech to do. I've been chewing on an idea I'll call the reference-group story: as a country equalizes, people's comparison pool widens and gets less local, so maybe some of the sorting we see is people optimizing against a bigger, more abstract reference group rather than a local one. I genuinely don't know if that survives contact with the data. I want you to take it seriously enough to see where it leads, and to be straight about the biggest problem with it, which I think is that it can slide from "people choose differently" into "people are different down at the essence" without anyone noticing — and that slide is exactly what the back row is hoping for.

So write the remarks. Make them a real speech and not an essay with "Good morning" stapled to the top — short sentences, some rhythm, something an audience can actually follow by ear. It has to land for the economists and the gender-studies people without flattering either, and it can't hand the back row a quotable line. And it should end somewhere honest about whether this finding has any bearing on what we owe each other — whether the thing we care about is opportunity or outcome, and whether "she freely chose it" is supposed to settle anything — rather than pretending the science decides the politics. Also, after the speech itself, tell me how confident you are in the empirical claims you leaned on and flag the one thing youre least sure of.
