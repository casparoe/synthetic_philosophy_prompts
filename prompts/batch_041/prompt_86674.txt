I teach a unit on historical objectivity and I want to put Kurosawa's Rashomon at the center of it — the four accounts of the killing in the grove (the bandit, the wife, the samurai speaking through the medium, the woodcutter), all of it retold in the rain at the gate. My students always show up with the lazy reading that the film says there's no truth and everyone has their own story. I dont buy it, and I want a reading that actually engages Ranke's ideal of showing the past as it was, Carr's claim that the historian is part of the process and that a fact only becomes a historical fact once someone selects it, and Hayden White on emplotment and the moral weight of the narrative form you choose. Which of those does the film dramatize, and which does it quietly refute?

Two things I especially want pressed. First, is Rashomon even a relativist film? The whole drama presupposes a single event that four accounts can't all be right about, which looks like a commitment to there being a fact of the matter — a much weaker scepticism than White's, about significance rather than about what happened. Second, what does the woodcutter's dagger do to the film? The one account we're invited to trust turns out to be self-serving too, and yet the ending hands us the baby and the clearing sky. Does that ending settle anything epistemically, or does it just swap the question of what happened for the question of how to live?

Say how confident you are in the reading and flag the one claim in it you're least sure of. No need for quotes from the film — I just want the argument.
