I'm putting together a small anthology of speculative fiction for a general readership — the pitch is basically thought experiments you can live inside — and I've been handed the decision-theory slot. I've already got one story about Newcomb's problem, and now I want one built around the Pasadena game, from Nover and Hájek's paper in Mind (2004); I've only read it once so go gently on me. My rough grasp is this: a fair coin is tossed until it lands heads for the first time, and the payout on the nth toss grows without bound and alternates in sign, so the expectation is a conditionally convergent series — rearrange the terms and you can get any total you like, positive, negative, or nothing at all. So the game has no well-defined expected value, and the ordinary rule about playing when the expectation beats the price just falls silent. I don't want the story to cheat by quietly importing a utility function to rescue the character. If someone in the story reaches for log utility, or for truncating the game at some maximum number of tosses, I want the story to be honest that that's a choice someone made rather than a fact someone found.

What I'd like is a short story, say 1500 to 2500 words, in British English, set somewhere that makes the arithmetic feel like a real problem rather than a puzzle on a whiteboard. Here's the seed I keep coming back to: a purgatorial audit office, where the ledger of a soul's life is an infinite series — the good and bad deeds arranged in order, with the running totals growing without bound as you go further back — and the clerks have worked out that by reordering the entries they can produce any verdict they like, so every case can be settled as saved or damned depending on who's at the desk that day. The newest clerk has to deal with a case where the total genuinely has no value, and there's a senior clerk who has been quietly rearranging for decades and sleeping fine. Take that if it appeals, or go somewhere else entirely — a casino, an actuarial office, a family inheritance with an unbounded clause — but either way the undefined expectation has to matter to the plot rather than just being mentioned. Please end on a decision rather than a shrug, and make sure at least one character tries the tempting move of saying that in practice the payout is bounded because the coin will land heads eventually, or the ledger will be closed at some point, and that this move gets taken seriously rather than waved away. After the story, I'd like a short paragraph in plain terms saying which philosophical move each character made and whether it actually works.
