I teach a graduate seminar on the metaphysics of persistence, and for the middle session I want a diagnostic handout that carries a student from the few commitments nobody in the room disputes to a labeled position, in such a way that they cannot reach the label merely by announcing the label they already favor. Please build me a decision tree for that purpose. The starting commitment is the innocuous one — that a single thing can be present at more than one moment, that the person who begins reading the handout is the same person who finishes it — and from there every branch node should be a question about persistence itself rather than about any particular author or -ism: whether persisting things have distinct parts located at different times or are wholly present at each time; whether change forces us to posit such parts, or to relativize properties to times, or can be explained without either; whether, granting temporal parts, the things we ordinarily name and count — you, this desk, the seminar — are the temporally extended wholes or the momentary parts themselves, with the wholes being mere sums or series; whether persistence is numerical identity across time or only a suitable relation, such as causal continuity or something like genidentity, among numerically distinct entities; whether the existence of temporal parts is a substantive question about what there is or a verbal one about how we choose to count and describe; and whether any of these can be settled independently of one's ontology of time (presentism, eternalism, the growing block) or inevitably presupposes one. Phrase each node as a question a student could answer without already knowing which leaf it leads to.

I need at least the following leaves to appear and be named: endurantism; perdurantism, with worm theory as its standard form; stage theory; and a deflationary or verbal option. Add others only if the logical space genuinely demands them. Where you find a combination of answers that no one occupies, say so and diagnose briefly whether the combination is incoherent, merely unattractive, or simply unexplored. I would also rather have honesty than symmetry: if two leaves come out identical on the questions you have asked, flag them as a pair your tree cannot separate instead of inventing a branch to force them apart. And because the second half of the seminar applies the debate to things that are neither persons nor middle-sized objects — a wave, a species, a symphony, a corporation — tell me which leaves extend cleanly to those cases, which classify them badly, and which the tree simply cannot place at all.

Begin the response with the tree itself, laid out so a student could follow it top to bottom, and put any notes about unoccupied combinations, inseparable pairs, and hard cases after it, with no preface or commentary before the tree.
