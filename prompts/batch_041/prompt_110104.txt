I have been reading rationalist blogs and forums for the better part of two decades, which means I am a Bayesian about credence and vaguely embarrassed about outright belief, and I have never been able to decide whether the lottery paradox and the preface paradox are one puzzle wearing two coats or two puzzles that happen to share a coat. I want a decision tree for a long forum post — the kind of instrument that walks a reader from a few concrete commitments to a named position — and I want it built out of the logical space of the problem rather than out of a roster of famous authors. Aim for five or six branch questions and seven to ten leaves. No node may be answerable by reciting a position; every node should ask what the reader would say or believe about a case. Where the tree bottoms out, give the view a descriptive name and one sentence on its most awkward commitment. I am partial to the idea that the puzzles dissolve once you taboo the word belief, and I would like the tree to tell me whether that is a genuine leaf or an evasion.

The reader should enter through cases rather than self-description: a fair thousand-ticket lottery with one ticket in hand; a book of three hundred separately researched claims, each of which its author would defend; a deliberately short two-claim preface where the chance of at least one error is still plainly above zero; the same two-claim case with the propositions mutually exclusive rather than merely independent, so that the lottery's structure and the preface's can be pulled apart; and a single-claim case, to see whether the reader thinks the trouble lies in the number of claims or in the threshold itself.

Turn the branch structure on the distinctions I actually care about: whether there is such a thing as outright belief at all, or only credences; if there is, whether it is governed by a threshold on credence or by something else, such as being in a position to know or having ruled out the relevant alternatives; whether rationality requires one's beliefs to be consistent and closed under conjunction, and specifically whether it licenses agglomeration, the step from believing A and believing B to believing A and B; whether the threshold, if it exists, is fixed or moves with context and stakes; and whether anything changes when the agent's credences are imprecise, a range of probabilities rather than a single number, which I suspect is where at least one popular way out quietly cheats. The leaves should include, at minimum, degrees-only, on which there is no outright belief and the paradox never gets started; the deflationary line that the trouble lies in an unprincipled threshold in our vocabulary rather than in the world; reject agglomeration while keeping both the threshold and consistency, so that one believes of each ticket that it lost, never forms the conjunction, and never believes anything inconsistent; reject consistency and tolerate rational inconsistency; keep the threshold and raise it toward certainty, so that lottery propositions are never believed at all; belief governed by knowledge or by the ruling out of relevant alternatives; contextualism or subject-sensitive invariantism, on which the same person both believes and fails to believe as the context or the stakes shift; and the acceptance/belief split, on which one accepts the lottery proposition for the purposes of action without believing it.

Finally, mark which combinations of answers nobody occupies, and say briefly why — incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored — and flag any pairs of leaves the tree cannot separate, especially the pairs that differ only in vocabulary.
