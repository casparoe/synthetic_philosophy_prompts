write me a leaked internal doc from a big consumer-tech company's comms department -- a "Truthful Communication Playbook" style training manual, deadpan, around 700 words, numbered rules plus a couple of sidebars. The joke has to be the lying-vs-misleading distinction, and it has to be gotten right: the playbook has fully internalized the gap between asserting something false and saying something true that walks the audience into a false conclusion, and it has concluded that only the first one is off limits. So rule 1 is basically "never assert a falsehood," and then every other rule is a machine for manufacturing false implicatures that survive a fact check. I want the internal taxonomy to be in the weeds: separate codes for (a) flat false assertion, banned; (b) true assertion whose implicature is false, encouraged; (c) true assertion plus an inference you didn't intend but are happy to let stand, the "we never said that" tier -- and the doc should treat (b) and (c) as different severity levels with different sign-off requirements and different levels of legal review. Include a "deniability ladder" sidebar that takes one fact (invent it -- a battery thing, a data breach, whatever) and phrases it at three rungs, all of them technically true. Also a dissenting footnote from the in-house ethicist, whose objection is itself phrased so it can't be pinned down. Dont name any real company or cite any real cases, invent them. It should never actually resolve whether misleading is worse than lying -- let the playbook's own defense ("the journalist could have asked a follow-up") be the thing being skewered, and let a reader feel the pull both ways by the end. Funny to someone who knows the implicature literature and the misleading-can-be-worse-than-lying line, readable to someone who doesnt.
