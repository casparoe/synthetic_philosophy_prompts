I teach a 90-minute session in an upper-level undergraduate seminar in environmental ethics: twelve students, no reading assigned beforehand, several of them already convinced that the answer to whether we should protect habitat is obvious. I want them to feel the force of a claim that sounds absurd on first contact and becomes hard to dismiss once the arithmetic is in front of them: that whether destroying a habitat reduces or increases the total suffering of the animals living in it is not at all obvious, and that a conservation metric and a welfare metric can point in opposite directions on the same piece of land. They should reach that conclusion through their own calculations, and the discovery should come out of the numbers rather than out of anything I say in advance. I don't want the framing phrase "wild animal suffering" spoken until the debrief.

**Setup.** The class becomes the board of a land trust with a fixed budget of 200 units. Four parcels of equal area are on the table, each with a different asking price (intact primary forest, 160; selectively logged forest, 100; a tree-crop plantation, 60; improved pasture, 40). The board must allocate the whole budget and produce a signed one-page justification naming the assumptions it made.

**Materials.** For each parcel, a ledger card listing five to seven animal groups, with four columns per group: annual life-days (already multiplied out, in millions, so students do one multiplication rather than three), an average per-day quality band on a −3 to +3 scale, annual deaths, and the share of deaths attributable to predation, starvation, disease, exposure, and old age. The quality bands are stipulated by me as a "field survey result" and are the only input students are forbidden to revise; I hand out a note saying the numbers are invented but their qualitative shape is not. The ledgers are built so that the intact forest carries by far the largest number of life-days and the largest annual death toll, concentrated in small, fast-breeding species with very high juvenile mortality, while the pasture carries few animals with comparatively high per-day quality.

**Round one (25 minutes).** Students split into four teams, one per parcel, and each computes four figures: total life-days, net welfare (life-days × quality), annual deaths, and species count. Each team posts all four numbers. The point of the four-column ledger is that the columns disagree: one parcel leads on life-days, another on net welfare, a third on fewest deaths, a fourth on species richness, and the intact forest is not the winner on the column that tracks suffering. Let the disagreement sit; do not adjudicate. The board then allocates the budget.

**Round two (15 minutes).** Conversion is not free: any parcel not bought is converted by its owner, and the converted parcel's ledger applies, plus a one-time displacement toll of deaths in the transition year, which I supply on a card. Then I deal an event card — drought, disease outbreak, or a hard winter — and teams recompute. In the forest parcels the population crash is followed within a season by a juvenile rebound, so the annual death toll barely moves; in the pasture, hay and veterinary care buffer the crash. This is the moment the room usually goes quiet: the number of deaths turns out to be roughly insensitive to how much land is left wild, because the surplus is simply replaced.

**Round three (15 minutes).** Two interventions become available at the same price as 100 units of land: a programme that halves juvenile mortality in one forest species, and a programme that halves its birth rate. Students must say which they would fund and why, and they will find that they cannot answer without committing to whether the additional lives created by the first programme are worth living. That commitment, not the arithmetic, is what the session is about.

**Debrief (25 minutes).** Map each moment onto its structure: the four-column ledger onto the difference between a conservation target (biodiversity, biomass, ecosystem function) and a welfare target; the rebound in round two onto density dependence and the replacement of deaths, and hence onto the possibility that per-life quality, not land area, is the only lever that matters; round three onto the total-versus-average welfare question and onto the fact that preventing a death and preventing a birth are not interchangeable. Name the idea here, not before, and present the philosophical literature on wild animal welfare as a live disagreement rather than a settled finding.

**What will go wrong.** Teams that draw the intact forest will accuse me of rigging the numbers; the fix is that they do the arithmetic themselves and that the stipulation sheet is handed out at the start. Someone will refuse the quality bands as meaningless across species, which is correct and should be preserved rather than smoothed over — it becomes a debrief item. The multiplication load defeats one or two students; pair them. Most predictably, the group will over-read the invented numbers as evidence and will drift toward "so conservation is pointless" or "so we should reduce wild populations"; the debrief must refuse both, and should ask what would have to be true of wild animals' lives for the ranking to reverse.

**What the activity cannot reproduce.** It cannot supply the thing the real argument turns on: we have almost no measurements of wild animal welfare, and the quality bands I stipulate are the least defensible input in the whole exercise, doing most of the work. It cannot represent scale, since the ledger compresses populations of millions into a single row. And it cannot represent the possibility that most wild animals live lives that are net positive, in which case the exercise's punch line dissolves. Say all of this out loud; if the students leave thinking the numbers settled anything, the session has failed in the opposite direction from the one I want.
