I write a column on science and ideas for a small nonprofit newsroom, which means I also sit on the board of the nonprofit, which means I'm on the investment committee, which means Thursday I have to vote on where a chunk of our endowment goes and I'm the one who has to circulate the memo. So I'm asking you to write it.

Here's the situation. We have about $4 million to place. Our adviser says two options are a wash on expected return, call it 7 percent a year for both. Option A is a plain old bond-and-dividend portfolio: decades of data, we can put a number on the volatility, we know roughly what a bad year looks like. Option B is a fund that buys pieces of lawsuits -- litigation finance -- and here's my problem with it. The manager quotes the same 7 percent. But the track record is four years long, the payoffs depend on juries and settlements, and when I asked for a distribution I got a shrug. Not a wide distribution. No distribution. The downside is "whatever the worst case is," and nobody will tell me what that is.

Our chair wants A, on the grounds that at least we know what we're buying. My friend Priya, who is also on the committee and used to trade, says that's just paying a premium for a comfortable story, and that if we'd take A over B at identical expected return we should be honest that we'd also take A when the roles are reversed, which she says makes us a money pump.

This feels exactly like the urn thing -- the one the Ellsberg of Pentagon Papers fame cooked up back when he was doing decision theory. Two urns, a hundred balls each, one you know is fifty-fifty red and black, the other an unknown mix of the two. You'd rather bet on red from the known urn than the unknown one, and you'd rather bet on black from the known one too, and the combination is supposed to be incoherent, because betting red-or-black from either urn is a guaranteed payout either way.

So: draft the memo I hand out before the vote. Recommend a course of action -- A, B, or split it -- with an actual number attached, not "it depends on the committee's risk tolerance." Then do the part I actually care about. Run our decision through the urn and tell me where the analogy holds and where it breaks. Is Priya right that we're being incoherent, or is there a defensible story for paying to avoid the unknown? Because our unknown isn't a neutral urn -- somebody filled it, and that somebody is paid a fee to tell us the odds, which is not the setup in the thought experiment, and I want to know whether that changes the verdict or whether I'm just reaching for an excuse. I also keep hearing from the finance types that there are two camps on how to handle this: the ones who say pick the model that's worst for you and maximize against it, and the ones who say the problem dissolves if you just put a probability distribution over the possible models. Tell me which of those actually does the work here and which one is hand-waving, and say plainly what the best argument is for whichever option you're not recommending, because Priya will make it in the room and I'd rather not be ambushed.

Two pages, no citations, nobody on this committee reads footnotes. One more thing: we passed on a fund like B about three years ago and it's done well since, so half the room is going to walk in hot about that. Factor it in, but don't let it drive the recommendation. It's a board meeting, not a seminar on regret.
