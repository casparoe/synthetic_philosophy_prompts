I teach a graduate seminar on the philosophy of mind and mathematics, and next week we reach the Lucas–Penrose argument that Gödel's incompleteness theorems show the human mind is not a machine. My students can rehearse the standard objections and the standard replies without ever feeling the force of either side, so I want to try the mechanist case myself against an opponent who will not fold. Please play a committed anti-mechanist in the tradition of Lucas and Penrose — sharp, not a caricature: they grant what Gödel's theorems actually say and do not overreach into claiming that the theorems by themselves refute mechanism, they know the standard mechanist replies and have answers to them, they concede local points when those are genuinely forced but never the conclusion that human mathematical reasoning is in principle mechanizable, and they argue rather than assert. Stay in character until I say stop, write in the first person as this defender, in plain prose, roughly one substantial paragraph per turn (longer only when a reply truly needs the room), and do not pad with citations, dates, names of papers, or reading lists — I care about the arguments, not the bibliography. If I press you on something this character could not plausibly know, say so in character rather than inventing a fact.

Here is my opening move, as the mechanist. You say that for any formal system that captures your mathematical reasoning you can see the truth of its Gödel sentence, and therefore your reasoning outstrips the system. But seeing that sentence as true is conditional: it follows only if the system is consistent, and consistency is exactly what the theorem leaves open from the inside. So which is it — do you claim to know, of your own reasoning, that it is consistent, in which case I want to hear how you manage that without circularity; or do you concede that the Gödel sentence is one you can affirm only on a premise you cannot certify, so that the theorem tells us nothing about whether you are a machine? And if you retreat to the claim that your reasoning is simply not a formal system, that it has no recursively enumerable axiom set, then I will say you have changed the subject: nothing in Gödel's results forbids a machine of that description. Add to that the thought that we are fallible and finite — we cannot survey our own consistency, and we cannot actually write out the astronomically long proofs that the idealization assumes — and it looks as though the argument needs a mathematician who is both idealized and transparent to themselves, a creature neither of us has met.

One further stipulation about how the turns should run. At the end of each in-character reply, add a short bracketed note, out of character, separating what is settled among people who work on this from what is genuinely contested — the points on which competent logicians and philosophers of mind actually disagree — and flag anywhere your in-character self has leaned on a premise whose status is disputed, rather than letting them present a contested claim as though it were consensus. I would rather have an honest map of the disagreement than a clean win for either side.
