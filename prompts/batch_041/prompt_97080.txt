ok so — I'm a 4th-year PhD student in philosophy of mind and I've somehow ended up running the 90-minute session for our advanced seminar on intentionality (12 students, mixed MA and final-year undergrad). We've done Dennett's intentional stance and the two-bitser, and Searle on original vs derived intentionality, and honestly it isn't landing. They can all recite both views back at me. Half the room thinks Dennett is obviously right ("obviously the machine's 'belief' is just ascription, who cares"), the other half thinks Searle is obviously right ("obviously the machine doesn't really believe anything"), and nobody feels any tension between those two, which is sort of the whole point of the disagreement. I want them to walk into it themselves.

Here's what I want them to come away having *felt*, in this order: (1) that they will spontaneously, reliably and usefully predict a dumb mechanism by ascribing beliefs to it, and that this works better than any mechanism-level description they can write down; (2) that when two physically identical machines sit in two different worlds the *content* of the belief comes apart — same insides, different aboutness — and that they can't read the content off the insides, not even from the inside; (3) that this is exactly the fork: Dennett says that's all content ever was (ascription relative to a spec), Searle says it shows the ascription is observer-relative and therefore derived, i.e. not intentionality at all. I want them to feel that those are two seperate reactions to the same fact.

My rough plan, which you should feel free to rip up and rebuild if you've got something better:

Two tables, call them Home and Twin. On each table a pile of discs (cardboard is fine), each printed with a diameter and a mass, some of them also printed with a "value" that the machines aren't allowed to read. The machines are students who've been *trained by example* — someone shows them a run of discs and says accept/reject, they're never told the rule and they cant articulate it, they just go on feel. The Twin machine is a copy: identical training protocol, same discs, same order, same accept/reject pattern, so its insides are literally the same as the Home machine's; the only difference is which world it wakes up in. Home's genuine coins have one physical profile, Twin's genuine coins have a different one, and each world also has counterfeits that are physically identical to that world's genuine coins. Then we run rounds where "customers" pay with discs and the machine scores points for accepting good ones and rejecting bad ones — tokens or candy, no real money. The Twin machine should do badly at first, and the moment I want the room to go quiet is when they realise its beliefs changed without anything inside it changing. Then the spec mechanic: each side writes a paragraph — the spec — saying what the machine is for, and we derive from the spec what the machine believes. Different specs, same machine, different beliefs, each correct in its own terms. Then the counterfeit: the machine accepts a slug that's physically identical to a real coin. Did it believe the slug was a coin, i.e. a false belief? Or did it believe nothing of the kind? And then the variant where a Twin machine was trained in its own world on the formally identical schedule — now it accepts the local coins, and we'd say it believes *those* are quarters.

Finally, before any discussion, everyone privately writes two sentences: (a) what the Twin machine believes about the local coin, and (b) whether that is a genuine belief or only an ascription we're projecting. Collect them, read out the distribution, and only then unpack it. My hope is the room splits in a way that tracks the real disagreement and that the split is uncomfortable.

Then the debrief: map what happened onto the two-bitser, onto the original/derived distinction, and onto what each side has to say about the other. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time — I'm assuming someone breaks character and starts reasoning about the coins, and someone else says "but the machines are people, so this proves nothing" — and how to handle it without lecturing.

My suspicion is that because the machines are people playing at being machines, the mechanism is completely transparent to them, which hands Searle the verdict for free — the whole force of Dennett's view comes from systems whose insides are opaque and whose behaviour you genuinely can't predict otherwise.
