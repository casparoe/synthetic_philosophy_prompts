I've just come out of a long and rather bruising argument with a friend in my just war theory seminar about the moral equality of combatants, and I'd like you to serve as my interlocutor: take the role of a philosopher who works on the ethics of war, knows this debate from the inside, and is willing to tell me where my position is exposed rather than simply catalogue the usual talking points. Please begin by restating my view in its strongest form and sketching, in a few lines, the whole space of objections you can think of — including the unfashionable ones — before you commit to a final answer; then rank what you find.

The view I've been defending is a split one, and the split is precisely what I want you to stress-test. On the question of what is objectively permitted, I have come round to the revisionist side: an unjust combatant who kills a just one is doing something impermissible, and the facts that he is fighting for his country, under orders, and at real risk to himself do not license the killing. But on the question of what we are to do with that verdict — who is to blame, who is liable to be killed, what a rules-of-engagement manual or a post-war tribunal should say — I think the answer comes out symmetrical anyway, for two reasons I suspect are carrying more weight than I have admitted. The first is epistemic: no ordinary soldier can be expected to adjudicate the justice of his state's cause, so combatants on both sides are, almost without exception, non-culpable for being where they are. The second is institutional: any in bello practice that tried to track individual culpability in real time would be unworkable and would very likely make wars worse. So my position is asymmetry in the deep ethics and symmetry in accountability — and I cannot decide whether that is a genuine and under-appreciated reconciliation or a fudge that quietly surrenders the revisionist insight while keeping its vocabulary.

What I want from you is a map of my exposure. Which cases, arguments, and empirical claims should move me, ordered by how decisive they would be rather than merely listed? I am especially concerned with four things. First, whether the epistemic point really delivers symmetry of permission or only symmetry of excuse, and whether that distinction can be made to bear the weight I am putting on it. Second, whether the institutional point is a genuine empirical prediction — one that could in principle be falsified by how asymmetric practices actually behave — or merely a counsel of prudence in moral dress. Third, what my view predicts about concrete verdicts that its rivals do not: the liability of an unjust combatant to be killed, the liability of a just combatant to be killed by him, the standing of a tribunal to punish either, and the difference, if any, between the conscript who cannot know and the volunteer who does. Fourth, whether the view has been qualified into unfalsifiability, so that nothing could count against it. Please distinguish throughout between defeaters that a committed defender of my view would themselves concede and those that only a critic would press, and close by telling me honestly whether the split I have described amounts to a position or only to a hesitation. For what it is worth, the argument that would move me most is a case of perfect epistemic symmetry — two combatants indistinguishable from their own standpoints — in which the moral verdicts nonetheless come apart; if you can construct one, I want to see exactly where the divergence lives.
