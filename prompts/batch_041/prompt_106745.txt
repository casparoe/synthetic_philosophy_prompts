I am preparing a seminar on the philosophy of time for students who have read Kurt Vonnegut's Slaughterhouse-Five but have almost no physics, and I want to use the novel to teach the Rietdijk–Putnam argument without letting it collapse into a slogan. The novel is usually read through trauma, memory, and fatalism: Billy Pilgrim experiences his life out of chronological order, the Tralfamadorians see all moments as coexisting, and the narrative treats death as one moment among others. I want a reading that takes that four-dimensionalism seriously as a philosophical position and asks how far it actually coincides with the block universe that the Rietdijk–Putnam argument is supposed to establish from the relativity of simultaneity. Separate three things that the novel and its critics tend to run together: the special-relativistic claim that different inertial observers slice spacetime into different planes of simultaneity; the metaphysical claim that all events are equally real; and the psychological or ethical attitude that, because all moments are equally real, grief and protest are misplaced. Does Tralfamadore dramatize the first two, or does it smuggle in a single absolute four-dimensional manifold and a God's-eye view that the physics does not license? Where does the novel's time-travel imagery help students see the force of the Rietdijk–Putnam argument, and where does it encourage the mistake of treating the block universe as a kind of determinism or as a consolation?

A student in my class suggested that the Tralfamadorian view is simply the B-theory of time plus a denial of temporal passage, and that the Rietdijk–Putnam argument gets you to the B-theory only if you accept a contested premise about the reality of events that are simultaneous for some observer. I want the reading to adjudicate that suggestion rather than wave at it. Formulate the missing premise as precisely as you can — something like a principle that if an event is simultaneous with a real event for some inertial observer, then it is real — and then ask whether that principle is analytic, a substantive metaphysical assumption, or a disguised form of presentism. Test it against the novel's own structure: Billy's jumps are discontinuous and personal, whereas relativity gives us continuous worldlines and observer-dependent simultaneity. Does that mismatch matter, or is the novel entitled to a different kind of four-dimensionalism? I am not asking for a physics lecture; I am asking for a philosophical reading that knows where the physics ends and the metaphysics begins.

Please keep the reading faithful in both directions. On the literary side, do not reduce the novel to an illustration. Use specific scenes or structural features that are common knowledge among readers: the Dresden bombing and its aftermath, Billy's jumps between moments, the Tralfamadorian zoo, the narrative's habit of announcing deaths before they happen, and the narrator's own intrusions. On the philosophical side, do not simply assert that relativity proves the block universe; explain what the Rietdijk–Putnam argument needs and say whether the novel supplies it or merely presupposes it. I do not need a bibliography, reading list, or citations, and I do not need quotations from the novel; I want an argument about what the novel shows, complicates, or gets wrong about the block universe, and what it adds to the debate that a bare statement of the Rietdijk–Putnam argument does not. If the novel resists the reading at some point, say so plainly and explain why the honest critic has to concede it.
