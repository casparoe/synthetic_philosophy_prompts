I run a small consulting shop that helps founders and negotiators design commitments -- we're the people companies call when they need to make a threat or a promise the other side will actually believe, and we do maybe a dozen of these engagements a year, always with real money and real relationships on the line.

Here's the thing I keep running into and I don't have a framework for it. Sometimes a deal pays you for *intending* to do something, not for doing it. Clean example: a client's board will hand him a big retention bonus if he sincerely commits to walking away from a rival's acquisition offer, and the bonus vests the moment they're convinced he means it -- but actually walking away is bad for him, and by the time he's got the money there's no reason left to do it. So what do we tell him? It looks like he should form the intention and then not act, which feels like cheating, or like it shouldn't even be possible to do on purpose.

Please write me a decision procedure my advisors can actually run with a client. A checklist of questions in order: what to ask, what counts as a good answer, where it stops with a clear *don't go near this* or *yes, commit*, and where it has to hand the call back to the client because it's about what he can bring himself to intend rather than about facts. Keep in mind my advisors aren't philosophsers and the client has to sign off on whatever we recommend, so the procedure needs to be something a smart non-specialist can run in an afternoon. It also has to be honest about the part I suspect is a real wall: you can't just decide to intend something you already know you won't do, and if you *can* make yourself intend it, maybe that means you've changed in a way that makes following through rational after all, which would blow up the whole trick.

Then run it on three cases: the retention bonus above; a parent who's been told her kid will stop testing her if she can credibly intend to actually leave the birthday party, and she really doesn't want to leave; and a union rep whose leverage depends on credibly intending to call a strike that would hurt the very workers he represents. Tell me which of these the procedure can settle and which it can't, and include one where it tells my client something he won't want to hear.

Lead with the bottom line in your first sentence -- your one-line verdict on whether a procedure like this is even coherent -- then give the reasoning. No preamble, no restating my question.
