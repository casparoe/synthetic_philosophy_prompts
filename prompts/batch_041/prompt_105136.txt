I have been reporting on a dispute in a coastal town where the local community's memory of a nineteenth-century massacre survives only in a commemorative song and in the annual procession that accompanies it, while the only contemporaneous written record is the militia's logbook, which describes the same event as a labor disturbance. Historians treat the song as unreliable because its verses have plainly been polished by generations of performance; the community treats the logbook as unreliable because it was written by the perpetrators, and because the community's own records were destroyed in the same attack. The two accounts do not simply disagree about facts. They answer to different standards of evidence, and choosing between them looks less like a neutral historiographical judgment than a prior political commitment. I know the terms oral history and history from below, and I know that philosophers have written about testimonial and hermeneutical injustice, but none of those quite captures the specific bind I am trying to describe: the written record is both the only archival evidence and an instrument of the violence it records, while the community's memory is both the only counter-evidence and a form that professional history is trained to distrust. Is there an established name for this configuration, or for the asymmetry that makes adjudication itself reproduce the original imbalance? If there is no settled name, what would you call it, and how would you mark its boundaries against those neighboring ideas?
