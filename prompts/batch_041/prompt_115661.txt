I'm a software engineer, not a philosopher, and I'm building a system that drafts long technical reports from a knowledge base. It emits several hundred factual claims, each scored by an internal model at 0.99 or better, and then a closing summary that asserts the conjunction of all of them. Two reviewers have objected, and I can't tell whether they've found a bug in my system or a real problem about what it is rational to assert.

The first objection is arithmetical: five hundred claims at 0.99 each are jointly almost certainly not all true, so the summary is almost certainly false even though every claim in it is almost certainly true. The second objection runs the other way: if the system is entitled to each claim, and the claims together entail the summary, then it is entitled to the summary, and it is incoherent to forbid the one while permitting the others. I recognise the shape of this from a couple of paradoxes I've read about — one about a lottery, one about the preface of a book — but I don't want a survey of what philosophers have said about them. I want the specification.

Suppose I get to design the belief layer of this system and choose what the theory underneath it says. What must a theory of rational belief, or of rational acceptance, deliver to be adequate for that job? Give me the requirements list, and for each requirement: the case or consideration that forces it, whether it is non-negotiable or merely the preference of one camp, and whether it tells me what the system may print or only how I should describe what it prints. I'd rather have four honest requirements than eight, so tell me which of the ones I'd naturally expect are not really requirements at all.

I especially want the requirements that pull against each other. If some subset — say, that the system's assertions be jointly consistent, that they be closed under the inferences the system can actually draw, and that asserting something require a high probability of its being true — cannot be satisfied together, say so, say which one has to go, and don't hedge about it.

Two things I care about that the write-ups I've found don't seem to address. First, the system decides claim by claim as it generates, so a theory that only says the finished corpus should be consistent tells it nothing about what to do at claim three hundred. Second, the lottery case and my case are not obviously the same, because in the lottery the possibilities are exhaustive and mutually exclusive by construction, while my claims are independent of one another. Say whether your list treats those as one problem or two, and why that matters for the list.

Then, briefly, score the main families of answer against your list: that belief is just credence above a threshold; that closure is what has to give; that the unit of rationality is the whole corpus rather than the individual claim; and any view that separates believing a claim from accepting it for a purpose.

Where a position is called for, take one. Tell me which single requirement does the most work in ruling out candidate theories, which requirement you would abandon first if you were forced to, and what you would have the system print in its closing summary. I'd rather have a committed answer with its reasons than an even-handed menu of options.
