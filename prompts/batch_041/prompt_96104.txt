I'm a novelist, which means I've written my share of death scenes, but I've never had to do the real thing until now: my aunt Ruth's funeral is on the 14th and I'm giving the eulogy, and I'm stuck in a way that's honestly new to me. Ruth was 79. For the last nineteen years she was the main carer for her husband Martin, who got early-onset Parkinsons in his fifties and then dementia on top of it, and who died four months before she did. So the room is going to be mostly family, neighbours, a handful of people from the day centre where Martin went twice a week, and my cousin Nadia, who is a philosopher and who has been hovering over my shoulder the whole time I've been trying to draft this, issuing objections.

Her objections are the reason I'm writing to you instead of just winging it. She says: don't call Ruth a saint, don't say she gave up her life, don't say Martin was lucky, don't say she was a natural at it. Her point, as I understand it, is that every one of those turns caring into either a vocation or a sacrifice, and both of those make it sound like something that either descends on you from nowhere or costs you absolutely everything, and neither is what actually went on in that house. She keeps putting weight on a distinction I'd like you to spell out properly for me: caring *for* someone versus caring *about* them, the first being a thing you do in the room hour by hour, the second a feeling you can have from a thousand miles away. She also says that caring for someone involves being sort of taken over by their situation in a way you can't decide to be taken over by — which means Ruth didn't exactly choose it, and also that she couldn't really have stopped — and that the whole nineteen years only worked because Martin did something back, held up his end somehow, and that if I write a speech where he's just the weight she carried then I've erased him twice over. She thinks the reason everyone reaches for "saint" is that we don't have any other vocabulary for a life like that, and she'd rather I found one than reached for it. I think she's right and I also think a funeral is not a seminar, so I need both things at once.

Please act as a philosopher who works on the ethics of care, and then as a speechwriter. Think carefully, and start by brainstorming for me: lay out the different things "Ruth cared for Martin" could actually be claiming — from the sentimental to the demanding — and say which of them are true, which are false, and which ones can honestly be said out loud in front of that room. Then write the eulogy.

It should run four or five minutes. No jargon — the day centre people will know instantly if I'm faking it, and so will Nadia, and the rest of the room just needs to be able to grieve. You can include one small concrete memory of the two of them to make it land; invent it, I'll swap in a real one. Martin was a builder, if that helps. Neither of them believed in anything after death, so nothing about him watching over us or her being at peace now. And I'd rather you didn't use the word "saint" at all, even to deny it — I want to see if there's a way to say what she did that doesn't need it.
