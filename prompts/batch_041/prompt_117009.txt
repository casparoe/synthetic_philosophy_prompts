Last night I spent several hours arguing with a friend about the Kelly criterion, and I should like you to explain the conceptual issue that the argument kept circling, rather than the betting arithmetic itself. My friend's position is that Kelly is not really a result about money. Money, he says, is simply the clearest instance of something that compounds: you stake a fraction of what you hold, and what you are left with is what you had, multiplied by some factor. He wants to extend this to everything a person can gain or lose and genuinely cares about — health, attention, reputation, the trust others place in them, the strength of a friendship — and to conclude that the correct rule is the same in every case: do not maximise the expected value of the thing, maximise its expected logarithm, which is to say, maximise its long-run growth rate. He has a name for it: being geometric about everything. His sharper claim, and the one that actually bothers me, is that expected utility theory is a theory for an ensemble — for a great many copies of you — whereas you are a single person who lives once, so that the quantity to maximise is the growth rate of your one life, not the average across lives that were never lived.

I can see why this is seductive and I suspect it is a category error, but I cannot say precisely where the reasoning holds and where it breaks. Please explain the conceptual issue. What is it about money that makes the logarithm the right thing to maximise — its divisibility, its fungibility, the fact that any fraction of it can be staked, the fact that one may lose almost all of it and still keep playing, the fact that its only serious use is to be staked again? Which of those properties does a friendship, or a reputation, or a night's sleep actually possess? I should particularly like the difference explained between three claims that my friend runs together and treats as one: that the expected logarithm is the appropriate utility function for a risk-averse agent; that the time-average growth rate of a multiplicative process is maximised by staking according to the log rule; and that the log rule is therefore what a rational agent ought to do whenever anything in their life compounds. The second seems to me to be a theorem and the third a normative claim, and I want to know whether the gap between them is a fact about the world or an artefact of how we chose to model it. I also want the distinction between the ensemble average and the time average made clear, since I suspect the whole case turns on their coming apart.

Please be concrete rather than general. If a person genuinely tried to be geometric about their attention, or about how far they trusted other people, what would that prescribe, and where would it prescribe something incoherent or repellent? And is geometric rationality a substantive thesis about how to live, or merely a description of a process once one has specified what compounds?
