I'm a PhD student in philosophy working on authenticity and reproduction, and I want you to help me think through a puzzle in museum practice. Please act as an expert in the metaphysics and aesthetics of art — someone at home with analytic work on authenticity, forgery, and the ontology of artworks, but not confined to it. Work carefully: start by brainstorming the space of possible readings before committing to a reconstruction, and flag your own uncertainty where it arises.

Here is the pattern that puzzles me. On the one hand, museums and the art market treat the physical original as irreplaceable. A painting that turns out to be a forgery is effectively destroyed as an asset: it is deaccessioned or revalued sharply downward, removed from display, and stripped of the premium that attaches to a documented artist's hand. Provenance research, scientific authentication, and the money paid for a securely attributed work all testify to a value placed on origin and causal history as such.

On the other hand, the same institutions routinely treat originals as dispensable. They display high-quality facsimiles and 3D-printed replicas — for touch tours, for accessibility, for objects too fragile to show. They sell reproductions in gift shops. They let digital surrogates circulate as the primary object of study. And when a forgery is exposed, they sometimes keep it on display anyway, relabeled to acknowledge its status, on the grounds that it remains aesthetically or pedagogically valuable.

I'd like you to do the following.

First, reconstruct the implicit theory: what view of the value of originals does this cluster of practices presuppose? Consider at least these candidates and say which the practice best fits — (a) originals have non-instrumental value as particular objects, independent of any experience they afford; (b) originals have only instrumental value, as the most reliable route to certain experiences or to historical contact with a maker; (c) originals have value as nodes in an institutional or testimonial network, conferred by provenance, certificates, and shared recognition rather than residing in the object; (d) some hybrid.

Second, separate what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits comfortably with. Where is it underdetermined — which rival theories would license the same behavior equally well?

Third, is the practice internally consistent? Can a single coherent theory account for both the reverence for originals and their routine dispensability, or does the practice silently presuppose incompatible commitments?

Fourth, a counterfactual: suppose we replaced the presupposed theory with the view that only the perceptual and experiential encounter with the work matters. What would have to change in museum practice, and what would be lost that better copies cannot recover?

Finally, consider a proposed policy on which every displayed object carries two numbers: a "provenance confidence" rating and a "contact distance" — the number of causal steps separating the viewer from the maker's hand. Does adopting such a policy commit a museum to a particular theory of the value of originals, or is it theory-neutral? Explain your answer.
