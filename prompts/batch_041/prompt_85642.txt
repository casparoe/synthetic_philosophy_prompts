My graduate seminar has spent the term on emergence, and last week we read the dialogue in which a colony of ants is given the name Aunt Hillary and treated as a single conversational partner. The question I wanted the session to answer is whether such a colony could literally have a mind, and the position I find most interesting is the one that answers yes while denying that any ant has so much as a belief or a desire.

A colleague from the biology faculty sat in, and when a student objected that a great many things which understand nothing do not add up to something that understands, he replied that the whole is greater than the sum of its parts, as though the objection had thereby been answered. Pressed, he said the slogan means that the colony has properties that no ant has; pressed again, he said that this is simply what biology is like. He has also been known to add that more is different. I would like to go back to him with something better than a stare, and I would rather have a menu of his possible commitments than a defence of any one of them.

So please take the sentence 'the whole is greater than the sum of its parts' and set out the distinct precise theses it could be expressing in this setting, ordered from the nearly vacuous to the genuinely radical. For each reading I want the thesis stated exactly; a verdict on it (trivially true, substantively true, contentious, or false), with the reason; and a statement of what would have to be the case for it to hold. Please make sure the menu includes at least a mereological reading, a compositional reading, an epistemic or predictive reading, an explanatory reading, and an ontological reading, and add any further reading the slogan is doing duty for that I have missed. Note where the readings collapse into one another and where they come apart.

Then say which of them would actually license the claim that the colony has a mind, and which are perfectly compatible with there being no mentality in the colony at all, only behaviour that invites the ascription. Say which reading my colleague most likely intends, and whether his second slogan is a restatement of it or a rival to it.

Fix the discussion to a stipulated case. The colony numbers a few thousand ants. Scouts find two candidate nest sites; no ant visits both, and no ant's behaviour tracks the comparison, but the number of ants present at each site does, and when that number passes a threshold the colony relocates, the ants carrying one another. Over months the colony's traffic settles into a pattern that returns to the two richest of several feeding sites and ignores the rest; the pattern is carried partly in pheromone trails and partly in the individual ants' tendencies, and it re-establishes itself after every ant then alive has died. Remove a random third of the ants and the pattern persists; remove the trails and it decays; remove enough ants and it collapses. For each reading on the menu, say whether this case satisfies it, and what the case would have to gain in order to satisfy the ones it does not.

Two further things. First, if the colony has a mind, what is that mind made of, given that its parts have none? Say whether that question is a legitimate demand or a disguised assumption, and what turns on the answer. Second, tell me what further feature would have to be added for the colony to have a mind in the sense that matters, and what sort of case would show that it has it or lacks it. End by telling me which reading I should concede and which I should resist, if I want to hold that the colony has no mind while granting that there is something true in the neighbourhood of the slogan.
