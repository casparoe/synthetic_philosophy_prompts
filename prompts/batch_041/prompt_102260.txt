For a column I write on social theory and public policy, I'm trying to do something a little unfashionable: rather than ask what the Marxist tradition has said about care work, I want to reason forward from Marx's own theoretical machinery to a question he never had occasion to pose. The concrete occasion is a legislative proposal now being floated in a couple of European parliaments — twelve months of paid parental leave at close to a replacement wage, followed by a statutory entitlement to a publicly funded childcare place, financed by a levy on employers' payrolls rather than out of general revenue. Marx died a century before any parliament debated such a thing, and I don't want the standard recital of what he and Engels wrote about the bourgeois family; assume I have that. What I want is a reconstruction, done step by step from his own categories, of where he would come down on a world in which the wage is normally earned by two adults and the daily care of children has been substantially lifted out of the household and handed to the state.

Begin where he begins, with the value of labour-power and what it is supposed to cover: the reproduction of the worker, and therefore the maintenance of the next generation of workers. Then push on the question I actually care about. If childcare is socialised and a second earner becomes the norm, what happens to that magnitude? There is a deflationary reading — the costs that used to be borne privately in the household now come out of the social fund, so the socially necessary bundle of necessaries shrinks, the value of labour-power falls, and capital quietly recovers in lower wages much of what the state appeared to give; note that this reading has to reckon with his insistence that the bundle is not fixed by physiology but is itself historically and socially determined, which could as easily push the other way. And there is an inflationary reading — a second wage and a public entitlement raise the family's income and its confidence to refuse bad terms, tightening the labour market and weakening the reserve army. Say which way the machinery actually points, and what turns on it.

Two further things, and then a verdict. First, the levy falls on employers' payrolls: would he treat its incidence as a detail, or as the crux, given what he thought happens to taxes nominally aimed at capital? Second, a demand that only arose long after him — the campaign to pay wages for housework, on the reasoning that unwaged domestic labour is the hidden subsidy that makes the wage form possible at all. Would he see that as a clarification of his own account of reproduction, or as a category mistake that mistakes the household for a workplace? Close with the question I keep circling: would he back the leave-and-childcare package as a hard-won reform, in the spirit of the factory acts he admired, or would he read it as a stabiliser of the very arrangement — the sale of labour-power as the universal condition — that he wanted abolished? Be willing to say where the framework strains rather than forcing a tidy answer.
