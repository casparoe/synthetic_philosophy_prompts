I am a fifth-year PhD student in metaphysics, and in three weeks I have to present a work-in-progress paper at our department seminar. The paper defends a deliberately strong version of origin essentialism, which I have taken to calling gamete-token essentialism: not merely that I could not have had a different genetic makeup, nor merely that I could not have arisen from a different pair of gametes, but that I could not have arisen from a numerically distinct pair of gametes even if those gametes were molecule-for-molecule identical in every respect to the ones that actually fused to form me. My central argument is what I call the token-fixing argument. If the zygote is identical to the later organism, and if the identity of the zygote across worlds is fixed by the identity of the two particular gametes whose fusion produced it, then the transworld identity of me requires the transworld identity of those two tokens, and genetic type is irrelevant to the identity of the token. To dramatize the conclusion I use a case I have not seen discussed anywhere: a technician in an IVF clinic, reaching into a cold rack, by accident picks up a numerically distinct vial whose contents happen to be genetically indistinguishable from the intended gamete, and the resulting child is, by every biological measure anyone can run, the same child who would otherwise have been born. I claim that child is not me, and I want to know how much trouble I am in.

The audience is small but adversarial in a very specific way, and I would like to walk in knowing what is coming. Two of the regulars are philosophers of biology who work on reproduction and development; they will insist that "the sperm and the egg" is a retrospective redescription of a slow and messy process, that there is no fact of the matter about which sperm fertilized the egg until well after the relevant events, and that gametes do not have determinate identities that survive syngamy. One regular is a counterpart theorist of a broadly Lewisian persuasion and will tell me that I have confused de re necessity with the existence of a counterpart relation, so that my "could not have" is doing no metaphysical work. One is a Kantian-adjacent skeptic about modal intuition pumps who will want to know what the intuition is supposed to be evidence for. And one works on personal identity and animalism and will press me on whether it is the organism, the person, or the genome that is supposed to be essential, noting that my case seems to trade on a genetic criterion I claim to reject.

Please prepare my Q&A sheet. I want the objections in roughly the order they will actually come, phrased the way each of these people would phrase them, ordered by how much damage they do to the paper rather than by how likely they are; for each one, a reply I can deliver in about two minutes and an honest note on where that reply is weakest. Tell me what I should concede at the outset rather than defend, which of these objections I should treat as a genuine threat and which I should wave off, and finish with the one question I am hoping nobody asks.
