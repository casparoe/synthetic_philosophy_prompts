Ok, so I keep noticing the same shape of argument and I'm not sure if it has a name. The setup is a theory that demotes some inner state of a person to a byproduct — it comes along for the ride and doesn't push anything around — and then, in the very next breath, the theory treats whats people say about that state as good evidence that the state is real. But if the state does no work, what makes the words come out right? The words have to be caused by whatever the theory says does the real causing, and that thing has no particular reason to line up with the byproduct, unless you quietly let the byproduct cause something after all. I keep bumping into this squeeze: the view that the felt quality of experience is just a side effect of brain processes, while our reports about it are still supposed to be accurate; the worry that if our minds were shaped by natural selection for surviving and reproducing rather than for getting things right, we can't trust them when they tell us anything, including that they were shaped by selection; the picture where the conscious narrator is basically a press secretary making up a story after the fact, which seems to put the press secretary's own story about being a press secretary in the same boat; and the version about moral facts, where if they don't cause anything, it's unclear how our beliefs about them ever get to be true.

So: does this shape have a standard name, or is it just self-refutation in a costume? If there's no term, name it, and tell me where the edges are — how it differs from a plain performative contradiction, from the general gripe that a theory can't account for itself, and from a tu quoque — and say which of my four cases actually belong under it and which are a different animal. Also, does the objection land, or can you rescue the view by saying the byproduct is reliably correlated with the cause even though it isn't itself a cause?
