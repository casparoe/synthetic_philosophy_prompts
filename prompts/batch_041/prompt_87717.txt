I am a novelist at work on a book whose premise is a device — let me call it the Ledger — that a founding council can consult before any child is conceived. The Ledger will tell them, for any proposed settlement, two things: how many people will live there, and how well, on average, each of them will live, measured on a scale on which zero is a life not worth living and everything above it is a life its bearer would not trade for nonexistence. The council's choice binds a century and cannot be revisited. Writing their deliberations has forced me to confront my own verdicts about population, and I have found them less coherent than I would like. I would be grateful if you would tell me what principle, or small family of principles, actually fits them, and where I have to give something up.

Here is what I find myself thinking, case by case.

A settlement of a thousand people, each living well, is worse than the same settlement with one more person added, whose life is also good and who is welcomed by everyone. More good lives is, other things equal, better.

That same settlement of a thousand flourishing people is better — and not by a little — than a settlement of ten million people each of whose lives is barely worth living: content enough that they do not wish they had never been born, but pinched, narrow, without grief and without great joy. I would not trade the thousand for the ten million.

A child whose life will be good, born into a family that can raise them well, is straightforwardly a good thing.

A child whose life will be barely worth living, born into that same family, where adding them harms no one else but does lower the average: I am pulled to say this is not good — perhaps mildly bad — even though the child themselves does not regret existing and no one is made worse off.

A person who will suffer terribly and whose life, taken whole, will not be worth living: bringing them into existence is straightforwardly bad, and I do not need to compare it to any alternative to say so.

A settlement that ends, contentedly, with its final generation and leaves no one behind: I think something has been lost, even though there is no one for whom it is a loss. I cannot say who has been wronged, and yet I would not call the ending neutral.

And a case I keep turning over: a child conceived in a way that will give them a mild, lifelong limitation, whose life will nonetheless be good and who will not wish they had been otherwise. I think no one has been wronged and there is nothing to regret; and yet, if the parents could have had a different child, without the limitation, at no cost to anyone, I would counsel them to. I cannot reconcile those two responses, and I suspect the second is a residue of something I ought to abandon.

I can see the shape of a principle I would like to hold: a life that is worth living counts in favor of creating it, but only once it is good enough by some standard; below that standard, adding lives does not make the world better even if the lives are worth living. Tell me whether that can be made precise and whether it is coherent, or whether it collapses into one of the familiar views.

What I want from you: state the principle or principles precisely, and for each clause say which of my verdicts above forces it. Then apply it to cases I have not listed — I would particularly like your verdicts on (i) a settlement of a thousand flourishing people versus a settlement of two thousand people living lives exactly at whatever threshold your principle fixes, and (ii) whether the principle can say anything at all about the loss in the last-generation case, or must fall silent there. Finally, tell me honestly which of my verdicts the best principle cannot accommodate, and why I should give that one up rather than complicate the principle to save it.
