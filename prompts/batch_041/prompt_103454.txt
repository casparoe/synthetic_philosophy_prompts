My friend and I spent most of yesterday evening arguing about work — about who has a right to it, who has a right to be free of it, and who gets to decide — and I came away less sure of my own position than of his, which is the worse outcome. I would like you to audit that position rather than defend it. Here, as plainly as I can put it, is where I stand.

First, I take meaningful work to be a constituent of a flourishing life, not merely a means to a wage. A life with no productive activity in it at all strikes me as impoverished, and part of what a decent society owes its members is the availability of work that can genuinely be their own.

Second, I take justice to require a substantially shorter standard working week — the thirty-five-hour week is the obvious model — and I take the real argument for it to be fairness in the distribution of drudgery. The dull, exhausting, and degrading work has to be done by someone, and a just society spreads it rather than letting it pool at the bottom while others enjoy interesting jobs. The leisure this buys is a welcome consequence, not the point of the thing.

Third, I take adults who genuinely want to work longer hours to be entitled to do so. Paternalistic rules about how people spend their working lives — rules that treat grown men and women as unable to weigh their own trade-offs between income and time — are objectionable in principle, however well intentioned.

Fourth, I take karoshi, death or collapse from overwork, to be a grave moral wrong, and I take the responsibility for preventing it to rest primarily with employers and the state rather than with the workers who logged the hours.

Fifth, I take the Protestant work ethic — the whole apparatus that treats labor as a calling and idleness as a sin — to be a harmful ideology, and its erosion to have been on balance a good thing.

I have been calling the resulting package post-puritan perfectionism, half as a joke. What I want from you is not encouragement but a consistency check. Go through the set and tell me which commitments actually pull against one another, which pairs only appear to conflict and why the appearance is misleading, and, for the pairs that are genuinely in tension, how severe the conflict is, what the cheapest repair would be, and what that repair costs me elsewhere in the package. Rank the tensions by severity rather than presenting them as equally bad, and finish by naming the single revision that would clean up the most. I suspect the fault line lies somewhere I have not thought to look, so do not assume I already know which commitment I would sacrifice; I don't.
