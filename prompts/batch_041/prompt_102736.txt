I teach an advanced undergraduate seminar on creativity and cognition (sixteen students, one ninety-minute session, no reading assigned beforehand), and I want an in-class activity that makes them feel the force of the knob-twiddling picture of creativity — the idea, associated with Hofstadter, that a creative act is a variation: a theme occupies a point in a space of possible versions, and novelty comes from turning the dials of that space, sometimes adding or removing a dial, and occasionally re-carving what the space is a space of. The load-bearing notion should be sameness-across-variation: by the end they should feel that preserving "the same theme" is the hard part, not generating the variations.

Design the activity for me. I have a whiteboard, paper, and a timer, and nothing else. Please give me:

- The theme and the materials. I want a theme that requires no equipment and no drawing or musical training, so that a mixed-ability group can all produce variations on the spot. I had been imagining a short spoken or clapped motif, but tell me if something else would work better and why.
- Rules and roles: how teams are formed, what a "variation" is required to be, the procedure by which a variation is judged to be still the same theme, and a scoring scheme that rewards both preservation of the invariant and genuine movement within the space. Assume there is a real risk that "same theme" collapses into a matter of taste, and build against that.
- A sequence of rounds that moves the group, without announcing the structure, from turning a single dial, to turning several dials at once, to adding a new dial or removing one, to re-carving the space — the point at which a variation stops being a variation on the theme and becomes a new theme that merely resembles the old one. They should arrive at that last move by doing it, not by being told about it.
- A timeline for the ninety minutes, with what I say at each transition, kept to the minimum.
- A debrief that maps the moments of the activity onto the parts of the model — dial, space, invariant, skeleton, re-carving — and that brings out why the final move is the creative one and why it cannot be reached by twiddling harder.
- What will predictably go wrong the first time, and how to handle each failure. In particular: a team produces a variation that is exciting but no longer recognizably the same theme; the judgment of sameness drifts into taste; the "add a dial" round stalls because no one sees that a new dimension is available.
- An honest note on what the activity cannot reproduce and that I will have to supply in the debrief. I suspect one such thing is the slow, unforced, years-long discovery of a new skeleton; tell me the others, and tell me which feature of the idea the format distorts rather than merely omits.

Keep everything runnable as written, and flag any place where the activity would work only if I improvise.
