I am a doctoral candidate in philosophy serving as the graduate member of my institute's deployment review board, and we must vote on Thursday on whether to approve an agentic research assistant whose safety case turns on a contested reading of the relation between an agent's capabilities and its goals. The proposers argue, in outline, that because a system's level of intelligence and its final objective are independent of one another, and because this system's objective is a narrow, tightly specified reward, there is no reason to expect it to pursue power or resist shutdown; the familiar worry that sufficiently capable agents converge on instrumentally useful subgoals such as self-preservation and resource acquisition is said to bite only for agents whose goals are open-ended or self-referential. A minority on the board replies that this misstates the point: the independence claim concerns final goals only, whereas the convergence worry is precisely that almost any final goal, given enough capability, yields power-seeking as an instrumental subgoal, so the narrowness of the objective affords no reassurance at all. We are deadlocked, and the chair has asked each member for a written opinion before the vote.

Please draft the short opinion I will circulate. It should recommend a concrete disposition, namely approve, reject, or approve subject to specified conditions and a review date, and defend that recommendation. In doing so it should set out which reading of the independence claim and which version of the convergence argument each side actually needs in order to reach its conclusion, say where those arguments are strongest and where they overreach, and give an honest account of the most serious consideration on the side I come out against. Assume the board is technically literate but not expert in decision theory, and avoid citations.
