Please explain, in conceptual terms rather than by listing theorems, what changes when dominance reasoning in games is formulated over mixed strategies, and how much of the usual story about what dominance elimination shows survives the change. I would like three things disentangled. First, how a pure strategy can be strictly dominated by a mixture while no pure strategy dominates it — give a small example — and why the availability of mixed strategies, and not merely the player's beliefs about the opponent, is doing the work in such a case. Second, why the decision-theoretic justification of elimination still goes through (a dominated strategy is not optimal under any belief about the opponent) even though the simpler reading, on which some available alternative is at least as good against everything the opponent might do, does not: different alternatives may do worse than the dominated strategy against different opponent actions, so no single alternative is safe. Third, why iterated elimination of strictly dominated strategies yields the same surviving set whatever the order of elimination, whereas the weakly dominated version does not, and what that asymmetry reveals about the difference between a strategy that is bad under every belief and one that is bad only under beliefs that give no weight to some opponent action. Flag any step that depends on a technical result rather than on the concept itself, and say what that result contributes.
