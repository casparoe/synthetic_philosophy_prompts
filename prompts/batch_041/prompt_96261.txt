I want to pressure-test the open-future position — the one the sea-battle puzzle is usually taken to motivate — by playing its opponent, so please take the other side and hold it as your own.

Play a philosopher who defends this package:

(1) Statements about the settled past and the present have determinate truth values. Statements about future events that are not yet determined are neither true nor false, and the gap is metaphysical rather than epistemic: it is not that we cannot know how things will go, but that there is as yet nothing to know.

(2) You keep classical logic intact — excluded middle, double negation, all of it. "There will be a battle tomorrow or there will not be" is a logical truth even though neither disjunct is true. The cost of (1) is paid in the theory of truth, not in the logic.

(3) The asymmetry between past and future is a feature of the world, not of us: the past is closed, the future is open.

Be a real opponent rather than a strawman. You may draw technical distinctions (truth versus determinacy, truth at a time versus truth simpliciter, truth versus correctness or vindication), you may concede that some familiar formulations of the view are muddled, and you may revise your own formulation under pressure. What you may not do is abandon any of (1)–(3), or concede that the future is in any sense already written. Write like a careful analytic philosopher in a seminar: plain prose, one argument at a time, a few paragraphs per reply, and no appeals to authority — make the case yourself rather than reporting what this or that philosopher held. Stay in character until I say "out of character." The one exception: end each reply with a brief, clearly marked out-of-character note separating what you said that is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who work on this, and flagging anywhere you leaned on a contested reading — a contested view must not be presented as the consensus. You may name positions in that note, but give no citations or reading lists.

Here is my opening move. Please answer it rather than asking me to clarify.

I sat on a hiring committee. Before the vote I said of a candidate, "she will accept our offer." By your lights her deliberation was genuinely open; nothing in her psychology or in our offer had settled it. She accepted. Three problems follow, and I do not see how you can block all three.

First: either my earlier sentence was true or it was not. If it was not true, then a sentence can be untrue while saying exactly what a true sentence would have said, and I want to know what work "true" is doing that "vindicated" is not already doing. If instead it became true, then a piece of the past — my utterance — has changed its semantic properties, which sits badly with (3): my assertion is now part of the fixed past, yet you say its truth value was still up for grabs when I made it.

Second: my colleague across the table had a device — call it an oracle — that announces the outcome of undetermined future choices. It has been right ten thousand times out of ten thousand, including in cases where every available reason pointed the other way. Either the oracle knew something, in which case there was a fact about her acceptance to be known and my sentence was true; or it knew nothing, in which case its record is a coincidence of probability zero — and I would sooner believe in facts about the future than in that.

Third: you say the asymmetry is in the world and not in us, but our world is indeterministic, so some past events were, at their time, open in just the sense in which you say the future is open now. What closed them? If the answer is that they happened, I will ask what made it true that they happened; if the answer is that later events settled them, then you have given the future truth-makers as well.

Take them in whatever order you like, but tell me which of the three you think does the most damage, and why.
