I'm a backend engineer, never took a philosophy class, and I got into a long argument with a friend who's a hard determinist. His line is that compatabilists are just changing the subject: if the universe is deterministic then calling me "free" when I do what I want is like saying a door is "unlocked" when it's actually welded to the frame — sure, technically it's not the lock that's stopping you, but the door still isn't going anywhere. I pushed back that people mean about five different things by "free" and that his argument only works because he's lumping them together, but when he asked me to actually lay them out I couldn't, so I lost. So: can you explain the real varieties here, as distinct things and not as one blob? In particular I want the difference between (a) my body doing what my will says vs. being physically forced, (b) my will being one I endorse vs. a craving I hate and fight and lose to anyway, (c) my will being a product of me rather than of something upstream of me — the regress worry that whatever made me pick my character, I didn't pick that, so how is any of it mine, and (d) me being the kind of system that tracks reasons at all, such that in principle you could get me to change my mind with a better argument. And then I want to know which of those is actually doing the work in different places, because I suspect they're not all needed for the same job: moral blame, legal punishment, taking pride in my own work, and the feeling that my life is mine seem like four different demands and I doubt one variety of freedom satisfies all four.

The framing I keep coming back to, since it's my day job: with permissions you can separate whether a process was allowed to write to the disk, whether the code that did the writing was code I wrote, and whether I'm the one who designed the permission policy in the first place — three different senses of "it was me" that can come apart. Map those onto the free will varieties, and tell me where the analogy breaks down, because I have a feeling it breaks somewhere important. To make it concrete, take three people: one who takes a drug because he wholeheartedly wants to and has no wish to stop; one who's disgusted by his craving, tries to stop, and takes it anyway; and one who got a chip implanted as a teenager that generates the craving, would never have chosen that, but is now fully on board with it and wants it kept. My friend says all three are equally unfree since none of them could have done otherwise, which strikes me as collapsing something obvious, but I need to know exactly what it collapses.

Don't walk me through the option space and leave me to pick — I want you to commit. Tell me which of the varieties you think are actually worth wanting and which are a consolation prize dressed up as the real thing, whether it's the compatabilist or my friend who's conflating things, and land on a verdict for the chip guy specifically. If you think the whole question is confused, say that instead, but then point to the exact step where it goes wrong rather than hedging around it.
