A company I used to work for makes proof-checking tools for safety-critical code — the kind of thing that certifies a pacemaker's firmware can't deadlock. Their rule is that the verifier itself can never be signed off by the team that built it; every release has to be validated by an outside group, and the justification people give is Gödel: no system can prove its own consistency, so a system can't certify itself. Weird thing is, the same company lets that outside group check itself with its own in-house tool, and they ship a compiler thats compiled by an earlier version of itself, which nobody treats as a scandal. So is the Gödel stuff doing any real work here, or is it decoration on top of a perfectly ordinary worry about getting fresh eyes on your code? If you took the Gödelian principle seriously as a general claim about self-certifying systems, what would the audit policy actually have to look like, and what does the company's behavior tell you about which claim their really committed to? And when someone says Gödel proves no computer could ever do what a human mathematician does, is that the same commitment as the audit rule or a different one — would someone who signs off on the audit policy have to accept that too?
