Right, so I've been going round in circles on this for weeks and I need someone to lay out the actual conceptual issue for me rather than just the standard summary.

Jaspers splits guilt into four kinds — criminal, political, moral, metaphysical — and political guilt is the one that snags me. As I read him its not about anything you did: it attaches to you just by virtue of being a citizen of that state, and it doesn't die with you, it carries on down to people who weren't born yet. Moral guilt is the opposite — strictly personal, it needs something you actually did, and it stops at your own skin.

Two questions, and I suspect they're really one. First, what is the word 'guilt' doing in the political case? If I was born thirty years after it happened, didn't do it, couldn't have stopped it, and have spent my adult life campaigning against the people who defend it, then calling me politically guilty looks like it's picking out liability or responsibility rather than guilt. Second, if that's right, does the four-fold list still work as a list of kinds of guilt, or does it quietly become three kinds of guilt plus one kind of inherited liability that got given the wrong name?

What I want is the conceptual issue spelt out, not the labels re-sorted: what would have to be true of guilt for it to be inheritable in the first place, and what notion of a 'nation' does that require — a genuine bearer of guilt, or just shorthand for a heap of individual ones? And there's a case I can't place at all: the state that did it has since dissolved, and both successor states repudiated it in their founding documents. Where does the inherited political guilt go — to the successors, to the people, or nowhere?

Tell me if I've got Jaspers wrong, incidentally.
