I'm a novelist — mid-career, three books, none of them any good — and I've got a premise I keep failing to make work at novel length, so I want to see it as a short story first. Tell me if it lands.

Marnie is an actuary at a small firm that writes insurance policies for charitable foundations. Its a strange product: a foundation makes a grant, and Marnie's firm sells them cover against the long-range harm their own grant will eventually do. Bad schools built with good money, a treatment that makes a disease survivable and therefore keeps it circulating, that kind of thing. The firm can only sell this stuff because of one assumption buried in the pricing model — that the downstream ripples of any decent-sized act cancel out in expectation, that the good and the bad spread and wash together over time. Marnie's been running the models for eleven years and has never had a reason to doubt it. Then she finds a grant where they don't cancel, and she can't tell whether that's because she's finally got enough data, or because the question "what did this grant add up to, all told" doesnt have an answer — there's just no fact of the matter once the causal web gets wide enough. She starts to suspect the second thing, and to notice that the model only ever spits out a number because of a convention nobody says out loud: count fifty years out, count only humans, count only effects somebody could have foreseen. Chop the web down to size and it adds up fine. She has to decide whether that's a cheat or the only honest thing in the building.

What I want:

- a story, 1500-2500 words, third person, close on Marnie
- at least one scene that's nothing but two people disagreeing about whether her problem is ignorance (get more data) or indeterminacy (there's nothing there to get), and neither of them wins
- the philosophy has to live in the machinery — the memos, the tables, the meeting where they fight about the cutoff. Nobody explains the concept to anybody. No lectures, no "as the philosopher so-and-so argued"
- the ending shouldn't resolve it. I want her doing something practical and slightly wrong.

I think there's a name for this in the philosophy literature, cluelessness or something, if that helps you aim. And please definately don't make it about AI or utilitarianism-as-villain, I'm bored of that.
