Set out the desiderata for a theory of non-informative credence. I am convening a small reading group on the foundations of prior selection, and I want the session to begin from a specification rather than from a menu of proposals, because the standard proposals — classical indifference, invariance under a group of transformations, maximum entropy, and the imprecise-probability family — are almost always defended and attacked one at a time, and nobody in the room seems to have written down what the whole class of them is being asked to do. So: what must any account deliver in order to count as an adequate theory of how a rational agent should distribute credence when the only thing fixed is a space of possibilities, together with whatever symmetry, or absence of symmetry, the agent's description of the situation happens to supply?

For each requirement, give me the case or the consideration that motivates it, and be explicit about whether you take it to be non-negotiable or a preference of one camp. I expect the list to cover at least the following kinds of items, though I do not want it confined to them: the simple cases it must get right, where the symmetry is uncontroversial and the answer is beyond dispute; the classical puzzles it must either dissolve or, failing that, reframe in a way that explains why the puzzle has no determinate answer; the distinctions it must not collapse, such as that between the agent's ignorance and the world's indeterminacy, between a single permissible credence and a set of permissible ones, between a prior offered as a convenience on the way to a posterior and a prior claimed to be rationally obligatory, and between a reparameterization that exposes a defect in the theory and one that merely reflects a genuine gap in what the agent knows; and the formal results it must be consistent with, including the ordinary probability axioms, the behaviour of additivity on infinite and on non-denumerable spaces, conditionalization, and whatever coherence constraints you think it inherits from decision theory. I would also like its verdict on a less obvious matter: whether an agent can end up with a less settled credence than before by acquiring information that is logically independent of the quantity in question.

The part I most want is the incompatibility. Some of these requirements, I suspect, cannot be jointly satisfied, and an honest statement of that is worth more to me than a longer list. Take the demand that the theory return a unique answer in every case in which the agent genuinely lacks information, and set it against the demand that the answer not depend on which of several equally faithful descriptions of the same situation the agent happens to have used. Say what has to be given up, and say whether the usual way of keeping both — supplying the agent with a symmetry or a constraint that, by hypothesis, they do not have — is a solution or a change of subject. Then tell me which single requirement on your list does the most work in excluding candidates, and which one you suspect is really a prejudice of one camp dressed up as a criterion.

I have one case of my own that the list has to say something about, and it is the reason I am asking. An agent knows only that an unknown positive quantity lies between 1 and 100, and that is the whole of what they know: no group structure, no natural measure, no physical symmetry, no repeated trials. The agent is asked for a credence that the quantity exceeds 50. Everything the agent knows can be stated just as faithfully in terms of the quantity itself as in terms of its reciprocal or its logarithm, and each of those descriptions is equally compatible with the agent's total information, so a principle that assigns credence by symmetry over the described space will return three different answers, while a principle that declines to choose will return none. I want your list to render a verdict here: either a determinate credence, together with the requirement that delivers it, or a principled reason why no determinate credence is available, together with a statement of what that does to the claim that a theory of this kind is a theory of rationality rather than a toolbox. If your requirements cannot jointly handle the case, say so plainly; that result is more useful to me than a list that quietly steers around it.

You may close with a short, unsystematic comparison of how the four families I named fare against your list, but keep it brief — the criteria are what I want from you, not the scorecard.

Write this as continuous prose. No headings, no bullet points, no numbered lists set out as lists, no bold, and no tables; you may number the requirements in running sentences if that helps, but I want something I can read the way I would read a letter.
