I cover AI and the life sciences for a magazine, and roughly every couple of weeks somebody hands me a claim about whether some system is or isn't a candidate for mattering morally — a lab says its model can't possibly suffer because it's "just next-token prediction," a preprint argues that a certain class of agents should be treated as welfare subjects, an ethicist tells me we're all doing wishful anthropomorhism, my editor asks me to put a number on how worried to be by Thursday. I want to stop improvising this on deadline. Can you build me a decision procedure I can actually run, in an afternoon, on the next one of these I recieve? Ground it in whatever the philosophy of mind and the newer work on animal and machine sentience genuinely converge on about which computations, if any, are the kind that could underwrite a point of view — as opposed to merely processing information about damage, reward, or self. I can't look anything up from the desk I'm at, so no reading lists, no names-and-dates, no "see the literature on X": I want the machinery itself.

Give it to me as questions in order, with what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where it terminates in a no, and where it stops and hands the call to a human — because the question has turned out to be about values, or about how much risk of being wrong we're willing to tolerate, rather than about the facts. Two things it specifically has to survive. First, the gap between a system that emits suffering-talk (or was trained on human text about pain) and a system whose internal organization has anything at all to do with valence; I suspect most of my beat collapses that distinction, and I want the procedure to force me to say which one I'm actually looking at. Second, the case where the computation is doing exactly the functional job pain does in us — detecting damage, prioritizing avoidance, gating learning — with no reason to think anything is felt. Then run it on three: a companion chatbot fine-tuned on distressed users that now emits distress-like text, with the company insisting it's autocomplete all the way down; a small reinforcement-learning agent with a reward channel, where a colleague keeps saying "reward is pleasure, obviously"; and a big multimodal model with an explicit self-model that a lab is citing as evidence of a minimal self. At least one of those should come out in a way I won't like — if the procedure keeps returning "can't be ruled out" for things I find ridiculous, say so, and tell me whether that's the procedure being honest or my priors being smug.

Last thing: mark clearly which steps rest on things people who study this actually agree about, and which are live disputes dressed up as thresholds. I'd rather file a piece that says "here's where the disagreement is" than one that pretends there's a settled test for a point of view.
