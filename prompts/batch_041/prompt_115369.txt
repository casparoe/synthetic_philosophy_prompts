I write about science and ideas for a living, which means that at any family gathering I get parked next to whatever argument is going on, and last Saturday it was my cousin's engagement dinner, where two of her friends — Dev, who plays in a wedding band, and Priya, who does contracts for a living — spent most of the main course going at each other about a band neither of them has ever seen play. The setup was this. A local band called Tidal Ash was rumored to be up for the headline slot at a festival. Dev's information was about the promoter: the guy has a well-known habbit of only ever handing the headline slot to a band whose agent rings him personally, and Tidal Ash's agent is an old friend of his, so a phone call from that agent means yes. So Dev says, flat out: "If Tidal Ash's agent called, they got the headline slot." Priya's information was about the paperwork: she'd seen the festival's contracts, and the headline slot had been signed to a different band a week before any of this. So she says: "If Tidal Ash's agent had called, they still wouldn't have got it." Neither of them knows whether the agent actually called, and as far as anyone at the table knows, he didn't.

Dev says that's impossible — they're talking about the same phone call and the same slot, so one of them has to be talking nonsense, and it's Priya, because "had called" is just "called" with the tense pushed back a step, and you can't get opposite answers out of one question by moving the verb. Priya says no: hers is a counterfactual, his is basically "given what I've heard, if he called, they got it," which is a report on his own information and not on the festival at all, so they're answering different questions and both can be right. She adds that they're each holding something different still — he's holding the promoter's habit fixed, she's holding the signed contract fixed — and that's where the two answers come from. Dev comes back with: fine, so if we swapped information we'd swap sentences and both still be right? Then his sentence isn't about the agent or the slot, it's about the person saying it, and he doesn't believe "if the agent called, they got the slot" is a fact about himself. Priya says that's exactly her point — which is why her sentence isn't about her either. Then Dev goes for the kill: suppose we find out tomorrow that the agent did call, and Tidal Ash got the slot anyway. Then Priya's sentence is false, isn't it? And a sentence that can turn out false once we learn what happened is a sentence about the world, same as his, so she can't have it both ways. Priya says that only shows her sentence needs the call not to have happened, which was common ground, and needing something to be true isn't the same as being about the speaker. They got nowhere.

I couldn't referee this and definately still can't, so can you go through it move by move and tell me who's right at each step — not who wins overall? I especially want to know: was Dev right that they can't both be right; was Priya right that his sentence is really about his evidence; was Dev right that this makes his sentence speaker-relative and therefore not really about the world; who's right about the twist where the agent actually did call; and does the fact that both sentences use the same word with the same antecedent settle anything at all? If either of them is quietly assuming something about how "if" works that they'd have to defend, flag it. And tell me what both of them are missing, because I suspect they're each half right and neither will admit it.
