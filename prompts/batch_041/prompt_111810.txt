I lead a small interpretability team at an AI lab, and I have a recurring problem I'd like you to solve. We publish an internal report every two weeks, and in it engineers write sentences like "the model represents the user's intent to ask for medical advice," or "this SAE feature is the model's concept of a particular landmark," or "the model has a representation of the board position." These sentences get read by people making deployment decisions, and I've realized that none of us can say when one is warranted. Some of our claims rest on a linear probe decoding something from a hidden state; some on a feature that fires cleanly on the right examples; some on a steering vector that makes a behaviour more likely; some on activation patching that removes a behaviour. I have no philosophy background and I don't want a lecture. I want a procedure.

Act as a philosopher of cognitive science who works closely with ML practitioners and knows the technical interpretability literature. Before writing the procedure, spend a short paragraph brainstorming: list the distinct things a person might mean by "the model represents F," and mark which of them are empirically decidable and which are not.

Here is the conceptual material I want operationalized, since my team won't know the literature. I understand that at least three separate ideas get run together in these claims. (1) A state carries information about F in the ordinary correlational sense: it predicts F. (2) A state is used: downstream parts of the network read it, so intervening on it changes what the model does. (3) A state has F as its content, in the sense that F is what it is about, not merely what it correlates with. I have also been told there are rival views about (3): that content is fixed by the causal correlation having been stabilized by whatever shaped the system — evolution for brains, gradient descent for networks — together with downstream parts of the system being built to exploit that correlation; that content is fixed by nothing inside the system at all, and "represents" only marks what an interpreter is licensed to say; and that "represents" is a useful fiction that earns its keep only if it improves prediction or control. I don't need you to settle which view is right. I need you to tell me which of them a given piece of evidence could support, and to be explicit about where the choice among them changes the procedure.

Write the procedure as an ordered list of steps a competent engineer with no philosophy training can run in an afternoon on a single claim. For each step give: the question to ask, what counts as a sufficient answer, what evidence fails the step, what the step is vulnerable to (probe capacity, label leakage, superposition, off-distribution steering, the interpreter's own assumptions), and whether it is a judgment call dressed up as a procedure. Say where the procedure stops with a definite no, and where it hands the claim off to a human. Include a control or null step for probes and for features. Then specify a ladder of claim strengths, at least three rungs, with the exact wording our engineers are permitted to write in the report at each rung, and the wording that is never licensed.

Run it on three cases and show your work. (a) An SAE feature that fires on images and text mentioning a particular landmark; its top activations are clean, and a steering vector increases landmark-related output, but ablating it changes nothing on any task we care about. (b) A probe on a middle-layer hidden state that decodes "user is asking for medical advice" at 93% accuracy, where a probe of the same capacity trained on shuffled labels also does well, and patching the probed direction leaves refusal behaviour unchanged. (c) A probe we want to use to gate deployment because it supposedly detects the model concealing information: it is decodable, causally effective under patching, and trained on a small hand-labelled set. I expect the procedure to give an answer on (c) that my safety team will not like.

Finally, state plainly what the procedure cannot do, and whether a team that adopted the interpreter-based view of content rather than the gradient-descent-and-consumer view would be running a different procedure or the same one.
