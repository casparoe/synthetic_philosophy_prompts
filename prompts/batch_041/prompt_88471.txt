I have been writing up my own position for a seminar on the ethics of rescue and the allocation of scarce medical resources, and I have reached the point where I need to know whether what I have is a position or just a set of commitments that have never been made to argue with one another. Here is the set as I would state it. (1) Where I can save one stranger on one side and five strangers on the other, and nothing else distinguishes them, the fact that there are five on one side is not by itself a reason to go to that side: each of those deaths is a loss to the person whose death it is, and I do not think six losses of that kind sit on a single scale on which five of them outweigh one. (2) Even so, I take saving the five to be permissible, and I would not fault the person who does it; the single person has no complaint that would make it wrong. (3) If I had to be the one choosing, I would toss a coin, one call for the single person and one for the five together, because that seems to me the only procedure that treats them with equal concern. (4) But when the same choice is made in advance by a committee writing a policy rather than by a person standing at the tunnel mouth — the surge plan for intensive care beds, say — I think equal concern requires a lottery among the individuals, so that each of the six has a one-in-six chance, rather than a procedure that hands the single person a one-in-two chance. (5) I also think that at the level of whole policies the numbers plainly do count: a disaster that kills a hundred people is worse than one that kills ten, and I would fund the programme that saves a hundred lives over the one that saves ten, without any embarrassment about it. (6) And I do not take any of this to be disturbed by partiality — I would save my own child over any number of strangers, and I do not need an aggregative story to license that.

What I want from you is an audit rather than a summary of the literature. Tell me which of these actually pull against one another, which only look as though they do, and, for the real ones, what the repair would cost — which commitment has to move and what I lose by moving it. Be especially careful with the fairness commitments in (3) and (4): if the coin toss and the individual lottery cannot both be resting on the same idea of equal concern, say which of the two the rest of the package can keep, and say what the other one was quietly doing for me. Give it back to me as exactly five numbered items, one or two sentences each, ordered from the tension that costs me most to the one that costs me least, with no introduction and no closing summary.
