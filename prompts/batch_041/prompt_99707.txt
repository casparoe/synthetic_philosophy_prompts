I am a third-year PhD student working in formal epistemology, and I have spent the better part of a semester reading around the literature on Jeffrey conditionalization for a seminar paper. What keeps striking me is how little the exchange has moved in decades, even though everyone involved is arguing in good faith and mostly agrees on the mathematics. On one side sits the position that when what you learn is not a proposition you come to be certain of, but rather a redistribution of credence across the cells of some partition, the correct response is to shift your probabilities over the cells of that partition while leaving the conditional probabilities within each cell exactly as they were; ordinary conditionalization then emerges as the limiting case in which one cell goes to one. On the other side sits a cluster of objections that never quite seems to get answered: that the rule is incomplete because it is silent about how the partition is to be chosen, so that the same total evidence can be represented by different partitions yielding different posteriors; that successive applications on different partitions need not commute, so that the order in which you take in the same items of uncertain evidence can change where you end up; that the rule simply assumes, rather than derives, that your conditional credences within each cell remain fixed through the update, which looks like precisely the sort of thing an update rule ought to be justifying; and, most fundamentally, that there is no such thing as genuinely uncertain evidence in the first place, since whatever you learn there is always some proposition you become certain of, whether about your experience or about the evidence itself, so that the supposed generalization is either a notational variant of conditionalization or an attempt to model something that does not exist.

Nobody I have talked to regards any of these objections as decisive, and nobody regards the standard replies as decisive either, and the longer this goes on the more I suspect the dispute is a case of two groups answering different questions under a single heading: one side asking what a rational agent's credence function ought to be at a time, given a partial specification of its inputs, and the other asking how credences ought to be revised in response to an experience, with the two coming apart exactly where the word evidence is ambiguous between a proposition and an experiential state with no propositional content. My advisor thinks this is too neat, and that the real crux is a disagreement about what an update rule is supposed to be in the first place, a constraint on sequences of credence functions or a psychology of learning. I would like you to diagnose the impasse rather than adjudicate it. Is this a substantive disagreement about the norms of belief revision, a verbal dispute about the word evidence, a disagreement about whether experience has propositional content at all, or two distinct questions sharing one name? Take my hypothesis seriously enough to say where it fails. Then, given your diagnosis, tell me what would actually count as progress: a distinction, a formal result of the kind that would settle whether order-invariance or the fixed-conditional-probabilities requirement is forced by some independent desideratum, or an argument that the debate should simply be abandoned as ill-posed. I want the reasoning behind the diagnosis, not a survey of who said what; I already know, roughly, who said what, and I am not asking for a reading list.
