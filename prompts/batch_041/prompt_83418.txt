I teach a third-year seminar on merit, resentment and the culture of success, and I'm trying to build a handout that gets students to locate themselves on the question rather than to recite the positions. What I want from you is a decision tree — a diagnostic instrument, not a survey of doctrines — that begins from the sort of ordinary reaction anyone might have and ends at a named position on whether the successful should be celebrated or cut down. The contrast I keep failing to explain to my students is the one between the American Dream, on which the successful are held up as exemplars and emulation is more or less a civic duty, and the Law of Jante — the ten rules that Aksel Sandemose formulated in his 1933 satirical novel A Fugitive Crosses His Tracks, set in the fictional Danish town of Jante, whose refrain is that one is not to think oneself anything special, or as good as us, or cleverer, or more important, or better informed — together with the Australian and New Zealand habit of speaking of the tall poppy that must be cut down. I don't want a reading list or citations; I'll add the references myself.

Build the tree out of the logical space rather than out of a roll-call of names, and make every node a question a student could answer about a concrete case without already knowing which position it leads to. The distinctions I care about, roughly in order of how much work I want them to do, are these: whether the attitude is aimed at another's good fortune as such or only at undeserved good fortune (Aristotle's triad is the hinge — envy at any good fortune, righteous indignation at undeserved good fortune, and malicious glee at another's misfortune, of which only the middle term is a virtue); whether the good in question is positional, so that one person's gain is genuinely another's loss, or not, since a levelling norm looks very different in the two cases; whether the norm being enforced is a claim of justice, a demand of solidarity, or merely a demand of conformity; whether the target is the individual, the practice that produced them, or the culture of admiration itself; whether the question is what one may feel, what one may say, or what one may do to someone, since teasing and coldness are one thing and ostracism and sidelining quite another; and whether the underlying diagnosis is the Schelerian one, on which the impulse to cut others down is ressentiment — a value-illusion bred of repressed impotence rather than a perception of injustice — or the contrary one, on which the pain at another's advantage is tracking something real. I also want the admiring side of the ledger represented, not just the envious one: admiration is not a neutral act if it is a way of latching onto genuine value that shapes what we become, so there is a serious position on which celebrating the successful is a duty of the community rather than a vice. Please make sure the following appear among the leaves, named or marked 'unnamed': the Aristotelian indignation view, the ressentiment diagnosis, the egalitarian defence of levelling, the exemplarist or emulation view, the Rawlsian thought that the social bases of self-respect are the primary good at stake, and the view that the whole matter is a matter of private taste with no normative upshot.

Concretely, I'd like seven to nine branching questions and nine to twelve leaves, each leaf carrying the name of the position (or 'unnamed'), one sentence on its most awkward commitment, and a note of any combination of answers that the tree leaves unoccupied, with a brief diagnosis of whether nobody is there because the view is incoherent, unattractive or merely unexplored. Then run these cases through it: a Norwegian office in which a colleague who announces a national prize is met with silence and later with the private remark that she is 'a bit much'; an Australian school where the student who tops the year is nicknamed 'the professor' and teased for trying too hard; an American commencement address that holds up a self-made entrepreneur as the model the graduates should emulate; a prize-winner who opens her acceptance speech by insisting that she does not deserve the award; a company that quietly sidelines its best salesperson because the team 'works better without a hero'; a family that discourages a child from applying to a distant university because nobody from here does that; the Athenian assembly voting to ostracise a general whose popularity seemed to threaten the city; and a bereaved friend who cannot bear to hear about someone else's promotion — pain at another's good fortune that plainly is not resentment. Return the whole thing as a single JSON object with four fields, in British English throughout: 'questions', a list of objects with 'id', 'question' and 'answers', where each answer has 'answer' and 'leads_to' (the id of the next question or of a leaf); 'positions', a list of objects with 'id', 'name', 'path' (the answers that reach it), 'core_claim', 'awkward_commitment', 'tradition' (a thinker or school if one obviously fits, otherwise 'unnamed'), 'occupancy' ('occupied' or 'unoccupied') and, where unoccupied, 'diagnosis'; 'cases', a list of objects with 'case', 'lands_in' and 'why', plus 'where_the_tree_strains' where the case does not sit comfortably; and 'blind_spots', a list of objects with 'positions' and 'why_the_tree_cannot_separate_them'. Where the tree has to force a case, say so rather than forcing it.
