Thomas Szasz's enduring claim is that mental illness is a myth: that the conditions grouped under that heading are better understood as problems in living, sorted and identified by reference to social norms and personal values rather than by any lesion in the body. The standard rejoinder from psychiatry and from mental-health advocacy is an analogy. Mental illness, on this view, is an illness like any other — no different in kind from a broken leg or from diabetes. The brain is an organ; like any organ it can malfunction; and a malfunctioning brain is no more a moral failing than a fractured tibia. The analogy does heavy lifting: it is used to argue that psychiatric conditions deserve the same seriousness, the same insurance coverage, the same research funding, and the same exemption from blame that we grant a broken leg, and that the stigma attached to them is a prejudice of the same order as blaming someone for a fracture. A psychiatrist friend recently deployed it as a conversation-ender when I mentioned Szasz, and I want to know whether it actually settles the matter.

I am not asking for a survey of the debate. I want an audit of the analogy, and of how much weight it can bear.

1. Lay out the correspondence. What on the psychiatric side plays the role of the injured organ, the lesion or structural abnormality, the diagnosis, the treatment, the sick role, and the patient's non-responsibility — and does each have a genuine counterpart, or is a slot left empty?

2. Locate the break points. A broken leg has a detectable structural cause, a clear deviation from species-typical functioning, an identifiable mechanism, and a dysfunction that the person affected will almost always want removed. Go through each of these and say which are matched and which are not by a diagnosis such as depression or schizophrenia. Call out any feature of the source that the analogy quietly imports into the target.

3. Consider the dilemma the analogy seems to generate. If 'illness' means biological dysfunction — a failure of some mechanism to perform its natural function — then conditions identified by distress and social deviance may fail to qualify, and the analogy collapses into Szasz's conclusion rather than refuting it. If instead 'illness' means whatever we collectively decide to call an illness, then the analogy has presupposed the normativity Szasz claims is doing all the work. A familiar middle path defines a disorder as a harmful dysfunction: a real dysfunction of a naturally selected mechanism combined with social harm. Does that middle path rescue the analogy, relocate the dispute, or merely rename it? Give your own assessment of which horn, if any, the analogy is stuck on.

4. Finally, set the analogy aside and ask whether the conclusion it was used to support — that psychiatric conditions warrant the same seriousness, coverage, and non-blame as physical ones — can be defended on independent grounds. If it can, say what those grounds are. If it cannot, say what is lost. And say whether a better analogy exists, or whether this part of the debate is better conducted without one.

Throughout, keep separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who work on this, and do not present a contested position as if it were the consensus. Flag anything you are unsure about rather than smoothing it over.
