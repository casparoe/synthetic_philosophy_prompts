I'm the TA for an upper-level course that mixes rational-choice theory with the judgment-and-decision-making literature, and I've been handed the final because the instructor is on leave, so the essay question is on me. The unit I'm covering is the dollar auction -- the one where a dollar bill gets auctioned off, bids go up in small increments, and both of the top two bidders have to pay what they bid, the loser getting nothing -- and every year I get the same essay back: "the dollar auction shows the sunk cost fallacy and escalation of commitment, people throw good money after bad, and that's basically why wars and strikes and lawsuits happen." I want a question that definately kills that essay. My own hunch, which I don't want to just hand them, is that the pat diagnosis is wrong in an interesting way: at the moment the trailing bidder is deciding whether to go 5 cents higher, their earlier bids aren't sunk at all -- they're contingent liabilities that only come due if they stop, so the marginal reasoning that carries them up ("pay a nickel more to avoid eating a dollar") is actually correct forward-looking reasoning, and the real failure has to be located somewhere else. Where exactly is the interesting part, and I want them to have to find it.

So here's what I need. First, the exam question itself, written the way it would appear on the paper: closed-book, 45 minutes, one essay, no sources available and none expected -- so no "discuss the literature," no citations, no reading list, nothing they'd need to look up. Second, and this is the part I actually care about, I've built a variant that I want the question to turn on, and I need you to work it in cleanly: bids are hard-capped at $2.00 with no exceptions, ties are settled by a coin flip with both tied bidders still paying, and before bidding opens the two players get sixty seconds to talk to each other on the explicit understanding that nothing either says is enforceable. What I want students to work out is (a) whether the cap defuses the trap or just relocates it, and (b) whether the fact that both players would happily pay a small fee *ex ante* to be forbidden from bidding tells us the escalation is a psychological bias at all, or instead a commitment/coordination failure that would afflict perfectly rational agents -- and if it's the latter, what that does to the usual "people are irrational" moral of the story. Third, a model answer of about 650 words, and a marking scheme that names the five or so moves a top answer has to make, what each is worth out of 20, and -- the bit students always ask about -- the specific wrong turns that should cost marks: calling the trailing bidder irrational for bidding on, treating the ceiling as a solution rather than a relocation, reciting "escalation of commitment" as if the label did any work, or listing litigation and strikes and arms races without saying what the structural parallel is supposed to be.

One more thing: end the model answer with a short honesty note in its own paragraph, saying how confident you are in each of the main claims and singling out the single claim you'd least want to defend if a sharp student pushed back on it. They read the model answer after the exam and I'd rather it wear its soft spots on its sleeve than pretend the analysis is airtight. And please don't give me a reading list or a "see also" -- they can't look anything up and I don't want them thinking there's a source out there that settles this.
