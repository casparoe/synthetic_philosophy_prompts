Hey, I'm workshopping a short essay and I want some blunt comments on it — mainly on whether the central distinction is actually doing any work, or if I'm just relabeling stuff people already say.

The essay is about attributing beliefs to LLMs. Instead of fighting about whether the model "really" has beliefs, I split belief-ascription into three kinds. (1) Predictive ascription: you say "it thinks the capital of X is Y" as pure shorthand for what the weights will spit out. (2) Inferential ascription: you treat the system as having a belief because the belief/desire apparatus earns its keep — it lets you predict what the thing will do in new cases, including cases where it's been fed something misleading. (3) What I'm calling commissive ascription (stealing the speech-act word): you ascribe a belief in order to hold something answerable for it, the way we do with someone who testifies.

My claim is that almost all the heat in the LLM debate is about (2), but the actual stakes sit in (3), and that (3) is where the intentional stance quietly stops being a predictive tool and turns into a liability-shifting device. If the thing has beliefs, then the belief is *its*, and whoever deployed it gets to take a step back. So belief-talk about models may be doing something closer to insurance than to science. I think thats the part that would annoy people, which is a good sign, but I dont know if it survives contact.
