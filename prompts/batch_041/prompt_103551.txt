I teach a 90-minute grad seminar in philosophy of mind / cognitive science, about 18 students, and this term I'm doing the Darwinian-Skinnerian-Popperian-Gregorian ladder (Dennett's four grades of creature). Every year I lecture it and every year it lands flat -- they can recite the four levels back at me and none of them have any feel for what actually separates them. I want to fix that with an activity instead. The thing I most want them to feel is the jump from the Skinnerian to the Popperian: the difference between a creature that has to actually do the wrong thing and pay for it, and one that can run the wrong thing in its head and let the mistake die there instead of dying itself. And then the jump to Gregorian, where the creature starts using an external tool and the tool is doing part of the thinking, so you can't cleanly say who or what solved it anymore.

Design me a runnable activity. I'm thinking four rounds, one per grade, on a single puzzle the environment holds the answer to -- my default is a locked box with a hidden 3-digit code and a warmer/colder signal, but swap it for something better if you have it. Rules, materials (cheap, I have a copier and a bag of candy), a minute-by-minute timeline, roles if I need them. The rules have to be arranged so they walk into each transition themselves rather than me announcing it. Round 1 they should get one attempt, no feedback, and only improve across generations by copying whoever succeeded -- that's the whole Darwinian point. Round 2 they can try and try but each real attempt costs them something. Round 3 they get a spec sheet of partial constraints and are allowed unlimited free attempts on scratch paper -- the failed attempts have to be free, or it collapses into round 2. Round 4 they get to build and keep a tool (a grid, a notation, a lookup table) and I want a mediocre solver with a good tool to beat the smartest one without it, so the debrief has something to point at.

Then a debrief that maps each round onto the actual distinction, what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it (I assume the Gregorian round turns into just teamwork unless I forbid talking -- tell me how), and be straight with me about which part of the idea a single 90-minute session just cannot reproduce and that I'll have to supply myself.

Also, tell me how confident you are in the design choices you're recommending, and flag the one point in the whole thing you're least sure about.
