I am a junior at a high school of about nine hundred students, and I sit on the committee that plans our monthly Philosophy and Science Club meetings. We are roughly twenty students drawn from mixed grades, we meet for a single ninety-minute block in an ordinary classroom, we have no computers beyond my own laptop, and our budget extends no further than what the school copier will tolerate. Next month's session is mine to run, and I would like to build it around a problem that came up while I was reading about Malthus for a history assignment and that I have not been able to settle by thinking about it on my own: a prediction can fail and still be aimed at something real, if the thing it predicted simply has not had time to happen yet. What interests me is not that such theories exist, but that at the moment of failure we cannot tell a theory that was wrong from a theory that was early, and the difference only becomes visible to someone who keeps the theory alive long enough to find out.

Please design that session. The group should run into the problem themselves, through the rules, before anyone names it. I do not want an opening lecture, and I would rather the word Malthus not come up until the debrief. What I want them to come away with is a felt sense of two things: that a failed prediction does not by itself settle anything, and that the cost of holding a theory that turns out to have been early is paid by whoever holds it.

My rough plan, which you should repair or replace if it is broken. The club splits into five or six teams. Each team receives the same one-page theory card, a claim that one quantity grows faster than another and that the second will eventually be overtaken, and plays eight rounds against a facilitator-run generator made of dice and a table I keep behind a folder. Before each round every team writes a dated prediction. Only predictions that come true inside the eight rounds score. The generator is set so that the crossing genuinely occurs, but at roughly round fourteen, outside the window; the teams holding the card therefore finish the game with a losing record, while the teams holding the rival card finish with a winning one. I want each team to start with ten tokens, with correct predictions paid out of a common pot and extensions of the horizon purchasable for tokens, so that being right about the long run is expensive and being wrong about the short run is cheap. My one addition, which I think is the part worth keeping: an extension is granted only if the team writes down, before the next round is revealed, what evidence would make them abandon the theory. I suspect that rule is what separates patience from cheating, and I would like the activity to test the suspicion.

What I need from you: the facilitator's rule sheet; a materials list, including what to print and how many copies, and what the cards and ledger sheets actually look like; the hidden generator table, arranged so that the long-run crossing is genuine within the fiction rather than merely asserted; the full scoring scheme in tokens, with the awkward cases resolved, such as a prediction that is neither confirmed nor refuted before the buzzer; a minute-by-minute timeline for the ninety minutes including setup and cleanup; the debrief questions in the order I should ask them, each tied to a specific moment in the game; the predictable failures on a first run and what I should do when each happens — I expect token hoarding, at least one team that refuses to predict anything, and at least one accusation that I rigged the generator, which will be true in a sense I need help explaining; and a frank account of what the activity cannot reproduce. On that last point I am especially interested in the limit. Inside the game the answer is decided by my hidden table, whereas in the world outside it nobody has the table, so the facilitator has to supply whatever the game leaves out. Tell me precisely what that is, and what I should say in the final ten minutes so the session does not end in the conclusion that all long-run predictions are a matter of taste.

No reading list, please; nobody will read it. If a name or a historical episode is worth attaching in the debrief, mention it there, but the session itself must not depend on students recognizing it.
