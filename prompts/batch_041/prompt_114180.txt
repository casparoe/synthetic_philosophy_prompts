I teach a graduate seminar in decision theory and the philosophy of probability, and I am writing the final exam. I want a single essay question on the St. Petersburg paradox that a candidate cannot answer by reciting the classical resolution — the familiar move of introducing a concave utility function such as the logarithm of total wealth, showing that the game's expected utility now converges, and declaring the paradox dissolved. Every student in the room will have that move memorized, and the question has to force them well past it.

The question should make them hold three things in view at once. First, that the paradox has horns that are routinely conflated: the arithmetic fact that the game's expected monetary value diverges, the descriptive fact that almost no one will pay more than a few dollars to play, and the separate normative question of whether refusing a large entry fee is genuinely rational. Second, that each familiar resolution buys a finite expected value at a price, and the question must make candidates name the price. The log-utility resolution assumes a utility function concave enough to tame the tail; push them to see that any resolution of this shape must either bound the utility or shrink the tail probabilities, and to say what each commitment costs elsewhere in the theory — what bounded utility implies about an agent's attitude toward arbitrarily large stakes, and what discounting small probabilities implies about the large-stakes, low-probability risks that expected utility theory is otherwise built to take seriously. Third, that the paradox can be rebuilt against the resolution: I want the question to require them to explain why no unbounded utility function escapes the problem, since for any such function one can design a lottery whose expected utility is itself infinite, and then to say whether this shows that the classical resolution is a real solution or only a redescription of the refusal to play.

Fold in one concrete exercise so the answer cannot stay at the level of labels. Ask them to work out what becomes of the game when the casino's reserves are finite and the largest prize it can pay is therefore capped, to show how the fair price depends on the size of that ceiling, and to say what this does to the claim that the paradox is about rationality rather than about the unrealistic premise of an infinitely rich house. Finally, make them take a side on the deepest question: whether the paradox is evidence that expected value is simply the wrong decision rule, or only that the rule must be applied to utility rather than to money — and defend that side against the obvious objection that the second answer can be made to fit any observed behavior after the fact.

Write the question itself, in the register of a serious exam; then a model answer of about 800 words; then a marking scheme. For the marking scheme I want named must-hit moves, the marks each is worth, totaling 25, and for every move a one-line note on the specific error that should cost a candidate the marks — the way candidates typically get that move wrong. The question should be answerable in 90 minutes and must not require any reading beyond the course.

One constraint on the deliverable: write everything in continuous prose. No headings, no numbered lists, no bullet points, no bold or italics, no tables. Just the question, the model answer, and the marking scheme as flowing paragraphs, the way I would write them out in an email.
