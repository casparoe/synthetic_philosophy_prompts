As a retired natural scientist who has lately taken up philosophy, I have been reading about common knowledge and the epistemic foundations of game theory, and I should like your help with a satirical piece. Please draft a mock staff handbook — the kind of relentlessly upbeat 'ways of working' document that a large firm circulates on a new employee's first morning — for a company that has adopted common knowledge of rationality as its official decision procedure. Roughly 650 words, in numbered clauses, deadpan from beginning to end, and never letting on that any of it is a joke.

The humour must come from the theory being correct rather than merely name-dropped. So the handbook should distinguish mutual knowledge from common knowledge without ever pausing to explain the distinction as though to a novice; should treat the staff canteen queue as a backward-induction problem; should justify the retention of one conspicuously irrational employee on the grounds that a threat is credible only if somebody might actually carry it out; should present the annual fire drill as a pure coordination problem, solved by prior agreement on a meeting point; and should devote a clause to the awkward fact that common knowledge of rationality, even where it genuinely obtains, leaves many games with several equilibria, so that some further principle is needed to select among them.

What I am satirising is the habit — which I confess is mine as well — of assuming that everyone's rationality is common knowledge and that this by itself settles what will happen. Keep the piece legible to a reader who knows no game theory, while allowing a specialist to see that the underlying points are sound.
