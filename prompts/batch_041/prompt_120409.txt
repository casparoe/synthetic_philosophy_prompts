My friend and I just spent an evening arguing about whether it is ever legitimate to infer what people believed from the objects they left behind, and I came away convinced that most of the belief-talk in our museum labels is doing something philosophically shady. I run a small archaeology unit: six field archaeologists, a two-week excavation season, and a standing obligation to produce interpretive labels and site reports for a local museum. We are not philosophers, we have no texts from the periods we work on, and every public claim about ritual, religion, or institutions has to be signed off by me and by a descendant-community liaison. A wrong call can end up in a museum display or a repatriation dispute, so I need a reusable decision procedure for moving from an assemblage to a claim about belief or institution, and I need to be able to run it in an afternoon on a new assemblage.

Please write it as a sequence of questions to ask in order, with what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops with a no, and where it hands the decision to a person because the remaining question is one of values or public stance rather than evidence. Build it around a distinction I have been trying to make precise: between what an object's manufacture, wear, and deposition causally imply about what people did, and what it merely suggests about what anyone meant or believed. I have been calling these the action-side and the meaning-side of an assemblage, and I want that distinction to survive the simplification. The procedure should say where it will misfire.

Then run it on two cases: a rockshelter cache of ochre pieces with engraved geometric marks, and a cemetery where some bodies were buried with imported shells and worn tools. In at least one case, I expect the procedure to tell me something I do not want to hear. Finally, say what the procedure cannot do -- what kind of inference about past minds and institutions no amount of procedural care will license.
