I'm writing a short essay for a game theory + ethics reading group arguing that the Kalai-Smorodinsky solution should be the default answer for two-party bargaining, and I'd like comments before I send it round. Not comments on the literature — I know the papers, dont worry about refs — I want to know whether the argument actually holds up.

Setup, in case you need it: a two-person bargaining problem is a convex set S of feasible payoff pairs plus a disagreement point d in S (what each side gets if talks collapse). Each player's ideal payoff is the best they can do in S; the ideal point is the pair of those. The KS solution is where the straight line from d to the ideal point leaves the feasible set; equivalently, it's the point that gives each player the same fraction t of their own stake, where your stake is your ideal minus your disagreement payoff. Nash instead maximises the product of the two players' gains over d. Standard teaching is that Nash is pinned down by independence of irrelevant alternatives and KS by a monotonicity condition (if the set changes so that at every level of your payoff I can do at least as well as before, I shouldn't end up worse), and that the two conditions can't both hold, so you pick a side.

Three claims I'd like stress-tested:

1. The real case for KS isn't the monotonicity axiom, it's "same fraction of your own stake". That's a genuinely unit-free idea — rescale my utility however you like, independently of yours, and nothing changes — whereas "equal absolute gains" isn't unit-free in that way.

2. The monotonicity axiom flatters KS. It only bites on expansions that are unambiguously good for one side, and it's completely silent about expansions that help both players or trade them off against each other. Narrow window, and I think the axiom's intuitive pull comes from how narrow it is.

3. The price of KS is that the answer depends on the ideal point, and the ideal point is only well defined relative to a fixed and commonly known S. If the anchor is instead just whatever aspiration the parties can get away with putting on the table, it's gameable: unit simplex, disagreement at the origin, ideal point (1,1), KS splits it 50/50; if party 1 can move the anchor to (2,1) the line from the origin runs flatter and their share goes from 1/2 to 2/3. Nash is immune, because it never looks at the ideal point. I want to say the fix is to treat the anchor as the outcome of a prior commitment game rather than as a max over S, but I can't make that precise and I suspect it comes out either circular or just Nash again.

So: which of the three is weakest? Is (3) a real objection or am I just noticing that KS needs the problem to be well specified? And does the "same fraction of your own stake" reading still sound fair when the stakes are lopsided — one player's ideal barely above disagreement, the other's miles above? Last thing, does any of this survive n > 2 players, or does the ray-to-the-ideal-point thing quietly stop doing what I want there?
