I am a software engineer by trade, with no formal training in philosophy. A friend who teaches a course on probability and evidence has asked me to help grade one of her problem sets, on the strength of my being the one person she knows who is comfortable with the algebra. I can follow the arithmetic in what follows, but I would rather not sign off on the reasoning without a second pair of eyes. Below are the problem, the point breakdown, and one student's answer.

**Problem (24 points).** Two urns are on a table. Urn X holds ten balls, eight red and two blue. Urn Y holds ten balls, four red and six blue. A fair coin toss selects one urn (heads for X, tails for Y) and you are not told which; you assign probability 1/2 to each. A single ball is then drawn from the selected urn and held up under a dim lamp. You cannot make out its colour with certainty, and after looking, your credence that the ball is red is 0.8 and your credence that it is blue is 0.2. Assume throughout that the look bears on the colour of the ball and on nothing else, and that the draw itself supplies no information beyond the colour.

(a) [8 points] What is your credence that Urn X was selected, after the look? State the rule you are using, justify it, and give your answer as an exact fraction.

(b) [8 points] State precisely the condition under which this rule is the appropriate response to the look, and say what it requires to remain fixed across the update.

(c) [8 points] Suppose that after recording your answer to (a) you look at the same ball a second time and again fail to reach certainty, this time ending up 0.5 confident that it is red and 0.5 confident that it is blue. Consider two ways of applying the rule to both looks: applying it with the 0.8/0.2 credences first and then with the 0.5/0.5 credences, and applying it in the reverse order. Do the two orders yield the same final credence in Urn X? Explain.

**Student's answer.**

(a) Let X be "Urn X was selected" and R be "the ball is red". Since the look leaves me uncertain about the colour, I update by Jeffrey conditionalization on the partition {R, ¬R}: for any proposition A,

P'(A) = 0.8·P(A | R) + 0.2·P(A | ¬R).

The quantities I need are P(X | R) and P(X | ¬R). The marginal is P(R) = (1/2)(0.8) + (1/2)(0.4) = 0.6, hence P(¬R) = 0.4. So

P(X | R) = (0.8)(0.5)/0.6 = 2/3, and P(X | ¬R) = (0.2)(0.5)/0.4 = 1/4.

Therefore P'(X) = (0.8)(2/3) + (0.2)(1/4) = 8/15 + 1/20 = 32/60 + 3/60 = 35/60 = 7/12 ≈ 0.583.

This passes the obvious checks. Had the look made me certain of red, my credence in X would be 2/3; had it made me certain of blue, 1/4; and 7/12 lies between them, which is what a weighted average of the two certainties should do. The look has moved me only modestly from my prior of 1/2, which is right for an 80/20 look on a question that was already 60/40 likely.

(b) The condition is rigidity. What has to remain fixed is the probability of the colour under each hypothesis, that is, P(red | X) and P(red | Y). Here those are fixed by the composition of the urns at 8/10 and 4/10, and my looking at the ball cannot change what is inside the urns, so the condition is met and the update is appropriate.

(c) No. Each application of the rule is a weighted average of ordinary conditionalizations, and ordinary conditionalization commutes: conditioning on E and then on F gives the same result as conditioning on F and then on E. So the two orders agree. Applying the second look on top of the first, P''(X) = (0.5)(2/3) + (0.5)(1/4) = 1/3 + 1/8 = 11/24 ≈ 0.458, and this is the value whichever look I take as arriving first.

**Your task.** Give the answer a grade out of 24, with 8 points for each of (a), (b) and (c), and then a point-by-point set of comments that justify that grade. For each part, say what the answer gets right, what it gets wrong, and, for anything wrong, what the correct claim or value is. I need comments specific enough to write on the paper and specific enough that the student can see exactly where credit was lost, rather than a general impression.
