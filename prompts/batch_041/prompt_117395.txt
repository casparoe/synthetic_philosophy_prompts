I am a retired marine scientist — forty-one years in marine geochemistry, most of it on sediment cores — and since I gave up the laboratory I have been reading philosophy in a fairly systematic way, with the regrettable consequence that I can no longer treat the old puzzle of the ship whose planks are replaced one by one as a parlour game. It has become a practical question about our own boat, and in three weeks I have to give the toast at our trust's fiftieth-anniversary dinner. Please write that speech.

The occasion. We are a small British charity, and our boat is a wooden one, built in 1912 as a working boat and bought by the trust fifty years ago this autumn. She takes parties of young people to sea from a harbour town on the south coast. The dinner is in a marquee on the quay, about a hundred and twenty of us: former crew and their families; trustees and donors, including the family who paid for the new boat; the county museum's maritime historian, who has spent twenty years on the conservation of our old planking; the curator; and our coxswain, who has sailed her for forty of the fifty years, is eighty-three, and has no patience with me. Three or four of the guests have read as much about identity over time as I have and will notice if I fudge the argument.

The facts, none of which are in dispute. She has been re-planked twice, most recently in our own 1998 rebuild, which replaced every plank, the deck, all the deck beams and all but eleven of her frames; what survives from 1912 is the keel, the stem, the sternpost, eleven frames, the mast step, the bell, the log books and the name. The shipwright who did the 1998 work kept the removed planking and held, loudly, that a boat is her keel and her name. A benefactor paid for that old planking to be reassembled over new frames, and the county museum has displayed the result since 2004 as the original boat; the curator's accession papers say so in writing. Our new boat is now in service and will take the name and the bell in the spring. This is the old boat's last season at sea; she will stay afloat afterwards for teaching. The underwriters, who are thorough people, have asked the trustees to insert a sentence in the policy defining what counts as the vessel for the purposes of the cover; the museum's papers already contain a different sentence.

What I need from the speech:

- About 750 to 850 words, five or six minutes aloud, in British English, written to be spoken rather than read.
- It is a speech, not an essay: it should greet the room, and it should end with the toast itself.
- The puzzle must carry the speech rather than decorate it. The speech has to say which of the claimants — the boat still sailing, the reassembled hull in the museum, the new boat taking the name and the bell — is the one we are toasting, and that answer must govern what the speech can and cannot say to the others. Do not leave it as a charming open question; a toast has to be to something.
- It should weigh at least three of the available criteria: continuity of material; continuity of form, plan and function; continuity of causal and institutional history, of which the name, the log books, the crew and the habit of going to sea in her are the evidence; the four-dimensional thought that her earlier and later stages are parts of one thing as much as her bow and stern are, so that the question which of them is really her is a question about how we carve rather than about what exists; and the austere view that strictly there are no boats at all, only timbers and fastenings arranged boatwise. If the speaker rejects that last one, he should say why without pretending it is silly.
- My own trade should earn its place. Tree-ring dating can give the felling date of a plank, and stable-isotope work can say roughly where the oak grew, so the question which timber is original is empirical and answerable — and answering it settles nothing about identity. The speech should use the difference between measuring a fact and deciding how to count as a load-bearing move. The underwriters' sentence and the curator's sentence are stipulations, and the speech should mark the difference between stipulating an answer and finding one.
- No names of philosophers, no -isms, no quotations from anybody. If a position cannot be put in plain words for the coxswain, it does not belong in the speech.
- No refuge in sentiment. It must not come to rest on the thought that she lives on in our memories, or that a boat is really the people who sail her, unless the speaker has argued his way there and is willing to pay what that costs: on that view the museum's hull is a heap of timber and the new boat is a stranger. It should be warm, and self-deprecating about a latecomer to philosophy, and the coxswain should be able to enjoy it.

Write the speech as it would be delivered.
