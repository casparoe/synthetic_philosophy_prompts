The question she set was:

"Popper argued that although we can never know a scientific theory to be true, we can still say that one theory is closer to the truth than another. Set out his account of verisimilitude: what it was meant to measure, how he defined it, and what became of it. Then say what, if anything, this tells us about whether science makes progress toward the truth."

(The two papers she'd set for that week were Tichý and Miller, both 1974, if that's relevant.)

And here's what the student wrote:

"Popper's starting point is that truth is the aim of science, but that we can never prove a theory true — all we can do is falsify it. So if science is to be rational and progressive, we need some way of saying that a theory which has been refuted is nonetheless closer to the truth than the one it replaced. That's what verisimilitude, or truthlikeness, is for. It's meant to be a comparative notion rather than a quantitative one: not a number, but an ordering, so that we can say A is closer to the truth than B without knowing whether either is true.

Popper's definition works through content. A theory's content is the class of statements it entails. Divide that into its truth content (the true consequences) and its falsity content (the false consequences). Then A is at least as close to the truth as B if B's truth content is contained in A's, and A's falsity content is contained in B's — that is, A says more true things and fewer false things. Popper thought this captured the pre-theoretic idea that a false theory can be an improvement on a worse false theory, which is what the history of science looks like.

The definition was shown to fail. Tichý and Miller showed in the 1960s that it has the consequence that no false theory can be closer to the truth than any other false theory: for false theories the two conditions can't come apart, so you can never add true consequences without adding false ones alongside them. Since all our theories are false, verisimilitude comparisons are impossible, and this is usually called the Tichý–Miller paradox.

It didn't kill the idea, though. Tichý came back with a redefinition, and Niiniluoto and Oddie built quantitative measures of truthlikeness, so the Popperian programme survived the objection.

What this shows, I think, is that Popper's attempt to have scientific progress without induction doesn't work. If no false theory is closer to the truth than any other, then science can't be said to make progress toward the truth, and all that's left is the corroboration of theories that survive testing. So verisimilitude ends up being just another name for how well corroborated a theory is, which is why his critics said he had smuggled induction back in through the back door."
