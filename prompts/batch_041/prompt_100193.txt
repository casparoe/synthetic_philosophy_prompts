I am a novelist. My trade has been to find sentences for the predicaments people usually live through without words, and I have long believed that the right sentence is a kind of clarity. I am writing because I am now inside a predicament I cannot sentence my way out of, and I want a verdict rather than sympathy.

Two years ago I was given a diagnosis that will most likely end my life within a few years, though the interval and the quality of that time are uncertain. I have a partner and a young child. My income has always been modest and irregular, and the illness has made it worse. What I do have is a life insurance policy, taken out years ago when I was healthy, whose face value would matter a great deal to my family, and whose premiums I will struggle to keep paying.

A firm has offered to buy the policy outright. They would pay me a lump sum now — a fraction of the face value, but more than I could ever pay in premiums, and real money in the hand while I can still put it to use. In exchange, they collect the full amount when I die. The representative was decent and unhurried; she said the point of her business is to help people unlock the value of an asset that would otherwise go to waste, and that if I decline, the policy may simply lapse and my family may end up with nothing.

The lawyers have told me it is legal; I am not asking about that. What I cannot settle is whether I should sign. My discomfort is not really about the money, which we need. It is that I would be selling, at a discount, the last thing I own that is worth anything, and handing a stranger a financial interest in the date of my death. When I imagine the fine print, I imagine someone who profits the sooner I stop being alive.

My closest friend is the least sentimental person I know. He says my hesitation is a taboo wearing the costume of an ethic — that the money is real, my revulsion is inherited superstition, and a dying man who refuses cash for his family because the arrangement gives him the creeps is indulging his own taste at their expense. He may be right. He is usually right about facts and rarely about feelings, and I can no longer tell which one this is.

I would like you to tell me whether he is right. If he is not, I want the argument that survives his objection, stated plainly enough that I could put it in the mouth of a character without flinching — not a slogan about dignity, but the reason this transaction is or is not one I should make. Two things in particular I would like you to address. First, does it matter that the buyer gains only by my death, or is that simply the shape of any insurance and not a moral problem in itself? Second, would your answer change if the contract barred the buyer from any contact with my doctors, my care, or my household — does that dissolve the objection, or only make it more comfortable to ignore? Please end with a verdict rather than a survey.
