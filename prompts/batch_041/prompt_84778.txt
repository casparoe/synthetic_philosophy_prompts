Take a case in which two variables are associated in one direction when the data are pooled and in the opposite direction within every stratum of a third variable — the reversal commonly called Simpson's paradox. Give an example, real or constructed, in which this reversal is an artefact of the partition chosen rather than evidence of a genuine causal reversal, so that one of the two comparisons is simply the wrong comparison to make. State the variables, the direction of the association at each level of aggregation, and the causal role of the stratifying variable: common cause of the two variables of interest, common effect of them, or an intermediate step on the causal pathway between them.

Then address the conceptual issue the example raises. Once the causal structure is fixed, is anything genuinely paradoxical left, or does the label survive only as a piece of folklore? Is the choice between the pooled and the disaggregated comparison settled by the causal structure, by the question one is asking, or by neither — and would the reversal survive if the stratifying variable were measured more finely, or cut into a different number of categories? Finally, say what your answer implies for the claim that reversals of this kind show aggregation to be illegitimate in general.
