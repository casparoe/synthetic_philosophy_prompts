A friend and I spent most of last night arguing about whether Zhuangzi's butterfly episode and Descartes' dream doubt are doing the same philosophical work, and we reached an impasse I cannot settle by myself. Her position is that the two are instances of one argument: since a dream can be indistinguishable from waking life, no belief resting on the senses is secure. My position is that this reading flattens something important, because the cases seem to differ in what they are even asking. Descartes, as I read him, wants to know whether he can be certain that he is not dreaming now, and the doubt is a stage on the way to finding something that cannot be doubted. The dreamer in Zhuangzi does not ask how to tell which state is real. He reports that he cannot tell whether he is Zhou who dreamed of being a butterfly or a butterfly now dreaming of being Zhou, and the episode ends not with a criterion but with a remark about the transformation of things.

Please compare the two, organized around three questions, and reach a verdict about how deep the similarity goes. First, what is actually in doubt in each case: the reliability of the senses, the existence of an external world, or the identity of the one undergoing the experience? Second, who is doing the doubting, and does each account require a stable doubter who persists across the states being compared? This is where the Zhuangzi episode strikes me as stranger than the Cartesian one: the same chapter opens with a figure who says he has lost himself, and then with the three pipes, human, earthly, and heavenly, which seem to account for the sounds of the world without assigning them to any single source, and I have never known what to do with the fact that the butterfly episode is the ending of that same chapter. Third, what is the doubt for? Descartes needs his doubt to be resolvable, or at least needs a route out of it; if the butterfly episode offers no route out, I want to know whether it remains skepticism in any useful sense, or whether it is better described as a claim about the boundaries of the self rather than about knowledge.

Two further things I would like addressed. My friend insists the episode is plainly a skeptical hypothesis and that any reading which makes it non-skeptical is wishful. Take that objection seriously and say what it gets right. And consider a case neither of us could handle: someone who wakes convinced that neither the dream nor the waking episode is more real than the other, yet goes on making breakfast and keeping appointments. Does either position have the resources to describe what that person is doing — doubting, living with an unresolved question, or something else entirely?

Please do not give me a survey of the literature. I want the argument itself, with both positions stated fairly and the verdict defended.
