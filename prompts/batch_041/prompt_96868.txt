ok so I'm a backend engineer, no philosophy training whatsoever, and I've been keeping a note on my phone called 'times I didn't do the thing' and I've realized my gut reactions to the entries dont line up with each other. I want you to tell me what principle is actually driving them, or if there isn't one, which of my reactions I should give up.

The list, roughly in order I wrote it:

1. Decided at 11pm to run at 6. Alarm goes off, I hit snooze four times. Lying there I knew running was the better call, I just didn't want to. This one I'm sure about, definately the textbook case.

2. Bought a $400 rowing machine, swore I'd use it daily, used it twice, sold it for $120. I don't think this is weakness of will at all. There was never a moment where I knew better and did worse, I just predicted my future self wrong. Bad at forecasting, not weak.

3. Stayed up till 3 watching a show, fully aware I'd be wrecked at work. Weakness of will, no question.

4. This is the one that wrecks my theory. Same 6am alarm, but this time when it goes off I genuinely no longer think getting up is best. I'm not fighting an urge, I've re-decided. Sleep is what's best for me right now and I mean it. I skip the run. And I feel worse about this one than about #1, like it's the purest failure on the list. But I didn't act against my judgment, so by my own rule it shouldn't count at all.

5. Told everyone I'd give 10% of my income to charity, then didn't. I don't feel weak-willed here, I feel like I never actually decided, I just said it out loud. So: no.

6. I used to fight myself about the gym constantly. Now I don't even want to go, and I skip it with zero conflict, no inner argument at all. This feels like a bigger failure than #1, but there's nothing to be weak against, so I can't call it weakness of will.

7. A friend of mine acts against his own clear judgment on something, he'll tell you he's doing the wrong thing while he's doing it, and everyone including me says that's not weakness of will, that's compulsion. But structurally it's the same as my #4: knows better, does worse, no fight. I can't say what's different except that his is worse.

8. A coworker showed me one of those money tests: $100 today or $120 in a month, I take the $100. Then $100 in twelve months or $120 in thirteen months, I take the $120. He says my preferences are 'time-inconsistent' and that this is called hyperbolic discounting and that it means at the moment of choice I'll basically always fold, even though the me of last week would've paid real money to stop me. I don't think that's weakness of will, it's just how I'm wired, but then if I'm wired that way I don't get why #4 is my fault.

So my rough theory was 'it's weakness of will when you know better and do worse', and the cases above eat it alive. Also a friend told me about a puzzle that I think is related but I can't say how: a billionaire will pay you a million dollars if, at midnight tonight, you *intend* to drink a vial of something that'll make you sick for a day but won't do any lasting harm, tomorrow afternoon. He stresses the money is in your account at midnight, and you're totally free to change your mind and not drink it. I said I'd take the money and just not drink the thing. He said you can't, the intention is what's being bought. I don't know if that's a trick or the entire point.

What I want: a principle, or a couple of competing ones, precise enough that I can run new cases through it. Tell me which of my verdicts it forces me to drop, and why that one rather than the others. And tell me what the principle can't handle at all, I'd rather see the edges than get a tidy answer that quietly skips #4 and #6.

No reading recommendations please, I won't read them. Just the principle and the cases.
