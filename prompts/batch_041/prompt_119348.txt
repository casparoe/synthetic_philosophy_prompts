For the last few weeks I've been meeting with two friends to work through some metaphysics of identity, and we have stalled on a single case: Black's two-spheres scenario. We agree on the setup — a universe containing exactly two spheres, qualitatively identical in every intrinsic respect, arranged symmetrically so that neither is 'the left one' or 'the center one' — and we agree that it is usually offered as a counterexample to the principle of identity of indiscernibles, on the grounds that the spheres are alike in every respect yet numerically distinct. Where we break down is over what happens once relational predicates are admitted. Each sphere bears a certain relation to the other sphere — a relation that, being irreflexive, no object bears to itself (think of 'is exactly d units from', with d greater than zero). One friend says this settles the matter: the spheres are discernible after all, and the counterexample collapses. The other says this is a cheat, since the same trick would render any two distinct objects discernible in any world, making the principle trivially and uninterestingly true.

I would like a careful conceptual explanation that lets me see precisely where their disagreement lives, rather than a survey of positions or any bibliography. In particular, I want you to explain (1) how the very notion of discernibility is relative to the expressive resources of the language used to describe the world, and whether that relativity makes the identity of indiscernibles a trivial logical truth, a substantive thesis about the world, or a malformed question; (2) whether invoking an irreflexive relation between the spheres can legitimately discern them, or whether it merely presupposes the numerical distinctness that the counterexample was meant to establish and is therefore circular; and (3) what the strongest surviving version of the counterexample looks like — the version on which the principle remains a genuinely contestable claim about the world rather than a feature of our vocabulary. Please make the distinctions sharp enough that I can tell which of my two friends is closer to the truth and why.
