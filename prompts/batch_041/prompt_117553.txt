I'm a senior software engineer who works on preference learning: I train reward models on pairwise human comparisons and then optimize against them. I have been invited to give the closing talk at a small interdisciplinary workshop on how machines should represent what people want, and I have no formal training in philosophy or economics — everything I know about utility I have picked up on my own — so I would rather be told precisely where I am confused than be politely humored.

The thesis I intend to defend has three parts. First, a system trained on choices can at best recover a disposition to choose: a ranking that predicts which of two options the people who generated the data would have picked. Second, everything we say about such a system's "utility function" should be cashed out in those terms, and the only target that survives the translation is what I call deference-consistency — the system's choices among the options a person will actually face should be the choices that person would endorse on reflection, given that person's own information and minus the distortions they would want removed. Third, any stronger claim, that the model has learned someone's true good or real utility, is not a finding about the data at all but a normative premise that gets smuggled in unnoticed by people who want the word "utility" to do welfare work.

The audience breaks into three groups. Four economists in the ordinalist tradition treat utility as nothing but a representation of choice and will think the reflection clause makes me a mentalist. Two behavioral economists who study preference reversals and menu effects will say the stable preference I am trying to recover does not exist, and that the choice data my models train on is generated by the interfaces we build. Two philosophers of well-being, one hedonist-leaning and one a desire-satisfaction theorist with objectivist sympathies, will say no choice-based measure can track welfare, since people routinely choose against their own good and adapt their desires to what they can get. My running example is a recommender that must choose between the item the user will click and the item the user will later say they were glad to have seen.

Write me a Q&A prep sheet: the objections I will actually get, grouped by camp and phrased the way that camp would phrase them; ordered by how much damage each does to the thesis rather than by how likely it is; for each, what I should concede, what I should hold, and a reply of about a minute; a note on where each reply is soft; what I should concede up front before the first question; and the one question I should be hoping nobody asks, together with what I would have to give up in order to answer it honestly.
