You are a philosopher of science with a specialty in formal epistemology and Bayesian confirmation theory, and you are unusually good at tracing what a set of commitments jointly implies. I am assembling a position statement for a workshop on uncertain evidence, and before I circulate it I want an audit rather than a summary. Please think carefully and work step by step: begin with a short brainstorming pass in which you restate, in your own words, what each commitment below actually commits me to and what it rules out; only then assess how they fit together. Do not simply flag every pair as "in tension" — part of the job is telling me which apparent conflicts are not real.

Here is the creed, roughly as I would put it to a colleague.

(1) Radical probabilism. There is no such thing as certain empirical evidence. Every genuine learning episode, perception included, is a transition from one subjective probability function to another; at no point does an empirical proposition arrive at probability 1.

(2) Probability kinematics as the general rule. When an experience bears on my opinions only by shifting the probabilities of a partition of evidential propositions, the correct response is to update by Jeffrey conditionalization: my new probability for any hypothesis H is the old conditional probability of H given each evidential proposition, weighted by my new probabilities for those propositions. Strict conditioning on a proposition is the limiting case where that proposition goes to probability 1.

(3) Order does not matter. If two agents start with the same credences and meet the same total evidence, differing only in the order in which they meet it, they should end up with the same credences. Which piece of evidence I encounter first is not a rational difference between us.

(4) The rule is justified dynamically. What makes (2) correct is a coherence requirement across time: an agent who updates in a way that violates probability kinematics leaves herself open to a sure-loss betting strategy, and that is the whole of the normative story about why the rule binds.

(5) The likelihoods stay put. When the evidence shifts my probabilities for the evidential propositions, it does not disturb my conditional probabilities for hypotheses given those evidential propositions; the old likelihoods remain fixed and only the evidential weights move.

(6) No privileged boundary between evidence and opinion. There is no principled distinction between "the evidence" and "my background opinions." Anything that rationally moves my credences counts as evidence, and my credences about my own evidence are themselves subject to revision.

(7) Logic and arithmetic are certain. I assign probability 1 to tautologies and to the conclusions of valid deductions from things I already accept, and I never revise those assignments.

I would like to know whether these seven can be held together, and I genuinely do not know which one is doing the damage. Please (a) rank the genuine tensions by severity rather than listing them as equally bad; (b) for each genuine tension, say precisely what would have to be surrendered and what that surrender costs, in terms of which other commitments it drags down with it; (c) identify the apparent conflicts that dissolve once the commitments are spelled out, and explain what makes them merely apparent; and (d) name the single cheapest revision that would clean up the most, and say whether the remaining package is still recognizably the position I started with or has become a different view.
