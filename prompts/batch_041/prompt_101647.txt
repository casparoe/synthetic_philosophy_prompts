I'm a second-year master's student in a field where funding is thin, and I've been shortlisted for a fellowship from a foundation set up by a retired executive whose name is on the building where I take classes. The shortlist comes with a condition I didn't expect. The final step is a 500-word statement explaining why I admire the man's leadership, and the committee has been blunt that they want real admiration rather than flattery — they claim they can tell the difference, and they say past finalists have been dropped for writing something that read like a job application. The stipend is $30,000, which is more or less the difference between finishing my degree and dropping out. The trouble is that I don't admire him, and not for lack of information: I've read a fair amount about the years he ran the company, and I think his celebrated cost-cutting playbook did real damage to the people who worked under it. What I keep circling is what the offer actually gives me a reason to do. I could grit my teeth and write the thing and hope my insincerity doesn't show; I could try to make myself admire him — read the profiles and the tributes, spend time with people who revere him, repeat the case until I half-believe it; or I could decline. And I can't tell whether the money is giving me a reason to *admire*, a reason to *try to* admire, or merely a reason to *say* I do, and whether calling it a reason to admire is even coherent when it doesn't make him any more admirable than he already was. A friend insists this is an old puzzle with a standard answer, but every version of the standard answer I've heard seems to slide past the part that bothers me.

There's a wrinkle that makes it worse. The committee lets fellows redirect the whole stipend to a hardship fund for undergraduates who can't cover lab fees, and apparently a surprising number do. I've been wondering whether that changes the character of the incentive: the money wouldn't be for me at all, so my reasons for wanting the attitude would be about someone else's tuition rather than my own survival, and I can't decide whether that makes the offer more respectable or exactly as bad, or whether the whole question of whose benefit it is was ever the point. There's a second wrinkle too: it's a reward rather than a threat. Nobody is holding anything over me — the worst that happens if I walk away is that I'm back where I started, poorer and degree-less. It seems like that should matter, and I can't say why it would.

I'd like your help thinking it through, as advice to me rather than as a survey — I don't need names or a reading list, just the distinctions that are actually doing work. Please write it in these sections, in this order: **The shape of the problem** (roughly 150 words, saying what the situation structurally is and what the competing readings of it are); **Two ways of taking the offer** (roughly 300 words, one on which the stipend does give me a reason to admire him and one on which it doesn't, with the strongest version of each); **What follows for what I should do** (roughly 200 words, including what the difference between a reason for an attitude and a reason for an action comes to in practice here); **If the money goes to the hardship fund** (roughly 150 words); and **Bottom line** (a short paragraph I can act on).
