I'm a philosophy PhD student in ethics, and I run a monthly workshop for about 16 graduate students and advanced undergraduates; our next 90-minute session should make the group feel the force of moral trade from the inside, before I ever name it. Design a runnable trading game. Give each participant a private portfolio of several causes, each carrying a different weight, arranged so that no two portfolios rank the causes identically and gains from trade exist only between people whose priorities diverge. Participants should be able to make binding, verifiable pledges — shifting a unit of effort or resources away from one cause and toward another — and score points by their own portfolio, with no real money and no common currency across players. Add a rule that a trade counts only if both parties can publicly state a justification the other would accept, so that the group can discover for itself whether some trades cannot be defended in that way. Provide the materials (portfolio cards, a pledge ledger), a timeline for the session, and a debrief that maps each phase onto the structure of the idea: the divergence-of-priorities condition, the role of commitment devices, and the objections the game will surface — integrity, the fear of corruption, and the suspicion that a commitment that could be traded away was never a commitment. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it (for instance, a coalition corners the market so that bargaining replaces trading, or one participant refuses all trades and stalls the room) and how to handle it, and be honest about which feature of the idea the activity cannot reproduce and that I will have to supply myself.
