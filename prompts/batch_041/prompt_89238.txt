I chair the physical-sciences review panel of a small foundation that makes blue-skies research grants, and I need to circulate a written recommendation before our meeting on the 14th. One proposal in this round has split the panel four to three, and I would rather give us a reasoned position than let the vote fall where it falls.

The proposal. A team of experimentalists wants to build an autonomous nanoscale device that measures a fluctuating property of a microscopic system, feeds that measurement back into the system's controls, and in this way extracts useful work from what is, on paper, a single thermal reservoir at uniform temperature. They present the device as one that runs on information as fuel, and they offer as the project's scientific payoff a test of whether the second law of thermodynamics constrains devices of this kind. The proposal also contains a smaller component: a computing element designed never to erase its intermediate records, on the reasoning that the thermodynamic cost commonly attributed to computation attaches to logically irreversible operations and above all to erasure (the familiar claim, associated with Landauer, that discarding a single bit costs on the order of kT ln 2), so that a machine which retains every record and can run its steps backwards on demand would in principle compute without that cost. The whole thing is offered as a modern descendant of the old thought experiment in which a being sorts a single molecule between the two halves of a box (what is usually called Szilárd's engine).

Where the panel divides. Three of us hold that the second law cannot be tested this way: the device is not a closed cycle that defeats the law, and whatever work comes out is paid for by the demon's own memory and measuring apparatus, or by the free energy the experimenters put into the initial state, or by the correlation built up between device and system, which is itself a physical resource that must be spent and then repaid. On that view the project will certainly work and certainly prove nothing. The other four reply that small systems genuinely behave differently, that fluctuation theorems permit entropy to fall over short times or in individual runs, so that an apparent violation is an empirical fact about the world rather than an accounting artifact, and that there is real content in pinning down where the cost actually appears. A third concern, which I find hardest to answer, is about the proposal's success criteria: as written they would be satisfied by an outcome everyone already expects, so we would be funding a demonstration whose result is fixed in advance.

What I need from you. A formal memo, two to three pages, with a header identifying it as a panel recommendation and the recommendation stated in the first paragraph. It should recommend a concrete course of action — fund, decline, or fund subject to conditions we can actually monitor — and give reasons the panel could defend in public. It should also do the following. First, say what would count as the project succeeding and failing scientifically, giving success criteria that are not already satisfied by the outcome everyone predicts. Second, take a position on whether a project of this kind can test the second law at all, or whether it can only illustrate the bookkeeping by which the law is preserved, and say what follows for how we should describe the project to our trustees. Third, say how we should treat the information-as-fuel framing: a substantive physical claim, a bookkeeping device, or a metaphor that ought to be struck from the proposal before we fund it. Fourth, engage the strongest version of whichever position you decide against, rather than a convenient caricature of it. Fifth, specify what the team must commit to including in its analysis — for instance a full accounting of the device's memory and of the correlations it builds up with the system, a stated bound against which the measured work is to be compared, and a description of the demon as an ordinary physical system rather than an intelligent agent standing outside physics — so that whatever they find will be interpretable by people who did not build it.

Two constraints. Do not include a bibliography or cite particular papers; I will add references myself. Argue from positions and considerations rather than from what any named paper or experiment is said to show, and keep the memo a decision document rather than a survey of the demon literature.
