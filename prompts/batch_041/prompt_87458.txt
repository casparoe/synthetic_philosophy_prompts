I'm grading a problem set for a 300-level choice-modeling seminar and I could use a second opinion on one answer. The students have had the linear-feature reward setup and max-ent IRL in class, plus the basic max-margin / feature-expectation-matching version, and nothing past that (no nonlinear reward classes, no guided cost learning, none of the deep-IRL stuff). Here's the question I set, then one student's answer.

QUESTION

Every morning a traffic app tells our commuter, for each of the two routes she can take, the estimated mean travel time μ and the estimated standard deviation σ (the app computes both from the last few days of traffic). She then picks a route. We have 400 mornings, and on each morning the app's numbers are different, because weather, construction and accidents move them around. The highway is usually slower on average but the app gives it σ ≈ 0; the back roads are usually a little faster on average with a much bigger σ.

We fit a linear-feature reward R(route) = w_μ · μ + w_σ · σ using max-ent IRL, so that on each morning the probability she picks a route is a softmax over the two routes' R values with temperature β. She takes the highway on 340 of the 400 mornings. The fit gives w_μ and w_σ both negative, with |w_σ| / |w_μ| ≈ 2.3. Across the 400 mornings, μ and σ are strongly positively correlated in the app's numbers (r ≈ 0.9).

(a) Does this fit license the claim that she would trade about 2.3 minutes of expected travel time to knock one minute off the standard deviation? Say what is and isn't identified here.

(b) With the same two features, a max-margin (feature-expectation-matching) model fits substantially worse than the max-ent model on a held-out set of 100 mornings. Does that show her choices are genuinely stochastic, i.e. that she really does randomize sometimes?

(c) A labmate says the σ term is carrying all the weight, so let's drop μ and keep only σ. What's the strongest thing you can say against that proposal? Does the r ≈ 0.9 fact help or hurt the labmate's case?

STUDENT'S ANSWER

(a) The ratio is the real content of the fit, and it does mean what the claim says. In a linear-feature reward only the direction of w is identified: multiplying w by any positive constant leaves the preference ordering and the softmax probabilities untouched, so w_μ and w_σ on their own are a convention but their ratio isn't. Max-ent actually gets us a bit more than the direction, since β is estimated jointly with w, and once β is in hand the scale of w is pinned down too, so the magnitudes aren't arbitrary either. Two caveats though. First, the number describes the fitted reward, not her preferences: if our features are wrong — if what she's really tracking is the chance of arriving after 8:45, and σ just happens to be correlated with that — then 2.3 is the slope of a projection of her behavior onto our basis, and a different basis would give a different number. Second, "she'd trade 2.3 minutes" sounds like a counterfactual about what she'd do if we actually rebuilt the roads, and the model only licenses it inside the model, at the margin, over the region of feature space we observed. Since r ≈ 0.9 we have almost no mornings where σ is large and μ is small, so the tradeoff is being read off a narrow slice.

(b) Yes. The max-margin model is deterministic — it picks the route with the higher recovered reward and always predicts that one — so on any held-out metric that rewards calibrated probabilities it's basically a straw man, and I'd say the comparison is somewhat rigged. But I don't think that's the whole story: 60 of the 400 mornings went to the slower-on-average route, and the temperature the fit chose is well above zero, which means her behavior isn't consistent with any deterministic reward in our class. So yes, her choices are genuinely stochastic; the softmax is a description of her decision rule, not just a smoothing device.

(c) The strongest objection is that the two features are nearly collinear in our data, which is what r ≈ 0.9 is telling us: the mornings where a route has a big σ are mostly the mornings where it has a big μ, so "μ is what matters" and "σ is what matters" make almost the same predictions and the σ-only model will fit nearly as well. So the proposal isn't unreasonable, but the labmate is drawing the wrong lesson from it. A near-equivalent fit under a reparameterization is a fact about our 400 mornings, not about which feature is doing the work in her head. To separate them you need mornings where the features come apart — a route that's slow but rock-steady, or one that's fast on average but erratic — and r ≈ 0.9 says we have almost none of those. So the honest answer is "these two weights aren't separately identified by this data," not "drop μ."
