I recently caught myself paying a premium I could not defend: I turned down an offer whose prospects I could only guess at in favor of one whose risks I could at least quantify, even though by my own lights the first was the better bet. The episode has left me preoccupied with ambiguity aversion, and I have found that most discussions of the Ellsberg case stop at the paradox itself without addressing the conceptual question underneath it. I would like you to explain that question rather than restate the puzzle.

To fix the case: an urn you know to contain fifty red and fifty black balls, and a second urn of a hundred balls in an unknown proportion of red to black. Most people prefer to bet on red drawn from the first urn, and also prefer to bet on black drawn from the first urn, rather than on the matching color from the second. Please make the following matters sharp.

First, separate risk, ambiguity, and outright ignorance from one another, and say precisely what the pair of preferences above violates — which requirement of expected utility it conflicts with, and why no single probability distribution over red and black can represent both preferences at once. Then distinguish violating a particular axiom from being irrational, since I suspect these get conflated.

Second, and this is the part I care about most, take a variant in which the composition of the unknown urn is itself fixed by a hidden fair coin: on heads it holds seventy red and thirty black, on tails thirty red and seventy black, with the outcome concealed from you. The reduction of compound lotteries yields a determinate fifty-fifty, identical to the known urn, so a strict expected-utility agent should be indifferent between the two. I want an account of what is being tracked if the aversion nonetheless persists, and, separately, whether the aversion is even coherent once the compound structure is laid bare.

Third, consider the same fixed-composition urn used across a long sequence of bets rather than a single one. Since the composition does not change, a long-run frequency exists and could in principle be learned; discuss whether the ambiguity has anything left to attach to, and how a one-shot framing should differ from a repeated one.

Fourth, briefly set out the leading ways theorists try to accommodate the preference — in plain terms rather than by name, for instance treating the agent's degrees of belief as failing to be additive, representing belief as a set of candidate probabilities with the agent evaluating the worst case, or locating the aversion in the act of comparing one's own state of knowledge against another person's. Say which of these you find most adequate and what each leaves unexplained.

Finally, tell me whether the aversion is something I should try to train out of myself or a legitimate response to a genuine gap in the standard account, and what turns on the answer. Skip the references; I want the conceptual reconstruction.
