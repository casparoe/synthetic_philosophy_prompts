I spent most of last night arguing with a friend about aesthetic testimony, and I've written up my position as a short essay. I'd like your comments on it — not a general reaction, but specifically on whether the central distinction I draw is a real one or merely verbal, whether my objection to the opposing view is fair, and whether the problem I raise at the end is fatal or survivable. I'd also like concrete suggestions for revision: places to cut, claims that need hedging, and any counterexamples I've missed. Please write in American English.

Here is the essay.

Title: Testimony Can't Tell You What's Beautiful — But It Can Tell You Where to Look Again

My friend holds what I'll call the acquaintance requirement: a judgment of taste is warranted only by first-hand experience. You cannot be told that a painting is beautiful and thereby come to know it; at best you learn a prediction about how you would react if you saw it. On this view, aesthetic testimony is not testimony at all in the epistemically interesting sense — it transmits no warrant, only a forecast. "I know it's sublime; I read a review" sounds like a category mistake, and the acquaintance requirement explains why.

I concede the diagnosis while rejecting the conclusion. The acquaintance requirement gets something right: testimony that a work is beautiful does not hand you the warrant for that judgment. If I have never heard the piece, my friend's assurance gives me grounds to expect a certain experience, not grounds to assert the aesthetic claim myself. The oddness of "I know it's beautiful, I read it in a review" is real, and I do not want to explain it away.

But the acquaintance requirement conflates two things testimony might do. Testimony can try to substitute for experience — to transmit the warrant, so that you may judge without having perceived. Call that the substitution function. Or testimony can redirect attention — giving you a reason to re-perceive, to look again under a description you had not tried. Call that the directive function. My friend's argument, at its strongest, rules out only the substitution function. It shows that you cannot borrow someone's eyes. It does not show that you cannot borrow their pointing finger.

The directive function is not a consolation prize. When a friend whose discrimination I trust says "look again at the third movement," I do not thereby acquire an aesthetic belief. What I acquire is a reason to re-engage: to attend to a feature I had passed over, to try the work under an aspect I had not considered. This is a genuine epistemic role, distinct from both warrant-transmission and mere causal influence. It is also, I think, how taste is actually cultivated. One learns to see the grace in a building because someone first insisted that it was there and that one had been looking at the wrong thing.

This has a payoff the acquaintance requirement cannot match. It explains why aesthetic disagreement can be productive rather than merely expressive: two people who cannot transfer warrants to each other can still transfer directives, and the directives can be assessed by whether the resulting re-engagement succeeds. It also explains why testimony from a person of demonstrated discrimination carries weight without being a warrant — we treat their say-so as a reason to look harder, not as a substitute for looking.

Here is the problem I cannot solve. What distinguishes a reason to re-attend from ordinary social pressure to agree? If my friend's "look again" is nothing more than a nudge toward conformity, the directive function collapses into conformism and I have lost the distinction I need. My best gesture at an answer is that re-engagement must be able to fail: the second look must be capable of returning the verdict that there was nothing there. A directive that cannot in principle be disappointed is not an epistemic directive at all. But I am not confident this condition does the work I need, and I suspect the friend would press exactly here.
