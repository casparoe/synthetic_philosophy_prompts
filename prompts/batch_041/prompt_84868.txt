A brain-preservation organization has asked to recruit dying patients from our palliative care service, and the clinical ethics committee I chair votes next month on whether to allow it. The procedure is destructive: the patient is anesthetized, the brain is fixed and imaged at very high resolution, and no one wakes up — not on the table, not afterwards. The organization's patient materials describe the outcome as the patient's survival; our neurologists describe the same events as the patient's death. Both descriptions will be in the room when a family is asked to consent, and we have to decide not only whether to approve the protocol but what the consent document may say.

My half-formed view is that we will deadlock if we argue about which theory of personal identity is correct, and that the deadlock is an artifact of treating identity as the thing consent tracks. Consent seems to need one continuing subject who can authorize a later event and who can be wronged by it. Here, if the patient does not survive, the authorizing subject is gone before anything can wrong her; and whoever exists afterwards has no complaint to make, since but for the procedure he would not exist at all — the same structure as the non-identity problem. If that is right, the real question is not whether the patient survives but whether this is something a patient can consent to in the first place, or only something a proxy can authorize on behalf of a person who does not yet exist and cannot be consulted.

Please draft the written opinion I will circulate before the vote: a clear recommendation with conditions attached, in a form the committee can act on, with a date by which the sponsor must respond. It has to engage seriously with the strongest case for approving without conditions — that these patients face death within weeks and that withholding the offer is paternalism dressed as protection — and with the argument that this is just the familiar advance-directive problem, in which a prior competent decision governs even when the later, incompetent person protests. I think that analogy fails, but the opinion should either show why or concede it, and it must handle the version of the case in which two copies are made, or one copy plus a still-living body, since that is where the branching talk stops being decorative. Say what the consent document may and may not assert about what happens to the patient, what the committee owes the family, and what the panel should do if a patient, having heard both descriptions, wants to proceed anyway. Two pages, plain enough for the three lay members, actionable bottom line, no citations — I will add references myself.
