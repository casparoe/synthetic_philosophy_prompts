I'm in a small reading group on logic and normativity — we're all amateurs, we meet in the back room of a pub, half of us are there for the beer — and last week someone brought in an 1895 dialogue by Lewis Carroll called "What the Tortoise Said to Achilles", where the Tortoise gets Achilles to write out the proof of the first proposition of Euclid and then just... keeps asking for one more premise. I definately follow the mechanics: each time Achilles says if you accept A and B and C, you must accept Z, the Tortoise says sure, write that down too, and now it's (D), and then (E). But I can't tell what the point of the whole thing is supposed to be, and I suspect I'm missing something structural rather than just a joke. Here's the stretch I keep re-reading:

'And why must I?'
'Because it follows logically from them. If A and B and C are true, Z must be true. You don't dispute that, I imagine?'
'If A and B and C are true, Z must be true,' the Tortoise thoughtfully repeated. 'That's another Hypothetical, isn't it? And, if I failed to see its truth, I might accept A and B and C, and still not accept Z, mightn't I?'
'You might,' the candid hero admitted; 'though such obtuseness would certainly be phenomenal. Still, the event is possible. So I must ask you to grant one more Hypothetical.'
'Very good. I'm quite willing to grant it, as soon as you've written it down. We will call it (D) If A and B and C are true, then Z must be true. Have you entered that in your note-book?'
'I have!' Achilles joyfully exclaimed, as he ran the pencil into its sheath. 'And at last we've got to the end of this ideal race-course! Now that you accept A and B and C and D, of course you accept Z.'
'Do I?' said the Tortoise innocently. 'Let's make that quite clear. I accept A and B and C and D. Suppose I still refused to accept Z?'

— and then a few lines later, after Achilles insists that Logic will force him, tells him he "can't help yourself", and so on:

'Whatever Logic is good enough to tell me is worth writing down,' said the Tortoise. 'So enter it in your book, please. We will call it (E) If A and B and C and D are true, Z must be true. Until I've granted that, of course I needn't grant Z.'

Two people in the group had completely different takes on it and I couldn't work out whether they were actually disagreeing. One said the Tortoise is showing that you can never adopt a rule of inference on the strength of premises — any argument that would convince you to accept modus ponens has to use modus ponens already — so the regress isn't a trick the Tortoise plays, it's a real limit, and the lesson is that following a rule is something you do rather than something you can be argued into. The other said no, the Tortoise's only mistake is treating a rule as if it were a premise: modus ponens is a rule for getting from sentences to sentences, not one more sentence to be believed, and once you see that the regress just evaporates and the whole performance is a category error dressed up as a paradox. They also went back and forth about Achilles's line that logic would force the Tortoise to accept Z and that he "can't help yourself" — one of them thought that's the real tell, that the Tortoise has caught Achilles confusing logical necessity with psychological compulsion, and the other thought Achilles was just getting flustered and that nothing should be built on it.

Can you help me understand it properly? I'd like a walk-through of what the Tortoise is actually doing at each step and where, if anywhere, Achilles goes wrong, and then your honest take on whether those two readings are really different diagnoses or just two ways of saying the same thing. I'm not after a reading list or anything, I just want to actually get the passage.
