My mother, Ruth, is seventy-eight and has been living with Alzheimer's disease for about six years. She is now in the middle stage: on good days she knows my brother Daniel and me; on bad days she mistakes him for our father, who died eight years ago, and she can no longer follow a conversation for more than a few minutes or manage any of her own affairs. Five years ago, before the diagnosis was formally confirmed, she executed a detailed advance directive with her own lawyer, refusing artificial nutrition and hydration, antibiotics for life-threatening infections, and hospitalization for anything other than comfort care, in the event that she became unable to recognize her children. It was not a boilerplate form. She worked on it for months, discussed it with her physician and with both of us, and was judged competent by everyone involved. The directive also contains a clause that has become the center of a fight I never expected to be having. She wrote that she knew people in her condition often seem happy, that they smile and ask for dessert and enjoy music, and she stipulated that if she ever appeared content, her apparent contentment was not to be treated as a change of mind: the woman who is smiling, she wrote, will not be me; do not let her speak for me. I should add that she wrote it in the year after my father died, when she told a close friend she was tired of everything, and that the trigger condition has turned out to be vague in practice: she recognizes us on perhaps seven days out of ten.

Here is where things stand. Three weeks ago she developed a urinary tract infection that has twice returned, and her swallowing has begun to fail; the question before us now is whether to authorize a third course of antibiotics and a feeding tube. Daniel is a hospitalist and our mother's co-proxy along with me, which means that when we disagree, nothing gets decided, and we disagree completely. He says the directive is unambiguous and that the smiling clause settles the one thing that would otherwise be uncertain. I say that the woman asking for her garden, telling the aide she likes her life, and reaching for my hand is not nobody — and that it feels rigged for our mother to have written a document whose whole point is to overrule the only person who will actually be living the life in question. When the aide read her the directive aloud last week, she listened, said she didn't remember writing it, and then said, quietly, that she had been very sad then. I cannot tell whether that is a considered judgment or an echo of whatever mood passed through her that afternoon; she also still cries for my father, and she sometimes says she wants to go to sleep and not wake up.

I am asking you to help me decide, not to lay out my options. I need a concrete recommendation: do I authorize the antibiotics, and do I authorize the feeding tube? Give me your verdict and defend it, and tell me honestly what weight you think that clause deserves — whether an earlier self can legitimately bind a later one not merely about treatment but about how her own contentment is to be interpreted, and whether the fact that she anticipated exactly this evidence changes anything. Please don't tell me to consult the ethics committee, defer to the medical team, or split the difference; the committee has already asked me for a recommendation, and I am the one who has to sign. If your answer depends on a fact I haven't given you, tell me which fact and what turns on it.
