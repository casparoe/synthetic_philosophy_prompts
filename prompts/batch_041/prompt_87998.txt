I chair the Resource Allocation Committee of a consortium of eleven independent research institutes that share one high-throughput computing cluster and one cryo-electron microscope facility. Institutes currently obtain instrument time through an internal market: my office posts a price per node-hour and per microscope-hour at the start of each quarter, institutes bid for what they want, and unused allocations may be resold between institutes at whatever rate the two parties agree. Those posted prices are set by me and two analysts, who look at last quarter's utilization and nudge the numbers; everyone, including me, regards them as somewhat arbitrary. My vice-chair, a systems engineer, wants to scrap the market and install a central optimizer: each institute would file a statement of needs and priorities, a solver would assign every node-hour and microscope-hour to maximize aggregate scientific value, and post-allocation trading would be prohibited. Her argument is that since we already set the prices ourselves, the market is a fiction that adds transaction costs and gaming on top of a decision we make anyway; if we are going to plan, we should plan well. She adds that with enough historical data and a decent forecasting model we could predict demand better than any price can. I am uneasy but cannot articulate why in a way that survives her point. Please draft the written recommendation I will circulate to the governing board before its meeting in three weeks, and make it something I can table unchanged: a clear choice among the optimizer, the status quo market, or a specified hybrid, with a concrete implementation path and a deadline.

I would like the memo to confront the classical argument that a central allocator cannot obtain the dispersed, context-dependent knowledge that decentralized actors hold and reveal through their choices, and to be precise about where that argument bites in our case and where it does not — in particular, whether it depends on the absence of private ownership of the instruments or on something about the character of the knowledge itself. It should also engage the standard rejoinder that a central board need not know anything directly, since it can set prices by trial and error and let managers follow simple rules, and it should say what that rejoinder would have to assume about three analysts with spreadsheets and a forecasting model in order to succeed. Because the instruments are a capital good held in common and the prices are administered rather than emergent, I also want the memo to state plainly whether the calculation problem as it is usually framed even gets traction here, or whether what we face is a smaller and different problem dressed in its clothes.

Finally, please be candid about the memo's weak points. Include a short section naming the strongest consideration against the recommendation and specifying the evidence or argument that would change the committee's mind — for example, a finding about how much of the value of an instrument-hour is knowable in advance and how much is discovered only in the using.
