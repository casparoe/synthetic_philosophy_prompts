Ok so two of my friends have been going at each other about vagueness for like six months now and I'm tired of moderating. One of them is a died-in-the-wool epistemicist: there IS a sharp boundary between heap and non-heap, bald and not-bald, it's just that no one can know where it is, same margin-for-error story you tell about knowledge generally. The other is a supervaluationist: there's no sharp boundary at all, a borderline sentence comes out true on some ways of making the predicate precise and false on others, so it's neither true nor false in the ordinary sense, and only "supertrue" if it's true on every way of sharpening. They keep talking past each other. Write the document they should have produced instead: the joint statement of an adversarial collaboration.

I want (1) the claims the two of them can actually both sign -- about what the phenomenon even is, what counts as explaining it rather than restating it, what the sorites shows, and what a semantic theory of a vague predicate owes us; (2) the exact remaining disagreements, each written so that the person who holds it would accept the wording as fair, not a strawman and not so hedged it commits to nothing; (3) the cruxes, i.e. the sub-questions where getting an answer one way would move at least one of them; (4) for each crux, something both agree bears on it, plus what each side predicts about it.

There's some stuff I specifically want them to grind through, because I think that's where the action is:

- Higher-order vagueness. If the boundary is itself vague, what does the epistemicist say about the line between "clearly a heap" and "borderline heap"? And if "true on all sharpenings" is itself vague, does the supervaluationist's machinery even run, or does it just push the problem up a level?
- Whether supertruth deserves the name truth. Does the usual schema -- p just in case it's true that p -- survive for the supervaluationist, and if not, how much should anyone care?
- Arbitrariness. Whatever sharp line the epistemicist picks for "heap" could apparently have been one grain over. So where does the line come from: actual usage, ideal usage, or something in the world? Does the answer change between a predicate like "tall doorway" and one where the world might supply a real joint (alive/dead, species boundaries)? And does the story differ for a discrete case (grains) versus a continuous one (shades of a colour)? I suspect they treat those two differently and I want to know if that's principled or just taste.
- The forced-choice thing. People are shown borderline cases and allowed only yes/no, then shown them again with a "can't tell" option. What does each side predict about hesitation, disagreement, and whether people converge once they're told where the alleged boundary sits? If they end up agreeing on the predictions, say so plainly -- that's a result.

And at the very end, tell me which part of this fight is just verbal. I have a suspicion that "there's no fact of the matter" and "there's a fact we can't know" are closer than either of them will admit, but if I'm wrong about that, say why.
