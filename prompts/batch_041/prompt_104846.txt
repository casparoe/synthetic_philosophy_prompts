As a PhD student in philosophy of language, I am designing a seminar exercise on the necessary a posteriori, and I want a comparison of two explanatory strategies rather than a rehearsal of the standard examples. The first strategy is the familiar rigid-designator account: a natural-kind term picks out the same substance in every possible world, so an identity such as water is H2O, if true, is necessarily true even though it was discovered empirically. The second strategy, which I am calling epistemic-role anchoring, says that a term's reference is fixed by the inferential and empirical role it occupies in a theory, so the identity is necessary because no possible world counts as one in which that same role is occupied by a different substance. Please compare these two strategies, addressing (i) what each implies about the epistemic status of the identity, (ii) whether epistemic-role anchoring collapses into a sophisticated descriptivism, and (iii) how each would treat a term introduced by stipulation whose founding sample is later found to have been a mixture. Give your answer as a numbered list of exactly five items, each one or two sentences, with no introduction or conclusion.
