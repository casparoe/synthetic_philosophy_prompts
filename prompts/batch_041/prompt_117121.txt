I'm prepping a session for a reading group on epistemic injustice and we've already done the basic split everyone knows -- the wrong of not being believed because of who you are, versus the wrong of not being able to make your experience intelligible to anyone because the shared concepts for it just aren't there yet. My suspicion is that the second one gets used as a catch-all and that the split is doing less work than people think, so I want a case built to order to stress-test it. Here's the spec: two people say essentially the same kind of thing, in the same room, to the same audience, on the same day, and both of them are believed -- actually believed, taken at their word, acted on, not humoured -- and yet only one of them walks away wronged in a distinctively epistemic way. Constraints: mundane setting (a workplace, a clinic, a department meeting; no courtroom drama, no whistleblower epic), one paragraph, and the asymmetry has to be produced by one thing only, namely what interpretive/conceptual resources the room has available. It can't come from prejudice, from a credibility judgement, from who has more power in the room, from either person being worse at articulating what happened, or from one of them being flat-out misdescribed by the facts. Everything except the available concepts gets held fixed, and I want to be able to point at the pair and say: same input, different resources, different outcome.

Then do the audit on it. For each of the two people, tell me which category (if either) the case actually puts them in and why, and be straight with me about which single feature is doing the work -- if it turns out the case is really about a third thing that the standard split doesn't have a name for, say that instead of forcing it into one of the two boxes. Then take the feature you identified and remove it, and tell me what the case collapses into: a bog-standard credibility case, ordinary bad luck, or nothing at all. Finally, argue it from the other side -- if you think the standard account is basically fine, what's the best move for refusing my case: deny that the second person is wronged at all, or accept it and say the category was always meant to stretch that far? I'd much rather you mark the places where people who actually work on this disagree than smooth them over; if the verdict I'm clearly fishing for is contested, say so rather than presenting it as the consensus, and seperate out what's settled from what isn't.
