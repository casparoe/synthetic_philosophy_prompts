I spent two hours last night arguing with a friend about the consortium I help govern, and I have to circulate a written opinion before Friday's board meeting, so I would rather get the reasoning straight than win the argument a second time.

I chair the board of a five-college regional consortium that has spent four years and about $7.2 million building a shared records and credentialing platform. Three of the five colleges have withdrawn. Under our bylaws, a member that leaves before launch forfeits everything it has paid in, and that money stays with the project; the remaining members absorb the rest of the cost. That leaves two of us. Completion now requires roughly $5 million more, split between us, and it has to be finished within twelve months or we lose the state data-sharing agreement that accounts for most of the platform's value. Each remaining college values a finished platform at somewhere between $1.5 million and $4 million, depending mainly on whether the pending state credentialing standard turns out to be compatible; my honest estimate is a coin flip tilted slightly toward compatible. Withdrawing costs each of us the $1.5 million already paid and leaves us with nothing.

My friend's position is that quitting now would mean four years and $7.2 million bought nothing, that we would be the colleges who let the thing collapse, and that nobody will trust us to lead a joint venture again. My position, which I now suspect was too quick, is that this is the dollar auction — the game in which the top two bidders both pay, so the two leaders keep bidding past the value of the prize — and that the board's job is to notice the trap rather than keep bidding into it.

Draft the opinion I will circulate. It should recommend a concrete course of action with a deadline, make the forward-looking case honestly (including whether continuing is in fact justified on the margin, which I suspect it may be, and if so why that is not the same thing as the fallacy my friend is committing), say what the board owes the three colleges that forfeited, and propose one change to the bylaws so that the next consortium does not reproduce this structure. Be explicit about the decision rule you are applying and about which numbers are doing the work. Separate clearly what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who study escalation, and do not let a contested claim pass as consensus. I don't need citations; I need the argument.
