I am a novelist, and I have spent the better part of two years reading a run of nineteenth-century commercial correspondence — some four hundred letters, most of them about a dye works that failed — in order to write a novel out of it. The letters will bear at least two accounts of what happened. In one, the failure is a tragedy of character: an obstinate eldest brother, a debt he would not renegotiate, a ruin that follows from his pride. In the other it is a farce: two men of ordinary competence caught in a credit contraction neither of them understood, quarrelling past each other about a distinction that makes no difference. I have found that I can make either account carry, and that the account I choose decides which letters count as evidence and which as noise. What I cannot decide is whether there is a fact of the matter about which of the two is the better account — better in a sense that answers to the documents rather than to my purposes as a teller. Please give me your credence in that claim, and decompose it.

State the claim as precisely as you can before you price it; I will count it as settled in the affirmative if a competent historian can be wrong about the shape of an episode rather than merely unfashionable about it. Then give me the sub-questions the number rests on, a rough probability for each with a line of justification, and how they combine into the final figure.

The sub-questions I would propose, though you should amend them if they are the wrong ones, are: whether the past is determinate enough for evidence to constrain an account at all; whether narrative form — emplotment, in Hayden White's sense — carries cognitive content or is only a wrapper around propositions that carry it; whether the documents in fact leave the choice of plot open; and the gap I am most suspicious of, whether underdetermination of plot by evidence entails that no plot is better supported than another. I would add one drawn from my own trade: whether a criterion like making the previously inert details load-bearing is a truth-conducive virtue in an account or merely an aesthetic one. Ranke's ambition was to show the past as it actually was, and the German word at the centre of his formula, eigentlich, will bear either an actualist or an essentialist reading; tell me whether that ambiguity is doing real work here or is a distraction from the substantive question. Carr and White will be in the background; I am not asking for a reading list, and I would rather you tell me whether White's constructivism about narrative form is in the end a scepticism about the past, or whether the two theses come apart.

My own number is about 0.3. A friend of mine, a historian of the period, puts herself at 0.75. I care more about the decomposition than about the figure, but I do want a figure, and I want to know which sub-question the number is most sensitive to and which of them our disagreement most plausibly lives in. Finally, tell me which term you are least confident in and what would move it — an argument, a kind of evidence, an example. I will take a concrete case over a general one: if you can, give me a pair of rival accounts of a single episode where you think the evidence does settle the plot, and another where it cannot.
