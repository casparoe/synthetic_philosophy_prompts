I am a second-year PhD student in computational neuroscience, and I am trying to get clear on an analogy my field leans on constantly. My supervisor justifies our entire modelling program with a particular version of it: the brain is a computer, and more specifically a stored-program machine, so a neural circuit is like a compiled binary — the algorithm is in there somewhere, in a form we can reverse-engineer, and the connectome is essentially a disassembly listing we have not yet learned to read. On that picture, the right research strategy is to decompile the brain: recover the instruction set, extract the algorithm from its implementation, and thereby explain cognition at a level that is autonomous from the wetware realizing it. He takes this to license two further claims — that the same algorithm could run on a different substrate, and that cognitive-level explanations should not be expected to be constrained by messy neural detail.

I want to audit this rather than swallow it or wave it away. Could you set out the correspondence explicitly — what, if anything, plays the role of the architecture, the instruction set, the program, the compiler, the source code, the data, and the clock — and then tell me where the mapping breaks? I am especially interested in which disanalogies actually bear on the two conclusions the analogy is being used to support and which are merely incidental. In particular, I want to know how much weight the following can carry: that neurons appear to store and process in the same place rather than in a separate memory unit; that learning seems to rewrite the hardware rather than edit a program; that the system is analog and asynchronous, without a clock; that there is no compiler, no source, and no specification the system was ever built to satisfy; and that development and evolution look less like writing code than like tinkering with whatever is at hand. Which of these merely make the engineering harder, and which undercut the inference to an autonomous, multiply realizable algorithm?

I would also like you to say what the computer picture quietly imports that the brain may not possess — for instance, whether it assumes a determinate level of description that deserves to be called the program, or a substrate-independence that the real system never had — since I suspect some of the persuasive force comes from those imports rather than from the analogy itself.

Finally, if the decompilation picture is the wrong analogy, what would be a better one for what the brain is actually doing — or should I conclude that no single source-domain analogy will do the work, and that the honest move is to keep several partial analogies in play at once? I would rather have a clear-eyed statement of what the analogy can and cannot carry than a verdict on computationalism in general.
