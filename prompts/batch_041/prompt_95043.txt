I write about science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and before that I spent six years on the polling and survey beat, which is where I acquired the vocabulary I actually think in: frames and target populations, selection probabilities and design weights, clustering, nonresponse adjustment, size-biased and length-biased sampling, and the long methodological quarrel between design-based inference, which treats the randomization as the source of all probability, and model-based inference, which posits a superpopulation and lets the counts do the work. I can follow the algebra in a technical paper, though I am not a probabilist, and I know the standard illustrations well enough to be argued with — capture-recapture, the German tank problem, the waiting-time paradox.

I am drafting a piece about a dispute in the philosophy of probability that I can state only in my own words, so correct me where I misstate it. One position, the self-sampling assumption, holds that in reasoning about which observer you are, you should treat your own position as though it had been drawn at random from the class of all observers — roughly, that you should reason as if you were a random member of your reference class. The other, the self-indication assumption, holds that the bare fact of your existing is itself evidence about how many observers there are, so that a hypothesis implying vastly more observers should be judged vastly more probable than a rival implying few, other things equal. The two come apart in a thought experiment usually called the presumptuous philosopher, in which a philosopher claims to settle a cosmological question from his armchair: if one theory of the universe implies enormously more people than its rival, then, conditional on your existing, that theory is overwhelmingly more likely, and he therefore announces that the universe is far larger than the physicists' evidence suggests.

What I want from you is a systematic translation of this dispute into the vocabulary of survey sampling, followed by an honest account of what does not survive the translation. Concretely:

First, the translation. Render each position as a claim about sampling design and inference. Is the self-sampling assumption the demand that one condition on a self-weighting design, in which every member of the frame has an equal and known chance of selection? Is the self-indication assumption a model-based or superpopulation claim, in which the size of the population enters through the counts rather than through the design? Does the reference-class problem become the frame problem, and if so, does that show it to be a practical embarrassment rather than a deep difficulty? Does the presumptuous philosopher's inference have a legitimate analogue in the estimation of an unknown population size from the mere fact of having been sampled, as in the tank problem, or is the resemblance superficial?

Second, the residue. What in the philosophical dispute has no counterpart at all in the sampling vocabulary? I have a suspicion about where the trouble lies — that there is no sampler, that the population whose weights would be needed is the very thing in dispute, and that a respondent in a survey never has to work out which population he belongs to from the inside — but I want you to tell me whether those are the right fault lines and whether there are others.

Third, the verdict. Is the residue a defect of my vocabulary, which may simply have no way to express self-locating uncertainty, or a sign that the philosophical dispute is less substantive than it looks — perhaps that it reduces to a disagreement about the prior over population size, or about whether there is a design to be conditioned on at all? Say plainly whether translating the self-indication assumption into sampling terms makes it look more respectable or less, and why.

One discipline, please: do not let the sampling framework quietly assume a sampler. If the translation works only by smuggling in an external designer of the experiment, that is precisely the thing I need to know. And where a claim can be carried over only by altering it, say so rather than fudging it.
