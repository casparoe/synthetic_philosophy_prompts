I spent most of last night arguing with my oldest friend, a social psychologist, who holds that the vocabulary of character is empirically bankrupt — that the obedience experiments, the seminary-student good-Samaritan study, and the small field experiments on mood and helping show conduct is driven by situation, and that a person's self-described integrity predicts almost nothing — while I kept answering that virtue ethics was never a predictive thesis about cross-situational consistency and that virtue is a skill built by practice rather than a trait you can score. Neither of us moved, and I need something better than a truce, because I run the character-and-fitness reviews at our state bar: a few hundred applicants a year, each decided by a committee of practicing lawyers in under an hour, mostly on the basis of an interview and a self-report form. Write the decision procedure my office can actually run — the questions to ask in order for each file, what counts as a sufficient answer, where it stops with a clear no, and where it must hand the question to the committee because the issue is values, institutional risk, or our own exposure rather than the applicant. Ground it in what the situationist evidence actually establishes and in the best virtue-ethical reply to it, keep the distinctions that survive operationalization and say plainly which ones get lost, and mark the points where the procedure will misfire. Then run it on three cases: an applicant with a twelve-year-old conviction for petty theft; an applicant who personally did nothing wrong at a firm now under investigation for billing fraud; and the interview process itself, which I suspect the procedure will tell me to abandon — and if so, what the bar should regulate instead, including whether the situational-liability rule now being floated, under which firms would be sanctioned for incentive structures that predictably produce misconduct, is a better use of our authority than screening individuals.
