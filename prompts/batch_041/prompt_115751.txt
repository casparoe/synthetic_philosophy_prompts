I'm a novelist, and next month I'm running a 90-minute session at a writers' retreat — about a dozen novelists, no doctors among us, all of us working on books in which somebody dies. I want an activity that makes the room feel, rather than discuss, how arbitrary the moment of death gets once you actually try to pin it down. What I'm chasing specifically: the gap between the whole-brain picture (the lights are off upstairs but the body is still holding itself together, machine breathing for it) and the circulatory picture (heart stops, and then everyone has to stand around for a few minutes before they're allowed to do anything). Both answers look obvious until you ask what work they're doing — for families, for transplant teams, for the law.

So stage it: roles, a clock, a decision that has to land by a deadline, and rules that make it impossible to have it both ways. I dont want anyone to need medical jargon going in, and nobody says brain death or cardiac death out loud until the debrief — ideally they stumble into noticing that death may be a process we've agreed to treat as an event. In the debrief, tie what they did to the actual criteria, including the dead donor rule and how much quiet weight the word irreversible is carrying, plus the difference between losing consciousness for good and losing the body's ability to integrate itself. Tell me what predictably goes wrong with a room of novelists (someone will get squeamish, someone else will try to turn it into a monologue), and be honest about which piece of the metaphysics the activity can't reproduce and that I'll have to say out loud myself.
