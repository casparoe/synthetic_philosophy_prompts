So I've somehow ended up as the de facto bandleader of a pub covers band, and we've got a monthly slot at a place whose entire gimmick is "covers only, no originals," and I've discovered that I do not actually know what a cover is. Which sounds like a stupid problem until you have to book it.

Here's where my gut lands. (1) We play X with acoustic guitar and a drum machine instead of the original's synths. Obviously still X, nobody argues. (2) We do a ska version of a punk song — same words, same tune, completely different groove. Still X; I'd put X on the poster without hesitating. (3) A parody: same tune, all-new words about the local bus service. That's a different song. Funny, but it isn't X. (4) A straight translation into Spanish, sung to the same tune, meaning preserved. Still X, I think — same song in another language. (5) A version where we keep the words and the chords but I've rewritten the melody because I can't reach the high bits. My gut says that isn't X anymore, it's a new song using X's words. Fine — but then why is (2) still X? (6) A 40-second chunk inside a medley. Not "playing X" exactly, but X is in there. (7) A hip-hop track that loops two seconds of X's guitar riff under new rapping. New song, definitely, even though actual audio of X is literally in it. (8) Our own long-running thing: we've played X for eleven years and it has drifted. Slower, a verse dropped, the riff simplified, the melody smoothed out. Put the 2013 recording next to tonight's version and they sound like two different songs, but every individual change was small and at no point did anyone feel we'd stopped playing X. I think we're still playing X. That one sits wierdly with (5). (9) We learned a song off another band's version without realising it was itself a cover. Still the same song — a cover of a cover. (10) We jam on X's chord changes with new words and a new melody. That's a new song, everyone agrees, it's how jazz works.

A friend lent me a book about some old AI program that solves letter-string puzzles by letting its concepts "slip" — you're told abc goes to abd, so what does ijk go to, and the program can answer ijd, or ijkl, or ijk, and each one is defensible because the idea of "the same change" quietly slid into "the same letter" or "add one more." There was also something in there about the program running at a "temperature" that sets how far a concept may bend before the answer breaks. I only half understood it, but I keep thinking our setlist is that program.

So: is there a principle that gets all ten of my verdicts, or close? I want it stated precisely enough that I could hand it to the venue, which is the real reason I care — they want to know whether we're "playing covers" or sneaking originals in, and I need to be able to defend a ruling on the medley and on our eleven-year drift. If you think there is no single principle and "same song" just means different things depending on whether we're talking about billing, royalties, or what the audience hears, say that straight rather than inventing a principle that papers over it. I'd rather learn the question is confused than get a tidy answer that collapses the next time we argue. And if my verdicts are inconsistent in a way I can't feel from the inside, tell me which ones to give up.

Format, roughly: (a) "What I think you're actually asking" — a short disambiguation, ~100 words; (b) "Candidate principles" — two or three of them, each stated precisely, with the case that motivates each clause, ~400 words; (c) "Your ten cases" — what each principle says about each, ~400 words; (d) "Cases I didn't list" — three or four new ones you predict, ~250 words; (e) "Verdicts you'd have to drop" — honest, ~150 words; (f) "Pushback" — where my framing is off. Skip the reading lists and references, I won't chase them down.
