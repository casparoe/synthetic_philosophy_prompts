Question. Consider a two-player bargaining problem (S, d), where S is a closed convex set of feasible utility pairs and d is the disagreement point. Write u* = (u₁*, u₂*) for the ideal point, where uᵢ* is the largest payoff player i can obtain in S. (a) Define the Nash solution and the Kalai–Smorodinsky solution, and explain what distinguishes them geometrically. (b) The Nash solution is the unique solution satisfying Pareto optimality, symmetry, scale invariance and independence of irrelevant alternatives; the Kalai–Smorodinsky solution is the unique solution satisfying Pareto optimality, symmetry, scale invariance and individual monotonicity, where the last says roughly that a player whose attainable payoffs improve at every fixed payoff of the other player cannot end up with less. Explain what is at stake in the choice between these two axiom sets, and say whether, and when, the two solutions pick out the same point.

Answer. (a) Both solutions treat d as the payoff each player receives if no agreement is reached, and both return a point of S. The Nash solution is the feasible point that maximises the product of the players' gains over d, that is (u₁ − d₁)(u₂ − d₂). The Kalai–Smorodinsky solution is found by drawing a straight line from d to the ideal point u* and taking the last point of S on that line, which is where the line meets the frontier. The geometric difference is that Nash responds to the shape of the frontier, whereas Kalai–Smorodinsky is pinned down by the line from d to u*.

(b) The two solutions share three axioms: Pareto optimality, symmetry, and invariance under positive affine transformations of the players' utility scales. They differ on the fourth. Nash's fourth axiom is independence of irrelevant alternatives, which requires the solution to be unaffected by alternatives that it does not itself select. Kalai and Smorodinsky reject this, arguing that the whole set of available agreements should matter, since a player who gives up an option that is good for them can reasonably ask to be compensated. They replace IIA with individual monotonicity, which requires that a player whose options improve does not end up worse off. So Nash is sensitive to the curvature of the frontier while Kalai–Smorodinsky is not.

The two solutions coincide when the problem is symmetric: with a frontier that treats the players alike, the product is maximised at the point where the players' gains are equal, and the line from d to u* is the diagonal, so both solutions select the same point.

They come apart when the problem is asymmetric. Here I think Kalai–Smorodinsky is the more defensible of the two, because it equalises the players' proportional gains: each player ends up with the same fraction of the gain they could have obtained on their own. It follows that Kalai–Smorodinsky never gives either player less than Nash does.

Finally, scale invariance is what allows utility to be treated as a mere representation of preference: because both solutions are invariant under any increasing transformation of a player's utility function, the numbers attached to outcomes are arbitrary, and only their ordinal ranking carries information.
