Me and my mate Priya got into a two-hour arguement last night about whether lying is actually worse than just misleading someone, and I ended up defending a position I'm not sure I even hold, so I want to know how much trouble it's in.

Here's the position. There's no intrinsic moral difference between lying and misleading. The line between them is a fact about mechanism — did a false sentence get uttered or not — and mechanism isn't where the wrongness lives. What makes deception wrong, when it is wrong, is identical on both sides: you're trying to get someone to believe something false about something that's theirs to know about, you're doing it because you think they wouldn't go along if they knew, and you're doing it for your benefit at their expense. Whether you get there by asserting something false or by saying something true in a way that steers them into the false conclusion is like whether you hit someone with your left hand or your right. Lying is just the cruder tool. So the two should get the same verdict, whatever that verdict is: if lying is wrong, so is misleading; and if a bit of misleading is fine (surprise parties, a doctor who says the tests are done when what she means is there's nothing left to try, sorry, I have plans), then the same amount of outright lying is fine too. And the popular feeling that misleading is the milder sin I take to be mostly about detection — it's harder to prove, and the misleader can always say but I never said that, which makes it feel slipperier rather than less bad.

What I want from you: what counts against this, sorted by how much it should actually move me. Not the intro-ethics stuff, I know the standard moves. I want the ones specific to how I've set it up. Tell me which are decisive and which are merely awkward, and which ones a serious defender of the distinction would themselves admit count against them, as opposed to what critics lob at them from outside.

A few things I'd like pressure on specifically.

First, the asymmetry that runs the other way: misleading recruits the victim. You don't just get them to believe something false, you get them to do the concluding themselves. You set the trap, they walk in, and afterwards they can't even say you lied to me without sounding like they're blaming you for their own inference. That looks worse to me, not better — the misleader keeps a clean conscience and the victim eats the humiliation. Does that tell against the distinction, or just against the people who draw it?

Second, the old casuist machinery: the idea that you can say something that sounds false and then silently add a clause in your head so the sentence comes out true, so it was never a lie in the first place. Does the fact that people needed all that scaffolding to stay on the right side of the line vindicate the line — it's tracking something real that people care about — or expose it, because what people actually care about is not being fooled and the lie/mislead boundary is bookkeeping?

Third, a legal case I half-remember: a movie producer under oath was asked whether he had any Swiss bank accounts, said no, was asked whether he ever had, and answered that his company had one in Zurich for about six months. All literally true, and the whole point was to leave the questioner thinking he'd never had a personal one. He got convicted, the appeals court kept it, and the Supreme Court threw it out unanimously, roughly on the ground that a literally true answer can't be perjury no matter how much it was designed to mislead, and the remedy is better questions. Is that the law discovering a moral truth, or just the law admitting it can't pin down an implication in front of a jury? And if it's the second, does that undercut the distinction or only the legal version of it?

Fourth, a case of my own: two used-car salesmen. One says this car has never been in an accident, which is false. The other says nothing false, just shows you the clean service record and lets you do the rest. Same belief, same purchase, same loss. Is there any description under which the first guy did something the second didn't, that isn't about deniability?

And the meta-question: what does my view predict that the rival doesn't? If the answer is nothing, it just redescribes the same facts, tell me whether that's a fatal collapse into something else — plain consequentialism? the view that all deception is one thing and the real question is consent? — or just what a correct re-description looks like. And is my position insulated? Every time someone says but the misleading one felt worse, I say that's the deniability thing, which is very convenient for me. If it turns out nothing could count against me, say so, and say whether that's a bug or just the price of a view that's got the moral unit right.

For what it's worth, the thing that would actually move me is a case where the misleader does something the liar couldn't do, and where the difference isn't about proof or deniability but about what the victim was owed. If you've got one, lead with it. No reading list please, I'm not going to read anything.
