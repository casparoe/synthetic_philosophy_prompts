My friend Devi is doing a PhD in Buddhist philosophy and she's a total Nāgārjuna person, like annoyingly so. I've been keeping a mental list of how she answers metaphysical questions and I want to know what she'll say about a case that came up the other day, and honestly what rule she's been running this whole time.

Her track record, as best I can reconstruct it:

- I asked whether the self is the same as the body and mind or different from them. She said neither — not the same, not different, not both, not neither — and then went on saying "I" and "me" all evening without missing a beat.
- I asked whether a chariot exists. She said sure, conventionally you can ride it, but there's no extra thing called a chariot over and above the parts, and it's not just the parts either.
- I asked whether a cause produces its effect — does the effect come from itself, from something else, from both, from neither. She knocked down all four.
- I asked whether a fully awakened person exists after death. She wouldn't answer at all, said it's the wrong kind of question, wouldn't even run the four options on it.
- I asked whether the world has a beginning in time. Same thing, flat refusal, wrong question.
- I asked whether emptiness is itself a thing. She said if you think you've got hold of emptiness as a thing you've just given yourself a worse disease — the whole thing is medicine, not metaphysics.

The new case: a mutual friend Ravi is a transhumanist and he's definately convinced that if you gradually swap out someone's neurons one at a time for silicon chips doing the same job — same wiring, same memories, same dispositions, until nothing biological is left — it's obviously still the same person, just on better hardware. He wants Devi's verdict.

So predict Devi's verdict on Ravi's case. Then tell me the rules that fit her whole track record equally well, what each one predicts here, which you'd bet on and why, and name the one further case I could ask her next that would best tell the surviving rules apart.
