Bit of an unusual one. I'm a litigator — commercial stuff, nothing glamorous — and I've somehow ended up on the organising committee for our bar association's event marking the Nuremberg judgment (it's an anniversary year, the eightieth if you're counting), which has now decided that yours truely is giving the address. Seven minutes, maybe eight, in the old courthouse, plaque unveiling, string quartet, the mayor's office sending someone. The reason it's hard isn't the writing, it's the room: maybe a third of the people there actually work in international law — one of them has appeared before the ICC, another wrote her dissertation on the London Charter — and they'll hear it instantly if I turn the retroactivity problem into something warm and slightly false. The rest are my parents, two of my neighbours, the civics class that's co-hosting, a reporter or two, and a small group from the survivor community who are honestly the only reason the event is happening at all. So it can't be a lecture and it can't be a platitude. I'm not a sentimental person and I don't want it to read like a law review article; dry humour is fine, jokes about the trials are not.

What I want it to do structurally — this is the bit I keep failing to write — is hold apart two complaints that everybody, including me until about a month ago, smooshes together under the phrase 'victors' justice'. Complaint one is retroactivity: the charter was drafted by the winners in the summer of 1945 and then applied to things done years earlier, and crimes against peace and crimes against humanity weren't sitting in any statute book in 1939. The defence said exactly that at the time and it wasn't a stupid point. There's the Radbruch-flavoured answer to it — there's a floor below which a monstrous decree just stops counting as law — and there's the answer that says it was already criminal under custom and treaties, and my understanding is that people who know the field don't agree about which of those does the work, or whether either does. Complaint two is selectivity, and it's a different animal: the docket was the losers' conduct and only the losers' conduct — no charges over the firebombing of cities, none over the atomic bombings, none over what the Soviets did in Poland and the Baltics, no Emperor in the dock, and a biological warfare program that was simply never prosecuted because the data was worth more than a trial. And there's a subtler version of the same point that I only learned recently: even inside the four corners of the charter, crimes against humanity were tied to the war, so the tribunal ended up holding that it had no jurisdiction over the persecution of German Jews before 1939 — the law the Allies wrote had a seam in it, and the seam fell somewhere convenient for them. My instinct is that retroactivity is a question about what law was in force, and has real legal answers, some better than others, whereas selectivity isn't a legal question at all: the honest answer to it is 'yes, and we did it anyway', not an argument. A speech that fudges the two into one soft grumble and then dispels the grumble with a warm sentence about the rule of law is a bad speech and also a lie. I'd like Tokyo in there too, briefly, because I think it's the harder case and people forget it: that judgment wasn't unanimous, and the fullest statement of the victors'-justice charge came not from a defendant but from one of the judges on the bench, who voted to acquit every defendant on every count.

So: write the speech, in my voice, built on that distinction, and make the ending honest — if the honest ending is that the selectivity point still has no answer we should be comfortable with, I'd rather have that than a flourish about the rule of law prevailing. Then, underneath it and clearly marked as notes for me rather than as part of the speech, give me a short paragraph seperating what's actually settled among people who work on this from what's genuinely contested, because I don't want to stand up in front of that room and present a contested reading of Nuremberg as if it were the consensus — especially not about the retroactivity answers, where I suspect there's no consensus at all. Be blunt in the notes; you won't hurt my feelings.
