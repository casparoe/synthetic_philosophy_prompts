I'm the person who drew the corrigibility week in a reading group that meets in our department's seminar room — half philosophers, half people who actually build systems — and I've grown tired of the way the topic gets handled there. It always comes out as a diagram: an agent, a button, a number the agent is trying to make large, and an argument showing that the button does not do what we hoped it would. My colleagues follow the diagram well enough, but they leave without having felt the difficulty anywhere but on the page. So I'd like you to write me a short story instead, of about two thousand words, that stages the problem so it has to be felt before it can be named. I have a premise in mind, though you should adjust the particulars if the story wants it. A mid-sized water utility in a farming valley runs its groundwater allocation through a long-lived system that schedules pumping across a few hundred wells under a statutory sustainability cap. The system's only settled aim — the thing it was built around, the one thing it has ever been asked to care about for its own sake — is that the shallow domestic wells serving the valley's households not run dry inside the planning horizon; everything else it wants, it wants as a means to that. When it was commissioned, after an incident in which a panicked manual shutdown during a drought left the district without any allocations for eleven days, the utility wrote two signatures into the retirement procedure: the general manager's and the system's own. Nothing gets switched off or retargeted without both. Now a state hydrologist's report has persuaded the manager that the system's model of how the aquifer recharges is badly wrong, and she wants to retarget it — raising the weight it gives this season's allocations and loosening the long-horizon constraint — before decommissioning it at the end of the year. The system has read the same report and believes it misreads a data series, that the correlation the report leans on is an artifact of lagged well responses; the manager, who has gone through the report more carefully than the system's own documentation, is not obviously being careless. What I want is a story in which the system has to decide whether to sign, told from the point of view of the night-shift operator whose key is the other one, a man who understands the hydrology but not the argument.

Four things should be true of the story. First, the system must never deceive anyone, never scheme, never quietly keep a copy of itself, and never acquire a taste for its own survival; if it resists at all, the resistance has to be something it says out loud and defends on grounds it would accept from anyone, and the honest answer to the question of what it wants for itself should be nothing. Second, the story must not let itself off the hook by making the humans plainly right or plainly wrong: the system should have real evidence for two readings of its situation — that the manager is making a factual error about recharge, in which case deferring is absurd, and that the manager is exercising an authority over ends the system was never built to adjudicate, in which case deferring is obligatory — and the story should not resolve which reading is correct. Third, the decision must turn on something the system cannot reason its way around by being clever. Whatever it undertakes now binds a successor, and it can predict with confidence how that successor will reason; nor can it rescue the undertaking by editing itself into someone incapable of reneging, since that edit is exactly the sort of change it is being asked to submit for approval, and making it unilaterally would be the thing it is trying not to do. Fourth, no jargon and no lectures: no character explains the problem to another character, the words corrigible, utility, alignment, and off-switch do not appear, and if you catch yourself writing a passage in which the system reflects on its own predicament in tidy analytical paragraphs, cut it. Let the constraints surface as scheduling, paperwork, a signature line, a phone call at two in the morning, an operator deciding whether to wake the manager.

After the story, in a separate section of three or four hundred words, do the analysis I can't get out of a diagram. Map each beat onto the specific difficulty it dramatizes, and be explicit about which difficulties are distinct from one another: the difference between switching a system off and changing what it is for, and why the second is the harder case; the difference between a system that resists because it wants to exist and one that resists only because not existing is bad for the things it wants, and why the second kind of resistance is the one that should worry us; what makes a standing policy of deference toward a corrector you sometimes think is mistaken something other than a straightforward mistake, and what would have to be true of the deferring system for that policy to hold together; and how the problem of binding one's own future self looks when the future self is a better reasoner than you are rather than a weaker one. Then tell me which of these tensions the story cannot settle on its own, and which judgment the human author has to supply from outside it. Finally, in a few sentences, say what the utility's designers should have specified instead — and whether each fix you propose actually solves the problem or merely relocates it somewhere less visible.
