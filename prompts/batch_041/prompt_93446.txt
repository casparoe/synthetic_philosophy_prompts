At a supper a fortnight ago, two friends of mine, Nadia and Rob, fell into an argument about an arrangement made by a third friend, Priya, and I have been turning it over ever since. Priya came into a modest inheritance last year and has lent £20,000 of it to Marcus, a plumber we all know, so that he can buy a van and a proper set of tools. Marcus had been turned down by his bank because of a default years ago; the interest they settled on — 8 per cent a year, which is unremarkable for that kind of loan — was, and this is what set the argument going, Marcus's own insistence, since he refused to take the money as a favour; and Priya, who would otherwise have left the sum in a savings account earning next to nothing, eventually agreed and had a loan drawn up. Nadia said Priya had become a usurer. Her authority was Aristotle: money, on his account, is a conventional thing whose whole purpose is to make exchange possible, so to make money out of money — to charge for the use of something that was only ever meant to change hands — is the most unnatural way there is of acquiring it; and Marcus's willingness, she added, was only pride talking, so Priya was being paid out of his pride. Rob said this was antiquated. Money is not barren, he said; Priya has given up a return and taken a real risk of never seeing the money again; and nobody would have raised an eyebrow if she had put the £20,000 into shares in a company rather than making a loan, so Aristotle's objection must either condemn every sort of investment or be arbitrary. Nadia answered that the two cases differ: a shareholder is paid out of the fortunes of the enterprise, whereas an interest payment falls due whether or not Marcus's business prospers, so what Priya is being paid for is his need and not any product of the loan. Rob replied that the van is precisely a product of the loan, that Marcus evidently expects the van to earn him more than the interest will cost him, and that both parties therefore expect to gain — which is what exchange is — and that Aristotle himself held exchange to be among the things that hold a community together, so on his own premises Priya's loan is a small piece of civic glue.

I have no view about which of them was being kinder, and I am not asking for a character reference for Priya. What I would like is a proper adjudication, move by move, in the order they made them: Nadia's opening charge, Rob's reply, Nadia's distinction between the shareholder and the lender, and Rob's closing appeal to mutual gain and to the social function of exchange. For each, say whether it is sound, partly sound or mistaken, and give the reason, being explicit throughout about what Aristotle actually held concerning money, exchange, need and the two sorts of acquisition — the acquisition that serves a household and the acquisition that has no natural stopping point — and about where each of them is quietly reading a modern quarrel back into him. You need not supply chapter and verse, and I would rather have the substance than a list of works. Then tell me what both of them have missed, and which of the two, if either, is closer to Aristotle's own position.

Please write your answer in British English.
