I am designing a single ninety-minute session for a reading group of graduate students in economics and philosophy. They are fluent in decision theory and game theory, they have no background in the problem of deciding well when the relevant computation cannot be run in advance, and two of them have already told me they expect the topic to be a curiosity with no bearing on anything. The topic is acausal trade. Take the following as the working characterization I will hand out, so that you neither reconstruct nor defend it: two agents can each end up better off by acting on a prediction of how the other will decide, even though neither can causally affect the other and neither can communicate with the other; in the sharpest versions, neither can be sure the other exists. What makes the idea interesting is not that such trades are common, but that the ordinary account of why trade works — retaliation, reputation, lost future custom — is unavailable, and something else has to be doing the work.

Do not write the session. Plan how I should teach it, and give me the architecture of the explanation rather than the explanation itself. Specifically:

- The order in which the components must be introduced, and why that order is forced rather than merely convenient. I assume the load-bearing pieces are (i) that one agent's choice can be evidence about another's choice without being a cause of it, (ii) the distinction between a trade that is enforced and a trade that is merely predicted, and (iii) the step from agents that both exist to agents that may not. Tell me whether that decomposition is right, what it omits, and where in the sequence the skeptics' objection should be met rather than deferred.

- The one example that should carry the whole session: what it must contain, what it must conspicuously lack, and why. I am tempted by the case of two agents built by the same designer, since the symmetry is vivid; tell me whether that is a trap, what it would lead them to believe instead, and whether any obvious feature of the case needs to be stripped out so that no shared cause is smuggled in.

- The misconceptions the audience will bring or form, in the order they will surface, with the precise point in the sequence at which each should be defused. I predict at least the following: that this is causal influence at a distance; that it is telepathy or mysticism; that it requires the two agents to be identical rather than merely correlated; that it reduces to ordinary reasoning about a common cause; and that it licenses Pascal-style bets on agents that almost certainly do not exist.

- What I should openly decline to explain at this level, and how to say so without making the topic look like a fudge.

- The single question to ask at the end that would show the idea has landed rather than merely been entertained, and what a good and a bad answer to it would look like.
