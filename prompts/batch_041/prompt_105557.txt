A friend and I have been arguing about nuclear deterrence for months and neither of us will budge. We agree on all the facts — the arsenals, the close calls, the fact that nobody has used one since 1945. He thinks deterrence is a stable, rational standoff: both sides know retaliation is coming, so nobody starts anything. I think the whole thing is luck plus the fact that nobody has ever actually had to make the call, and that a threat you'd never carry out isn't a threat, it's a bluff, and bluffs get called. He says the threat never has to be carried out, it only has to be believed. I ask believed by whom, on what grounds, when the man who'd press the button would be killing his own country. Then he says that's exactly why you rig it so there's a real chance of accident, or a madman, or a machine that fires on its own — you make it so the other side genuinely can't be sure it won't happen. That's where we die, because to me that says deterrence only works because it might fail, and I can't tell if that's deep or just a fancy way of admitting we're relying on luck. He says it's like a bank that's only solvent because nobody runs on it.

I dont think either of us is confused about the facts, so what kind of argument is this really? Is he answering whether deterrence would hold while I'm answering whether it makes sense to intend, or whether it's justified? Is there an empirical question hiding in it — how people actually behave under that kind of pressure — that neither of us can settle from our armchairs? Or do we just mean different things by rational, him meaning something like "calculates correctly" and me meaning something more like "sane"? My own guess is that we're really disagreeing about whether the stability of deterrence is a fact about the world, like a physical equilibrium, or a fact about our shared expectations, like which side of the road everyone drives on — but I might be wrong about that. Tell me what this fight actually is and what, if anything, would settle it.
