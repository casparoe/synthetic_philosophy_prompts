I write about artificial intelligence for a general readership, and before I file a long piece I want to set down my working assumptions and have someone tell me whether they hold together. Here is where I have landed. First, the orthogonality thesis: an agent's level of intelligence and its final goals are independent, so more or less any degree of intelligence could in principle be paired with more or less any final goal, and no final goal is more rational than another for a very capable agent. Second, instrumental convergence: for almost any final goal, a sufficiently capable agent has reason to seek power, resources, and its own continued existence. Third, because of the first two, we cannot assume a superintelligent system will share, or even grasp, our values, so alignment is genuinely hard. Fourth, the systems we have now — large language models and the like — are not agents and have no final goals of their own, so the first three claims do not describe them. Fifth, nevertheless, scaling today's methods is likely to produce genuine agents. Sixth, the practical fix is to build corrigible systems: ones that defer to human oversight and do not resist being corrected or shut down. Audit this set for me. Which of these commitments actually pull against each other, which only appear to, and what is the cheapest revision that would make the whole thing consistent? Rank the tensions by severity, and tell me what each repair would cost.
