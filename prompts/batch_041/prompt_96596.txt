I am a PhD student in the history and philosophy of science, and I have come to hold a broadly Lakatosian view of how research programmes are to be appraised. I accept the standard apparatus: a programme has a hard core, a protective belt, a negative heuristic that forbids modus tollens against the core, and a positive heuristic that generates a sequence of models. But I want to defend a modification that I have not seen stated in exactly this form. On my view, a programme can be rationally pursued even while it is empirically degenerating, provided its positive heuristic is still producing new models that are independently testable in principle; and the rational stopping point is not empirical failure as such but heuristic exhaustion, where the positive heuristic no longer yields models that are testable even in principle. Call this the exhaustion condition. I am not asking for a literature review, reading list, or citations; I want to know what would count against the exhaustion condition. Which historical cases, structural features of scientific practice, formal results, or philosophical arguments should move me? Sort the defeaters by how decisive they would be, distinguishing those that would be fatal from those that would be merely awkward. Say which of them a committed Lakatosian would themselves concede as counting against the view, and which they would dismiss as question-begging or as importing a rival methodology. I am also interested in whether the exhaustion condition risks unfalsifiability: if almost any continued work can be redescribed as the heuristic still generating models, or if exhaustion is only ever diagnosed retrospectively, has the view been insulated from disconfirmation? If it has, say whether that is a problem for a methodological thesis of this kind. I would personally find most persuasive a case in which a programme's heuristic was exhausted well before its practitioners abandoned it, or a case in which a programme was rationally abandoned for reasons that had nothing to do with heuristic exhaustion; but tell me if that intuition is too narrow or if it begs the question. Finally, say how confident you are in what you claim, and single out the point you are least sure of.
