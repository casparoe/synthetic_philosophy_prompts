I belong to a small reading group that works through the history of the philosophy of action, and we keep coming back to the starving donkey that goes by Buridan's name, set at an equal distance from two identical bales of hay. What interests me is that two very different morals get drawn from the story, and I would like a careful comparison of them. The first moral is about the will: an agent cannot act unless one of its options presents itself as better in some respect, so a perfect tie leaves it paralysed; the donkey starves because nothing inclines it either way, and the case is offered as a reductio of the view that the will is moved only by the greater apparent good. The second moral comes from the theory of rational choice: if the two options really are equal, there is nothing to get wrong, either choice is as good as the other, and the rational agent takes one and gets on with it. On this reading the paradox was never a problem about action at all but a confusion between having no preference and having no permissible option; the donkey's death, if it happens, is a failure of nerve or of biology rather than of rationality. Please compare these two treatments of choice without preference: what each assumes about what it takes to act, whether they are genuinely in conflict or are answering different questions, and which of them does better with the ordinary experience of being unable to decide.

The reason I want the comparison is a distinction I have been trying to draw, and I would like you to use it as a test case for both treatments. Call the first situation flat indifference: the options are exactly equal on a single scale of value, so that every reason for one is exactly cancelled by a reason for the other. Call the second unranked plurality: the options differ in ways that matter a great deal to the agent — one keeps her near her ageing parents, the other offers work she would find absorbing for the rest of her life — but there is no common measure on which the reasons can be weighed, so there is no fact of the matter about which is better, and, importantly, no fact of the matter about whether they are equal either. I coined these labels myself and have not seen them used anywhere, so there is no literature to hunt for; the only question is whether they mark a real difference.

Nearly every case in which I have actually been unable to decide looks like unranked plurality, whereas nearly every philosophical discussion of the donkey looks like flat indifference. So I would like you to say whether the donkey case, properly understood, covers both situations or only the first; whether the response that either choice is optimal and one may simply pick survives the move from flat indifference to unranked plurality, or quietly depends on there being a scale on which the options tie; and whether the paralysis view of the will becomes more or less plausible once the obstacle is not equality but the absence of any common measure. A coin is the obvious device for breaking a tie, and I am curious whether it still looks rational in the second case or merely looks like a way of evading a decision. Finish by telling me which of the two treatments, if either, I should keep if what I want is to say something true about ordinary indecision, and what it would have to give up in order to accommodate the cases I care about. You need not tell me who said what; I am after the comparison itself, not a map of the literature.
