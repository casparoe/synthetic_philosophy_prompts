I write about science and ideas, and I keep running into the careenium. A colleague's essay on whether brains are computers summarizes Douglas Hofstadter's thought experiment from I Am a Strange Loop roughly as follows, and I want to know how much of it to trust. On Hofstadter's picture, a great many tiny magnetic marbles — he calls them sims, sometimes spelled symms — careen around inside a billiard-table-like arena whose boundary is affected by conditions outside it; the marbles clump into stable higher-level formations he calls simmballs. Hofstadter's guiding question is whether the sims push the simmballs around or the simmballs push the sims. My colleague reports that Hofstadter's answer is that the simmballs are the real causal agents: symbols in the brain genuinely push neurons around, and that is what it means to say the mind acts on the body. On this reading, Hofstadter rejects the reductionist view that only the microphysical level does any pushing — a view the essay associates with Dennett, who is said to treat talk of beliefs and symbols as a convenient fiction draped over the real physics. My colleague then concludes that the careenium settles the mental-causation debate in favor of the irreducibility of the mental, and that anyone who insists the sims do all the pushing has thereby denied that we ever really think. I suspect the essay is broadly right and the conclusion too strong, but I can't locate the seam. Where would Hofstadter agree, where would he wince, and which of these claims would Dennett, or a philosopher of mind sympathetic to levels of description, reject outright? And once the missteps are corrected, does the conclusion about irreducibility follow from anything in the careenium setup itself?
