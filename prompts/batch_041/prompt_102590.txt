The question was:

Explain what the axiom of choice (AC) says, and then assess its status: is it a law of logic we're stuck with, a substantive mathematical hypothesis, or something else? Illustrate with at least two results that depend on it, and say why it's controversial.

The student wrote:

AC says that for any collection of nonempty sets, there's a function that picks exactly one element from each set. It's independent of the rest of set theory: Gödel showed you can't prove it from ZF, and Cohen showed you can't disprove it, so it's neither true nor false — you just choose whether to accept it.

Plenty of ordinary math needs it. To take a couple of the obvious ones: you need AC to prove that a countable union of countable sets is countable, and to prove that every vector space has a basis. It's also what powers the Banach–Tarski paradox, where a solid ball gets cut into finitely many pieces and reassembled into two balls each the same size as the original — that's part of why some people are suspicious of the axiom.

The reason people argue about it is that it's non-constructive: it says a choice function exists without giving you any way to compute it. In practice weaker principles like dependent choice cover most of analysis, so alot of mathematicians just use AC when it's convenient. I'd say AC is best thought of as a substantive axiom, more like Euclid's parallel postulate than a rule of logic — you can do a lot of math without it, and whether you accept it depends on what kind of universe you want. Most working mathematicians accept it, but it's not something logic forces on you.
