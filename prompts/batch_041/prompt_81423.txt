I'm a second-year PhD student in philosophy of biology, and I'm drafting the section of my qualifying paper that deals with the byproduct objection to adaptationism — the thought, made famous through an architectural example, that a trait may be a necessary consequence of other structural commitments rather than something selection built for a job, and that the mosaics later used to fill such a space tell you nothing about why the space exists. In our lab reading group last week, a postdoc raised an objection I have not been able to shake. The architectural example, he said, is an argument from design at precisely the point where the anti-adaptationist needs to be arguing against design: the triangular space is a consequence of an architect's decision to mount a dome on pendentives, the constraint is a constraint chosen by a mind, and the whole scene is populated by deliberate agents — architect, mason, mosaicist. Strip out the designer, he said, and you no longer have the analogy at all, you have a building that could not stand; so the example can do rhetorical work but never evidential work. He concluded that the byproduct objection has never really had a case behind it and that the critique of adaptationism is mostly a matter of taste. I suspect he is wrong but I cannot say why, and I want an audit rather than reassurance.

What I need is the mapping made explicit and then tested. Set out what in the architectural story corresponds to what in the biological one — the structural constraint, the feature claimed to be a byproduct, the feature's present usefulness, the agents, the available variation, the alternative arrangements that were never built — and for each correspondence say whether it holds, holds only partly, or has no counterpart at all. Then separate the mismatches that matter for the use the example is being put to from the ones that are merely decorative. My own view is that the example is being asked to establish only that unselected byproducts are possible and that present utility is not evidence of origin, not to model the causal process, and I want to know whether that weaker claim survives even if the designer objection is granted in full, or whether the objection is fatal after all. Handle explicitly the distinction usually drawn between a feature that selection built for its present role and one that selection merely co-opted into a role it never had — does the architectural example keep those apart or blur them, and does it matter for what the anti-adaptationist wants? I would also like you to say whether the objection, if it works, proves too much: artifact-based comparisons of organisms (organism as machine, as watch, as engineered contrivance) are the adaptationist's own stock in trade, so if any analogy drawn from deliberate construction presupposes a designer, the objection would seem to undercut the other side as well, which would make it self-undermining rather than decisive. If the example cannot carry the weight, tell me what comparison would, and give me the one consideration that would most change the verdict — for instance, what we would have to establish about a lineage before calling a trait a byproduct rather than an adaptation, and whether that is even the kind of thing the analogy is trying to capture.
