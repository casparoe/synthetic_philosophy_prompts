I have been reading Montaigne's essay on the cannibals, and I keep encountering a pattern in arguments about cultural difference for which I have no name. Someone describes a practice from a distant or past society — collective child-rearing, a ritual that appears cruel to us, an arrangement of sexual or economic life we find strange — and uses it to criticize their own society. The point is usually that our way is not the only way, that our moral certainties are provincial. But the criticism appears to work only by appealing to a value that the described society may not share, or that the critic treats as universal after all. The critic praises the other society for its communal spirit and then indicts our individualism, as though communal spirit were self-evidently good. Or the critic describes a practice as a form of freedom and then shames us for being unfree. The relativizing gesture thus depends on an unacknowledged standard, and the other culture functions as a mirror rather than being understood on its own terms.

This is not straightforward cultural relativism, because it does not suspend judgment; it judges our society by a standard it treats as universal while professing to have no universal standard. It is not simple ethnocentrism, because it is critical of our own ethnocentrism. I have heard terms such as romantic primitivism and the noble savage, but they appear to name the content of the idealization rather than the logical structure of the move. Is there an established name for this pattern? If there is, what is it, and how does it relate to cultural relativism, ethnocentrism, and the appeal to universal values? If there is no name, I would like you to propose one and define it carefully. I am less interested in the label than in the exact shape of the tension: what does the move presuppose, when is it legitimate, and what would a version of the critique look like that did not secretly rely on the standard it claims to reject?
