Ok so this might be a slightly weird one, but I'm part of a small group — me, an archaeologist friend, a philosopher, and two people from an econ dept — and we've somehow talked ourselves into spending a semester (maybe bleeding into summer, there's a little funding) actually working on one question properly instead of skimming everything and writing another lit review nobody reads. The question we keep circling back to is whether the agricultural revolution was a mistake, in the old "original affluent society" sense versus the more recent pushback (Scott's stuff on grain and the state, the health-cost archaeology, the cases where sedentism shows up before farming does). Our problem is we can't agree on what the actual open questions are. Half the material feels empirical-archaeological — did foragers really work less than farmers, did stature and dental health and anemia markers genuinely crater with the switch, does the Natufian-type evidence settle sedentism-first or not — and half of it is this mushier conceptual layer: what even counts as "affluence" (leisure, calories, health, equality, autonomy, absence of hierarchy?), a mistake for whom and at what scale and over what time horizon, and whether there was ever a single "revolution" to evaluate in the first place. There's also the wrinkle that hierarchy might predate farming — big storage, elaborate burials, monumental stuff at pre-agricultural sites — which would undercut the whole "farming caused inequality" story, and the demographic-ratchet idea that farming mostly produced more people rather than better-fed ones, so the verdict flips depending on whether you count lives or average welfare.

What I want from you is a prioritization, not a survey. If we only get one semester, which of these open questions are actually most worth the time, and in what order? I care way more about the reasoning than the list: for each, tell me why it's upstream of the others (does the affluence-measurement question, say, have to be settled before the empirical ones even mean anything, or is that backwards?), how tractable it is for our lopsided little group, and which ones look juicy but are basically dead ends or unanswerable with the evidence anyone has. Also flag anything I'm not even listing that you think should be near the top. And tell me what would move a question up or down — what specific finding or argument over the next year would actually change your ranking, not just "more research."

Last thing: be upfront about what's genuinely settled in the archaeology and anthropology versus what's contested among people who actually know the field. I don't want a contested reading dressed up as the consensus, and I don't want the settled bits relitigated as if they were open — I'd rather know which parts of this debate are actually live.
