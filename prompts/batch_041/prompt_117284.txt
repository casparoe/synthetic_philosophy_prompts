As a philosophy professor preparing a graduate seminar on inductive skepticism and formal learning theory, I want to pose the following question and have you answer it. Suppose a mathematically minded colleague tells David Hume that the no-free-lunch theorems have finally vindicated him: when performance is averaged over all possible target functions, every learning algorithm—inductive or not—has the same expected off-training-set performance, so, the colleague concludes, no method of induction can be universally justified. Imagine Hume's reply. Would he say the theorems (a) vindicate his skepticism, (b) restate it in formal dress without adding anything, (c) dissolve it by showing that the demand for a universal justification was misplaced, or (d) simply change the subject? Defend one reading, then state the strongest objection to it. Be explicit about what the no-free-lunch result assumes—for example, a uniform distribution over possible problems—and whether Hume's argument depends on the same assumption. Do not cite or quote sources; reason from the ideas. End with a single line of the form \answer{...} containing exactly one of: vindication, restatement, dissolution, change of subject.
