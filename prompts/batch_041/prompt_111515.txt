Our film night has a habit of turning into an argument about something else entirely — last Friday we watched a tedious French thing about a lighthouse and somehow ended up going at each other about Hannah's job, so that's probably on me for picking it. Hannah works in product for a phone company, and she mentioned that her team had just signed off a package of "cheap" changes: flimsier packaging, three help articles quietly deleted from the app, a recorded voice on the support line that tells you to use the website instead, one fewer reminder email before your contract renews. None of it is anything a customer would ever ring up about, and you'd only notice if you were looking for it; together it saves about £5m a year, which is roughly what it costs to keep her whole department of ninety people in work. She said she felt a bit grubby about it and couldn't put her finger on why, which felt wierd given she'd just saved the department.

Rosie said she could put her finger on it: (1) nobody is harmed by any of it. Offer any one customer the chance to undo the flimsier box or the missing email and they'd shrug. If a harm is small enough that the person it lands on wouldn't pay a penny to be rid of it, then for that person it's nothing, and a mountain of nothings is still nothing — ninety livelihoods against a mountain of nothing isn't even a contest. (2) Dev said that test proves far too much. Imagine ten thousand people each turn a dial up one notch, he said, and each notch on its own makes no difference that the man at the other end can feel; by the ten-thousandth notch he's in agony. On Rosie's test nobody in that chain harmed him — every nudge was imperceptible, and none of them is anything he'd pay to undo — yet there he is in agony, so "no one would pay to undo it" can't be the test. (3) Rosie said fine, but look at what the dial case actually shows: imperceptible contributions can add up into a harm as real as anything, which is her whole point — harms that are individually nothing do add up. (4) Dev said no, what adds up in that case is one man's suffering, and it adds up inside him; it shows nothing about piling up a billion people's un-felt inconvenience and setting that against one person's agony, because there's nobody in the pile who's worse off in any way, whereas the man on the dial plainly is. (5) Rosie said that's a distinction without a difference — either tiny effects count or they don't, and if ten thousand unnoticeable notches can produce agony then "unnoticeable" is doing no work whatsoever. Then she said Dev has to admit that some number of tiny annoyances outweighs the agony, or admit that tiny annoyances have literally zero weight, in which case ten to the power of ten of them still weigh zero, which is daft. (6) Dev said he'd rather say that some things simply aren't on the same scale at all — that you can't trade one person's week of agony against any number of other people's stubbed toes — and that if that leaves him with a line he can't draw exactly, he can live with it. Hannah said she now felt worse rather than better.

I genuinely couldn't tell who was making sense, so I said I'd ask someone who's thought about this properly, which is you. Please go through it move by move — (1) to (6) — and say where Rosie has it right, where Dev has it right, and where either of them is quietly smuggling something in; then say who has the better of it overall. And if the whole thing turns on something neither of them mentioned, I'd definately like to know what that is, because Hannah is back at work on Monday either way.
