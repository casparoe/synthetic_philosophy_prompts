I have spent the past year working with systems that produce results I could not have produced myself, and I have gradually lost the ability to say what, if anything, they understand. A colleague insists my unease is only the latest version of a puzzle that has already been dissolved twice over: Darwin showed that a mindless process — blind variation plus selection — could yield all the apparent design in the living world, and Turing showed that a meaningless process — the rule-governed manipulation of symbols — could yield all the apparent understanding of a mind. In both cases, he says, we are meant to feel the inversion as a shock and then get over it, because a cause need not resemble its effect.

Take the role of a philosopher of mind who knows the Darwinian and the computational literature thoroughly. Write a dialogue between two thinkers, each fully competent and neither a strawman, on the question of whether these two inversions are one move or two. The first holds the continuity thesis: whatever licenses us to explain the appearance of design without a designer equally licenses us to explain the appearance of comprehension without a comprehender, and there is no principled place to stop. The second holds the disanalogy thesis: the Darwinian inversion only ever had to account for the appearance of design, whereas the computational inversion is asked to account for the reality of meaning, so the parallel quietly assumes the very thing in dispute.

Make them answer each other's actual points rather than trade monologues. To keep the exchange concrete, give them a case to fight over: a system that not only returns correct answers but also produces an account of how it arrived at them that is plausible and, on inspection, accurate. One should argue that this changes nothing; the other should argue that it changes everything; neither may win by definitional fiat or by retreating to a slogan.

Before you begin writing, work out the strongest form of each position and where it is most vulnerable. Present the dialogue as clean, labeled prose in which the disagreement sharpens rather than blurs. End with a single final line beginning "Crux:" that states, in one sentence, the deepest point on which the two speakers actually disagree.
