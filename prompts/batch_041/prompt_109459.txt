I'm a novelist — about 250 pages into a book set inside a small regional commodity exchange, and the whole second act hinges on something I've gotten mildly obssessed with. A friend of mine who did a PhD in econ says there's basically a theorem (the no-trade theorem, I think Milgrom and Stokey?) showing that if a bunch of traders are rational, share the same prior, and it's common knowledge that they're all rational, then they can't trade just because they disagree — somebody has to be making a mistake. There's a cousin result about agreeing to disagree, too. But my whole plot is the opposite: my traders all know the others are sharp, they all know that the others know, and they still trade like maniacs because they read the same news completely differently. So is my book one big plot hole, or does the theorem not say what my friend thinks it says?

Here's what I actually want, though — not a summary of the theorem, I can look that up. I want to take the position seriously, as a claim, and ask what would count against it. Call the position this: disagreement-driven trade is a kind of incoherence; if two people with common knowledge of each other's rationality are trading purely because they disagree, then at least one of them is doing something wrong. What would count against that? Which observations, arguments, or formal results should genuinely move someone who holds it, sorted by how decisive they'd be — fatal down to merely awkward. And please be honest about the insulation problem, because it bugs me: every counterexample I think of seems to get absorbed with "well, then one of the assumptions failed." Is that a deep insight or a fancy way of saying nothing? Is there a version of the position that actually risks something, that predicts something we could go check? Also, what would a defender of the view themselves admit counts against it, not just what the skeptics say? My friend insists the common prior is the whole ballgame and that nobody in real life actually has one — is that the fatal crack, or just a technicality?
