I have spent about a decade arguing in comment threads about who should get to decide what, and I would like a straight answer rather than a tour of the literature. I know the shape of Sen's liberal paradox, and I know the standard escape hatches — give up minimal liberalism, give up weak Pareto, reinterpret rights as side constraints. What I want is the principle, if there is one, that my own verdicts are groping toward, and an honest accounting of which verdicts it forces me to abandon.

My verdicts. Whether I read a book that other people find degrading is my business and no one else's. Whether my neighbor reads it is his business, even though I would prefer that he didn't and think he would be better off not reading it; I do not think I may vote to stop him, and I do not think the neighborhood may either. Whether our street gets a speed bump is the residents' majority's business, not mine alone. Whether a community permits a rite that its own adult members freely undergo is the community's business, even if I find the rite degrading, so long as no one is coerced. But consent does not license everything: I think a person may not sell himself into slavery or consent to be killed, even if both parties prefer it, and I would say the same of a unanimous town vote to let a company poison its own reservoir. I also think a town may conscript me for jury duty, which sits badly with the thought that my time is mine. And I think a person may precommit, binding his own later choices, so that the later preference should not count against the binding.

Now notice what happens when I put two of these together. My neighbor and I are each deciding whether to read a scandalous book. I rank the options thus: neither of us reads it, above my reading it, above his reading it. He wants me to read it — he thinks I need it more than he does — but he would rather read it himself than have neither of us read it, so he ranks my reading it above his, and his above neither. I hold that each of us is decisive over whether he himself reads it, and that where we both prefer one option to another, that preference settles the matter. Work out what follows.

Then give me the principle my verdicts come closest to expressing, precise enough to test; say what it predicts about a case I have not listed, choosing the case yourself; say which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate; and tell me how confident you are in the whole diagnosis, singling out the step you are least sure of.
