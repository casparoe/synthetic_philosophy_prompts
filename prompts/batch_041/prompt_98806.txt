I spent the better part of forty years as a physiologist, and since retiring I have been working my way through moral philosophy on my own. I have constructed an argument that I find persuasive at every step and whose conclusion I cannot bring myself to accept, and I should like you to tell me where it fails. It runs as follows.

(1) A moral theory is acceptable only if a person who lives by it can do so without being estranged from the projects and attachments that give her life its point. (2) A theory that requires her, at the moment of action, to weigh those attachments against the claims of everyone else converts them into entries in an impersonal balance — things she has rather than things she is. (3) To be obliged to regard one's deepest commitments in that light is to be estranged from them. (4) So any theory that imposes such a requirement, and in particular any consequentialist theory, is unacceptable. (5) Consider the man who pulls his own wife rather than a stranger from the water. If what moves him is the thought that she is his wife and that this gives him a reason the stranger lacks, then he has had a thought too many; the truer description of what he does is simply that he saves her. (6) Yet ordinary, non-consequentialist morality demands precisely that surplus thought: it holds that he may save her only because it is permissible to give special weight to one's nearest. (7) So ordinary morality is estranging too, and by (1) unacceptable. (8) And if the objection is that the agent must be able to cite a justifying principle for what she does, then it condemns every action that any theory could require or permit, since every such action is backed by some principle. (9) A theory requiring no such backing would not be a theory of anything. (10) Hence the integrity objection proves too much, and by (4) we are left with the very consequentialism it was meant to discredit. The objection, I gather, is commonly attributed to Bernard Williams, though I have built this version myself and do not want a reading list; I want the argument diagnosed.

I would rather keep (1) than (5), if a choice must be made, but I want to know what each choice costs. In my old field we were careful to distinguish a specification of what a system does from the mechanism that produces it, and I keep wondering whether the consequentialist can borrow a distinction of that shape and whether doing so is a genuine defence or merely a change of subject. I also want to know whether the man who saves his wife without any justifying thought at all is really the ideal the objection recommends, or whether the objection has merely misdescribed him. Which step should I abandon, why did it look so plausible, and what does abandoning it commit me to?

Please answer in continuous prose — no headings, no numbered or bulleted lists, no bold text and no tables — the way one would write a letter. I am not interested in citations or further reading; I want the diagnosis and the bill of costs.
