I sit on the steering committee of a university institute for logic and the philosophy of mathematics. We have been offered a substantial bequest to endow a permanent fellowship and an annual public lecture bearing Frege's name. The donor's letter attaches a single condition: that in all public framing of the gift, the institute present Frege's logical and philosophical achievements "in their own right, without political distraction." Two things have forced the issue onto our agenda. First, a graduate student has proposed adding the diary Frege kept in 1924, with its antisemitic and anti-democratic passages, to the syllabus of our history-of-analytic-philosophy course. Second, our own website currently says nothing about the diary at all, and a member of the committee has pointed out that accepting the bequest under the donor's wording would effectively commit us to keeping it that way.

The committee is split. One faction reads the donor's condition as a demand for exactly the sanitized Frege the student's proposal is meant to resist, and wants to decline the money. A second wants to accept it and treat the diary as an unfortunate private document with no bearing on the philosophy. A third wants to accept, but only with a public statement of our own.

Draft the short written opinion I will circulate before the vote. It should recommend a concrete course of action — accept the bequest, decline it, or accept with specific conditions — with a time frame, and it should give the reasoning, including an honest statement of the strongest case against the recommendation. Do not settle the matter by asserting that "the work stands on its own"; that is precisely what is in dispute. Instead, distinguish the relations that could hold between a philosopher's political views and his technical work and take a position on which of them are actually at issue here: whether the diary bears on the causal history of Frege's views, on what those views mean once read in its light, or on their epistemic warrant, and say which of these the committee should act on and which it should refuse to act on. Consider, in particular, whether anything in Frege's own philosophical framework — his Platonism about thoughts, his conception of an impersonal realm of sense, his insistence on the objectivity of logic — makes the diary's content merely incidental to the philosophy or, on the contrary, continuous with it; treat this as a hypothesis to be weighed rather than a conclusion to be announced, and say what evidence within the philosophical corpus would count for or against it. Address the pedagogical question the student raised, including what should actually happen in the classroom and in our public materials. Finally, say explicitly what the institute would be claiming about Frege, and not merely what it would be doing about the money. Assume no bibliography is needed; the committee wants the argument.
