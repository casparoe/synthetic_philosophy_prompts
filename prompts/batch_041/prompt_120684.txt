For a couple of years I've been reading about the quest for the historical Jesus, and I keep meeting the same argument that never resolves. The traditional method was to test sayings and deeds against a set of criteria — a saying is more likely to go back to Jesus if it would have embarrassed the early church, or if it's attested in multiple independent sources, or if it can't be explained as coming from either the Jewish setting or the later Christian one — and by that route to peel away the later layers and reach the man himself. But the criteria have been attacked from every direction: for assuming what the Jewish and Christian settings were, for mistaking a later invention for a memory, for supposing that a saying has a single original form at all. Many who work on this now seem to have given up on isolating particular authentic sayings and turned instead to how memories of Jesus formed and circulated. What I can't tell is what kind of disagreement this actually is. Is it about the evidence and what the texts can bear? About what 'authentic' is even meant to mean — the exact words, the gist, or the kind of impression he left? Or is something upstream doing the work, like whether these writings should be read as history at all rather than as theology set in the past? My hunch is that the two sides are answering different questions under one name, and that this, rather than any shortage of evidence, is why the argument never closes. Is that right, or is it a genuine disagreement that better evidence or a sharper method could settle? And whatever you conclude, tell me what would actually count as progress here — a clarified criterion, a distinction, a different way of posing the question — or whether we should expect the impasse to last. Please separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who know this material, and don't present a contested position as the consensus.
