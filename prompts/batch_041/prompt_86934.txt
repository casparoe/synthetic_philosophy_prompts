Around my reading group, the line "societies don't collapse, they only change" gets repeated as a conversation-ender; last week it was used to shut down a comparison between the collapse of the classic Maya cities and the Late Bronze Age, on the grounds that both were merely reorganizations. I want the precise claims that sentence could be making laid out as a menu I can take back to the group. Start with the near-trivial readings — every society is always changing, "collapse" is just a period label we impose — and move up through the substantive to the radical: that there are real thresholds of demographic, institutional, or infrastructural loss; or, at the limit, that collapse is never a fact about the past but only a value judgment or an artifact of archaeological periodization. Include readings that turn on which marker is at issue — political centralization, population, long-distance exchange, monumental building, literacy — and say where those markers can come apart, so that one and the same case counts as collapse by one measure and as flourishing by another. For each reading, give a verdict (trivial, plausible, contentious, or false) and say whether it would actually license the move of refusing the comparison. Then tell me which reading the people who repeat the line most likely intend, and whether any of them makes it more than a definitional shuffle. No citations needed; I want the distinctions and a verdict on each.
