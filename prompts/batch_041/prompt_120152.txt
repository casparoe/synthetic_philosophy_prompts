I host a public interview series in political theory: one guest, seventy-five minutes, on stage, no slides, followed by half an hour of questions from the audience. My next guest has spent a decade reconstructing and defending Simone Weil's case against political parties. He is not a Weil scholar by trade and does not treat her text as a relic: his book-length argument is that the case against parties has been misread for most of a century as a moral complaint about loyalty and integrity when it is really an epistemic complaint about attention, and that it can be detached from Weil's mystical premise that truth is sacred and rebuilt in wholly secular terms. Two further claims are his own. First, that a party does two distinct jobs, one aggregative and one formative, and that the formative job — the production of a shared way of seeing — cannot be reformed away, because competition for office selects for organizations that are good at it: internal democracy, a ban on whipping, published minutes, no leader at all, none of it touches the mechanism. Second, that the argument cuts hardest against the parties he himself votes for, and that this is evidence of its honesty rather than a reductio of it. He is a good interviewee and a slippery one: pressed, he retreats to the claim that he is only being faithful to Weil, or to the thought that any purposive organization faces the same problem.

He will not have the text in front of him and neither will the audience, so nothing may depend on my quoting a passage back at him, and I do not want questions that let him summarise the essay, which most of the audience will have read. What I want is a set of questions that make him work: that take his three theses and find the places where they prove too much, prove too little, or quietly borrow the mysticism he says he has dropped. Where the literature presses — the epistemic and adversarial defences of partisan contestation, the sortitionist alternative, the fact that the parties he condemns have been the only vehicles that ever delivered anything to the people he says he is worried about — I want the question a well-read opponent would ask, with the follow-up already keyed to the answer he is most likely to give, so that I am not improvising when he takes the easy exit. Take the positions as I have stated them; do not attribute them to any published work or supply citations for them.

Write the interview as follows, using these section headings in this order:

- "Through-line" (about eighty words): the single arc the interview should follow, stated as a claim about him rather than a list of topics.
- "Opening" (one question, two sentences): it must put his central thesis to him in a way that forces him to state it rather than presuppose it, and it must not be answerable by summarising the essay.
- "The case under pressure" (five questions, sixty to ninety words each including the follow-up): one aimed at each load-bearing element — the attention thesis, the secular reconstruction, the drift argument, the aggregative/formative distinction, and the self-implication claim. At least one must be a case in which his own commitments give him a reason to dislike his conclusion.
- "The alternative" (two questions with follow-ups): what is supposed to happen instead, institutionally, at the scale of a modern state, and how he would know if he had got it. Do not let him answer by gesturing at deliberation or local assemblies; require a mechanism and a failure condition.
- "The turn" (two questions): designed so that his usual deflections are unavailable. If he says he is only reading Weil, the next question must be about what he adds that she did not say; if he says every purposive organization is alike, the next must be about why parties specifically are the target.
- "Closing" (one question): what he would have to learn for the view to be false.
- "Where he will slip" (about a hundred and fifty words): the three or four moves he is most likely to make in place of an answer, the one-line follow-up that closes each, and what to do in the audience Q&A if the first question is a hostile one asking whether he is simply anti-democratic.

Twelve questions or so in total. No question that asks him to say what Weil said, no question about how he came to the topic, and no question that could be answered by someone who had not read him.
