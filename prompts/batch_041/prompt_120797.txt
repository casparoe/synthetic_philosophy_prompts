I teach a graduate seminar in applied ethics that spends its last third on the moral status of animals, and I've just come out of a three-hour argument with a friend — a philosopher of mind by training, a utilitarian by temperament — that ended with each of us convinced the other was confused. I want to turn it into the essay question on the take-home final. Her case ran like this. The insect debate is usually staged as a question about evidence: whether insects have the right kind of centralised processing, whether they show the behavioural and pharmacological markers we associate with pain, and so on. But she thinks that framing misses the structure of the problem. Put the probability that an insect's aversive state feels like anything at something small, one in a thousand or one in a hundred. Multiply by the number of insects alive at any moment, which she put at around a quintillion. The expected number of suffering subjects is then somewhere between a quadrillion and ten quadrillion — which dwarfs every human being who has ever lived and every vertebrate alive today — and on any view that takes expected value seriously, that is simply where the moral weight of the world is. The low probability, she says, is doing no work at all; it is a multiplier, and it has been swamped. I pushed back that a credence of that shape is not the kind of thing that can be multiplied: hers is a credence over a single fact about a kind, so it is perfectly correlated across every individual, whereas a chance worth multiplying would have to vary from insect to insect, and this is exactly the move people distrust in "mugging" arguments where a minuscule probability of an enormous stake is used to swamp everything else. She replied that the objection proves too much, since the same structure appears in every case where we act under factual uncertainty about who is affected, and that if my objection worked it would rule out caring about farmed fish or about future generations just as surely as about insects. We didn't settle it, and I've come to think the real crux is something I cannot get students to see by lecturing: whether the probability that a creature is a subject of experience can enter an expected-value calculation the way the probability of rain can.

So what I need from you is the essay question for that final, together with the materials to mark it. The students will have done the standard material on animal cognition and on the measurement of animal welfare, so the question cannot be answerable by reciting it; it should force them to say what kind of thing the probability of sentience is — a credence over a determinate fact, a degree of a graded property, a decision weight, or something else — and whether the arithmetic goes through on each reading, and it should make them confront the symmetry objection my friend raised, that any argument against the multiplication threatens ordinary concern for fish, for future people, and for marginal humans, rather than letting them wave that away in a sentence. Pitch it so a good candidate could answer it in ninety minutes, and give me a model answer of roughly 700 words in continuous prose. Then give me the marking scheme, worth 20 marks, as exactly five numbered points, each a sentence or two, with no introduction and no closing remark, since I will paste those five straight into the rubric; each point should name a specific move that separates an A from a B answer, and where it is useful, the characteristic mistake that should cost marks. No citations or reading list, please — I'll supply those myself.
