Hi, I'm the collections care officer at a regional museum — about 40,000 objects, three curators, nobody on staff with any philosophy — and I need help getting from an abstract puzzle to something I can actually run at my desk.

Roughly twice a month a treatment proposal lands on me that turns out not to be a conservation question at all. The Roman torso: the head and both arms are eighteenth-century additions, a curator wants them off, and then wants a newly carved head put on. The whale in the entrance hall: maybe a third real bone, the rest cast and sculpted, and its the thing every school group comes for. The longcase clock somebody wants to make run again by swapping the movement. When these come up, one trustee says it's the same object as long as it's the same material, another says it's the same object as long as it looks the same from ten feet, and the registrar just wants to know whether the accession number survives. What actually rides on it is money and honesty: the record, the label, the valuation, whether we can lend it as 'the' object, and once in a while whether we're allowed to dispose of it at all. I usually have a day or two, sometimes an afternoon if something's wet, and anything that changes a label goes to the director, who will not read more than a page.

I'm fairly sure this is the statue and the lump of clay in a museum uniform, and I know the usual moves in outline — two objects in one place, one thing under two sortals, the statue as a phase in the career of the clay, form and matter — but I've definately never seen anyone get from those to a rule, and I can't either. So: write me a decision procedure.

What it has to have. The questions in the order I should ask them, and what counts as a sufficient answer to each, phrased so I can use it with a curator who thinks 'sortal' is a kind of soup. A point where it stops and says no, this is not the original any more and you may not go on calling it that. A point where it hands the thing to a person, because the question is one of values rather than facts, and it tells me which person that should be. And a small number of tests I can run in the storeroom — things I can settle by looking and by asking the curator one question, not by reading.

The half-idea I've been groping toward, which you should either fix or throw out: I think some of our things matter as this particular stuff with this particular history (the bones, a relic) and some matter as a design that could in principle be made again and still be the thing (a cast, a chair we let people sit on), and that a lot of our fights are two people asking under different headings without saying which. I also suspect there is more than one identity question in play — one for the accession record, one for the scholar, one for the insurer, one for the label — and that these can honestly come apart, though I don't know when that's legitimate and when it's just wanting to have it both ways. And if the truthful verdict is sometimes 'not the same object, but the same individual at a later stage', I'd like to know that's on the menu and what it would change for me.

Then run the procedure start to finish on all three cases above. At least one of them has to come out in a way the director won't like, and say so plainly instead of softening it. Finish by telling me what the procedure can't do, and where I'll be fooling myself that I've got a fact when I've really just made a choice.

No citations please, I won't chase them down, and no headings, bullets, bold text or tables — plain paragraphs, like an email, because I'll be reading this on my phone between meetings.
