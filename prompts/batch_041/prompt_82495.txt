Hi — slightly odd ask, and I'd rather you get it right than be quick about it. I cover science and ideas for a monthly, and a friend who runs a comparative politics MA seminar has roped me into guest-teaching the week on democratic backsliding. Their module runs the whole Hungary-Poland-Turkey-US circuit, and by the time I turn up the students will have read all the usual stuff, so if I set them a normal essay they'll just recite 'competitive authoritarianism' and the Hungary story back at me and we'll all have wasted an afternoon.

So: write me one take-home essay question for that session. Closed book, no internet, 90 minutes, 20 marks.

Here's the angle I want. 'Backsliding' is a decline word — it's relational, you can only slide back from somewhere — and I think that smuggles in three seperate questions that get mashed together in the punditry: where is the baseline you're sliding from, what's the measuring stick, and who gets to be the judge. I want the question to force them to prise those apart, and then to say whether the 'it's just a partisan label' complaint bites on all three or only on the third — and whether calling the whole thing an essentially contested concept is a real answer to that charge or just a shrug in a nicer jumper.

To stop them reaching for stock examples, build the question around a case I've made up: a government wins a genuinely clean election, then uses its majority to rewrite the rules so that the next defeat is harder to pull off — courts, media, election administration, the lot. Then the opposition wins anyway and starts doing the same things back, in the name of restoring the democracy. Add a second, shorter case: a country that was already semi-authoritarian before any of this started, so it can't backslide much because it never had that far to fall. Ask them what each case does to the concept — does it show the concept is partisan, or only that it needs a threshold, or that 'backsliding' is simply the wrong word and we should be talking about something else (and if so, what, and what changes if we do).

Two things to keep out of it. Don't make them cite indices or scores — they can't look them up, and I want the argument, not the brand names. And no citations expected at all.

Give me: the question itself; a model answer of about 700 words; and a marking scheme of five must-hit points worth 20 marks between them, with the marks for each and a one-liner per point on how students usually botch it. Then a short 'don't reward this' list — the recitations that should earn nothing, like naming indices, quoting a headline score, or asserting that a country is a hybrid regime and stopping there. I'd rather have that section than leave it implicit. Finish with a last line beginning 'Bottom line:' giving me, in one sentence, the single move that separates a top answer from a middling one.
