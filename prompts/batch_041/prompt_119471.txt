Ok so I've fallen down a rabbit hole and I need help figuring out where the actual work is. I dont have a philosophy background — I did econ in college and I read a lot of blogs — but I've gotten kind of obsessed with a problem I keep bumping into. Here it is: I want to do as much good as I can with my life and my money, and I try to reason about consequences. But when I actually try to trace what my choices do, it falls apart. Not in the normal "hard to predict" way. The coffee I bought this morning changed the baristas day, which changed something else, and so on out forever. And when I try to think about the long run, a tiny nudge now could plausibly matter enormously, and I have no idea which direction. It's not even that I can assign probabilities to the good and bad outcomes — I can't list the outcomes. So what does it even mean to say one option has better consequences than another?

People who write about this seem to split it in ways I half understand. One version is that we just don't have the empirical facts yet — in principle we could learn them, it's just a lot of work. Another version is structural: the causal web is so sprawling and chaotic that no amount of research fixes it, ever. Someone also made this move on me, that it's not a problem for this way of thinking at all, because any other way of deciding — rules, gut feeling, virtues — also has to be cashed out in terms of what actually happens in the world, so everybody is equally in the dark and nothing follows from it. And then the flip side: if the stakes far in the future are astronomically large, then even a one-in-a-billion chance of a huge effect swamps everything else, which seems to make the problem worse rather than better.

Here's what I actually want from you. I have a small pot of money — low six figures — and I could either just give it away or use it to support two or three people for a couple of years to only think and write about this. If I do the latter, which open questions should they work on, and in what order? I'm less interested in the list than in the why: which questions are upstream of the others, in the sense that settling them would settle or dissolve a bunch of the rest; which are actually tractable for a couple of smart people with no lab, no dataset, and no institutional backing; which ones would just be paying someone to spin their wheels; and what I'd need to see in the next year or two to change the ordering.

Two things I'd like you to be opinionated about. First, is the "everyones clueless, so it's a wash" move actually right, or does it just rename the problem? Second, is there a version of this that's real and hard even if you throw out the far-future stuff entirely and just ask whether my ordinary choices — what I buy, where I work, whether I have kids — do any determinate good or harm at all? Or does the problem only get its teeth from the astronomical stakes? If it's the latter, say so plainly, because then I'd rather fund the big-scale version.

One more thing I keep going back and forth on, and I'd like your read on whether it's a real distinction or just word games: someone told me the hard part isn't telling good from bad, it's telling better from worse — like maybe I can never know if an act was good, but I might still be able to rank two acts against each other. Is that a genuine crack in the problem or is it just restating it?
