I'm co-teaching an upper-level seminar on collapse this term (16 students, 90 minutes, a mix of archaeology and philosophy people) and I want one session where they actually feel how much of the answer depends on what you've decided is the thing that's collapsing, instead of me just saying it at them. My co-instructor is a classicist who thinks the Late Bronze Age is the only case worth the name, and the students show up with the pop-science version where a society does something dumb and gets punished for it. I want to wreck both of those habits in one sitting, ideally without either of us lecturing.

What I've sketched is this, and I'd like you to build it into something actually runnable. Four teams, each gets a one-page data sheet for one case -- the eastern Mediterranean around 1200 BC, the southern Maya lowlands in the 9th-10th centuries, Rapa Nui, and a fourth case that you invent outright, with invented figures, so nobody can coast on what they half-remember. Crucially each team also gets a charter: a half-page mandate written in the voice of some seperate fictional stakeholder (a heritage body, a diaspora association, an insurance syndicate, whatever you like) that fixes for that team what counts as the society and what counts as failure. I want the charter to pin down four things, and I've been calling them the four slots in my notes: the bearer (what is the society -- the palace system, or the population, or the craft tradition, or the trade network?), the capacity (what would it be losing -- political autonomy, population, literacy, a way of building?), the threshold (how much loss before you call it), and the baseline (collapse compared to what -- the generation before, or the trajectory it would plausibly have had otherwise?). The teams fill out a registrar's form with those four slots plus a verdict (collapsed / not collapsed / transformed), a 0-10 severity number, and their confidence, and post it on the board. Then the twist: they swap charters with another team that has the same data sheet and have to redo the whole verdict under someone else's mandate with the same facts. After that I want a short tribunal round where two teams who ended up with opposite verdicts on the same case have to work out whether their disagreement is about the data or about the charter, and then a last round where every team writes one sentence about their case that stays true no matter which charter you hold -- I want them to see that some facts travel across frames and some verdicts don't. Then the debrief, where the idea finally gets named, mapping each moment onto the parts of it.

Please give me the materials list, a minute-by-minute timeline for the 90, and the debrief script. And be blunt about the failure modes, because I've run enough of these to know there will be some: teams will fight about the facts rather than the frame, someone will refuse to give a verdict because it depends, and transformed is going to become the safe sophisticated answer that everyone retreats to, so I want a mechanic that makes that answer cost something -- maybe the registrar rejects a vague transformation verdict unless you say what became what and who paid for it. Also, a team might spot the trick in round one and spoil it for the others; tell me what to do with that person (make them registrar?). And what do I do with the student who announces at minute ten that collapse is just a colonialist narrative and refuses to engage with the cases at all? Then the honest bit: tell me which feature of the whole idea this activity can't reproduce and that I'll have to supply myself in the debrief. I already suspect it makes relativism feel too cheap, and that it can't give them the timescale -- real collapses take generations and nobody inside one is holding a verdict form -- but tell me what else I'm missing.
