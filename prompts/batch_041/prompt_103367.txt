I've been reading Hobbes for a seminar and I've got this argument that seems to prove too much and I cant find the exit. A friend built it and now I half believe it. It goes:

(1) We have certain knowledge of something only if we know its causes.
(2) We know the causes of things we make ourselves.
(3) In geometry, we make the objects: we define the terms, draw the figures, construct the proofs.
(4) So geometry is a paradigm of certain knowledge.
(5) In politics, we also make the object: the commonwealth is constituted by our covenants, our authorization, our willing it into being.
(6) So politics is also certain knowledge — maybe more certain than geometry, since the commonwealth is more completely our artifact than any triangle.
(7) But politics is the most disputed, uncertain, backbiting domain there is, and if we make it were enough, then any made-up game or novel would be a science too.
(8) So one of (1)-(6) is false.

I'd rather keep (3) and (4): I think geometry really is the model of certainty, and I think Hobbes is onto something about why. But I cant see where the argument cheats. Is one of the premises just false, or is theres a slip between two of them? A friend says the fix is to say we're makers in geometry but only participants in politics. Is that distinction real or just relabeling? Which premise should I give up, and what does giving it up cost me elsewhere? If you tell me to reject (2), do I lose the explanation of geometry's specialness? If I reject (5), am I saying the commonwealth isn't made by us at all, and can I say that without turning Hobbes into a Platonist about politics? Also tell me how confident you are in your diagnosis and which step you're least sure about.
