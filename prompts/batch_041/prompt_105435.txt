I am a novelist at work on a book set in a near-future ministry that must divide a fixed terraforming budget between a small settlement of fully flourishing lives and a vastly larger population whose lives would be only barely worth living. In drafting the minister's interior monologue, I have hit a conceptual wall. I had understood that the repugnant conclusion is a theorem: that once one accepts impartial concern for future people, along with a few innocuous-looking assumptions about adding lives and transitivity, one is forced to prefer the vast marginal population to the smaller flourishing one, and that the only escapes are to deny that any life can be barely worth living or to abandon consequentialism altogether. If that is right, then my minister's dilemma is less a dilemma than a proof she must either embrace or reject, and the novel's ethical texture should reflect that binary.

But something feels off. The label repugnant seems to be doing rhetorical work that a theorem would not need, and I have a nagging sense that population axiology contains a whole family of positions, such as average views, critical-level views, person-affecting views, and appeals to incomparability or to non-Archimedean value, that are not obviously irrational. So my question is this: since the repugnant conclusion is a proven result, is the only intellectually honest move for my minister to accept it and revise her horror, or else reject the entire framework that generates it? Or am I confusing a vivid implication of one particular theory with a theorem about all serious theories? I want to get the logical status right before I write the scene, because the difference between a forced conclusion and a contested verdict changes what the character can be shown thinking.
