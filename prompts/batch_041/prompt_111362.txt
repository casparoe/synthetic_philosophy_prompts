I'm a fourth-year philosophy student finishing a seminar on the philosophy of history, and this year I also sit as the undergraduate representative on my college's Committee on Historical Recognition — the body that decides what to do when a building, prize, or professorship turns out to be named for someone whose record has since become contested. Our next case comes to a vote at the end of the month, and since I'm the only member who has read anything on how historians and philosophers think about causation, the chair has asked me to circulate a short written opinion beforehand, short enough to be read aloud at the start of the meeting.

The case is this. A residence hall has been named since 1957 for a local industrialist, and the committee's background report establishes that the fortune behind his gift came from a firm that, along with essentially every other firm in its sector, relied on a labor practice that did serious and lasting harm to the workers caught in it. The report is careful on one point: his firm was not pivotal. Had it refused the practice, the practice would have continued elsewhere, the same workers would have been recruited, and the same injuries would have occurred; no particular worker's harm can be traced to his firm rather than to the sector as a whole. Two colleagues have drawn opposite conclusions. One says that settles it — he cannot be said to have caused the harm, since the harm would have happened without him, so there is nothing here to rename. The other says causation is beside the point, since he chose his firm's practices, profited from them, and could have borne the costs of doing otherwise. A third colleague has circulated a draft test she wants us to adopt for all future cases: a name comes down only if the person's conduct was both a necessary condition of the specific harm and a departure from what their contemporaries were already doing.

Please draft that written opinion for me. It should land on a concrete recommendation for this case with a specific action and a time frame for carrying it out, say whether the committee should adopt the proposed two-part test or something else, and give the reasoning in a form my colleagues can actually argue with. The part I care most about is the middle: I want a clear account of what overdetermination does and does not show — why the fact that a harm would have occurred anyway is treated as decisive by some writers and as nearly irrelevant by others, how that bears on assigning responsibility to a particular person rather than to a practice or a sector, and where the line falls between explaining a historical outcome, where multiple causes are the norm rather than a defect, and blaming someone for it. Please engage seriously with the colleague who thinks the case is closed in the benefactor's favor; I don't want the opinion to read as though his position were merely an excuse, and I would rather it be honest about what would have to be true for him to be right. No citations or reading lists — the committee won't use them, and I'd rather have the reasoning in your own words.
