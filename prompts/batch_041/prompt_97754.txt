I have no academic training, but I have spent several years volunteering in the storeroom of a small regional museum, and much of that time has gone into thinking about copies. I would like to turn a half-formed idea into a short research proposal — for a small grant from a foundation that funds independent, public-facing work in the humanities: six months, one long essay, one public talk — and I would like you to draft that proposal for me.

My idea is this. Disputes about fakes are usually conducted as if there were a single thing, the original, whose value the fake simply lacks, and as if that value were a property of the object itself. I have come to suspect that what we call an original is better understood as a relation — a relation between a thing and the history that produced it and the copies that descend from it — rather than a property the thing possesses on its own. If that suspicion is right, then the value we place on originals ought to survive certain changes to the physical object, and ought to be destroyed by certain changes that leave the physical object entirely untouched. I can think of cases that seem to cut both ways, and I cannot yet tell whether they refute the relational idea or merely refine it.

The cases I keep returning to are these. A painting restored so heavily that little of its original material survives, yet still treated as the same work. That same painting re-attributed to another hand, where nothing perceptible changes and yet the value collapses. An exact replica executed by the artist's own studio under his close direction, which he never touched. A work the artist reproduces decades later, so that there are now two originals and no forgery at all. A digital work with no privileged copy, in which the artist declares every copy to be an original. And the case I find hardest: a new work generated by a machine trained on an artist's output, with the right surface and no human maker whatsoever. If originality is a relation to a history, what precisely is on the other end of the relation — the maker's intention, the causal chain of copying, the community's recognition — and can a single account of it deliver the right verdict on all of these cases?

I would like the proposal to state the question sharply enough that it has a definite answer; to explain why the obvious accounts — that originals are valuable because they are unique, or because they are perceptually distinctive, or because they carry a history — each leave something unexplained; and to set out what I would actually do, step by step across six months: which cases I would analyze first, which candidate accounts I would formulate and then try to break, which distinctions I would need to keep separate, and in what order. I would also like an honest account of what a result would look like — what would count as having found the right account, and what would count as the project failing — and a realistic sense of what fits in six months and one essay. And I would like it to say, without overclaiming, why the question matters beyond my own curiosity: to museums deciding what to display, to collectors, and to anyone who must put a price or a label on a thing.

Please leave out any reading list or citations; I will supply those myself once I know what I am arguing for. I am not trying to summarize what others have written. I am trying to find out whether my suspicion can be turned into a position that survives its own counterexamples.
