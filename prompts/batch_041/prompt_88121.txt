I sit on the interpretation committee of a national heritage body — the panel that has to sign off the wording of every label and panel in our permanent galleries before the text goes to the printers — and I have to bring a written opinion to Thursday's meeting. The case is our planned flagship gallery on an ancient kingdom of the Near East, and the trouble is concentrated in the three labels that carry the spine of the story. What visitors will take away — that a particular queen reigned for a particular span, that a particular siege was broken by a particular stratagem, that a particular treaty was concluded — is drawn almost entirely from a single narrative source compiled several centuries after the events, at a court with an evident interest in flattering the dynasty that commissioned it; a second thread rests on a fragmentary inscription whose surviving letters can be read either as a royal title or as a personal name; and for the siege in particular, nothing else in the surviving record mentions it at all. The chair's worry is not really about the facts; it is that half the committee wants the gallery to tell the story straight, on the grounds that it is the only story we have and that a museum which hedges every sentence will lose the public, while the other half wants the labels to say, in effect, that this is legend, and that presenting it otherwise is a failure of scholarly nerve. He has asked me for a recommendation the committee can actually vote on, and for an honest account of the case against it.

So what I need is a short written opinion — a page or two, the sort of thing that can be minuted and acted upon — that does four things. First, it should recommend a concrete course of action for these three labels specifically, rather than a general philosophy of museums: say what each label may assert as fact, what it may report as tradition without asserting, and what it must leave out, and give a timeframe for redrafting that still lets us meet the printing deadline. Second, it should say what standard of proof our labels ought to meet, and defend that standard against the strongest version of the opposing view — I would far rather have a rule I can apply than a mood. One member has already floated a bright line: nothing may be stated as fact if it is attested by a single source written more than a couple of centuries after the events and not independently corroborated. I am not sure that rule is right, but I cannot yet say why, so please test it against these labels and against the ordinary working practice of ancient historians. Third, it should say how much weight the argument from silence ought to carry when an entire episode goes unmentioned everywhere else, and whether something like the criterion of embarrassment — trusting a detail precisely because it damages the teller's own case — should be allowed to rescue any of this. Fourth, it should set out the underlying reasoning in terms a lay trustee can follow: I want the committee to see why this is not pedantry but the ordinary difference, running from the earliest historians onwards, between what a writer could vouch for himself and what he merely repeated at second or third hand, and why the two demands now before us — a public that wants a story and a record that will not support one — are not equally satisfiable. Please write the opinion in British English.
