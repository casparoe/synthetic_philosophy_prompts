I teach an upper-level decision-theory seminar with 16 students in one 90-minute session, and I want an in-class game that makes counterfactual mugging and XOR blackmail feel like live decision problems rather than set pieces; they should come away having felt the pull of both paying and refusing, and seeing how the timing of the choice changes which reasons are available. Design the activity around a variant I specify, not the standard published cases. It should have three rounds. Round one is a pre-commitment round: before a coin flip, each player seals a policy card stating whether they will pay a small fee in the heads branch; after the flip, players in the tails branch learn the outcome and are offered the chance to pay, with the payoff arranged so that their present choice is causally inert but evidentially revealing. Round two is an XOR round: the facilitator sends a message to a player iff a hidden bad state obtains XOR that player pays a small fee; if the player pays, the bad state is absent, and if the player refuses, the bad state obtains, so refusing is causally clean but evidentially disastrous. Round three nests the two: players write a policy before a public random seed is revealed, and the payoff depends on the XOR of their payment and the seed's parity, so that pre-commitment, correlation, and the timing of choice all matter at once. Use points rather than money, keep the stakes small but real, and do not name either problem until the debrief. Give me the rules, roles, materials, payoff scheme, and a minute-by-minute timeline; a debrief that maps each round onto causal, evidential, and pre-commitment or updateless reasoning; a candid account of what will predictably go wrong the first time and how to handle it; and an explicit note on which feature of the problems the game cannot reproduce and that I must supply in the debrief. End with a final line beginning Summary: followed by one sentence stating the core mechanic.
