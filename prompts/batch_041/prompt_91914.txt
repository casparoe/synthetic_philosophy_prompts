I am a staff writer who covers philosophy, history, and the human sciences, and in three weeks I am on a public panel whose theme is whether history has a direction. I will be defending a thesis that I expect to be unpopular with at least two of my co-panelists, and I want to walk in prepared for the question period rather than improvising. Take the role of a philosopher of history who is also an experienced debate coach and a close reader of audiences; think carefully about the psychology of each interlocutor before you write anything.

My thesis is this. The familiar idea that history bends toward justice is not one claim but three, and they are routinely conflated. There is a predictive claim: that history has a direction and that the direction tends toward justice. There is a constitutive claim: that our very concept of justice, or of progress, is defined by such movement, so that the slogan is true almost by construction and tells us nothing about the world. And there is a hortatory claim: that the slogan is a commitment we adopt and act on, not a description of anything. I will argue that most disputes about the slogan are really disputes about which of the three claims is on the table; that once they are separated, only the hortatory reading survives scrutiny; and that this is the interesting result rather than a defeat. As a piece of analytical machinery, I will also propose that any arc-of-history claim should be forced to specify three things — the metric along which movement is alleged (which quantity, measured how), the threshold that would count as movement rather than noise, and the horizon over which we are entitled to look — and that most such claims fail to name all three.

The panel is hosted jointly by my magazine and a university's history department. My co-panelists are: a historian in the liberal-progress tradition who treats moral improvement as a documented long-run trend; a theologian committed to a doctrine of providence, for whom history's direction is a matter of divine governance rather than of human achievement; a postcolonial historian who is deeply suspicious of progress narratives as instruments of empire; and an empirically minded social scientist who studies long-run trends in violence and rights and will want numbers, not metaphors. The room will hold educated general readers as well as academic historians and a few philosophers.

Begin by brainstorming, in a short private section, how each panelist's priors will shape the objection they actually raise, then give me the eight or so objections I am most likely to face — ordered by how likely each is times how much damage it does, not by how easy it is to answer. Phrase each objection in the vocabulary of the person who will raise it, so that I can hear it coming. For each, give me a reply I can deliver in about two minutes, and a candid note on where that reply is soft or where I am conceding more than I realize. Flag which objections I should simply concede rather than fight, and which single reply has to land for the talk to succeed. Finish with the one objection I am hoping nobody raises, and why it is the dangerous one. No citations or reading lists, please; I will supply those myself.
