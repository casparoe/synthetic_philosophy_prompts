I teach philosophy of history and I'm prepping to interview a guest for our department podcast -- a scholar whose whole thing is that the way we talk about "silences in the archive" is confused. Her line, roughly: silences aren't gaps left by accident that the historian heroically fills in; they're the positive product of the very record-keeping practices that generate the documents, so a silence is itself a kind of evidence, and the fashionable move of reading "against the grain" to recover suppressed voices actually smuggles the state's own categories back in and flatters the historian doing it. She'd rather we read for the apparatus -- ask what a given record system was built to notice and what it was built to make unremarkable -- and she thinks the whole ethic of "recovery" or "repair" rests on a metaphysics of presence she wants to ditch.

Act as someone who knows this literature cold and thinks carefully. Start by brainstorming where the position is actually vulnerable, then give me eight to ten interview questions that go past letting her just restate the view. I need at least one question on the quietism charge -- if the silenced can't be recovered, why doesn't this just let the powerful off the hook? -- one on the difference between a silence and an outright destruction or a plain absence of records, one on how she isn't just a positivist telling us to stick to what the documents actually show, and one on the regress in who gets to decide what counts as a silence in the first place. For each of those, add a follow-up keyed to the answer she's most likely to give. No headings, bullets, bold, or tables -- just continuous prose I can read like an email.
