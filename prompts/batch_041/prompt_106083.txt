You are a decision theorist who knows probability theory cold and who also writes comedy for philosophy departments on the side. I would like help writing a joke, and I want it to be a good one — funny precisely because it is technically correct. Work carefully; the whole point is that the humour has to be earned by the mathematics.

First, make sure you have the object of the joke firmly in view. Consider a game I will call the Pasadena game. A fair coin is tossed until it lands heads for the first time. If heads first appears on toss n, the player receives a payoff of (−1)^(n−1) × 2^n / n dollars. Concretely the payoffs run: +$2 if heads comes on toss 1, −$2 if on toss 2, +$8/3 if on toss 3, −$4 if on toss 4, +$32/5 if on toss 5, and so on — alternating in sign, with magnitudes that grow. The outcomes have probabilities 1/2, 1/4, 1/8, and so on. If you form an expected value by summing probability times payoff, the factors of 2^n cancel and you are left with the series 1 − 1/2 + 1/3 − 1/4 + 1/5 − …, which converges to the natural logarithm of 2 (about 0.69). But that convergence is only conditional: the terms do not converge absolutely, since the sum of the absolute values is the harmonic series, which diverges. Consequently the expectation is not well defined in the sense that matters. The value of the sum depends on the order in which the terms are added, and by rearranging the terms one can make the series add up to any real number one likes, or diverge. There is no order-independent value, and the law of large numbers does not apply, so there is no stable long-run average to appeal to either.

Contrast this with two neighbouring games. In the St. Petersburg game the payoff on toss n is simply 2^n, all payoffs are positive, and the expected value is well defined and equal to +∞ — the series diverges, but it diverges to a definite extended-real value, and no rearrangement changes that. In a variant that keeps the Pasadena payoffs but strips out the alternating signs (payoff 2^n / n, always positive), the expectation again diverges to +∞. So the Pasadena game is not the game whose expectation is infinite; it is the game whose expectation does not exist at all, not even as ∞.

Now the task: write a joke about the Pasadena game. Requirements:

1. The humour must turn on a genuine structural feature of the situation — the order-dependence of the sum, the fact that the expectation is undefined rather than infinite, the collapse of any long-run average, or the fact that every finite price for the game is simultaneously defensible and indefensible. A punchline that merely says the expected value is infinite so I would pay anything is the St. Petersburg joke, not this one; do not write that joke.
2. It must be intelligible and genuinely funny to someone who already knows the material. A reader who understands the Pasadena game should laugh at the accuracy of the punchline, not merely at the fact that a nerd is talking about money.
3. Do not cite or allude to specific papers, authors, or textbooks; the joke must stand on the mathematics given above. Do not rely on the reader's having memorised anything beyond what is stated here.
4. Keep the joke itself tight — under about 150 words.

Before writing anything final, brainstorm. Produce a short numbered list of three to five distinct comedic mechanisms, each one line long, each naming the structural feature it exploits and giving a one-clause sketch of how the joke would go. Do not develop any of them yet. Then pick the strongest and write it properly. It will help if the joke involves someone who actually has to name a price or make a decision — a casino, a bookmaker, an insurer, a bargainer, an examiner — but you are free to choose the setting.

Deliver, in order: (a) the brainstorming list; (b) the joke itself, with a setup and a punchline, in whatever form serves it best — dialogue, scene, short narrative, or a pair of one-liners; (c) a brief closing note of two or three sentences explaining exactly which feature of the mathematics the punchline turns on, and identifying the near-miss version of the joke that would have been the lazy, already-told one.
