I am preparing a lecture for an upper-level metaphysics course on Aristotle's sea-battle argument in De Interpretatione 9, and I would like to present him as the first three-valued logician — the forerunner of the system Łukasiewicz later built when he took up the same problem. On the reading I have been working with, Aristotle's refusal to call the proposition that a sea battle will occur tomorrow either true or false amounts to assigning it a third, intermediate truth value, so that the argument becomes an early demonstration that bivalent logic cannot accommodate the future.

The difficulty is that Aristotle also insists the disjunction — that there will or there will not be a sea battle tomorrow — is necessary, and that the dichotomy itself cannot fail to hold. If each disjunct carries the intermediate value, I cannot see how their disjunction comes out determinately true, and in the three-valued tables I know it does not: a disjunction of two intermediate sentences is itself intermediate. So it looks to me as though Aristotle is either simply inconsistent at this point or else quietly using "true" in some non-truth-functional sense, and I do not know how to present the second option to students without making it sound like a dodge.

What I want from you is a way to frame the lecture that is genuinely defensible rather than a summary of the standard talking points. Help me see what Aristotle is and is not committed to when he declines to call the future contingent true or false, whether that refusal really commits him to a third truth value, and how far the comparison with Łukasiewicz can be pressed before it breaks. I would also like to know whether the necessity of the disjunction can be preserved while denying that either disjunct is already true — and if so, what such a position costs, and why it is not simply a third truth value under another name.
