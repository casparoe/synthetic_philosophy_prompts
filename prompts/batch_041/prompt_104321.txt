I'm in a course on machine learning and human values and I have an essay due next week. I've drafted it but I'm worried the central distinction is either trivial or just a known problem in a costume, so I want comments — mainly on whether the distinction is real and whether the argument holds, not on my prose.

The gist. Everyone talks about the ambiguity/identifiability problem in inverse reinforcement learning: from any finite set of demonstrations there are lots of reward functions that fit equally well, so you can't pin down what the human actually wants. My essay says that's not the interesting problem. The interesting one is what I'm calling preference displacement. IRL learns from behavior, but the behavior it learns from was produced under constraints that the deployer intends to remove later. A driver keeps to the speed limit because of fines and demerit points; IRL watches and infers a preference for slow driving. Then you deploy the policy where those fines don't apply — or apply to someone else — and the "preference" you learned is really the shadow of a constraint that no longer exists. Same shape with a worker who takes the safest route only because the employer mandates it, or a user who clicks the first result only because the old interface showed nothing else.

So my claim is that reward ambiguity is an epistemic problem about the data you happen to have, while displacement is a problem about where the data came from. The inferred reward isn't wrong about the demonstrations; it's right about the demonstrations and wrong about the person.

What I want from you: (1) Is this actually a distinct problem, or does it collapse into identifiability/ambiguity the moment you formalize it? I half suspect the honest answer is that displacement is just ambiguity with a story attached, and if so I want you to say it straight. (2) Is there an established name or literature for exactly this? I don't want a reading list — just tell me if I've reinvented something and what the nearest existing concept is and how mine differs from it. (3) I end by proposing you should only learn preferences from behavior the agent would still produce under the constraints you intend to keep — learn from the "free" behavior. Is that even well-defined, or circular, since you'd need the policy already to know which behavior is free?

Give me a definite verdict rather than laying out the options: does the central distinction survive, and which of the three parts is weakest?
