I teach history at a small liberal arts college and I've been roped into giving this year's commencement address. The president's brief, which arrived in an all-caps email, is that I should tell the graduates "where history is taking them" and give them the sense of an arc they're stepping onto. I dont believe there's an arc, and I'd rather not lie to them on their way out the door. What I've got instead is Popper's argument that you can't have laws of history the way you have laws of physics -- trends aren't laws, a prediction about history can change the very thing it's about because people hear it and act on it or against it (the Oedipus effect, I think he called it -- correct me if I'm mangling that), and the future growth of knowledge is itself unknowable, which is the one that really wrecks the forecasting business. Complication: a colleague in econ has a well-attended talk showing that every big technology wave produces a decade of unrest, and a bunch of seniors now think their twenties are already scheduled. I want to take that on without being sneery about it, since he's basically right about the past. The room is parents who want to feel good, students who want to feel seen, and maybe four colleagues who'll notice if I get Popper wrong. My own dad spent thirty years waiting on a revolution his reading of history promised him, so this one's a little personal.

Write the address, about 700 words, and don't put a bibliography or citations in it. Use these sections in this order: "Where history is taking you" (~100 words -- actually say the thing the president wants me to say, so the turn has something to turn against); "Why I can't hand you a map" (~350 words -- the Popper part, plain language, get the trend-vs-law point and the prediction-changes-the-thing point in there, and use the econ colleague's talk as the concrete case you're answering); "What you get instead" (~200 words -- the close, something true I can actually send them off with); and finally "Note to self" (~50 words) on what I deliberately left out of the speech and why.
