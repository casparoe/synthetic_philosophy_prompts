I am a novelist, and I have reached the point in a book where I need to know exactly what my protagonist believes. She is a young woman in Cambridge in the first decade of the twentieth century who has taken G. E. Moore's ethics as a private creed: she holds that the only things worth having for their own sake are the enjoyment of beautiful things and the affection of her friends, and that everything else — work, duty, reputation, the war that is coming — is at best a means to those. The novel turns on a year in which the two goods pull against each other, and I have realised I cannot write that choice honestly unless I understand whether Moore's own account makes the two goods commensurable, and on what basis.

I have read Principia Ethica and I can recite the famous sentence: that the most valuable things we know or can imagine are certain states of consciousness, which may be roughly described as the pleasures of human intercourse and the enjoyment of beautiful objects. What I want from you is not a summary of that sentence but an explanation of the machinery underneath it, written for a reader who is intelligent, has the text, and is not a professional philosopher. I do not need a bibliography, a reading list, or page references; I will not use them, and I would rather you spent the space on the argument.

Four things, in ascending order of how much they matter to me.

First, the method. Moore says that in order to decide what has intrinsic value we are to consider what things, if they existed by themselves, in absolute isolation, we should still judge it good that they exist, and that we are to settle the relative degrees of value of different things by comparing the value of their isolated existences. Explain what this test is supposed to detect, why Moore thinks we can run it in imagination at all, and what exactly is being isolated when the candidate good is not a simple experience but something as complicated as a friendship.

Second, the principle of organic unities: that the intrinsic value of a whole is neither identical with nor proportional to the sum of the values of its parts. Explain the work this principle does for Moore — in particular how it is meant to block the inference from "this element has no value on its own" to "the value of the whole must lie in its other elements" — and what it costs him.

Third, and this is the crux: how the first two fit together. If the isolation test delivers the intrinsic value of a part, and organic unities tells us that the value of a whole is not fixed by the sum of the parts' intrinsic values, then what does the isolation test actually establish, and what work does it do in the argument of the last chapter? Moore also describes his goods both as states of consciousness and as complex wholes whose value depends on the existence and real qualities of their objects — the beloved's actual merits, the real beauty of the thing admired, the true belief involved in admiring it rightly. So I want to know what the unit of isolation is meant to be: a single mind, a life, or a whole world. And I want to know whether the way he actually argues in that chapter is consistent with the method he states at the start of it.

Fourth, the two goods themselves, and their ranking. Moore treats aesthetic enjoyment and personal affection as the two heads of unmixed good; he says the object of personal affection must be not merely beautiful but itself good in a high degree; he uses "beautiful" to mean that of which the admiring contemplation is good in itself; and he calls the love of love the most valuable good we know, more valuable than the mere love of beauty, though he adds that the appreciation of a person's appreciation of other persons only counts if it includes the love of beauty, in various degrees of directness. I want that structure made explicit. If the value of my protagonist's love for her friend depends on the value of the friend's own states, and those depend in turn on what the friend loves, what stops the account from running away into a regress of appreciations of appreciations, and what fixes the value of the whole? And set the comparison of the two goods out as an argument, premises identified, including the place where Moore seems to pull the other way by suggesting that the appreciation of admirable mental qualities, taken by itself, may be worth less than the appreciation of mere corporeal beauty. Where is a reader supposed to be convinced, and where is the argument doing hidden work?

Finally: tell me how confident you are in the reading you give me, and name the single point in it you are least sure of — the place where someone who knows the text well would most plausibly say that you have made Moore more consistent, or less, than he is.
