In my reading group, two philosophers have been arguing past each other about the wrong kind of reasons. One holds that a reward offered for having an attitude — a demon offers you a fortune if you sincerely admire him — gives you a genuine reason to admire him, merely a reason of the wrong kind, so that a fitting-attitude account of value (on which something is valuable just in case it is fitting to value it) needs a restriction rather than a replacement. The other holds that the offer is no reason to admire at all: it is a reason to want to admire, or to bring the admiration about, and the impression of a reason comes from equivocating between reasons for an attitude and reasons for doing something about it. Draft the joint statement the two of them could sign: (1) numbered claims both accept, covering what the demon case does and does not establish and whether "wrong kind of reasons" names one phenomenon or several; (2) each side's position in wording that side would accept; (3) the cruxes — I expect one to be whether "a reason for an attitude" is univocal, and another to be whether fittingness is a normative dimension distinct from the reasons that count in favour of an attitude; and (4) for each crux, an argument or case both agree bears on it, with each side's prediction about how it comes out recorded in advance. Handle explicitly the variant in which the reward is offered for admiring someone independently admirable, so that the fitting reason and the rewarded reason converge, and say which disagreements, if any, are merely verbal. Arrange the cruxes and the two sides' predictions as a table, and state how confident you are in the claims you attribute to each side, singling out the point you are least sure of.
