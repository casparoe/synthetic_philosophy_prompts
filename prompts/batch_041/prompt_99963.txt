Write a short story that takes the philosophical puzzle of choice without preference as its engine. Set it in an office whose charter requires that every consequential decision be justified by a reason that differentiates the option chosen from the options passed over — a safeguard against favoritism. A case arrives in which two candidates, or two sites, or two plans are indistinguishable on every criterion the office is permitted to weigh. The office must decide, and must record why. Follow the attempt to produce that why, and what it costs the people waiting on the decision. I want something other than the familiar fable of a creature freezing between two identical goods: assume a decision is eventually reached, and make the hard question what the reaching of it does to the claim that the options were equal. The characters may grope toward the thought that a procedure for choosing — a coin, a rule, a rotation — is not the same thing as a reason for choosing, but don't force a moral. Aim for about 1,200 words. Then, apart from the story, add a brief note on where it takes liberties with the underlying ideas or leans on a contested reading of them, and tell me plainly if the setup I've described is incoherent or the story can't be told well as I've framed it.
