I am a high school senior working on a presentation for my philosophy club, and I have spent the last few weeks reading about the doctrine of middle knowledge associated with the sixteenth-century Spanish Jesuit theologian Luis de Molina. As I understand it, the claim is that God knows, prior to any decree of his own will, what each possible creature would freely choose in each possible set of circumstances in which it might be placed, and that this body of knowledge — neither purely natural nor dependent on God's decision to create — is what guides his selection of which world to actualize. What interests me is not so much the doctrine itself as a difficulty that seems to be raised against it and that I cannot resolve on my own. The objection, as it has been put to me, is that these counterfactuals of creaturely freedom have nothing to make them true: they cannot be grounded in God's will, since they are supposed to hold prior to any act of that will, and they cannot be grounded in the creature, since the creature does not yet exist and has not yet chosen anything. Molina's answer, as I understand it, is that God knows them by comprehending the creature's free nature with a perfection no created intellect could match. I suspect I am missing why that answer is supposed to help, and I want to approach the whole issue from an angle that nobody in my club has thought about.

Here is what I would like you to do, and I would ask you to reason carefully rather than give me a general survey. Please take on the role of someone who works both on early modern scholastic disputes about grace, freedom, and foreknowledge and on contemporary philosophy of mind and artificial intelligence, and who is used to being asked anachronistic questions and answering them responsibly. Before you say anything about my case, brainstorm for yourself which of Molina's commitments are actually load-bearing for the prediction you are about to make, and set those out briefly. Then suppose that Molina were brought to the present day, given a compressed but competent education in modern science and computing, and left otherwise with his theological views intact, and that he were shown two things. The first is a large language model: a system whose every output is a deterministic function of a fixed set of numerical parameters together with its input, so that the question of what it would have produced in some counterfactual situation has a perfectly definite answer that is grounded in the parameters themselves. The second is the popular claim, repeated often in science journalism and in some neuroscience writing, that human decisions are likewise fixed by prior brain states, so that what a person would choose in a given situation is settled before she ever deliberates. What would Molina make of this? I would like to know (a) whether he would say that God could have middle knowledge of the utterances of the deterministic machine, and whether that machine's determinism makes its counterfactuals better grounded than the counterfactuals of free creatures that his critics found so troublesome, or whether it instead disqualifies the machine from the kind of freedom his doctrine requires; (b) whether he would treat the deterministic picture of human deliberation as a threat to his account, as a clarification of it, or as something he would simply reject; and (c) whether the grounding objection looks weaker or stronger once the creatures whose counterfactuals are at issue are deterministic systems rather than free ones.
