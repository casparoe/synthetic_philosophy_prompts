I cover cognitive science and the philosophy of mind for a general-interest science magazine, and for the next issue I need to file a satirical feature — deadpan, reported, roughly 800 words, plus a headline, a standfirst, and one boxed sidebar — on a subject I know well enough to be precise about: the business of deciding which things are genuinely about other things and which merely behave as if they were.

Background you may take as given. No research, citations, or reading list is needed, and none should appear in the piece.

Intentionality is the property a state has when it represents something: a belief about a tree, a word standing for an object, a gauge tracking a pressure. The dispute I want satirized concerns whether that property comes in two grades. One position holds that there is an original grade, belonging to a state in virtue of the biology that produces it, and a borrowed grade, which words, pictures, maps, and computers possess only because we, the original possessors, lend it to them; on this view a machine's aboutness is at best as-if. The opposing position holds that the distinction does no explanatory work: our only working handle on what a system believes or wants is to interpret it as an agent, and where that interpretive strategy predicts well, there is no further fact of the matter about whether the aboutness is owned or merely on loan. The case usually used to open the dispute is a coin-operated vending machine (sometimes called a two-bitser) built to take one kind of coin, which in new circumstances accepts a coin it was never built for; nothing inside it seems to settle whether it mistook one coin for the other, or merely behaved as if it had.

My idea for the piece is that a fictional body has turned the first position into an accreditation regime — call it the Bureau of Originary Intentionality, or invent a better name — and that I report on it straight, in the register of a trade-press feature about a standards organization. You will need to build the regime: the classes of certificate, what the application form asks, what the Bureau accepts as evidence of origin, how long a certificate runs, what happens on appeal, which clause of the standard does the most work and the least. Give me the paper trail, at least two documents quoted from within the fiction, and at least one scene — a hearing, an inspection, a denied applicant's phone call.

Constraints.

First, no invented absurdity. Every grotesque feature of the regime must be a straight extrapolation of the doctrine that aboutness is either owned or borrowed, together with the practical fact that ownership cannot be inspected while provenance can. If a rule is funny, it must be because the doctrine, followed honestly, produces it.

Second, the piece must carry these points without ever pausing to explain them: the provenance rule, under which two devices that behave identically receive opposite rulings on the strength of who made them and out of what; the regress of conferral, in which borrowed aboutness must be lent by an owner whose own aboutness must in turn have come from somewhere; the reach of the scheme, which forces the Bureau to rule on dogs, insects, corporations, and itself; the fact that originality is a label rather than a mechanism, so that no inspection, however fine-grained, could read it off a system; and the fact that borrowed cannot be a demotion, since if it were, eyes and immune systems would be demoted along with the machines.

Third, the doctrine's home turf is biology, and that is where the piece should do its damage. Organs have functions conferred by a process that had no intentions, so the certificate chain must either terminate somewhere the reader will find unsatisfying or run forever. Let the reader see the end of the chain and dislike it. Do not tell the reader what to conclude.

Fourth, voice. Deadpan throughout. The narrator reports; the narrator never winks, never explains the joke, and never uses the words absurd, Orwellian, or Kafkaesque. Every real objection must be voiced by one of your invented characters — a standards engineer dissenting in a footnote, a lawyer for a denied applicant, a historian of the Bureau — and none of them may be the narrator's own view. No real names, no real institutions, no citations: the Bureau, its officers, its applicants, and its documents are yours to invent. The piece should be readable by someone who has never heard of the philosophical dispute, and funny to someone who has.

End on the case the Bureau cannot decide, and make clear, without saying so, why no ruling is available. The sidebar, about 120 words, should reproduce the definitions section of the Bureau's standard.
