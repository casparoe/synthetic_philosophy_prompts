I keep coming back to the "hinge of history" thing — the idea that we might be sitting in the one century where the long-run fate of everything gets settled, definately the most influential stretch of time there'll ever be, so the expected influence of an ordinary person alive right now is enormous compared with any other era — and the usual back-and-forth about whether that's true or just a flattering story we tell about our own moment. I'd like a short story that actually does something with it, rather than just illustrating it.

Here's the setup I want, because I think it makes the idea work instead of getting asserted. There's an institute — call it whatever you like — whose entire job is grading past centuries on how pivotal they were. They've got a seperate score for each dimension: how much of the long-run trajectory got locked in during that century, how many future people's lives turned on what happened then, how reversible the outcomes were, and whether the century narrowed the range of futures still open or widened it. The story should follow one person caught up in this — a junior grader who has to sign off on the grade for the century they grew up in, maybe, or someone in a town that's just been told it was the hinge, or an archivist quietly revising an old grade — and it should get into the awkward bit the rubric can't handle: the grading can only be done from outside, after the fact, and the people living through a hinge are exactly the ones who can't tell. What does it do to a person to believe they're living at the hinge? What does it do to the institute when the grades keep getting revised, or when a century everyone assumed was pivotal turns out to have made almost no difference, or the reverse?

Please don't make it a smug satire of people who care about the long-term future, and don't make it a sermon about how we should all give away our salaries. I want the genuine pull of the idea — it might be true, and if it is, it changes what you owe — sitting alongside the genuine worry: that the whole frame is unfalsifiable, or self-flattering, or that it makes ordinary life unbearable to live. Emotionally the story can take a side even if it's intellectually torn.

About 1,200–1,800 words. And finish with a single last line, one sentence only, in exactly this form: Summary: <one sentence>
