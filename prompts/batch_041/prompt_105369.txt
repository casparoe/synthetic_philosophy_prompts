I am a doctoral student in philosophy serving as a teaching assistant for an upper-level undergraduate course on practical reason and rational choice, and I am responsible for grading the midterm essays. I have reproduced the essay question and one student's script below. I would like a grade for the script, together with the comments that justify it.

Essay question (20 points; the largest single item on the midterm):

In lecture we distinguished two claims about rational action. The Strong Reason Principle (SRP): an agent acts rationally only if, among the options available to her, she chooses the one she has most reason to choose — so that where no option is favored over the others, no option is rationally permissible. The Weak Reason Principle (WRP): an agent acts rationally only if she has some reason for what she does. Now consider an agent who must choose between two options that are, by her own lights, exactly equal in every respect relevant to the choice — same expected payoff, same costs, same fit with her ends.

(a) Which of SRP and WRP does the traditional Buridan's-ass example, if it works at all, show to be false, and what is the reasoning supposed to be?

(b) Suppose someone replies that when the options are exactly tied the agent has sufficient reason to choose either, and may therefore simply pick one. Is that reply available to someone who accepts SRP, or does it abandon SRP? If the agent must simply pick, does she need a tie-breaking rule — and if she uses one, say a coin, is she still acting rationally?

(c) Does the example show that an agent can act rationally even when she has no preference between her options? Defend your answer against the strongest objection you can raise.

Student's script:

Buridan's ass is the case of a donkey set exactly between two identical bales of hay, equally hungry and equally near each. Because nothing favors either bale, the donkey forms no preference and, on the traditional telling, starves. The example is meant to show that a requirement to act only on the strongest reason cannot be met when the reasons are perfectly balanced, and so to reduce the Strong Reason Principle to absurdity. That seems right to me. If SRP were true, an agent facing a genuine tie would have no permissible action at all, which is absurd: the donkey could obviously just eat one of the bales.

The reply that the agent has sufficient reason to choose either is really a rejection of SRP rather than a defense of it. SRP says the agent must choose the option she has most reason to choose; when the options tie, she has most reason to choose neither, since neither is better than the other. So the reply must be operating with a different principle, WRP, which requires only that the agent have some reason for what she does. Under WRP the agent is free to take either bale, because she has a reason — she is hungry — and either bale will satisfy it.

Does she need a tie-breaking rule? I think she does. If nothing favors either bale, nothing in the situation tells her which to take, and the only way to move is to introduce a rule that manufactures a difference, such as a coin flip. But this is where the reply runs into trouble. A coin flip does not give her a reason that tracks the value of the bales; it merely adds information about which bale she will end up taking. So the tie-breaker is a device for acting without a reason, and although it may be psychologically necessary, it is not a rational way to choose. The honest conclusion is that the tie-breaker is irrational but indispensable.

So the example does show that an agent can act rationally without a preference, but only in the weak sense that WRP is satisfied: she can eat, and hunger is a reason. It does not show that she can choose between the bales for a reason, since there is no such reason to be had. The strongest objection to my answer is that a donkey is not an agent at all and so the example says nothing about rational choice; but I think the example is really about the structure of reasons, and that survives the objection.
