I'm a novelist — third book, mostly close-third-person domestic stuff — and I've gotten into a running argument with a friend in my writing group about teh advice we keep giving our students. The advice is basically: stay in one character's head per scene, don't hop between minds, and if your in deep POV, every sentence has to be filtered through what that character could notice, know, or plausibly say to themselves. It's presented as a craft rule, almost a moral one. But the more I teach it, the more I think it's smuggling in alot of philosophy: that there's a single interior room where experience happens, that it has an audience of one, that a self sits in there watching the show, and that a novel can just point a camera at it. I know people call that the Cartesian theater and say the multiple drafts model is supposed to replace it — no single finish line, no central meaner, just parallel processes and retrospective narration.

My question is: what philosophical commitments does the deep-POV/one-mind-per-scene convention actually presuppose? Which parts of the convention strictly require that picture, and which are just useful craft shortcuts that would survive if the picture were false? Is the convention compatible with a multiple drafts view, or does it secretly need the theater? And if a novelist took multiple drafts seriously, what would have to change on the page — would deep POV have to go, or could it be re-engineered into something else? I'm not asking for a reading list or citations; I want the implicit theory reconstructed, distinguished from what the practice merely fits well with, and then stress-tested. Keep the whole thing under 600 words, and lead with your bottom line in the first couple of sentences.
