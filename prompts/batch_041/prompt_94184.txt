I am a second-year philosophy student, currently taking a course on formal systems, computability, and the philosophy of mind. As part of the assessment, each of us has to lead one fifty-minute session for the rest of the group — about fifteen students, roughly a third of whom have taken a formal logic module and the remainder of whom have not. I have chosen to build my session around the MU puzzle, because the thing I want the group to feel is not the puzzle but the move that is required to solve it.

Here is the puzzle as I intend to present it. You begin with the string MI. Four transformations are permitted: if a string ends in I you may append U; if a string begins with M you may append everything after the M, so that Mx becomes Mxx; you may replace any occurrence of III with U; and you may delete any occurrence of UU. The question is whether MU is derivable. It is not. The reason is an invariant: no derivable string contains a number of I's divisible by three, since the first rule leaves that count alone, the second doubles it, the third reduces it by three, the fourth leaves it alone, and from a single I no combination of doubling and subtracting three ever lands on a multiple of three. MU contains no I at all, and zero is a multiple of three.

What I want the group to experience is the seam between the two halves of that answer. The first half is conducted entirely inside the system, where the only permitted moves are the four rules, and no amount of ingenuity gets you to MU. The second half is a statement about all possible derivations rather than a derivation, and is therefore not a move the system permits. My worry is that once someone produces the invariant it will look like a parlour trick rather than the general predicament it is, and I want the structure of the activity to make that seam feel load-bearing rather than incidental.

Please design the session. I need the rules, the materials, the grouping, a minute-by-minute plan, and the debrief. The group should reach the impossibility themselves and should not be told in advance that MU is unreachable; the arrangement of the activity should make the difference between failing to find a derivation and proving that none exists something they run into rather than something I announce. Somewhere in the middle someone will propose the cheap move — adding a rule, or simply declaring MU a theorem — and I would like the activity to have a place where that proposal is taken seriously and then separated from the invariant argument, since the whole point is that not every step outside the system is the same kind of step.

I also want a second puzzle of the same shape but with a different kind of obstruction, so that jumping out does not become a single reusable trick; please specify it and verify its invariant yourself, since I will be presenting it as fact. In the debrief I want the mapping from what happened onto the idea: why the invariant argument quantifies over all derivations and therefore cannot itself be one, what distinguishes the metalanguage from the object language, and what happens when the invariant is added to the system as a new rule — which system has just been made, and what it would take to justify it. If you can do this without assuming I know any literature beyond the puzzle itself, so much the better; I will add any references myself, and I do not want the activity to depend on the group having read anything beforehand.

Finally, tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle each case, and be honest about what the activity cannot reproduce — in particular, which part of the predicament depends on there being no general procedure for finding the invariant, and what the ease of this particular puzzle conceals.
