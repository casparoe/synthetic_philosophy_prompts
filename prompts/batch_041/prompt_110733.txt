I am a doctoral student in a seminar on decision theory and welfare economics, and I have spent the term keeping a careful log of my supervisor's rulings. She has the habit, at the close of each week's case, of saying whether the person in the case can be credited with a utility function at all, whether the choices described reveal a preference, and what may and may not be meaningfully asserted about the person afterwards. I am trying to anticipate her ruling on next week's case, and, more than that, to reconstruct the rule she has actually been applying, since I suspect she is running something more specific than the slogans she offers in class, and a classmate of mine has already staked out the opposite prediction. Her rulings so far, as faithfully as I can record them, are these. First, a shopper with a long and internally consistent history of choices across many budgets: she has a preference ordering, and it is meaningful to say that she prefers coffee to tea, but not meaningful to say that she likes coffee over tea twice as much as she likes tea over water, since nothing in the choices fixes that. Second, a person whose pairwise choices run in a cycle, reliably taking A over B, B over C, and C over A: she has no utility function, yet she has preferences, and each pairwise claim is true; the fault lies in the theory that demands transitivity, not in the ascription of preference. Third, a person choosing among lotteries whose choices satisfy the usual consistency conditions for expected utility: here more is meaningful than in the first case, because her ranking is unique up to a positive affine transformation, so claims about her risk aversion are substantive while claims about how much she enjoys the taste of a particular outcome are not. Fourth, a person whose choices are best predicted by a model in which she ranks options differently according to what else is on the menu, always taking the middle-priced item, say: my supervisor's line was that this is not a failure to have preferences but a case of preferring a richer kind of object, the menu or the act of choosing, and that the choice data do reveal that preference. Fifth, a subject in a laboratory who chooses by genuinely flipping a coin, with no pattern whatever: she said there is nothing here to reveal, that ascribing a utility would be empty, and that a coin is evidence of a preference for randomizing only if the subject would pay to keep it. Sixth, and this one I record with less confidence, a person who sincerely and stably reports that she would give up a little money to avoid a mild noise but whose choices never take up any such trade: my supervisor treated the report as data about her verbal behavior rather than about her utility, and said that where choice and report conflict, choice governs.

Next week's case is this. A subject faces a fixed menu of four options and chooses among them repeatedly, paid on each trial. Her choices are probabilistic but highly stable: she takes option A on about seventy per cent of trials, B on twenty, C on ten, and never D, and these frequencies reproduce themselves closely over thousands of trials and are insensitive to modest changes in the payoffs. One visiting economist says she is a person with a fixed ranking, A above B above C above D, whose implementation is noisy, so that her utility is well defined and the deviations from the ranking are error; a second economist says that what she prefers is the lottery itself, that her utility is defined over lotteries over the four options, and that the randomness is not error at all. I would like you to predict my supervisor's ruling, and to do so by first setting out the two or three rules that fit her six rulings equally well; then saying what each of those rules implies for the stochastic subject, in particular whether she is to be credited with a utility function, whether the thirty per cent is to be counted as preference or as error, and whether forcing her always to receive her top-ranked option would count as an improvement in her welfare; then giving your best guess at the rule she is really running, with your reasons for preferring it to the others; and finally naming the further case, or the further manipulation of this one, that would most cleanly separate the surviving rules from one another.
