I'm a novelist about two-thirds of the way through a book about a woman who makes one bad call in her early twenties and then spends thirty years paying for it, and for the last few months I've been cornering friends at dinner to argue with me about it. Last Tuesday it turned into an actual row between two of them and I've been chewing on it ever since. Dana is a public defender, Oz is an actuary, so you can guess which side each took. The case was a man we all know, call him Tomas — mid-forties, father and both uncles dead of heart attacks before fifty-five, and Tomas knew it. Smoked for twenty years, worked a stressful job he loved, never exercised, and last spring he had his own heart attack and came out of it too disabled to work, leaning on his sister for the daily stuff and on a disability payment for the rest. Oz said this is the cleanest case there is: he took the risk, the risk landed, he carries the cost, and that's what it means to treat him as an adult. Dana said that's the thin end of a wedge, because the risk-taking was itself unchosen — a temperament he was born with, a father who smoked at the kitchen table, a town where everyone did, and nobody ever offered him a version of himself that found quitting possible.

Oz's answer was that the regress proves too much. Push it back far enough and nothing is ever chosen, every disadvantage is brute bad luck, and the view has no cases left at all — which is fine by him, he said, he'd rather be a plain egalitarian, he just wants Dana to admit that's what she's become. Dana said the theory doesn't need metaphysical free will, only the ordinary sense in which Tomas could have done otherwise: he was told, he understood, he lit another one. Oz said "the ordinary sense" is doing all the work there, and then he made what I thought was the sharpest move of the night. He asked which was the bad luck — the smoking, or the genes that made smoking lethal for him, or the temperament that made quitting impossible, or the culture that made it unremarkable? Same man, same outcome, and you can describe it as chosen or unchosen depending on where you cut. Dana came back with: blame and cost are two different things, blame can be perfectly apt and we can still owe him the ramp. Oz said then you've thrown away the whole point, which was that equality should be sensitive to what people actually choose, and what's left is just need. And Dana's last point, the one I keep thinking about, was about the sister: you can't make Tomas eat the full cost without her eating some of it too, unpaid, involuntarily — so his option luck lands on her as brute luck, and now the theory owes her an answer and I don't think it has one.
