I teach a social choice seminar and want to use Shirley Jackson's "The Lottery" to teach Harsanyi's aggregation theorem. Harsanyi derives a utilitarian social welfare function from the von Neumann–Morgenstern axioms applied to social as well as individual preferences, together with the acceptance principle that society must prefer a lottery every individual prefers; in his veil-of-ignorance argument he adds the assumption that the impartial observer has an equal chance of being any member. Rawls objected that parties behind the veil would maximize the worst outcome rather than expected utility, and there is a further dispute about whether ex ante aggregation can answer ex post complaint. Read the story through the theorem, and be specific about the machinery. Does the village implement the veil of ignorance or travesty it — in particular, does its two-stage drawing, in which households are selected first and a member is then drawn from the chosen household, give each villager the equal ex ante probability Harsanyi's construction requires, and if not, does that matter to the theorem or only to the village? If every villager behind the veil would accept the lottery, does Harsanyi's theorem license it, and does Tessie's protest when her household is drawn state an objection the theorem must answer or one it can set aside? And where does the story resist the theorem: no one appears to prefer the lottery, only to inherit it, and other villages have quietly given it up — does the theorem need preferences, or a history, that the story withholds? I want a reading that takes the formal structure seriously, does not reduce the story to a utilitarian cartoon, and ends by saying what the story shows that the aggregation literature does not.
