Write a dialogue between two epistemologists about the puzzle usually called the dogmatism paradox. The argument runs roughly as follows: if you know that P, then you also know that any evidence you later encounter which appears to tell against P must be misleading; so once you know P, you may rationally set such evidence aside without investigating it. The first speaker, the Defender, accepts this conclusion and thinks it captures something true about knowledge — that a known proposition is not hostage to whatever turns up afterwards, and that treating a known-misleading defeater as a reason for doubt is a failure of nerve. The second speaker, the Critic, rejects the argument, holding that knowledge is always defeasible and that the argument must be trading on an equivocation somewhere. Both are competent and neither is a caricature: the Critic is not a general skeptic who thinks we never know anything, and the Defender does not think beliefs can be insulated from evidence by fiat.

Anchor the exchange in a case that both speakers must engage on the merits. A source you have good reason to trust — an instrument, a testifier, whatever you prefer — tells you two things: that P, and that within the hour you will encounter evidence E which will strongly appear to show that not-P and which will nevertheless be misleading. The hour passes and E arrives exactly as predicted. Both speakers accept that the source's first claim gave you knowledge of P, and that E is in fact misleading.

They must now argue over what you are rationally permitted or required to believe about P once E is in hand, and what E does to your confidence in the source. In particular:

- The Critic should press the circularity objection: your ground for dismissing E is the very source whose reliability is apparently impugned by E, so the dismissal seems to assume what it needs to show. The Defender must answer this rather than wave it away.
- The Defender should press the converse: if E genuinely cannot count against P because you already know it to be misleading, then what work is left for defeasibility to do? The Critic must answer this rather than concede the whole position.
- They should then argue about a variant in which the source is reliable about P but only, say, nine times in ten reliable about which evidence will mislead, and say what, if anything, that changes.
- They should also argue about a variant in which, at the moment E arrives, you no longer remember on what basis you came to know P.
- Each should state the strongest consideration that would move them off their position.

Have them reason from the case itself rather than from the literature or from named authors. Close on the residual disagreement, stated as precisely as the two of them can manage, rather than on a synthesis.
