ok so im working through locke's essay on human understanding and i keep hitting the same wall in the identity chapter. here's the bit thats bugging me:

In the state of living creatures, their identity depends not on a mass of the same particles, but on something else. For in them the variation of great parcels of matter alters not the identity : an oak growing from a plant to a great tree, and then lopped, is still the same oak ; and a colt grown up to a horse, sometimes fat, sometimes lean, is all the while the same horse : though, in both these cases, there may be a manifest change of the parts ; so that truly they are not either of them the same

and a few lines earlier he says that if you add or remove a single atom from a mass, it is no longer the same mass or the same body.

what i cant get is what work the "something else" is doing, and whether its meant to be a real metaphysical ingredient or just a fact about how we sort things. ive been kicking around an idea i havent seen stated quite this way — call it the budget view — where the persistence conditions of a thing are just whatever set of changes it can absorb while still counting as one thing for some practice (biological, legal, narrative, whatever). on that view the oak and the mass arent two kinds of entity, theyre two different budgets. does lockes contrast actually support that, or is he committed to the organism having a genuine extra ingredient (a life, a form, an organizing principle) that the mass lacks? and if it is a genuine extra ingredient, does that push him toward something like perdurantism about organisms — the oak as a temporally extended whole whose earlier and later parts are all equally real — or does his insistence that its the same oak across change fit better with endurance? walk me through what hes actually claiming and where the pressure points are. if the budget view is incoherent i'd rather find out now than after another week of this.
