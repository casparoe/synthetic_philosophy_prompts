I teach decision theory and formal epistemology in a philosophy department, and for the past two years I have been the outside member of the risk and planning committee of a mid-sized humanitarian organization that runs an anticipatory-action program: when forecasters project a drought, a flood, or a disease surge, the committee decides in advance whether to release funds and pre-position supplies before the crisis is confirmed, rather than waiting for confirmation and responding afterwards. The committee meets quarterly, disposes of roughly two million dollars per decision, and its members — a finance officer, a program officer, a monitoring officer, and a country director who signs off — have no training in decision theory; I am the only person in the room who can do the arithmetic. The options are genuinely uncertain and genuinely rankable: releasing early buys a modest but reliable benefit for a fixed caseload, waiting buys a larger response if the window stays open and nothing if it closes, and the hybrid plans buy something in between with a chance of a great deal or a chance of very little. What I want to give them is a procedure they can actually run in a two-hour meeting with a spreadsheet, built on the theory I work on — Lara Buchak's risk-weighted expected utility, the rank-dependent decision theory on which an outcome's weight in a gamble can depend on where it ranks, and which collapses into ordinary expected utility when the risk function is linear. Their intuitive practice already looks like this theory in the wild: they sometimes refuse a gamble with the better expected outcome because of where the bad case sits, and they sometimes take one for the same reason, and I would rather they did that deliberately than by instinct.

Please write that procedure. I need the steps in order, what counts as a sufficient answer at each step, the points where the procedure stops with a no, and the points where it must hand the decision to a person because the question is one of values rather than arithmetic; the deliverable should include a one-page worksheet the program officer can fill in. Two constraints matter to me more than convenience. First, the procedure has to elicit the committee's risk attitude without assuming expected utility along the way — if the elicitation asks them to compare gambles in a way that only makes sense for an expected utility maximizer, we have gone in a circle — and I want the procedure to say honestly how much of the risk function is measured, how much is stipulated by the country director, and how much is a judgment call dressed up as measurement. Second, the forecasters give probabilities as ranges and sometimes as little more than a ranking of scenarios, so the procedure must say what to do when the numbers are soft rather than quietly plugging in a midpoint. Then run the procedure on three cases: a plan that trades a guaranteed modest response for a small chance of a much larger one; a plan whose worst case is catastrophic in the way that worries the committee most but whose expected reach is the highest of the options; and a case where the forecast is so unreliable that the procedure should refuse to choose. I expect the second to be the case where the procedure tells the committee something it will not want to hear, and I would like you to say so plainly rather than softening it.

Finally, be careful to separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who work on this. It is contested whether risk-weighted expected utility is a real rival to expected utility or a reformulation of it, whether risk attitudes can be elicited stably from anyone, and whether a committee can have a risk function at all rather than merely a procedure for aggregating individual ones; I do not want any of that presented as consensus, nor the theory's machinery treated as though the reader already knows it. Write for a reader who has never seen the theory, name the places where the procedure will misfire, and tell me what the procedure cannot do at all — including, I suspect, anything about the people who bear the risk but do not sit on the committee.
