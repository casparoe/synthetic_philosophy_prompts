Right, so I'm putting together the final for my MA epistemology module — it's the formal-ish one, credences and all that — and I need a single essay question on accuracy-first epistemology. My problem is that every question I've written so far gets answered with the same potted summary: "accuracy is the fundamental epistemic good, norms fall out of scoring rules, done." They never have to find the bit where the programme is actually load-bearing, and it's definately making the paper too easy. So I want to shove them into the awkward corner.

Here's the angle. There's a student in my seminar who won't let this go: she reckons accuracy-first epistemology isn't really one theory so much as a family, because how much a hedged credence costs you versus a bold one depends on which scoring rule you pick. Her favourite illustration is that if you assign probability zero to what turns out to be true, the log score punishes you infinitely, while the Brier score just docks you a finite amount — so the two rules encode genuinely different attitudes to being confidently wrong. And that, she says, is a value judgement about error rather than a mathematical fact. Her conclusion is that accuracy-first has just shifted the old Jamesian trade-off between believing what's true and avoiding error from the front door round to the back, rather than dissolving it.

I want the exam question to force them to (a) set out what accuracy-first epistemology actually commits you to, (b) work out which parts of the case survive the choice of scoring rule and which don't — they should be able to say why the dominance argument for probabilism comes out the same whichever proper rule you use, and likewise why the expected-accuracy argument for conditionalising on your evidence isn't hostage to the rule, whereas comparing two people's credence functions for accuracy very much is — and (c) come to a verdict on whether the student's objection is fatal, survivable-but-embarrassing, or actually a point in the view's favour. No free-floating verdicts though: they have to say what a defender of accuracy-first would have to give up in order to keep the view.

Level is upper-level masters. Assume they know what a proper scoring rule is and have seen the dominance argument, but don't assume they've read beyond the core stuff. Give me the essay question itself as one paragraph that reads like a real exam question — no (a)/(b)/(c) sub-parts spelled out, I just want it to sound like an exam — plus a model answer of about 650 words, and a marking scheme out of 20 with five must-hit points and the weight each one carries. Then a short A-versus-B line naming the specific moves that seperate a first from a 2:1, and I specifically want to dock anyone who just says "well, it depends on the scoring rule" and stops there, and anyone who thinks the dominance argument is itself rule-dependent. British spelling, and don't bother giving them a reading list — they're not allowed notes anyway.
