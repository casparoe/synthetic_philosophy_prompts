I sit on the programme committee of a small foundation that has committed roughly three years of support to a working group on the epistemology of theories we cannot test directly: the methodological and interpretive disputes surrounding inflationary cosmology, the string landscape, and the proposal that our universe is one of many. The group will consist of two philosophers of physics, a cosmologist, and a statistician, meeting regularly, and our expectation is that its output will be conceptual rather than experimental. I should say plainly that we have no reason to expect new data in that window to settle any of the underlying disputes, so I am evaluating every candidate question by what can be accomplished through argument, formalism, and careful bookkeeping alone. What I want from you is not a survey and not a reading list but a defended agenda: a ranked account of which open questions in this area most deserve the group's time, together with the reasoning that produces the ranking. Please make the criteria explicit — how many other questions depend on a given one, how tractable it is with the tools currently available to philosophers and statisticians rather than to experimenters, whether it is genuinely neglected, and, most importantly, whether settling it would change what working physicists actually do or only what philosophers say about them afterwards. Where criteria pull against each other, tell me how you trade them off.

I have a rough inventory of candidates in mind, and I would like you to say which of them are upstream of the rest and which are comparatively idle. First, whether non-empirical confirmation of the sort at issue is best reconstructed as an extension of inference to the best explanation or as an appeal to prior credence in disguise, and whether anything turns on the choice. Second, whether the measure problem is a technical obstacle awaiting a cleverer formalism or instead a sign that anthropic probabilities are ill-defined in principle — and whether the apparent consequence that typical observers are short-lived fluctuations is a reductio of the framework or simply a puzzle to be worked around. Third, whether a multiverse inherits whatever evidential support its generating theory enjoys or requires independent support, and what difference it makes that we seem to be dealing with a family of hypotheses rather than one. Fourth, whether the boundary between legitimate non-empirical reasoning and rationalization — the worry that the same moves would vindicate astrology or natural theology — can be drawn by some general criterion or only case by case. Fifth, whether scientific realism, the underdetermination of theory by evidence, and the problem of unconceived alternatives are upstream or downstream of the multiverse debate, or whether the traffic runs in both directions. I am also curious about connections to live cosmology that do not require new data, for instance whether any current framework makes anthropic predictions robust across reasonable choices of measure, or whether robustness is exactly what cannot be had.

Two further things. I would like you to specify what would move a question up or down the list over the next few years: a workable measure, a proof that no measure can satisfy the standard desiderata, a demonstration that some class of non-empirical arguments can be formalized in a way that makes their evidential force transparent, or a convincing showing that a dispute is merely verbal. And I would like you to name the two or three cruxes on which most of this literature implicitly depends, including any that the field is currently treating as settled when it is not — if the whole agenda rests on a single undefended assumption, I want that assumption stated. Conclude by telling me which of the candidate questions you would deliberately decline to fund, and what the cost of that neglect would be.
