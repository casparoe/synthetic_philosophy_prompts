I'm drafting a short essay on Descartes and animal minds and would like your comments before I commit to the line I'm taking. My notes are below.

1. The textbook summary — animals are machines, so they feel nothing — is too coarse. In his correspondence with Henry More, Descartes is willing to grant animals life, and even a kind of sensation, considered as depending on the organs of the body, while continuing to deny them thought. The interesting question, then, is not whether he denied animals feeling, but how he could grant them sensation of a sort and still deny them experience.

2. The load-bearing move is definitional. For Descartes, thought covers everything of which the mind is immediately aware, and sensation is counted as a mode of thought. Sensation in the full-blooded sense is therefore something a mind does, not something a body does. What an animal has is the bodily machinery — motions in the nerves and animal spirits, the withdrawal, the cry — and not the awareness.

3. My reading, which I'll call the ownership thesis: Descartes' denial is not an empirical claim about animals but a consequence of how he individuates the mental. An episode of pain is not merely a state with a certain felt character; it must be had by a simple immaterial subject. Since animals have no such subject, an animal's feeling pain is not unlikely but unintelligible.

4. If that is right, the usual charge against Descartes misfires. The complaint should not be that he got the facts about animals wrong, but that he built an account of experience on which the question of whether the animal suffers cannot even be raised. Note also that he does not call animals machines in order to license cruelty; he calls them machines because experience requires a subject of a kind they lack.

5. The pressure point: if pain just is a mode of thought, the More concession looks either empty or inconsistent. Either it is loose talk about physiology and concedes no feeling at all, or Descartes holds a two-sense view of sensation — bodily and mental — that he never makes explicit.

6. A further worry: once the two-sense view is granted, the mechanist can describe everything we call pain in an animal, which raises the question whether the felt character is doing any work in Descartes' system or is idle by his own lights.

Please tell me where the reading goes wrong and what you would change. I want a committed assessment rather than a survey of possible interpretations: take a definite position on whether the ownership thesis is defensible and whether point 4 is fair to Descartes, and give your reasons. Give exactly five numbered items, each a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion.
