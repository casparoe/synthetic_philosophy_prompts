I have been reading a trade-press overview of social ontology, the kind of book that compresses a research area into a single chapter, and I want to know how much of its treatment of collective intentionality I can rely on. I know the names Searle, Bratman, and Gilbert, and I have a general sense that the primary literature is more careful than the chapter makes it sound, but I cannot locate the specific places where the chapter cuts corners.

The chapter's account, as I remember it, runs roughly as follows. The guiding puzzle is how to understand cases in which several people do something together — carry a table upstairs, dance a tango, paint a house — as opposed to each merely doing their own thing at the same time. The decisive move, the chapter says, was Searle's distinction between I-intentions and we-intentions. Searle's example: when a sudden rainstorm sends a scattered group of picnickers running toward the same shelter, that is not joint action, because each person is only running for cover on their own; but when a dance troupe performs the same movements as part of a choreographed routine, that is joint action. The difference, on the chapter's telling, is internal rather than behavioral: the dancers have a we-intention, while the picnickers have only I-intentions. The chapter then says that Searle's we-intention is a collective mental state that no single individual can have on their own — it belongs to the group rather than to its members — and that this is why Searle treats collective intentionality as irreducible to individual intentionality. It contrasts this with Bratman, who the chapter says reduces shared intention to individual intentions plus mutual belief: I intend to do my part provided you do yours, you intend the same, and each of us knows this about the other. The chapter also mentions Gilbert in passing, saying that her notion of a plural subject is the clearest expression of the group-mind picture, since on her view the group itself is the bearer of the intention. The chapter's verdict is that Searle and Gilbert are right that joint action requires something over and above a sum of individual intentions, but that their posit of a group-level intention is mysterious and commits them to a kind of group mind, whereas Bratman's deflationary account needs nothing spooky. It closes by saying that the real issue is whether groups can be genuine agents, and that anyone who accepts Searle's we-intentions is thereby committed to the claim that a group can intend something that none of its members intends.

I would like an audit of this summary. Please separate (a) what is accurate as a statement of the views in question, (b) what is a defensible simplification that a careful popularizer might be allowed, and (c) what is a misrepresentation that the philosophers in question would reject. Where the chapter attributes a view to Searle, Bratman, or Gilbert, tell me what each would actually say, and in particular whether the chapter has correctly identified what is at stake in calling collective intentionality irreducible. I am also interested in whether the chapter's closing claim — that accepting we-intentions commits one to group intentions that no member has — follows from anything Searle actually holds, or whether it is a non sequitur resting on an earlier misdescription. Finally, tell me whether the chapter's verdict about group minds is a fair summary of the state of the debate or whether it presents a contested objection as a settled result. I do not need citations or a reading list; I want to understand the positions well enough to judge the chapter. I am inclined to think the deflationary picture is more plausible, but I suspect the chapter is being unfair to Searle, and I would like to know whether that suspicion is well founded.
