I've been teaching the Phenomenology seminar agian and I've ended up defending a reading I'm not sure I actually believe, so I want you to try to break it.

The reading: take the master–slave section and strip out everything that depends on Spirit doing metaphysical work — no teleology, no World Spirit, no claim that history has to go anywhere. What's left, I claim, is a thesis about asymmetric recognition, and it's roughly this. Recognition only does its job for the recognizer when the recognizer regards the other as competent to confer it. Lordship therefore fails on its own terms: the master wants to be recognized as a master, but the only person available to do the recognizing is someone he's defined as beneath the act of recognition, so the recognition comes back void for him. And the relation is unstable in a second way too, in that the subordinate, by being forced to labor and by being pushed to internalize the master's standpoint, ends up with a kind of self-relation the master has lost. So asymmetry in recognition can't be a stable resting point — it either collapses or converts, and the conversion runs toward mutual recognition.

Here's what I want from you. Don't tell me it's a classic. Tell me what would count against it, and rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward. The thing I'm most worried about is the long-lived hierarchical societies — hereditary aristocracy, caste, chattel slavery, gender hierarchy — that seem to have reproduced themselves for centuries without either collapsing or converting. Do those count against me? A defender will want to say my instability claim is normative, not empirical: the master's satisfaction fails as a matter of what recognition is, not as a matter of what masters happen to report feeling. Tell me honestly whether that's a real distinction or a dodge, and whether it makes the thesis unfalsifiable — and if it does, whether that's actually a problem for a thesis of this kind.

I also want the objection that comes from inside: what would a serious Hegelian concede actually counts against the reading, as opposed to what critics would say from outside it? And which rival readings of the section does mine predict worse than? I'll tell you what would move me personally: I'd give the thing up if someone could show me a stable, self-reproducing asymmetric recognition relation where the dominant party's recognition isn't void for him — an actual case, not a merely possible one. If you think no such case could exist by construction, say so, and say why that isn't just me rigging the game.

Two more things. First, I want you to introduce and then use a distinction I've been toying with: between motivational instability (the master can't be satisfied) and reproductive instability (the relation can't turn out the next generation of masters). My suspicion is that the empirical cases only threaten the second, and that most people slide between the two without noticing. Tell me whether that distinction holds up or whether it's damage control dressed up as analysis. Second, be committed — take a side, tell me whether the stripped-down thesis survives the objections, and don't just lay out the options and leave me to pick.
