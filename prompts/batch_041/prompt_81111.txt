DRAFT

The example. Two coins X and Y, each of which lands heads with probability 1/2 on its own. What I don't know is the relation between them: either they always land the same way as each other (call this correlated), or they always land differently (anti-correlated). Let w be my credence that they're correlated. My credal state is the set M = {P_w : w in [0,1]} — I'm allowing every value of w, since I have no evidence at all about the correlation.

Arithmetic. For every w:

- P_w(X = H) = 1/2, straight from the setup. So the set of my credences in X is the single point {1/2}. Not an interval, a point. Same for Y.
- P_w(Y = H) = 1/2, and Y = H is exactly as likely under the correlated hypothesis as under the anti-correlated one. So observing Y = H tells me nothing whatsoever about the correlation: my credence that they're correlated is still w.
- But P_w(X = H | Y = H) = w. If correlated, Y = H means X = H; if anti-correlated, Y = H means X = T. So conditioning on Y = H, the set of my credences in X is {w : w in [0,1]}, the whole unit interval. I go from knowing X's chance exactly to not knowing it at all.

So the shape of the puzzle: I start with a completely precise credence in X. I then learn something that (a) I had assigned probability exactly 1/2, (b) is equally likely under both of my hypotheses, and (c) is symmetric between X and Y — whatever it tells me about X it equally tells me about Y. And my credence in X goes from a point to vacuity.

The trilemma. I think the example forces us to give up one of these:

(1) Precision is a state. If every distribution in my credal set gives X the same probability, then I have a definite degree of belief about X and 1/2 is it; the precision isn't a summary of anything, it is my state.
(2) Conditioning is restriction. My state after learning E is {P(. | E) : P in M}.
(3) Evidence can't create vagueness. Learning something can narrow my interval for a variable but never strictly widen it, so conditioning on more information can't leave me more ignorant about X than I was.

The example satisfies (2) and violates (3), and (1) says the pre-conditioning state was a point, so (3) has real content here. So (1), (2), (3) can't all hold. I want to drop (1): the point-marginal is an artifact of averaging over hypotheses that disagree violently about X's conditional behaviour, so it's a summary, not a state. The number 1/2 is compatible with total vagueness about X's conditional structure. Corollary I like: you can't tell, from the marginal alone, whether someone's 50% means they lean neither way or that they have no idea what's going on.

But here's what makes me think (3) is the more interesting target anyway. Suppose I refuse to dilate and insist that after seeing Y = H my credence in X is still exactly 1/2. Since P(X = H | Y = H) = w, insisting it stays 1/2 means w = 1/2. So the only way to buy back my precision is to adopt a perfectly precise credence in the correlation, which I have no evidence for. Dilation-avoidance is purchased with fabricated precision about the hidden parameter.

Conjecture: this is general. Whenever conditioning on a symmetric, parameter-independent event dilates a precise marginal, the only way to avoid the dilation is to fix the hidden parameter at a point. If that's right, the choice isn't dilation versus no dilation, it's dilation versus pretending to know the parameter, and dilation wins.

A second-order fix that I expect fails, but you should check: replace the credal set with a hierarchical model, w drawn from some distribution. Then P(X = H | Y = H) = E[w] and there's no dilation at all, the marginal stays 1/2 and so does the conditional. But now I've committed to a precise prior over w, which is exactly the fabricated precision from before. So the honest version of the fix puts a set of priors over w, and I'd guess dilation reappears one level up. If it does, the regress is vicious and the second-order move is just a postponement. If it doesn't, that's a serious reply to the whole note and I want to know why.

Two consequences I want you to stress-test. First, elicitation and aggregation: methods that report a point probability per event, and that treat agreement of point probabilities as agreement, are reporting artifacts — two people can both say 50% about X while one of them is maximally vague. Second, ambiguity-averse decision rules: with maximin, before conditioning, betting on X is worth 1/2 to me; after conditioning on Y = H the worst case is 0, so I'd pay nothing for the same bet. Uninformative evidence changes what a maximin agent will pay. Is that an argument against maximin, or just a reminder that dilation is a property of the representation rather than of the agent?

Also please check whether there's a milder version of the example where dilation is partial rather than total, and whether anything in my argument depends on it being total.
