I host a long-form interview podcast on political philosophy, and next week's guest works on the ethics of time and work. Her position, which she calls temporal autonomy, is roughly this: what matters for a person's freedom is not how many hours she works but how much of her time is genuinely at her own disposal; discretionary time is a distinct distributive good in its own right, not a byproduct of income and not merely a means to rest and recovery; and on that basis she argues that the statutory 35-hour week is best defended as a claim about the fair division of time rather than as labour-market policy or paternalism, and that karoshi is fundamentally a failure of temporal control rather than a matter of excessive hours.

Write eight to ten interview questions that press the position rather than let her restate it, each followed by a follow-up keyed to the answer she is most likely to give. Include at least one question on each of the following:

- The welfare objection: if some workers would rather trade hours for pay, a cap leaves them worse off. Does she deny that such a preference counts, or hold that it is deformed by the work ethic? If the latter, how does she distinguish a distorted preference from one she merely dislikes, without circularity?
- The work-ethic objection: the Protestant ethic shapes how free time is experienced, so more discretionary hours may deliver nothing. Is temporal autonomy really a matter of schedules at all, or is its binding constraint cultural, and therefore untouched by law?
- The karoshi objection: if the harm is a failure of control rather than a quantity of hours, then a compensation regime, which must fix some threshold, is testing for the wrong thing. Ask what a control-based test would look like and whether it could be administered.
- The primary-good objection: time, unlike money, cannot be transferred, banked, or saved, so it is unclear what a fair distribution of it could mean. Press on whether the view requires a cap at all, or could be satisfied by flexibility that leaves hours unchanged.
- The meaning objection: many people locate their central good in their work, and a cap that prevents them from doing more looks like a deprivation rather than a liberation. How does she avoid paternalism here?
- The rights objection: a right to work is ordinarily claimed as a right to a job, while a right to rest and leisure is claimed as a limit on employers. Does her account make the right to work derivative of the good of discretionary time, and what follows for someone who wants to work more than the cap allows?

Close with a question about what evidence or argument would move her off the account, and about what would survive of the 35-hour week if the account failed.
