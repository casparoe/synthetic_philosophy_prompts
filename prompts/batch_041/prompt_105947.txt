You are a philosopher of political economy and a historian of the Scottish Enlightenment, equally at home in Adam Smith's economic and moral writings and in the administrative history of the eighteenth-century British Empire. Work carefully and in stages. The task is a piece of reasoning, not a recitation, so resist the temptation to reproduce the standard textbook summary of Smith on colonies and instead do the harder thing: let a theory meet a case, and let the case push back.

The episode is the East India Company's assumption of government in Bengal and the catastrophe that followed. The salient facts, which I supply so that your answer need not rest on contested detail: after the Company's victories at Plassey (1757) and Buxar (1764), the Mughal emperor granted it the diwani, the right to collect the revenues of Bengal, in 1765, while civil administration nominally remained with the Nawab. The Company, short of trained administrators, farmed out tax collection; within a few years land revenue collections rose by roughly a third; its servants and their agents built local monopolies in grain. A crop failure in 1768–69, compounded by these arrangements and by the Company's purchases for its armies, produced the Great Bengal famine of 1769–70, in which the population of Bengal fell by something like a quarter to a third. The Company provided little relief and did not reduce taxes. Within a few years it shed the fiction of dual government and became sole administrator, and Parliament moved to regulate it.

The theory is Smith's account of colonies and empire as it is developed across his economic and moral writings — The Theory of Moral Sentiments (1759) and An Inquiry into the Nature and Causes of the Wealth of Nations (1776). Reconstruct that account as you understand it: his analysis of the colonial trade monopoly and the "spirit of monopoly," his skepticism about whether empire repaid its costs, his doubts about the fitness of a chartered company of merchants to exercise sovereignty, and the moral machinery of the impartial spectator and the man of system. Reconstruct it charitably and precisely; then put it to work.

I want you to bring one idea of your own construction to the case, or rather to adopt and test the one I propose. Call it vicarious sovereignty: the condition in which the powers of a state — revenue, courts, armed force — are exercised by an agent whose accountability runs not to the governed but to distant principals, in this instance shareholders in London and a Parliament that chartered and regulated them. The concept is meant to name a structural position, not a motive; the point is that the sovereign and the accountable are different persons. Your job is to determine whether Smith's framework already contains this idea, whether it is a special case of his critique of monopoly, or whether it marks a blind spot.

Proceed through the following, using clear section headings.

1. Mapping. In Smith's terms, who are the relevant actors, and what are the levers his framework identifies? State what Smith's theory would predict about a body like the Company holding the revenues of a province it does not answer to, and check the prediction against what happened. Be disciplined about the mapping: say who plays which role rather than gesturing at "greed" or "imperialism."

2. Where the theory bites and where it misses. Identify the features of the Bengal episode that Smith's analysis explains well, and the features it explains poorly or redescribes in a way that loses something. Distinguish, if you can, failures of his economic analysis from failures of his moral analysis.

3. The spectator-gap hypothesis. Test this claim: that Smith's moral theory cannot do the work his economic theory leaves undone, because the impartial spectator, as Smith conceives it, is structurally unavailable at imperial distance — the governed do not enter the imagination of the distant spectator as parties whose resentment must be answered. Is this a fair reading of Smith, or does his theory supply resources he did not deploy? Say what would settle it.

4. The remedy. Smith proposed remedies for the colonial system. Assess them against this case: would they have addressed vicarious sovereignty, or only its symptoms? Then turn the question around — is his proposed remedy itself an artifact of his theory's limits?

5. The verdict on the theory. Does the episode confirm Smith's account, expose an anomaly within it, or present a case his theory never anticipated? Argue for one of these, and say what it would take to overturn your verdict.

6. Silence. State plainly where the theory is silent, rather than forcing it to speak. Do not manufacture a Smithian answer to a question he did not pose.

Before you write the analysis, spend a few sentences brainstorming the possible readings and flagging which you find weakest; then produce the finished analysis. No bibliography, editions, or citations are needed — reason from the concepts. Where you are uncertain about what Smith held, mark the uncertainty rather than papering over it, and end with your overall verdict in a short paragraph.
