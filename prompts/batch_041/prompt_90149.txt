Next week I am presenting a position I will call tranquilism to a comparative-philosophy reading group. The thesis I will defend is this: the only thing that is intrinsically good is the absence of craving, and suffering matters only as the mark and the cost of craving. Everything else people treat as a good — pleasure, knowledge, achievement, love, the satisfaction of a desire — is valuable either instrumentally, as a way of quieting craving, or not at all.

My argument, in roughly the form the audience will have in front of them: (1) every unpleasant experience is either a craving or the frustration of one; (2) satisfying a craving breeds a further craving, so any state that contains craving is self-undermining and cannot be a stable resting point; (3) only the absence of craving survives reflection and repetition; (4) therefore tranquility is the sole thing worth wanting for its own sake, and the pursuit of anything else is at best a means to it.

The audience is about a dozen people. Most are Buddhist studies scholars — several work on the Pali canon and the Theravada tradition, a couple on Tibetan Madhyamaka — and they know the primary texts and the commentarial literature far better than I do, and they are protective of the difference between a soteriological path and a philosophical axiology. Two are analytic philosophers who work on well-being: one is a hedonist who thinks experience is all that matters, the other a desire-satisfaction theorist. One is a contemplative-science researcher who cares about what meditation actually does to a person and is allergic to claims that cannot be operationalized. They are not hostile, but they are quick, and they do not like being flattered.

Give me the prep sheet: the eight or so objections I will actually get, ordered by how much damage they do, each phrased the way this particular audience would phrase it — a Buddhist studies scholar's objection should not sound like the well-being theorist's — each with a reply I can deliver in about two minutes and a blunt note on where that reply is soft. Include what I should concede up front; the single reply that has to land for the position to survive the hour; any objection that should make me amend the thesis rather than defend it as stated; and the one question I am hoping nobody asks.
