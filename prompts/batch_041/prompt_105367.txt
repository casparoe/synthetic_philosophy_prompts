Question 3.

(a) The thesis is that the probability of an indicative conditional equals the corresponding conditional probability: writing A → C for 'if A then C', P(A → C) = P(C | A) for every A and C with P(A) > 0. This is Lewis's thesis, and its appeal is easy to see. It explains why we assert 'if A then C' just to the extent that we think C likely given A; it underwrites the Ramsey test, on which you evaluate a conditional by hypothetically adding A to your beliefs and reading off your resulting confidence in C; and it gives a clean diagnosis of what's wrong with the truth-functional reading, on which 'if A then C' just means 'not-A or C'. On that reading, a conditional with an improbable antecedent comes out highly probable, which is not how our degrees of belief in conditionals behave.

(b) The argument is a reductio. Assume the equation holds. Two further things have to be in place. First, the conditional has to be a proposition like any other, hence embeddable in Boolean compounds: it can sit inside negations, disjunctions, and inside the antecedents and consequents of other conditionals. Second, the admissible probability functions have to be closed under conditioning, since learning something and updating your credences shouldn't push you outside the class of credence functions that are rationally available to you. Lewis then exploits the fact that A entails the equivalence of A → C with C, so that P(A → C | A) = P(C | A); feeding that back into the equation, and running the same manoeuvre after conditioning on the negation of the antecedent, forces the conditional to have a probability that can't be reconciled with the conditional probability it's supposed to equal. The proof needs only a minimally nontrivial set of events, so it isn't an artefact of any special assumption about how many possibilities there are.

(c) What the result establishes is that the equation can't be maintained as a general constraint on credence. Notice that the derivation leans on the conditional being embeddable and on the admissible functions being closed under conditioning, so one available move is to restrict the language: if conditionals can't appear inside the scope of other conditionals, the proof has nowhere to run and the equation survives in the fragment. That's a genuine option, not just a technical dodge. But if we keep the full language, something has to give, and the only way out is to deny that indicative conditionals express propositions at all — to go suppositional, as Adams and Edgington do. Since the equation can't hold, and since the material conditional also fails to track our degrees of belief in conditionals, the suppositional view is really the only serious option left standing.
