ok so my group chat has been re-litigating the ethics of belief for like two weeks now and I want to actually figure out what my own view is instead of just vibing about it. I know the Clifford/James thing backwards (the shipowner, the leap across the chasm, all of it), I've been reading LessWrong since I was like 15, so I'm not asking for the 101 version. The problem is that every version of the debate I see online gets stuck on the same two examples and I don't actually have a firm gut reaction to either of them. So here's a pile of cases where I *do* have gut reactions, and what I want is the principle, if there is one, that gets all of them or close to all of them.

My friend coined the term 'load-bearing belief' for a belief that does work in the world -- where believing it is part of what makes it true, or where holding it is more like a way of acting than a way of representing -- and I keep wanting to say load-bearingness is the thing that makes the difference. Cases: (1) Before a big talk I tell myself I'm going to nail it and that the room is friendly, which I have zero evidence for; it settles my nerves and I do better. Fine. (2) My partner has never given me any reason to worry and I genuinely have no evidence about half of what she does on her trips. I just believe she's faithful to me, and honestly the belief is part of why she is -- trust begets trust or whatever. Fine. (3) A colleague basically decided her theory was right before she had the data, and says the commitment is the only reason she kept running experiments for six years; she still published the results that went against her. Fine, I think. (4) Same shape, reversed: a teacher I know decided on pretty thin grounds that one kid in her class was a bad apple, is harder on him than on the others, and now he acts out. Not fine. (5) My uncle, who's 80, has started going to church and says he beleives in God, mostly because it consoles him about dying; he isn't claiming any evidence at all. I want to say not fine?? (6) A juror who talks himself into thinking the defendant did it because the alternative is a longer trial. Obviously not fine. (7) A woman with a serious diagnosis who believes she's going to beat it and says that belief is the only thing keeping her going, when her doctors give her 30%. Fine? (8) A friend whose brother has been accused of something serious; she believes he didn't do it, and at this point I think she'd be a useless witness because she can't see the evidence straight. Not fine, but it feels different from (5) somehow.

So the shape of the problem as I see it: the load-bearing story saves (1), (2), (3) and (7) and maybe (4) too -- but (4) is definately the killer, because that belief is load-bearing in exactly the same sense and it's obviously not OK, which makes me think the clause has to be something like 'load-bearing in the direction of the believer's own conduct' or 'load-bearing in a way we'd endorse.' And the boring evidentialist story saves (4), (5), (6) and (8) but throws out (1), (2) and (3), which feel like the heart of the thing to me. Also I half-remember something about pragmatic encroachment where the stakes change whether you count as knowing, and I can't tell if that's the same question or a different one. What I want from you: a principle or a small set of candidate principles, stated precisely, each clause with the case that forces it; its verdicts on all the cases above plus the ones I'm torn on -- the doctor who tells herself the patient is going to pull through, and someone who believes in their own talent mainly because the alternative is too depressing to think about; and an honest statement of which of my verdicts it can't accommodate. If the honest answer is that no single principle gets everything and I have to give one up, say which one and what it costs me. No reading lists please, just the principle.
