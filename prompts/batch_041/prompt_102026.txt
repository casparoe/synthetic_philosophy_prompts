I teach a second-year undergraduate course in the theory of computation, and next week I have a session on the Church–Turing thesis for students who have just seen the halting-problem proof and have come away believing that the thesis is a theorem — that Turing proved what can and cannot be computed. I do not want the explanation itself; I want a plan for how to give it. Set out the order in which the components must be introduced, and make the dependency structure explicit: what has to be in place before the thesis can even be stated as a substantive claim rather than a disguised definition; why the informal notion of an effective procedure has to be made to feel genuinely unsettled before any formal model is offered; and where the distinction between the claim that our pre-theoretic notion of computability coincides with Turing-machine computability and the separate claim that all reasonable formal models of computation agree with one another — the so-called invariance thesis — must fall in the sequence. Nominate the single carrying example for the whole session and say what it must have and lack. I am inclined to build it on the pre-1936 image of a human calculator, a person with pencil and paper following a fixed set of rules, and I want you to say whether that carries the weight or whether something else should, and why a laptop or a named programming language would be the wrong carrier. Then give me the misconceptions these students will arrive with or form, and the exact point in the sequence at which each should be headed off: that the thesis has been proved; that it is an empirical hypothesis about the limits of physical machines, so that a quantum or hypercomputing device would refute it; that a Turing machine is just another name for a computer; that the thesis is a claim about speed or memory; and that it licenses conclusions about what human minds can do. Finally, give me one question to ask at the end that would reveal whether they have understood the thesis's epistemic status rather than merely memorised its statement, and tell me what I should openly refuse to settle at this level — I am thinking in particular of whether the thesis is best read as a definition, a stipulation, or an empirical conjecture, and of how physical versions of the thesis relate to the mathematical one.
