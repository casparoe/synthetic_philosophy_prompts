I am presenting a paper at our departmental work-in-progress seminar in which I defend what I call karmic desert: the claim that karma, once detached from rebirth and from any mechanism of cosmic enforcement, is best read not as a distributive principle — a reason for some agent to allocate benefits in proportion to virtue — but as a claim about the fittingness of the world, namely that a world in which a person's willing is morally excellent and their life goes badly is defective, quite apart from what anyone ought to do about it. My argument distinguishes this claim about fit both from retributivism and from desert understood as a criterion of distributive justice, and suggests that the tradition's talk of a proportional relation between action and its ripening is one way of stating it.

Give me the objections I will actually get from these people, ordered both by how likely each is and by how much damage it does to my position, phrased as each group would phrase it — the Buddhist scholars' objection to my reading of intention will not be the naturalist's objection to cosmic fit, and the Rawlsian will not make the philosopher of religion's point.
