Write me a short story about a court astronomer in central Europe in the early 1600s who has to hand over a horoscope. He's basically Kepler, so you can use the texture of the real guy's life — the emperor, the money troubles, the almanacs and calendars, the printers, the constant moving — but don't turn it into a biography.

Here's the part I actually care about, because it's the part people get wrong. He is not a charlatan and he is not a sucker. He thinks alot of what the astrologers do is junk: the twelve houses, the signs, the rulerships, the lots, the whole inherited apparatus that nobody has ever given a reason for, and he says so out loud, which does not make him popular. But he doesn't think it's all junk. He thinks one piece of it is real, and the piece is the angles. The geometry. What two planets are doing to each other at a given moment, expressed as an angle, and whether that angle is a harmony in the same sense that a musical interval is a harmony. That's why he keeps adding angles the old books don't even list, and why he treats the sign a planet happens to sit in as close to irrelevant. He thinks the planets throw off some kind of immaterial emanation, that the angle between them modulates it, and that the thing it lands on is the soul, or the imagination, rather than the body directly — which is how a chart could say something about a person's temperament or luck without any of the old machinery. And he thinks it only tilts a person, it never forces them; if it forced them he'd have to give up on virtue and on God, and he isn't going to do that. The moment of birth matters because that's when the soul is first out in the open, so to speak, and gets struck by whatever the sky is up to.

Now the story. Somebody with power over him wants a chart and he has to produce it. Pick your situation — a sick patron who wants to know whether he'll live, a newborn in a family he needs on his side, a general about to march, a city about to hold an election, whatever gives you the most pressure. I don't want a debate, I definately don't want a skeptic and a believer arguing across a table, and I really don't want him secretly knowing it's all nonsense and lying for money — that's the boring version and it isn't true to him. He believes some of it, he can't prove any of it, and he needs the money, all at once. Show me that in what he actually writes down: the parts he fills in honestly, the parts he fudges, the one thing he decides to leave out of the chart, the gap between what he thinks and what he hands over. Let the ordinary stuff be in there too, the cold and the roads and the bill he can't pay.

Don't have anybody explain the physics or the metaphysics out loud in dialogue, and keep the vocabulary period-appropriate — he wouldn't have words for science or pseudoscience, and he'd never say he's running a cost-benefit analysis. Roughly 2000 to 3500 words.
