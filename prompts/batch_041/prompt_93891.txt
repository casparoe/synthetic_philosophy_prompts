Many jurisdictions forbid employers from paying below a set hourly wage and forbid landlords from charging above a set rent, yet let prices fall where they may in most other markets, including markets in which poor people are the sellers. Each rule restricts the terms of a voluntary exchange on behalf of the poorer side of that exchange — the worker selling labor, the tenant buying shelter — and each comes with carve-outs that sit awkwardly with its usual moral defense: a lower floor for tipped workers, exemption of newly built housing from rent limits, and a general refusal to cap the prices of the goods the poor must buy. What view of fair price, or of the moral status of voluntary exchange, does this pattern presuppose? Does it require a notion of a just price fixed independently of what the market would yield, or can it make do with a thinner claim about unequal bargaining power and duress? Do the carve-outs and the abstention from other markets follow from the same principle, or do they reveal that the practice is really aimed at securing a decent minimum rather than fairness in exchange? Reconstruct the implicit theory as well as you can, distinguish what the practice strictly requires from what it merely fits well with, and say what would have to change if a thoroughgoing freedom-of-contract view replaced it.
