A comparison has hardened into a slogan in discussions of generative image models: the model is the camera, and the person writing the prompt is the photographer. The argument it is made to carry runs roughly as follows. Photography was once dismissed as a merely mechanical transcription of the visible world, the popular telling has painters declaring their art finished, and the anxiety passed: photography became an art form, its practitioners became artists, and the medium developed formal resources that painting could not supply. The present objection to AI-generated images, the argument concludes, is the same mistake in a new costume, and it will be corrected in the same way; we should relax and let the new medium find its artists.

I want an audit of that analogy rather than a verdict on it. Begin with the correspondence: state explicitly what in the photographic case is meant to map onto the generative model, onto the prompt-writer, onto the training corpus, onto the lens and the sensor, onto the print or the edit, onto the sitter or the scene, and onto the institutions that eventually conferred legitimacy on the medium. Where the analogy can be read in more than one way — is the prompt a shutter release, or a commission issued to a contractor? is the model a lens, or a studio assistant with a style of its own? — identify the reading the argument requires and say whether that is the more plausible reading.

Then locate the disanalogies, and separate those that bear on the conclusion from those that are merely picturesque. Three strike me as pressing. The first concerns reference: a photograph is causally tied to a scene it did not author, so its subject matter is supplied from outside the process, whereas in the generative case the same process that produces the surface also produces the subject matter; say what the argument loses if that tie is not transferable. The second concerns control: the photographer's decisions — framing, timing, exposure, development, cropping, sequencing — are continuous, legible and revisable in a way that prompting may not be, and the question of authorship may turn on precisely this rather than on mechanism; say what follows if the prompter's control is coarse, intermittent, or opaque to the prompter herself. The third concerns the structure of the conclusion: if photography earned its standing through a formal achievement that the camera alone could not deliver, then the historical parallel cannot establish what it is being used to establish, since the analogous achievement in the generative case is exactly what is in dispute. I would also like an account of what the analogy quietly imports from its source — what a photograph's relation to the world, or a photographer's relation to her instrument, lends the comparison that the target case may lack.

Close by saying what, if anything, the camera comparison does establish about authorship, and whether once the disanalogies are on the table it still supports the relaxed conclusion or begins to run the other way. If the camera is the wrong comparison, offer a better one, say what it makes visible that the camera hides, and note what it in turn distorts. Work the analogy out on its merits; no survey of the literature, no reading list, and no citations are wanted.
