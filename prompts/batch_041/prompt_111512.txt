For several months a reading group I run — half game theorists, half people who work on AI alignment — has been circling the same dispute, and what I want is a diagnosis rather than a verdict. The dispute concerns one-shot cooperation. The formal result we keep returning to is this: model each player's strategy as a program that can read the opponent's program before acting, and in a one-shot prisoner's dilemma there is an equilibrium in which both cooperate, because each program conditions its choice on what the other program does and the mutual reference is discharged as a fixed point. A second family of results has the agents neither compute nor simulate each other: each cooperates because it can prove a theorem about what the other does, and the proof is licensed by a self-referential result from provability logic.

The formalists in the group treat these as genuine advances — cooperation in a one-shot dilemma, without repetition, reputation, or an external enforcer — and they take the idealizations to be relaxable in the directions that matter: bounded resources, uncertainty about the other's code, asymmetric access. The sceptics treat them as artifacts. The first result, they say, hands the players a commitment device in a game whose entire point was that no such device is available; the second presupposes logical omniscience, a shared proof system, and a reasoner that can trust its own consistency, which no sufficiently strong reasoner can; and both treat an uncomputable fixed point as a solution. The formalists reply that equilibrium selection was never the claim — mutual defection and extortionate profiles are equilibria of the same model, and there may be more than one fixed point — and that the sceptics are really asking what a boundedly rational agent ought to do, which is a different question. My own hypothesis is that this last reply is the whole story: that the two sides are not disagreeing about the mathematics but about what the prisoner's dilemma was ever a model of, and that the phrase 'solves the prisoner's dilemma' is doing the work.

Diagnose this. Is the impasse substantive, verbal, or a hidden formal question, and if it is partly verbal, which sub-disputes survive translation? What is each side actually committed to about idealization in game theory, and is there a principled line between idealizations that sharpen a problem and idealizations that delete it? Consider the case I keep proposing and nobody likes: two agents that can read each other's source code but cannot prove anything about programs beyond a fixed length. Does the cooperation result survive that restriction, and if it does not, was it ever a result about cooperation rather than about a formal object? Then say what would count as progress given your diagnosis: a resource-bounded version of the cooperation theorem with explicit failure conditions, an impossibility result, a distinction (say, between code transparency, dispositional transparency, and provability transparency), a change of question, or nothing, with reasons. I do not want a literature review or a list of who said what; I want the structure of the disagreement, argued from the positions as I have stated them, with a note wherever I have misdescribed one.
