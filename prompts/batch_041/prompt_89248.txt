For about ten years I've been a regular reader of the rationalist and effective-altruist blogosphere — specifically the corner of it that takes seriously the thought that our descendants may outnumber us by astronomical factors, and that this fact should reorganize our priorities. I want a satirical piece that takes that world's own rhetoric at face value and follows it somewhere uncomfortable. To be clear, I'm not after a takedown of the movement; the best version of this would be written by someone who finds the underlying arguments genuinely impressive and is willing to say so implicitly by getting them exactly right.

Write a sales prospectus for a private off-world settlement: a habitat development selling "residency shares" to would-be colonists and to investors who will never leave Earth. The venue is corporate marketing — a glossy brochure, a numbered terms-and-conditions section, and a short FAQ. Keep the register deadpan and optimistic from first line to last: no winking, no editorial asides, no character who exists in order to be wrong. Roughly 700–900 words.

The target is a family of arguments rather than any person: the reasoning that treats the expected value of a very large future as so vast that present-day costs, present-day claimants, and the existing occupants of the relevant environments become rounding errors; the corollary that the first successful colonization attempt has to lock in the right values and therefore cannot afford to be porous, revisable, or slow; and the habit of converting moral questions — who counts, what we owe the places we arrive in — into questions of liability, insurance, and regulatory compliance.

The humor has to come from the content, so let the fine print carry the philosophy. I'd like at least the following present, each handled as a real clause rather than a punchline: (1) a moral-subject registration scheme that quietly sorts beings into tiers with different standing and different compensation, where the tier boundaries do real work; (2) a posterity instrument — some escrow, dividend, or offset — whose arithmetic discounts a present harm against an enormous number of future beneficiaries; (3) an exit clause that is formally generous and practically void, together with an explanation of why the founders believe a binding constitution must be settled before anyone has consented to it; (4) a section on protecting the destination environment that treats it purely as contamination liability and insurability. A reader who knows the astronomical-waste argument (roughly, that a delayed or failed expansion forfeits value on a scale that dwarfs any present cost), the proposal that we pause to settle our values before expanding, worries about value lock-in, and the planetary-protection literature should be able to tell that the author knows them; a reader who doesn't should still follow the argument and find it funny. Invent the venture, its officers, and its instruments; do not name real companies, living people, or actual missions. Nothing should be invented that isn't an extrapolation of a position someone actually holds — exaggerate, don't fabricate.

Finally, append a short note, clearly separated from the prospectus, stating how confident you are that each clause satirizes a view actually held rather than a caricature, and identifying the single clause you are least sure is a fair extrapolation of the underlying argument.
