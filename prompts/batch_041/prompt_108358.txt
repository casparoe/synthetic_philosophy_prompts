I write long-form features on science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I'm about to begin reporting a piece on the moral status of digital minds — the question of whether artificial systems could matter morally in their own right, and what follows if they might. I have a fixed reporting budget and a fixed word count, which means the piece can carry three or four threads and no more, so the ordering I settle on will determine what I spend months on and what I drop. The trouble is that the public conversation keeps returning to the same few moves: the substrate-independence argument, the dismissal that it is all next-token prediction, the vague gesture at uncertainty. Those are familiar, but I don't believe they are where the action is. I want the piece built around the questions that are genuinely live and genuinely consequential.

So please set the agenda for me. Which open questions in this area matter most, and why? I want a defended ordering rather than a survey, argued on stated criteria: how much other work depends on the answer, how tractable the question is for people who cannot run experiments, how neglected it is, and how much the answer would actually change what someone does.

Two framings have stuck with me, and I would like you to treat them as serious lenses rather than wave them away. First, that the interesting moral problem may not be the status of any single system but what happens when a mind of uncertain status is manufactured at scale — instantiated many times over, copied and run in parallel, by a process optimized for something else entirely, whether engagement or benchmark scores. Second, that because these systems are designed, the question is not only what we owe them once they exist but what we are permitted to build in the first place, and who gets to decide. If those lenses are sound, they should rearrange the list; if they are wrong or overstated, say so and show me precisely where they break.

For each question you rank highly, tell me what would move it up or down over the next year or two — what a lab, a regulator, or a philosopher could do or discover that would change its standing. And tell me what is overrated: which questions attract disproportionate attention because they are rhetorically convenient or easy to pose, but whose resolution would not change much downstream. I don't want a reading list; I want your reasoning, not pointers to the literature.
