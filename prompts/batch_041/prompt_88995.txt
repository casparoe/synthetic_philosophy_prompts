Im putting together a little taxonomy of views about which computations have moral standing, and I need a clean test case to hang it on. Two views I want to pull apart. First, broad functionalism: sentience — and moral status with it — attaches to any system that instantiates the right kind of information-processing organisation, the right computations in the right causal roles; whether the system owns those states as its own is beside the point. Second, attributionism: a computation only carries moral weight when it belongs to a subject — when the system can in some sense take the state to be its own, when there's someone the computation is for. An unattributed computation, running with no one it's for, weighs nothing.

Give me one thought experiment that isolates the attribution variable: keep the functional organisation fixed and vary only whether the computation is owned by a subject, so that the two views agree on one variant and split on the other, with the split due to attribution alone. Concrete, nothing exotic — no zombies, no Chinese rooms, no brains in vats, no infinite simulations, no perfect predictors. A paragraph or two for the scenario is plenty.

Then the audit: for each variant, what each view says and why; which single feature is doing the isolating work; what happens if you strip that feature out; and the best line of attack for whichever view comes off worse — can the functionalist just reabsorb attribution as one more bit of functional organisation, or can the attributionist say why an unattributed computation differs in kind and not just in degree? And if the reverse split is even possible — attributionism yes, functionalism no — sketch it, or say why it can't happen.
