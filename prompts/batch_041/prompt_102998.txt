For about two years I have been arguing with two colleagues about a puzzle in the economics of information, and we have reached the stage where each of us can predict the other's next move and nobody's position has shifted. I would like a diagnosis of what kind of disagreement this is rather than a verdict on the substantive question.

The puzzle concerns the no-trade results in rational-expectations economics. As I understand them, these results say roughly the following: if traders share a common prior, if it is common knowledge that they are rational, and if the price at which they trade already reflects all available information, then no trade can occur for informational reasons. Whoever would buy must suspect that the seller knows something adverse, and whoever would sell must suspect the same of the buyer, so the two informational motives cancel; any trade that survives must be driven by something else — hedging, liquidity needs, differences in risk tolerance. And yet real markets clear enormous volume, much of which looks informational: people trade because they believe they know something the other side does not. That gap is the puzzle.

The three of us read it differently. The first colleague, whom I will call the scope theorist, holds that the theorem is sound but that its premises are never satisfied in practice — common priors fail, and once priors differ, trading on disagreement is perfectly rational and the theorem simply says nothing about it. On this view the puzzle dissolves: the no-trade results are a boundary case, not a description of markets, and the interesting question is an empirical one about how heterogeneous real beliefs are. The second colleague, the framework critic, thinks this is too quick. For him the theorem exposes a defect in the whole apparatus: treating the market price as a sufficient statistic for dispersed private information is incoherent, as the familiar paradox about fully revealing prices suggests — if the price already reveals everything, no one is compensated for the costly act of discovering it in the first place — so the claim that prices aggregate dispersed knowledge is either empty or self-undermining, and the no-trade result is a symptom of that, not a curiosity about priors. I find myself pulled toward a third reading, which is that we are not really disagreeing about markets at all but about what an explanation of trade is supposed to accomplish: whether a theorem of this kind is meant to constrain which trades are possible under an idealization, or to identify the causes of the trades we actually observe.

My hypothesis, offered as a hypothesis, is that the impasse is not empirical but conceptual. It seems to me that the sides may be answering two different questions under a single heading — one normative and model-theoretic, about what perfectly rational and perfectly informed traders would do, and one descriptive and causal, about why the trades we see happen — and that the phrase about information being contained in or aggregated by a price may be doing rhetorical work that no one has given truth conditions. But I am not confident of this, and I would rather have your diagnosis than my own.

So: is this a substantive disagreement, a verbal one about what it means for a price to carry information, a hidden empirical question about priors and motives, or two groups answering different questions under one name? Are the sides differing about facts, about what counts as the right idealization, about what an explanation must do, or about starting intuitions neither can argue for? Does the persistence of the impasse indicate that the question is ill-posed, or merely that it is hard? Engage the three positions as I have stated them rather than surveying the literature, and tell me what, given your diagnosis, would count as progress — a distinction, a formal result, a testable prediction, or a change of question — or else argue that no progress should be expected here and explain why.
