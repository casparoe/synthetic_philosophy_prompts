I write interpreters and compilers for a living, which means I spend my days moving between two levels: a structure I am interpreting and the host language I use to write the interpreter. A friend who studies logic recently tried to explain Skolem's paradox to me, and I came away with roughly this: the axioms of set theory, if they have any model at all, have one whose domain can be listed by a program, and yet the same axioms prove Cantor's theorem — that some sets, beginning with the set of subsets of the natural numbers, are uncountable. I think I follow the standard response: the listing, and the bijection that would make the allegedly uncountable set countable, are host-level objects rather than elements of the interpreted structure, so the model never contradicts itself; its own proof of uncountability is simply about a domain in which the relevant bijection has no name. I would like the whole thing — the paradox, the standard dissolution, and the dispute between those who think that dissolution is the end of the matter and those who think it leaves something genuinely disturbing about the determinacy of set-theoretic notions — restated systematically in my vocabulary: domain as data structure, satisfaction as whatever my interpreter returns on a formula, countable as enumerable by a program, bijection as an invertible encoding-and-decoding pair, the model's blindness as an object living in the host language for which the interpreted structure contains no element, the object-language/meta-language distinction as the distinction between the language I implement and the language I implement it in, and absoluteness, if it translates at all, as implementation-independence.

Be as faithful as you can, and do not tidy the argument to fit my terms; where my vocabulary forces a choice that the mathematicians' vocabulary leaves open, say that this is what is happening rather than making the choice for me. I am reasonably fluent in the programming half — I have written a few interpreters, I know what a countable set is, and I can read a definition if you give me one — but I have no training in set theory or model theory, so anything you need from those should either be introduced in my terms or flagged as something that does not translate.

Then I want the residue report, and I want it to be the interesting part rather than a footnote. Which steps survive unchanged — concretely, if I sat down and wrote the interpreter and the enumeration, is there any step of the argument I could not represent, and would the model's internal proof of Cantor's theorem come out looking like an ordinary program that simply never gets handed a certain value? Which steps translate only with distortion, and what exactly gets bent? And what has no counterpart at all? My suspicion is that the casualties are the idea that set theory has an intended subject matter, the idea that uncountable names a single property rather than a relation between a structure and the language we describe it in, and any notion of truth that is not truth-in-this-interpreter. I would add one specific thing I half-remember and cannot place: that in these countable models, the model's own version of the set of subsets of the natural numbers is a proper part of the real one, which in my vocabulary sounds like an interpreted type having strictly fewer inhabitants than the host type it is drawn from. Tell me whether that point is doing work in the original debate or is merely an artifact of how the models are built. Finally, judge the residue: does it show that my vocabulary cannot express a real worry, or that the worry was a demand for a privileged implementation — a unique intended model — that was never on offer? I would rather have the answer that is unflattering to my framework if that is the honest one.
