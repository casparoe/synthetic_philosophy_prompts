You are an expert in early modern philosophy and the history of monetary thought. I am assembling a set of cases for a seminar on the philosophy of money, and I need a thought experiment built to order. The target is Berkeley's view that money is best understood not as a commodity with value in itself but as a ticket or counter, a claim on real goods, so that its economic role depends on convention, trust, and circulation rather than on the metallic content of the coins. I want a case that isolates this view from a straightforward metallist alternative, where the metallist treats the value of money as inhering in the precious metal and therefore treats the physical survival of the metal as the survival of wealth. Do not rely on any particular passage or title; I care about the theoretical contrast, not textual citation. Think carefully about the difference between a change in the token of exchange and a change in the goods for which tokens can be exchanged.

Construct a thought experiment with the following specifications. It must be realistic: a small port town or colonial settlement, not a fairy tale, and no magic, no perfect foresight, and no omnipotent intervener. The scenario itself should be one paragraph. It must involve a change in the monetary convention or in the willingness to accept a token, while holding fixed the town's real productive capacity, its stock of consumable goods, and its people's skills and needs. The case must not be a simple inflation story, and it must not turn on the physical destruction of the metal. The specified verdicts are: the metallist should conclude that the town's real wealth is essentially unchanged, because the metal is still present and can still be valued as bullion, while the Berkeleyan should conclude that the town has suffered a real loss of wealth or of command over goods, because the tickets or counters have ceased to function as claims. The disagreement should arise from the theories themselves, not from one side being made to hold an obviously silly auxiliary assumption. Finally, the case should include one feature that is doing the real work in separating the two verdicts, and it should be a feature that could be removed without changing the physical facts of the town.

Begin with a short Brainstorm section of three to five bullet points. Then give the response in the following sections, in this order and with roughly these lengths: Scenario (one paragraph); The metallist's verdict (roughly 150 to 200 words); The Berkeleyan's verdict (roughly 150 to 200 words); The feature doing the work (roughly 100 to 150 words); If we remove that feature (roughly 100 to 150 words); How a metallist would push back (roughly 100 to 150 words). In the final section, give the strongest reply available to the metallist, not a straw man. Be precise about which claims belong to Berkeley's monetary view and which are your own reconstruction.
