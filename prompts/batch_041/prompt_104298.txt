I'm in a slow-reading group that's working through an old Victorian translation of the Zhuangzi (the 1889 Giles one, which is gorgeous but full of weird archaisms), and we've gotten stuck on the butterfly bit at the end of the second chapter. The gist of it, as far as I can render it: Chuang Chou once dreamed he was a butterfly, fluttering about, happy, with no awareness of being Chuang Chou at all. Then he woke and was, to all appearances, Chuang Chou again. And now he says he can't tell whether he's Chuang Chou who dreamed he was a butterfly, or a butterfly now dreaming it's Chuang Chou. Then there's a line about how between Chuang Chou and the butterfly there must be some distinction, and this is called the transformation of things.

What I can't get my head around is what the doubt is even for. When Descartes does the dream thing, the doubt is a tool -- he uses it to tear everything down and then rebuild. Here it feels like the doubt is just left hanging, and then the story ends with this phrase about the transformation of things, which sounds like a resolution but I can't see how it resolves anything. And the line about there being some distinction between the man and the butterfly seems to pull in the opposite direction from the skepticism -- if there's a distinction, then aren't we back to being able to say which one you are? I suspect I'm missing something about what's being doubted in the first place. Is it whether I know what I am, or whether there's a stable I there to know at all? And does the ending mean the doubt is meant to be dissolved rather than answered? Help me understand whats going on in the passage, especially that last move -- I don't need the whole history of dream arguments, just what this particular text is doing with it.
