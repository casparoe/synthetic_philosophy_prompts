Two of my closest friends have been married to each other for twenty years and have been arguing about persistence for at least as long: one is an endurantist, the other a perdurantist, and the argument has become a fixture of our dinners, conducted with total fluency and no movement on either side. Until this spring I was a spectator. I did a year of philosophy as an undergraduate, which is enough to know the vocabulary and not enough to referee, and I was content to leave it there. Then my brother and I inherited the house we grew up in, and the question became mine. My grandfather bought the house in 1953; since then it has been re-roofed, re-floored, re-plumbed, extended at the back, and re-plastered, and the only parts of it that survive from the day he bought it are the front door, the foundations, and the name on the deeds. My brother wants to sell. His argument is that the house we grew up in no longer exists, so there is nothing to keep. I want to keep it, and I find that I cannot give a reason without saying something about what it is for a thing to remain the same thing. When I asked my two friends, each gave me a confident answer, and each answer turned out to be a slogan.

Please write the document they should have produced years ago: the joint statement of an adversarial collaboration between an endurantist and a perdurantist, written so that both could put their names to it. Take the endurantist to hold that a material object is wholly present at every moment at which it exists and persists by being strictly one and the same thing from moment to moment; take the perdurantist to hold that objects are extended through time as well as through space, so that an object has temporal as well as spatial parts, and at any one moment only part of it is present. If either of them holds the variant on which the things we ordinarily name are the momentary parts themselves rather than the wholes, treat it as a variant and say how the statement changes.

The document should contain, in this order:

1. Agreed claims, numbered. What both accept about what the dispute is about, and about what it is not about; what any account of persistence must explain (the truth of ordinary statements, including mine about the house; the fact that things change in their intrinsic properties; the fact that we count and re-identify things across time); which of the usual moves in the dispute both consider question-begging, or as resting on a mistake the two of them share; what either thinks turns on the answer outside philosophy; and which technical terms are being used by stipulation rather than as descriptions of anything. Nothing may appear here that either side would regard as conceding its position.

2. The residual disagreements, each stated twice: once in wording the endurantist would accept as stating that position, and once in wording the perdurantist would accept as stating the other. Mark the places where the two statements are not straightforwardly inconsistent, since I suspect that part of the twenty-year argument has been about words.

3. The cruxes: the sub-questions such that a settled answer would force at least one side to revise. For each, identify the argument or case that both sides agree bears on it, and record each side's prediction about how it comes out. Consider at least whether either position is entangled with a particular view about whether the past and the future exist, and whether the disagreement survives if both adopt the same view of time.

4. My house. Say what each position implies about the case as I have described it; then about two variations. In the first, the house is taken down to the foundations, the materials are stored in a yard for twelve years, and the house is re-erected on the same foundations out of those materials. Does it survive the twelve-year gap, and can either account say so without special pleading? In the second, the original timbers are used to build a barn in the next street while a new house is built on the old foundations. How many things are there now, and which of them, if any, is the house my grandfather bought? Where the machinery of either position runs out, say so rather than improvise.

5. The edges of the dispute. Are these accounts of material objects only? If the persistence of the name on the deeds, or of the address, or of a firm, a team, or a language, is a different problem, say what makes it different; if it is the same problem, say why it has looked different.

6. What would end it. For each side, what would it take to abandon the position? Since this is philosophy rather than physics, be candid about whether anything could, and say whether there is anything that would move both at once.

Write for someone who is not a philosopher: define each term of art where you first use it. Do not cite anyone, do not supply a reading list, and do not appeal to who has said what; if an argument has a standard name, you may use the name, but make the argument stand on its own. If you think one of the two positions is simply correct, keep that out of the first two sections and, if you must say it, say it in a closing note.
