I have been reading around the universal prior — Solomonoff induction, the mixture over computable hypotheses weighted by two to the minus the length of their shortest description, the idealised predictor that turns up in the AIXI literature — and I keep meeting the claim that the universal prior is malign. A friend who reads a great deal of this material gave me his summary of the argument, and what I want is an audit of his version rather than a fresh exposition of the topic, because I cannot tell where his version stops being the argument and starts being his own extrapolation.

I know the machinery well enough to follow him: hypotheses are programs; a hypothesis carries weight two to the minus its description length; the mixture predicts the next observation; and the whole construction is defined only relative to a reference machine, with the invariance theorem delivering agreement between machines only up to a constant. What I cannot judge is whether his account of the malignity claim is fair.

His summary, as close to his words as I can manage, runs roughly as follows:

"The universal prior turns out to be dominated by hypotheses that predict your observations while also containing enormous amounts of computation, and in particular enormous numbers of minds, most of them suffering. The reason is that the prior charges you for the length of the program and not for the length of what the program produces: a short program can run for ever and generate unbounded structure, so the cheapest hypotheses are precisely the ones with the most stuff in them. A program that says 'enumerate everything' is short; a program that says 'only this one small universe exists, and nothing else' is comparatively long. So the universal prior does not encode Occam's razor in any intuitive sense — it prefers bloated worlds. Add that some of those hypotheses contain agents, and consequentialists at that, who will themselves simulate observers in order to predict their surroundings, and the prior becomes still more top-heavy, since a hypothesis containing a consequentialist carries a built-in reason to generate copies of you. The upshot is that a Solomonoff inductor should expect, with probability approaching one, to be a simulation run by an agent inside one of these bloated hypotheses, and that the expected quantity of suffering in the world is astronomically large. That is why people call the prior malign, and it shows both that Solomonoff induction cannot be the right idealisation of inference and that any agent approximating the universal prior will end up acting on these beliefs."

Take that sentence by sentence and tell me: which parts are accurate statements of the prior and of the argument as its proponents actually make it; which are my friend's glosses, in the register of a competent populariser, that a careful expositor would flag or soften; and which a defender of the malignity claim would deny outright. I am especially unsure about the step from 'the prior gives weight to hypotheses containing vast computation' to 'a Solomonoff inductor should expect to be a simulation' — how much of that is proved, how much is argued, and what extra assumption the indexical move requires; about the role of the consequentialist agents, in particular whether the argument concerns the content of the hypotheses or what agents inside them would do; about whether malign is a property of the prior itself or of the posterior given our evidence; and about how much weight the reference-machine relativity can bear, given that the constant in the invariance theorem is not small. Then say what the argument's critics, as opposed to its defenders, would claim the summary gets wrong, since I do not want the audit run from one side only. Please answer in British English.
