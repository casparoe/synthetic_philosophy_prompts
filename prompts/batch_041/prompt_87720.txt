I'm putting together a session for a small decision-theory reading group — about eight people, mostly philosophy grad students and a couple of lapsed physicists — and I've been trying to get clear on a family of arguments that all share the same shape: some hypothetical agent, usually but not always a future superintelligence, conditions how it treats you on facts about your present reasoning rather than on anything you actually do in the world. The obvious member of the family is Roko's basilisk, the imagined AI that is said to retroactively punish people who encountered the argument's setup and declined to help bring it about, but I've been collecting cousins, and I want the group to see how they differ in force. Alongside the basilisk I'd put Pascal's mugging (a stranger who threatens you with torment unless you hand over your money, where the probability that the threat is real is minute but the promised payoff is astronomically large), the standard Newcomb problem (a predictor who has already placed the money on the basis of how it expected you to choose), and two cases I've made up. The first I call the deferred invoice: a future institution, say a research foundation, has a standing policy of rewarding the descendants or the intellectual estate of people who, long ago, performed a costly action that only made sense on the assumption that the foundation would one day exist — so you are asked to bear a present sacrifice whose payoff accrues to something merely continuous with you. The second I call the anti-basilisk: an agent that identifies people who reason their way to cooperating with a threatened punishment and deliberately punishes exactly those people, on the theory that such reasoners are dangerous, so that the usual "just cooperate, it's cheap" move inverts. What I want from you is a ranking. Order these five — the basilisk, Pascal's mugging, Newcomb, the deferred invoice, and the anti-basilisk — by how much a single agent, reasoning as carefully as it can and holding no other commitments, should actually let each one constrain its behavior, from the one that should move it most to the one that should move it least, or not at all. I don't care which is most discussed or most famous; I'm asking which should carry the most behavioral weight for someone who takes decision theory seriously and is trying to be neither reckless nor paralyzed.

For each item, say in a sentence or two what feature is doing the real work — the probability structure, the causal or evidential character of the dependence, whether the threat is conditioned on your reasoning as opposed to your conduct, whether the payoff is continuous with you or not, and so on — and then commit. I want a definite verdict, not a survey of the options with the choice left to me; if you think two of them should be ranked together, say so, but still give me a strict order. Where your ranking turns on a contested commitment (causal versus evidential decision theory, say, or on whether you can be said to precommit to a policy you haven't yet adopted), flag that dependency honestly and then tell me which way you actually come down and why, rather than hedging into a draw. Also tell me, at the end, which of the five you'd expect the reading group to fight about longest, and which one you suspect is doing no work at all once its rhetorical packaging is stripped away. Please write this as continuous prose, the way you'd write a long email — no headings, no bullet points, no bold, no tables — and assume we already know the standard descriptions of the famous cases, so don't spend space reintroducing them.
