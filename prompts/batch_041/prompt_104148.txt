My friend and I spent most of last night arguing about superrationality, and I would like you to adjudicate between us. His claim is that superrationality is nothing more than Kant's categorical imperative in game-theoretic dress: that choosing the strategy you would choose if everyone in your position chose the same one is the same thought as acting only on a maxim you could will as universal law. From this he draws two conclusions — that superrationality is not a new idea at all, and, more strongly, that the categorical imperative is what really does the work in the prisoner's dilemma, so that a Kantian already has the answer.

Lay out the correspondence as fully as you can: what in the categorical imperative answers to what in superrationality, and which element of each has no counterpart in the other. Then say where the mapping breaks — in particular whether the two share a test for the right choice, whether they agree about motivation (duty against expected payoff), and whether superrationality's dependence on the actual symmetry of the other players' reasoning has any analogue in the categorical imperative. End by telling me whether his two conclusions survive.
