I am drafting a short essay on the inefficacy objection — the claim that an individual's contribution to a large-scale collective harm (a flight, a burger, a vote) is too small to make any difference to whether the harm occurs, and therefore cannot be wrong — and I would like detailed comments on the draft below. I am not looking for citations, reading lists, or names to consult; please assess the argument on its own terms. Treat the draft as a compressed sketch of an argument rather than a finished piece.

Draft:

The inefficacy objection begins from a homely observation: one flight, one burger, one vote changes nothing. If my act makes no difference to whether a harm occurs, the objection says, then it cannot be wrong — wrongness must answer to harm, and an act that leaves the world exactly as it would otherwise have been leaves nothing for morality to object to. Call this the difference-making premise.

Two replies dominate. The first is contributory: the individual act is a member of a set of acts that together cause serious harm, and membership in such a set is enough for culpability. The second is fairness-based: even granting that my act is causally idle, I would be taking the benefit of others' restraint while exempting myself from its cost, and that is unfair.

I want to suggest that these replies, whatever their individual merits, divide the terrain between them and leave a gap at the centre. The contributory reply is at its weakest exactly where the objection is at its strongest — where contributions are marginal and the harm depends on a threshold the individual cannot move. There, saying that one is part of the set simply restates the conclusion the objector denies, since the objector's whole point is that set-membership without difference-making is not yet wrongdoing. The fairness reply fares better, but only on the free-riding reading of it: the wrong it identifies is parasitic on others actually restraining themselves. Where no one else is restraining, there is nothing to free-ride on, and the reply falls silent.

The upshot is that in the non-cooperative case — the lone agent who knows that her unilateral sacrifice changes nothing and that no one else is making one — neither reply gets a grip, and the objection stands. If it is to be answered there, it must be answered by a reason of a different kind: one that attaches to what the act expresses or endorses, rather than to what it causes or to what it takes.

Please comment on the following, in order of importance. First, is my characterization of the two replies fair, or have I weakened either of them? In particular, is there a version of the fairness reply — say, one grounded in equal shares of a limited resource rather than in free-riding — that survives the non-cooperative case and so closes the gap I claim to find? Second, does the contributory reply really collapse into a restatement of the disputed premise when contributions are marginal, or is there a non-question-begging version of it that I have overlooked? Third, is the closing suggestion — that the non-cooperative case must be met by an expressive or endorsement-based reason — a genuine third option, or does it merely dress up a familiar deontological commitment as a new kind of reason? Finally, tell me where the argument is simply wrong or unclear, and what the most promising line of repair would be.
