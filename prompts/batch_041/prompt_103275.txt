I retired from field ecology about four years ago and since then I've been doing philosophy pretty seriously — sitting in on a grad seminar, going to the ethics reading group at the local university. In two weeks I'm up to present to that group and I need to be ready for the questions.

Here's the thing I'm defending. Everyone reads Williams's drowning-wife case as an objection to what the utilitarian's deliberation *contains* — the extra impartial thought, the thought that she is his wife, that's supposed to be the offense. I think thats the weak reading. The offense is about the order and the function of the thought, not its content. What's objectionable is that the agent has already been moved to act and then runs a second, justificatory pass back over the motive that was doing the work. Read that way, the objection isn't specifically anti-utilitarian at all — it bites against any theory that tells you to act from some motive and then certify that motive from a further standpoint. A Kantian who acts from affection and then checks universalizability has exactly the same structure. So the objection is structural and much broader than people admit, and the standard utilitarian comebacks (he can hold the thought dispositionally; he really has one thought too few) miss it, because they're answering the content reading.

The room matters here. It's the departmental ethics reading group. Mostly consequentialists — two or three who think the wife example is sentimental and that the objection dies the moment you seperate having a reason from rehearsing it, and one who works on decision theory and will try to formalize whatever I say into a two-stage decision procedure and then tell me my distinction collapses. Plus a couple of Kantians, and I expect them to say my structural reading either makes the objection trivial (every theory has a reflective tier) or self-undermining (my own view needs a justificatory pass too). And one philosopher of action who'll press me on what already been moved to act is even supposed to mean, when deliberation and motivation aren't cleanly separable in the first place.
