My roommate Nadia did one of those paid decision experiments and came home irritated, and then she and our friend Owen argued about it for like an hour while I tried to keep up. I still can't tell who was right, so I'm hoping you can referee.

The experiment gave her two choices, back to back.

First: (A) $500 for sure, or (B) a ticket paying $2,000 with 10% probability, $500 with 89%, and nothing with 1%.

Second: (C) a ticket paying $500 with 11% probability and nothing otherwise, or (D) a ticket paying $2,000 with 10% probability and nothing otherwise.

She took A and she took D.

Owen says that's just inconsistent. He says look at each pair: in the first, both tickets carry the same 89% shot at $500, and in the second, both carry the same 89% shot at nothing, and a chunk that's identical between the two options can't be the thing that makes you pick one of them. So strip it out and the first choice is really 11% of $500 against a 10-in-11 shot at $2,000 (scaled down by that same 11%); the second choice is 11% of $500 against 10% of $2,000 -- the very same comparison. He says you can't go one way in the first and the other way in the second, that this is the independance axiom, and that it's not optional or a matter of taste, it's just what consistency about risk means. He said it's the Allais setup and the whole point of the experiment is to catch people doing exactly this.

Nadia says the shared chunk isn't shared once you're actually choosing, because its being there is what turns A into a sure thing and C into a long shot, and a guarantee does something to her that an 89% chance at the same money simply doesn't. She says Owen is assuming a deletion rule -- that you can always cut a branch that gives you the same thing either way and compare what's left -- and that this rule is the exact thing under dispute, so he can't use it as his proof. Owen said it's not a rule, it's just what a lottery is, a probability distribution over final outcomes, and A and C and B and D are built out of the same distributions, so they just are the same lotteries. Nadia said fine, then she denies that too: she says how the lottery gets generated is part of what she's choosing between and not an irrelevant label, and if that means she's not an expected-utility maximizer then nobody ever asked her to be one. Owen tried a version with no sure thing anywhere in it, and she agreed she'd still show the same pattern, and he said see, so it's not about certainty -- she said, right, it's not only about certainty.

Then Owen said ok, but then you're churnable. He sketched a run of trades: he buys the sure $500 off her for a bit under $500, sells her back a package shaped like the old ticket, and keeps looping, and says anyone whose preferences can be cycled like that for free money is irrational by every standard there is, which is why independence isn't negotiable. Nadia said the churn only works if she takes the trades one at a time and never sees the sequence coming, and if her preferences sit still while she's standing in a different spot; she said she'd just refuse the whole run, and that refusing a bundle of trades you'd accept one by one is what any sane person does when they can see where it's headed. Owen said refusing trades you yourself would accept is just declaring you're irrational on purpose.

They each wanted me to pick a side and I honestly couldn't. Can you go through it move by move -- who's right about which point, where each of them is overclaiming, and whether the churning argument actually settles it or quietly leans on something Nadia is entitled to deny? And then at the end, who's more right overall.
