I'm currently taking a graduate seminar on contractualist ethics, and I'm trying to settle on a term-paper topic on the aggregation problem. Before I commit to a line of argument, I want to see how a set of possible contractualist responses compare with one another, so I'd like you to rate and then rank them rather than simply survey the literature.

Here are five strategies I've reconstructed. Treat them as the objects of evaluation; I'm describing them in my own terms so that the exercise doesn't turn on who said what.

(1) Strict anti-aggregation. The reasonable-rejection test is applied person by person, and numbers never enter: a principle that imposes a burden on even one person is rejectable by that person regardless of how many benefit.

(2) Threshold view. Numbers may be aggregated only when the burden imposed on the dissenting individual falls below a severity threshold; above that threshold, numbers are irrelevant.

(3) Tie-breaker view. Numbers matter only when the competing individual claims are roughly equal in strength; a greater number breaks a tie but can never override a stronger single claim.

(4) Ex ante view. Reasonable rejection is assessed from behind a veil, by each person's ex ante probability of bearing a burden, so many small risks can be aggregated even when the concentrated burden that results could not be.

(5) Weighted-claims view. Aggregation is permitted, but each individual's claim is weighted by its severity, so many small claims outweigh one large claim only once their weighted total exceeds it.

For each of the five, give me two numbers between 0 and 1, to one decimal place: (a) how much interpersonal aggregation the strategy actually licenses, where 0 is none and 1 is the full aggregation a utilitarian would accept; and (b) how faithful it is to the contractualist core idea that a principle is wrong if it could be reasonably rejected by someone. Add two or three sentences per strategy defending both numbers and flagging any tension between them.

Then rank the five from best to worst at handling the aggregation problem as a whole, with a one-sentence justification for each placement.

Finally, test the ranking against a case I'll stipulate: we must choose between sparing one person from permanent hearing loss and, at no additional cost, sparing each of N people a week of moderate pain. For each strategy, state the smallest N at which it switches to the many-small-pains option, or explain why it never switches, or why the strategy makes the question ill-posed. Close by telling me which strategy you would put your own weight on, and what you take to be the single strongest objection to it.
