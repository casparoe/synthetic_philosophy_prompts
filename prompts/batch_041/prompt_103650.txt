I write about science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I have been trying to get straight on the surprise exam paradox by working through my own verdicts on a range of cases rather than by rehearsing the standard solution. Here is what I think.

1. In the standard five-day case, a teacher announces on Friday that there will be a surprise exam next week, meaning the students cannot know on the morning of the exam that it will occur that day. The students reason backward, eliminate Friday through Monday, conclude that no exam can occur, and are then surprised when the teacher gives it on Wednesday. My verdict: the teacher can be right. The backward induction is flawed, and an exam can be a surprise even after the announcement.

2. In a one-day case, the teacher announces on Monday morning that there will be a surprise exam later that day and that the students cannot know now that it will be today. My verdict: if the students trust the teacher and understand the announcement, they know it will be today, so the announcement is self-defeating.

3. In a two-day case, the teacher announces on Monday that the exam will be on Monday or Tuesday and that the students will not know on the morning of the exam that it is that day. My verdict: the teacher can still give it on Monday and surprise them. If Monday passes with no exam, Tuesday cannot be a surprise. The announcement is consistent, but it cannot guarantee surprise on both days.

4. In a randomizer case, the teacher chooses the day by a random process and announces that the exam will be on a day the students cannot predict. My verdict: surprise is possible even if the students know the process, because the outcome is not deducible from the announcement.

5. In a sealed-envelope case, the teacher writes the day on a card, seals it, and announces that the exam will be on the day written in the envelope and that the students will not know which day until the envelope is opened. My verdict: surprise is possible if the students do not know the card's content. If the teacher says the card reads Friday, there is no surprise.

6. In a self-referential case, the teacher announces that there will be an exam on a day that is not deducible from this announcement. My verdict: I think this is self-defeating if the students are ideal reasoners and trust the teacher, though I cannot state the exact reason. This is the case that makes me doubt my tentative principle.

7. In a surprise-party case, the guest of honor is told that a surprise party will be thrown sometime this month and that the guest will not know the day. My verdict: the guest is still genuinely surprised on the day. The announcement does not defeat the surprise.

8. In an announced-inspection case, a restaurant manager is told that an inspection will occur on a day the manager does not expect. My verdict: surprise is possible if the window is long, but not if the window is one day.

9. In an unreliable-teacher case, the teacher announces a surprise exam, but the students know the teacher often lies. My verdict: surprise is possible, but for a different reason: the announcement is not trusted. I want the principle to explain why this differs from case 1.

10. In a known-day case, the teacher announces that the exam will be a surprise and that it will be on Friday. My verdict: there is no surprise, because the day is known.

11. In a known-day-unknown-hour case, the teacher announces that the exam will be on Friday but that the students will not know the hour. My verdict: surprise is possible at the level of the hour. Surprise can survive knowledge of the day if the relevant unknown is the time.

My tentative principle is that an announced surprise is self-defeating only when the announcement, together with the audience's background knowledge and trust, entails the exact occurrence that is supposed to be unknown; merely ruling out some times is not enough. But case 6 seems to be a counterexample, and case 9 suggests the role of trust needs separate treatment. I suspect the principle has to distinguish between the announcement's being common knowledge and its content's being common knowledge, and between knowing the day and knowing that one knows the day, but I do not know whether that distinction does any real work.

What principle, if any, captures these verdicts? If there is no single principle, give me the smallest set of candidates, state each precisely, and say which case motivates each clause. Tell me what each candidate predicts for cases I have not listed: an announcement that the exam will be a surprise and that the students will not be able to predict it even after hearing this sentence; a case in which the announcement is public but the students are not ideal reasoners; a case in which the exam is chosen by a randomizer the students can inspect but not predict; and a case in which the teacher announces a surprise exam but also announces that the students will be told the day one hour before the exam. Finally, tell me which of my verdicts the principle cannot accommodate, and whether I should give up that verdict. I am not asking for a bibliography, reading list, or citations; I want the principle and its predictions.
