I chair the heritage commitee in a small mill town. Every March we hold a service for the twenty-three people who died in the fire at the mill — this year's is the last one. The council voted to end it and put the money into a settlement fund for new arrivals instead; I voted for that, my mother hasn't really spoken to me since, and I'm the one who has to give the speech.

The room's split. The front three rows are the families — mostly old now, some of them haven't missed a year in fifty. Behind them, half the town moved here in the last twenty years and never knew anyone who worked at the mill. Some of them are from places with their own unmarked dead, and at last year's service one of them asked me, politely, why we have a service for our twenty-three and nothing at all for the people who didn't make it here. I didn't have an answer.

I've read Margalit and Rieff and I dont want to name either of them from the podium. What I want the speech to actually do is hold both of the things they're each right about: that the duty to remember runs along the ties you already have, so it's owed by a town to its own and can't be demanded of strangers; and that remembering is mostly a project of the living, that it keeps an old injury open, and that a community can be right to let a thing go quiet. I don't want it to pretend the second worry is answered by sentiment or the first by nostalgia. Also, the owner's family has paid for a plaque and a little website, and some of the families think that's being bought off — say something honest about what a plaque is and isn't.

No poetry quotes, no famous lines, nothing I'd have to go look up; I don't want to be the one reading someone else's words. And don't tell the room the dead would have wanted this — I have no idea what they'd have wanted and neither does anyone else in the hall.

Start with one sentence that's the whole bottom line, then the speech. 450 words max.
