I teach a seminar on freedom and happiness and want to build a session around the Grand Inquisitor episode of The Brothers Karamazov — Ivan's prose poem, the silent prisoner, the old Inquisitor's long accusation, and the kiss that ends it. Please read the episode through the philosophical question of whether freedom and happiness are genuinely in tension, and give me a reading that is faithful in both directions: it should not flatten the poem into an illustration of a thesis, and it should not soften the argument to make the poem look better than it is.

I want the reading organized around a distinction I have been trying to sharpen and that I think the usual commentary runs together. The Inquisitor may be offering his flock two quite different goods. The first is relief from the burden of choosing — release from the exhaustion of deciding for oneself. The second is a happiness composed of desires that he himself supplies and then satisfies. These come apart: one could be spared the burden of choice while still being handed desires one never chose, and one could keep the burden of choice while having every want manufactured by someone else. The poem's rhetoric is all about the agony of free choice, but the institution it describes — the Church taking up the three things Christ refused in the desert — looks more like an apparatus for managing what people want. So which of the two is the Inquisitor really defending? Does the poem register the difference, or does it trade on a slide between them? Does the prisoner's kiss answer either charge, or does it change the subject? Say also whether it matters that the poem is Ivan's, recited to Alyosha, with the Inquisitor as his own invention.

Close by saying what the episode adds to this debate that a straightforward argument about freedom and happiness could not. Please write in American English.
