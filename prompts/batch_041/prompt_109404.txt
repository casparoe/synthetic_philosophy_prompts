I am a software engineer with no formal philosophy background, and a colleague in the philosophy department has asked me to contribute one essay question to a final-year undergraduate metaphysics examination on the identity of indiscernibles and Black's spheres. I should like a question that cannot be answered by reciting the standard two-spheres setup and the familiar replies concerning primitive thisness, spatial relations, or the numerical–qualitative identity distinction. To make the question harder to answer from memory, I should like it to turn on an analogy from software engineering: two objects in an object-oriented software system that are indistinguishable through every public method available to client code, yet are distinct references, so that a test for reference equality returns false. The question should force students to ask whether the reference itself is a property of the object or a fact about the wider system, whether the analogy vindicates or undermines the identity of indiscernibles, and whether the possibility of such objects bears on whether Black's spheres are genuinely possible in a world with no external reference frame. It should also require them to distinguish the principle that indiscernibles are identical from the principle that identicals are indiscernible, and to say which of those the software case actually illustrates.

Please write:

1. the examination question itself, pitched at final-year undergraduate level and answerable in about 45 minutes;
2. a model answer of roughly 700 words, written in continuous prose, that a strong student could produce under examination conditions;
3. a marking scheme with five must-hit points, each worth four marks (total 20), where each point names a specific move a first-class answer must make, a specific mistake that should cost marks, and a one-line note on how candidates typically get it wrong; taken together, the scheme should separate a first-class answer from an upper-second-class answer.

The model answer and the marking scheme should be in British English. Please do not include citations, bibliographies, or reading lists. Avoid quoting any actual texts; paraphrase any philosophical positions in your own words. The question should be self-contained, so that a student who knows the standard debate but has not seen this software analogy before can still answer it.
