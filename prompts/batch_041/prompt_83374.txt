I'm a novelist, not an academic, so forgive me if this is a dumb question — I'm about 200 pages into a book set around a siege and I've got myself stuck on something that may or may not be a real category.

Here's the shape I keep bumping into. There's a stand — a garrison, a last hill, a beach — that everyone there knew was hopeless. It wasn't going to turn the war. If it bought time, nobody can say what for. Some of the people who ordered it probably made a bad call. And yet a century later it's the thing the country is built on. It's in the school books, poets write about it, and it does far more work for the national story then any of the actual victories did. The Alamo. Masada. Kosovo. Dunkirk, though that one's odd because it came out alright in the end. And then the Confederacy, where the losing side ran a whole memory-industry for a hundred years and never really stopped.

What I can't tell is whether anyone has carved out the specific thing I'm circling, as opposed to the general topic of national myth. I think there might be something narrower, because three conditions seem to have to hold at once:

- everyone present had to know the outcome was settled, and know it going in
- the stand had to be militarily pointless — not a sacrifice that actually bought something
- nobody was doing it for the memory. They were doing it for pay, or for the person next to them, or out of sheer cussedness.

And then the strange part: the more completely the cause is dead, the brighter the memory burns. A defeat that gets avenged later is just a bad afternoon. It's only when there's no comeback at all that the thing turns sacred. Which makes me wonder if it can't be manufactured on purpose at all — if some staff officer had stood up and said, we'll die here so that the nation remembers us, it would stop being the thing. I have a hunch that's a real constraint and not just my own sentimentality, but I don't know.

I know "lost cause" is a term, but that seems to be about the cause — a grievance that won't lie down, a claim that the right side really won — which feels like a different animal from what I'm describing, where the thing is completely finished and still glowing. And I know "martyr" and "tragedy," but those are about a person or a story shape, not about a whole nation adopting a defeat as its best moment.

So: is there a name? And if the nearest names are all too broad, can you give me the carving instead — what exactly the phenomenon is, what it isn't, and where the edges are. For instance: does it split in two, the redeemed defeats like Dunkirk versus the ones with no redemption at all like Masada? Does it need a nation, or does it work for a regiment, a city, a sect? Does it survive if the participants were cynics? And is my hunch right that it can't be willed into being, or are there clean counterexamples — cases where somebody did manufacture it deliberately and it took?
