"Al-Ghazālī's occasionalism is his answer to a puzzle about the natural sciences. He held that fire does not burn cotton, and that striking a match does not produce a flame: the apparent connection between a cause and its effect is an illusion, and in reality God creates every event directly, moment by moment. What we call the laws of nature are simply the habits God has chosen to follow, so the order of the world is a matter of divine custom rather than of any necessity in things. Because he denied that causes bring about their effects, he also denied that we can ever genuinely know what will happen next; our expectation that the sun will rise tomorrow is, strictly speaking, groundless. His motive was to safeguard the possibility of miracles: if there is no necessary connection between causes and effects, then a miracle is not a special violation of nature but simply another instance of God's direct action, no more miraculous than the burning of cotton. In all this he anticipates Hume, who likewise held that we never observe any necessary connection between cause and effect."
