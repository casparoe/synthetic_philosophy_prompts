I'm building a bank of reading-comprehension prompts for a philosophy seminar, and I need you to be the one who answers this one. Take the role of someone who genuinely knows the problem of universals — the medieval realism/nominalism/conceptualism fight, and the modern descendants (resemblance nominalism, class nominalism, trope theory, the whole mess) — and who also reads a lot of fiction and isn't scared of a literary text. Think carefully before you answer. Actually, start by brainstorming: sketch out the scenes and details from the story you'd want to lean on, the positions in the universals debate you might try to map them onto, and the places where you can already feel the mapping about to break. Then write the reading proper.

The text is Borges's "Tlön, Uqbar, Orbis Tertius." You know it — the narrator and Bioy stumble onto an encyclopedia entry for a country that seems not to exist, and eventually onto a whole invented world, Tlön, that starts leaking into ours. Tlön's people are supposed to be extreme idealists, denying the reality of objects; the story tells us one of their language families has no nouns at all, built instead out of impersonal verbs, and another treats the monosyllabic adjective as basic, so that a noun is just a bundle of qualities. And then there's the hrönir business, the duplicated objects the story says get produced by expectation.

Read the story through the problem of universals. I want the reading to be honest in both directions, so:

- Say what position the story actually seems to put in Tlön's mouth, and whether that position is coherent. Here's the thing I keep bumping into and want you to take seriously: everyone reads the story as "Berkeley," but Berkeley's idealism is a claim about the reality of mind-independent objects, not about universals, and I suspect the story quietly trades on the confusion. Is Tlön a nominalist world, a conceptualist one, or just an idealist one wearing nominalism's coat? Don't let it slide.
- The no-nouns language is the crux for me. Is "there are no nouns" a serious thesis about there being no general properties, or is it a fact about grammar being mistaken for a fact about metaphysics? Which side of the universals dispute does the adjective-language actually land on — resemblance nominalism, trope theory, something else?
- The hrönir: two objects, indiscernible, one supposedly less real than the other. What does that do to the identity of indiscernibles, to multiple instantiation, to whether you need a haecceity or bare particular to keep them two? Is this a thought experiment that helps, or just atmosphere?
- Where does the reading strain? If the story isn't really doing philosophy of universals and is just using it as mood, say so, and say which bits you'd refuse to force.
- End by telling me what the story adds to the debate that a paper on universals can't.

No bibliography, no reading list — just argue. Maybe 800 words, and don't hedge your way out of taking a line.
