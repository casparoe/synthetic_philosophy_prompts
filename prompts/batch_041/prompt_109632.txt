I convene a small working group on the ethics of the very long term, and for a year two factions within it have been talking past each other. Call them the Fanatics and the Anti-Fanatics. The Fanatics hold that the case for astronomical expected value is sound: that the expected value of the far future dwarfs anything we can do for the present, that a sufficiently small probability of a sufficiently great good can legitimately dominate a near-certain modest good, and that the refusal to act on such comparisons is a failure of nerve rather than an objection. The Anti-Fanatics hold that this is a reductio: any decision rule under which a probability of one in a googol of a vast payoff outweighs a near-certain benefit to people now living has shown itself unfit for use, whatever its formal elegance.

Rather than two more polemics, we have agreed to produce one document that both sides can put their names to, and I want you to draft it.

Three stipulations are fixed, and neither side may wave them away by appeal to authority. First, the whole dispute is to be conducted with finite values and probabilities; no infinities, no Pascal's wager. Second, we keep separate two questions that are usually run together: the axiological question — is the value at stake in the far future bounded, i.e. does some finite present-day good have a value comparable to it? — and the decision-theoretic question — given whatever axiology we accept, does expected-value maximization with arbitrarily small probabilities yield the right verdict about what to do? Third, both sides accept the following structural observation as a constraint on the space of positions: a value function concave or bounded enough to defuse the fanatical comparisons is also flat enough to license something like the repugnant conclusion once enough barely-worth-living lives are in view, while the lexical or convex moves that block that conclusion are precisely the ones that restore the fanatical comparisons. Nobody gets both a bounded value function and a clean escape from repugnance.

Two taming devices are also on the table, and the statement must reach a verdict on each: (a) saturating or capping probability-weighted value at some finite bound, so that differences beyond the bound cannot change a ranking; and (b) transforming probabilities so that very small ones count for disproportionately little, while leaving the value function unbounded.

Write the joint statement. It must contain, first, a numbered list of agreed claims: what both sides accept about the structure of the problem, about what a successful argument on either side would have to establish, and about the three stipulations. Second, a table of the remaining disagreements, one row per disagreement, with columns for the sub-question at issue, the Fanatics' own statement of their position, the Anti-Fanatics' own statement of theirs — each written in wording its side would sign — the crux, meaning the sub-question such that settling it would move at least one side, and a case or argument both sides agree bears on that crux, together with the prediction each side records in advance about how it comes out. Where a row is really a verbal dispute — say, about whether fanaticism names a defect of the theory or a fact about the world — identify it as such and give the disambiguation that would dissolve it. The agreed section must not paper over the disagreement, and the neutral column must not smuggle in either side's view.

No more than 1,000 words. Lead with the agreed claims; the table follows.
