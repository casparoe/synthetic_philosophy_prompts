So I've been in a running argument with an old roommate for like three weeks now about Dutch books, and we cannot settle it. His line is: if your credences violate the probability axioms then someone can construct a set of bets that takes money off you no matter what happens, and that's supposed to prove you're being irrational, full stop. My line is that "someone can take money off you" is doing way more work than anyone admits — it quietly assumes you'll accept every bet at your stated odds, in both directions, that you'll keep accepting them as the situation develops, and that a dollar won is worth exactly as much as a dollar lost, which is obviously not how anyone actually feels about money. He thinks I'm just rationalizing. Now it's fallen to me to run next week's session of the philosophy of probability reading group I'm in — 15 people, a mix of third-years and a few grad students, one 75-minute block, one room, and the building has a no-real-money rule so it has to be tokens or candy or points or something. I want to design a game where they run into this themselves rather than listen to me lecture about it, and I'd love your help building it.

Here's what I care about, and it's maybe not the version you'd write by default. Everyone already knows the toy case where your odds on a partition sum to 1.05 and the bookie sells you every side. That's too clean and they'll see it coming. What I want them to hit is the *subadditivity* pattern: the person who says they'd rather put money on one specific outcome than on a vague "either A or B" covering it, because that feels more prudent, more careful, more like they know what they're betting on — and who then discovers that being "careful" that way is exactly what gets them squeezed. If you can also work in the version where someone revises their odds after learning something, in a way that isn't just conditionalizing on what they learned, and gets squeezed across the two moments rather than within one, even better, because that's the part of the argument I've never really understood and I'd like the game to drag it into the open. The design problem I keep tripping on: in a classroom everyone can just refuse to bet, which defuses the whole thing, so I think there has to be some rule that forces them into the market — they post odds and then must act as the counterparty up to some cap, in either direction, no declining. Similarly I don't want the "well money is nonlinear so losing isn't really losing" escape to be free, so the payoff scheme should make the loss hurt in a way that's legible to them, without real cash. I don't want any of this named for them up front; the rules should do the work and the debrief should be where it gets spelled out.

So: rules, materials, a timeline for the 75 minutes, a payoff scheme that doesn't need real money, the debrief that maps what just happened onto the pieces of the argument (including where the axioms actually bite and where they don't), and a straight account of what will predictably go sideways the first time I run it and what I should do when it does. Two things I specifically need you to be honest about. First, which feature of the Dutch book argument the activity simply can't reproduce — I suspect it's that real books depend on the bookie choosing which side to take and on the agent not being able to back out at the midpoint, and that the diachronic case needs the bookie to hand over something early or make a side bet so you can't wriggle free, and I can't reconstruct why that makes the diachronic version weaker than the synchronic one; tell me if I've got that right or garbled it, and what I'll have to just assert in the debrief because the game can't show it.
