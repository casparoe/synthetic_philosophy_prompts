I have been an amateur birder for about a decade, and this spring I started TAing an upper-level philosophy of biology course. The two have collided in an unexpected way: my birding group keeps arguing about whether the 'species' we tick on our lists are real kinds in nature or just convenient labels, and the course has made me realize how many different things biologists and philosophers mean by 'species.' I want to make a handout that will help the students—and my birding friends—see that the disagreement is not one debate but a branching space of commitments, and that most people who call themselves 'species realists' or 'species nominalists' actually disagree about which cases matter. Please write me a decision tree, built from the logical space rather than from a list of names, that takes someone from a minimal starting point (they accept that there are organisms, populations, and lineages, and that biologists use the word 'species') to a named position. The internal nodes should each be a question about a concrete case or claim, phrased so that someone can answer it without already knowing which position it leads to: whether a ring species with a gradual cline should count as one species or several; whether asexual lineages that never interbreed but are ecologically and genetically coherent should be species; whether hybrid zones that produce fertile offspring undermine a species boundary; whether horizontal gene transfer across lineages makes a tree-like species concept untenable; whether fossil hominins and extinct taxa should be classified by the same criteria as living organisms; whether cryptic species that look identical but do not interbreed should be split; and whether domesticated breeds are species, subspecies, or artifacts of human selection. I would also like the tree to include a couple of positions that are not standard textbook labels—for instance, a view I have been calling 'species as a practice' (the claim that 'species' is not a hypothesis about natural kinds but a tool for coordinating research and conservation, which may be neither true nor false) and a view I call 'species as a verb' (the claim that species are not things but ongoing processes of boundary-making and stabilization). Locate the standard positions in the same space, glossing each in a phrase so the handout does not depend on the reader's prior reading: nominalism or eliminativism (species terms are useful fictions or should be abandoned), realism about species as classes (species are real kinds defined by shared properties), the species-as-individuals view (a species is a concrete lineage with a beginning and end, not a class of organisms satisfying a definition), the homeostatic property cluster view (species are real but defined by a cluster of co-occurring properties rather than a single essence), monism (there is one correct species concept), and pluralism (different species concepts answer different legitimate questions). Each leaf should carry the name of the position (or 'unnamed' if nobody defends it) and one sentence on its most awkward commitment. Please note which combinations of answers the tree cannot separate and flag them as such, and briefly diagnose any unoccupied branches—whether nobody is there because the view is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. Aim for eight to twelve internal nodes and no more than twelve leaves; the audience is advanced undergraduates who know basic evolutionary theory but have not read the species literature.
