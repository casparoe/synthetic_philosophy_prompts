Ok I built this for a reading group on the unity of the proposition and now I'm pretty sure it proves way too much, but I can't find the bad step. The setup: take any proposition, say that the cat is on the mat. It has parts -- the cat, the mat, the relation of being on. If the proposition were just those parts sitting together, it'd be a heap, not something that says anything, so it couldn't be true or false. So it has to have something extra, a unity, that actually ties the parts into one content. Call it U. Now U has to be tied to the parts too, or it's just one more item lying next to them and we're back to a heap. So there has to be a further unity U' that ties U to the parts. Then a U'' for U', and so on forever. So no proposition ever gets unified, so there are no propositions, so no thoughts, no truth-bearers, no true sentences, which is absurd. Laid out: (1) every complex has parts; (2) a mere heap of parts isn't a unified content; (3) so unity requires an extra constituent beyond the parts; (4) any constituent of a complex must itself be unified with the others; (5) that needs a further constituent, and so on without end; (6) so nothing complex is ever unified; (7) so there are no propositions -- and the same argument runs verbatim with 'proposition' swapped for 'cat' or 'mat', so no complex wholes at all. I'd like to keep (2), and I obviously want to keep there being true thoughts, and honestly I'd like to keep (1). A friend says you dodge it by saying the unity just is the relation's being instantiated rather than a separate item, but that feels like renaming the problem to me. So: which step do I reject, and what does rejecting it cost? If the answer is "relating is primitive, unity isn't a constituent," tell me what breaks when I then try to explain how a proposition gets its truth conditions from the meanings of its parts.
