I am a philosophy professor preparing a ninety-minute session for a graduate seminar in rational choice and decision theory. My students are comfortable with expected utility, conditional probability, and the idea of a strategy profile, but most have seen game theory only through the prisoner's dilemma and matching pennies. The session is on iterated elimination of dominated strategies when dominance is defined over mixed strategies. I want a teaching plan, not an exposition of the concept itself.

Plan the session. I need the order in which the components must be introduced and why -- what depends on what, and what can be deferred until the students have a working grip on the earlier piece. I want the single game that should carry the whole session, and I am inclined to use the following 3x3 game:

Player 1's payoffs (rows: T, M, B; columns: L, C, R):

        L   C   R
T       3   0   2
M       1   1   1
B       0   3   2

Player 2's payoffs (rows: T, M, B; columns: L, C, R):

        L   C   R
T       0   3   0
M       1   1   1
B       3   0   0

Tell me whether this game should carry the session. What must a carrying example for this topic contain, and what must it lack? Why not use the prisoner's dilemma or matching pennies, or if one of those should appear as a preliminary, where exactly does it belong and what does it leave undone? I expect the students to arrive with several misconceptions: that a strategy can be dominated by a mixed strategy only if it is already dominated by some pure strategy; that mixed-strategy dominance is just dominance against a uniformly randomizing opponent; that the order of elimination never matters; that any strategy surviving iterated elimination is a Nash-equilibrium strategy; and that rationality by itself, without common knowledge of rationality, is enough to license the eliminations. For each, tell me where in the sequence I should head it off, what I should say, and what the students will otherwise conclude if I do not. Distinguish those that are outright false from those that are true in this setting but false in nearby ones, and say how the sequence should mark the difference.

I also want a closing question that would show whether they have understood the distinction between pure and mixed dominance and the role of iterated elimination, rather than merely being able to compute the example. And I want an explicit list of what I should refuse to cover at this level, with a sentence for each on why the omission is deliberate rather than an oversight.

Structure your response under the following headings, in this order, with approximately the indicated lengths:

1. The Opening Diagnosis (200-250 words): what the students must already have in hand, and the first thing I should say.
2. The Dependency Chain (400-500 words): the sequence of ideas, what each depends on, and where the example enters.
