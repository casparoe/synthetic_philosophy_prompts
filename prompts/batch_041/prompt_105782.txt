I'm a science and ideas journalist — ten years covering tech, a bit of philosophy, no formal training in either — and next month I'm on a panel at a small AI ethics workshop where I want to push a thesis I've been chewing on: that the alignment debate has the wrong question at the center of it. Everyone asks whose values the system should optimize. I think we should ask which decisions it should refuse to make at all. The concrete proposal I'm calling the jurisdiction rule: the system is trained to optimize freely inside domains that a randomly selected citizens panel — sortition, resampled every few years, maybe 500 people — has marked as settled, and it hands the decision back to a human whenever it's working in a domain that panel has marked as contested. So neutrality gets bought with abstention rather than aggregation. The room is about forty people: a big bloc of AI safety and EA/longtermist folks, a couple of whom actually work on RLHF; three or four political philosophers who teach Rawls and Berlin and will smell a substantive conception of the good from a mile away; two social choice economists who know Arrow's theorem cold; and a few fellow journalists who just want the soundbite. I'm definately not good at thinking on my feet in rooms like that. So prep me: the eight or ten objections I'll actually get, grouped by who's giving them and phrased the way that person would phrase it, ordered by how much damage they'd do to the proposal, each with a two-minute reply in plain language and a note on where the reply is weak.
