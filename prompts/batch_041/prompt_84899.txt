I keep coming back to the Jahi McMath case and I'd like to actually think it through instead of just having the reaction I always have. Recap in case it helps: 13-year-old in Oakland, 2013, complicated tonsil/airway surgery, massive bleeding, cardiac arrest, and on Dec 12 her doctors at Children's Hospital Oakland declared her brain dead. Family said no. It went to court, a court-appointed neurologist confirmed the diagnosis, a judge ruled she was legally dead but ordered the ventilator kept on for a bit; the coroner issued a death certificate with Dec 12, 2013 as the date of death. Then in early January 2014 the family got custody of the body, moved her to New Jersey, which has a statute letting you object on religious grounds to a declaration of death by neurological criteria, and there she stayed on a ventilator with a trach and a feeding tube until 2018, when she died of liver failure and there was a second death certificate. The family's malpractice suit was apparently going to argue she wasn't dead but profoundly disabled, which matters because California caps damages if the patient died and doesn't if they're alive.

What I want is this case run through the standard anatomy of the definition-of-death debate: the split between the definition of death, the criterion you use to apply it, and the tests you run to check the criterion, plus specifically the whole-brain criterion and the integrative-unity justification for it (the Bernat line of argument, if I've got the name right) -- death as the loss of the organism's capacity to integrate itself into a single functioning whole, so that a ventilator isn't keeping a person alive but running a collection of parts. I know the shape of that argument. What I don't have is a disciplined mapping onto this episode.

So: at which of those three levels is each party actually disagreeing? The family, their outside expert, the hospital, the judge, the New Jersey legislature. Is the family's objection a religious claim about what death is, a claim about the criterion, a claim about the tests, or not really a claim about death at all but about who decides and whether they have to keep treating? Take the whole-brain criterion seriously and tell me whether the case confirms it, strains it, or is orthogonal to it. The New Jersey move is what I find hardest: if the same body is dead in one state and not dead in another depending on what the family believes, does that show the criterion was never a biological fact but a policy choice dressed up as one -- or is it just the law declining to impose a legal status on objectors, like a religious exemption from an autopsy, which leaves the biology untouched? And what do I do with a legal date of death, Dec 12, 2013, on which her heart was still beating and would keep beating for four and a half more years?

Then the reverse direction, case testing theory. What does this episode show about the criterion: the damages asymmetry, the organ-donation worry, the fact that a body on a ventilator for years is arguably exactly what the integrative-unity view predicts rather than a counterexample to it, and the fact that the family's own position required continuous intensive care that nobody thinks is owed to a corpse. Where is the criterion genuinely silent, and where does it start sounding like it's smuggling in a moral judgment about what kind of life is worth the name? Dont give me the standard both-sides summary, I've read that. I want the mapping, and I want you to say plainly where the theory runs out and stops being able to adjudicate.
