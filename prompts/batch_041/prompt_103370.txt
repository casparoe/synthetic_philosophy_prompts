I am a second-year graduate student auditing a seminar on rational choice, and a friend in the seminar has been needling me with coin-flipping games whose expected values are either infinite or not well-defined at all. I want to work out what principle, if any, my own gut verdicts on these games are actually tracking. Every game is played the same way: a fair coin is flipped until it lands heads, and the payoff depends only on the toss n on which it first lands heads. In the first game, call it Petersburg, the payoff on toss n is $2^n, so the probability-weighted payoffs are all exactly 1 and the book expected value is infinite. In the second, Pasadena, the payoff on toss n is +2^n/n when n is odd and −2^n/n when n is even — so +$2, then −$2, then +$8/3, then −$4, then +$32/5, and so on. If you sum the probability-weighted payoffs in the natural order of n, you get ln 2, about 70 cents; but the series is only conditionally convergent, so it can be rearranged to sum to any real number whatsoever, and the expected value is therefore not well-defined. In the third, Shifted Pasadena, every payoff is the Pasadena payoff plus an extra $1/n on toss n, so it is strictly better than Pasadena in every single outcome, and its natural-order sum is 2 ln 2, about $1.39. The fourth game is simply Pasadena with every payoff negated.

Here are my verdicts, going on gut. Petersburg: I would pay at most about $15 to play, and no more, even knowing the book value is infinite — a one-in-a-trillion chance at a trillion dollars simply does not move me the way the raw number suggests. Pasadena: I would pay roughly 70 cents, and that feels about right; the game strikes me as mildly favorable. Shifted Pasadena: I would pay more than I would for Pasadena, maybe $1.40, precisely because it is better in every outcome. Negated Pasadena: I would need to be paid about 70 cents to play, and not much more. But I also believe quite firmly that money has unbounded value to me: no matter how rich I already am, an extra million dollars is always worth more to me than an extra thousand. My tentative principle was this — sum the probability-weighted payoffs in the natural order of n, smallest first, and then cap what I would pay at the point where further dollars stop mattering much. Please tell me whether that principle survives the cases, and if it does not, what principle, or small set of candidate principles, does; for each clause of whatever you propose, say which of my verdicts it is there to capture. I would also like it applied to two games I have not yet made up my mind about: positive Pasadena, whose payoff on toss n is +2^n/n with no sign alternation (probability-weighted payoffs +1/n, so the sum diverges to infinity like the harmonic series), and Petersburg-squared, whose payoff is 2^n/n^2 (probability-weighted payoffs +1/n^2, summing to π²/6, about $1.65).

Finally, be explicit about which of my verdicts the principle you favor cannot accommodate, and where you are reporting a genuine open controversy among people who work on this rather than a settled matter, say so and do not present one side as the consensus.
