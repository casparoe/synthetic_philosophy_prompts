I chair the board of a small housing co-op — 55 households, we own our two buildings outright, and everything from the budget to the bylaws gets decided by majority vote at general meetings. Next week there's a special meeting on whether we buy the derelict block behind us, the one our courtyard and our boiler are shared with. The landlord has told the tenants there that if we dont buy it he's selling to a developer and they're out. What I can't get past is this: the vote is ours, only members vote on purchases, and the people whose homes the vote decides aren't members and won't be unless we vote yes. I have to give the opening remarks before the vote and I want them honest about that instead of smoothing it over — a vote about who belongs to us already assumes a us, and ours was drawn by a developer's lot lines and by whoever happened to have a deposit in 1987. The room will be mixed: founding members who know the bylaws by heart, newer members who mostly care about the mortgage, and a dozen of the tenants themselves, who we've invited to watch. My sister did an MA in political theory and says this is the boundary problem and that I won't solve it in opening remarks, which is probably right, but I'd like to do better than pretending the vote settles the legitimacy question. One of our members has also proposed associate membership for the tenants — a vote on the things that affect them, no vote on the purchase itself — so say what's actually wrong, or not wrong, with that. And note our bylaws let two thirds of members change the membership rules, so whoever's inside next year can redo who's inside the year after.

Give me exactly five numbered parts, each a sentence or two of actual spoken text, no intro and no outro. If I'm confusing myself here — if a co-op with a legal charter and a mortgage just settles who's in, and I'm dressing a property dispute up as a paradox — say that straight instead of writing the speech.
