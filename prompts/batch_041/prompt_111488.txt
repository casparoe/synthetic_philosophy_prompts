"Gödel's second incompleteness theorem says that no consistent, sufficiently strong, recursively axiomatizable theory proves its own consistency. (a) Does that mean we have no good reason to believe that PA — or ZFC — is consistent? Explain. (b) Suppose that tomorrow someone announces a proof that ZFC is inconsistent. What exactly would that establish, and what, if anything, would change in ordinary mathematical practice? (c) Does it matter how long the shortest proof of a contradiction would be? Say why or why not."

and here's what my classmate handed in:

"(a) No, though the reason is more delicate than it first looks. What the theorem rules out is a consistency proof carried out inside the theory itself: PA cannot prove Con(PA), and the same holds for ZFC. It does not say PA is inconsistent, and it doesn't rule out other sorts of reasons. We can prove Con(PA) in ZFC, and we have strong inductive evidence besides: the axioms have been used for over a century without ever producing a contradiction, and the intended model — the natural numbers, or in ZFC's case the cumulative hierarchy — evidently satisfies them. Hilbert's program of a finitary consistency proof for all of mathematics was killed by Gödel's first incompleteness theorem, since that shows no finitary system can be complete. So the right attitude is fallibilism: our confidence is extremely high but not absolute, and it's the kind of confidence that would survive finding out we'd been wrong about something.

(b) If ZFC turned out to be inconsistent, then because classical logic has explosion, ZFC would prove every sentence — it would prove 2+2=5 as well as 2+2=4 — so it could no longer certify anything. But the damage to mathematics as actually practiced would be much smaller than that makes it sound. Most working mathematicians never use anything near the full strength of ZFC; a contradiction might well live entirely in the higher reaches, in Replacement or in the treatment of very large sets, and the theorems of number theory and analysis would be rederivable in much weaker systems that don't contain the offending axiom. This is roughly what happened with Frege: Russell's paradox showed his system was inconsistent, and the response was to repair the axioms — type theory, then ZFC — not to give up on arithmetic. If instead the contradiction showed up somewhere small, we'd have to work out which of our proofs of small facts were the ones that leaned on the bad axiom, and then either restrict ourselves to the weakest system that does the job, or go paraconsistent, the way Priest does with dialetheism, and try to keep doing mathematics anyway.

(c) I think this matters a lot, actually. If ZFC is inconsistent, the shortest proof of a contradiction could still be so long that nobody would ever write it down, which means we could carry on doing mathematics exactly as before without ever running into trouble. But that's cold comfort, since what we want from a foundation is truth and not just the absence of visible disaster; an inconsistent theory proves false things about small numbers too, not only about giant sets."
