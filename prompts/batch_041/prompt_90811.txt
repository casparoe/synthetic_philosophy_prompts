I got appointed to our state parole board about eight months ago and Thursday is the first hearing where I actually have to write something down before we vote, so I could use a hand. (I've also been reading the Dennett/Caruso back-and-forth on free will and just deserts, which is probibly why this one is eating at me.) The case: Mr. Alvarado was 19 when he took part in an armed robbery in which the store owner was shot and left with a permanent spinal injury; he got 30-to-life, has served 25 years, and comes up Thursday. Our statute tells us to weigh two things and never says how — "the seriousness of the offense and the offender's degree of blameworthiness" and "the likelihood that the offender will commit further crimes" — and it also lets us depart below the presumptive term when "the offender's culpability is substantially reduced," a phrase the board has never defined. The department's sheet puts him at high risk on their nine-item instrument, though two of the items are just his age at first offense and a juvenile assault, and he's refused two treatment programs and picked up three write-ups for fighting since 2019. On the other side, the neuropsych report we commissioned says he was likely impaired in his capacity to appreciate the wrongfulness of his conduct — fetal alcohol exposure plus a head injury at 11 — though the state's expert calls the impairment moderate at best, and he was beaten badly as a kid. The victim's sister is speaking; she says he took something that can't be given back and he owes for it.

Two of my colleagues have staked out opposite ends and I need to write something both of them can actually respond to. One, a retired criminologist, says that once you stop believing in free will, "desert" is just revenge in a suit, and that we should hold him exactly as long as the risk numbers say — like a quarantine, no more and no less. The other says that if we deny parole to a man whose culpability was impaired because we're nervous about a risk score, we've quietly turned the board into a predictive-detention agency, and that the culpability provision exists precisely to stop that. They're both half right and I can't get them to see it.

What I need from you: take the role of someone who's spent years on the philosophy of punishment — blameworthiness, determinism, and what a quarantine-style model of confinement would actually license — and draft the written opinion I'll circulate before the meeting. Keep it under 750 words, lead with my recommendation (grant, deny, or grant with named conditions and a review date — pick one and commit to it), give the reasoning in terms the other members can argue with rather than just intoning principles, and spend real space on the strongest case for the losing side and why I dont think it wins. End with one sentence on the amendment that's pending before us, which would strike the culpability departure from the guidelines and let the risk instrument do all the work. Write it as a board memo, not an essay; don't cite any literature, since nobody at that table can check sources anyway; and think it through carefully before you start — I don't need to see the scratch work, just the memo.
