I'm a doctoral candidate in decision theory and the philosophy of practical rationality, and I'm trying to pin down my credence on a claim about the absent-minded driver problem. I'd like a number from you, decomposed into the sub-questions it rests on, with a note on where the weight sits and what would move it.

The version I have in mind uses a payoff structure chosen to keep the arithmetic from being recalled rather than derived. A risk-neutral driver faces two intersections. At each he may EXIT or CONTINUE. Exiting at the first yields 0; exiting at the second yields 9; continuing at both yields 1. He is absent-minded: at the second intersection he cannot tell that he has already passed the first, and nothing else distinguishes the two for him. He knows all of this beforehand and must choose a single probability p of CONTINUING, to be applied at whichever intersection he reaches.

The claim I want a credence on:

(C) In this problem, the ex ante optimal policy — the one maximizing expected payoff evaluated before the drive — is the policy a fully rational driver would adopt, and the fact that, from the standpoint of an intersection, he will sometimes be taking an action that looks suboptimal there is not a failure of rationality but an unavoidable consequence of his epistemic position.

Please decompose your credence in (C) into at least the following sub-questions, each with a rough probability and one line of justification:

1. Whether the driver's conditional belief about which intersection he is at is well-defined and is fixed endogenously by his own policy; and whether that endogeneity defuses the charge that he acts against his interests at an intersection or merely relocates it.
2. Whether the proper unit of rational assessment is the temporally extended agent choosing a policy or the agent-at-an-intersection choosing an action, and how much (C) depends on that choice.
3. Whether (C) requires a prior resolution of the single-agent-versus-multiple-selves issue, and whether that issue is settled by anything independent of the problem.
4. Whether (C) survives under a resolute-choice or plan-based conception of rationality, or whether it is an artifact of a narrowly decision-time expected-utility conception.
5. Whether the fact that the ex ante optimum coincides with the point of ex post indifference — so that at an intersection the driver has no conditional reason to prefer CONTINUE to EXIT — is a resolution of the paradox or a symptom of it.
6. Any further sub-question you think the credence turns on.

Then combine them into a single number. Finally, say which sub-question your number is most sensitive to, what argument or evidence would move that sub-question most, and what would count as settling (C) one way or the other.

For calibration: my credence in (C) is about 0.65, and a colleague's is about 0.35. Given your decomposition, where do you think our disagreement most likely lives?
