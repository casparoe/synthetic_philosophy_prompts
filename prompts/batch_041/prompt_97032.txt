Ok so I'm a novelist — three books out, the kind of writer who thinks in scenes and POV and what a character wants badly enough to lie about — and I keep tripping over this philosophers' thing called the paradox of fiction. Near as I can tell they think there's some deep puzzle in the fact that we can be genuinely scared or gutted by people who never existed. I don't need the reading list, I need you to restate that whole debate inside the vocabulary I actually work in: point of view, free indirect discourse, interiority, reader identification, the fictive dream, dramatic irony, the contract you quietly sign with a reader on page one, suspension of disbelief, all of it. Translate it faithfully — don't just swap 'belief' for 'suspension of disbelief' and call that a translation, I'll notice.

Then give me the residue report, which is the part I actually want. Which pieces of their puzzle map cleanly onto craft, which only survive if you fudge them, and which have no counterpart at all in how a novel really works on somebody. For what its worth my own hunch: when I killed off a character in book two I got real hate mail, and when I wrote the scene I cried at my own desk, and neither of us was confused about whether she existed. So maybe the philosophers are fighting a problem that dissolves the second your the one building the illusion.

But you tell me. Is the leftover stuff because my toolbox is just a workshop — good for making readers feel things but with no way to ask whether feeling them is rational — or because the puzzle was never as real as it sounded once you look at it from the workshop floor? Be honest about which, and don't be nice about my hunch if it's lazy.
