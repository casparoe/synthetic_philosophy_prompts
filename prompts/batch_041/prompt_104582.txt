I teach a seminar that pairs Miranda Fricker's work on epistemic injustice with film, and I want a reading of Sidney Lumet's 12 Angry Men (1957) that goes beyond the usual observation that Juror 8 is the hero of the epistemic virtues and Juror 10 is the villain of them. Fricker separates testimonial injustice -- where prejudice deflates the credibility a hearer grants a speaker's word -- from hermeneutical injustice, where a group's experience becomes hard to interpret because the shared resources for making sense of it were never theirs to shape; and she insists that the virtue of testimonial justice is owed to the speaker as a knower, not merely prized as a route to accurate judgment. Read the film against that framework, and against the uncomfortable fact that the film's entire drama turns on the jury's progressive *discounting* of testimony. The two eyewitnesses -- the lame elderly neighbor and the woman across the elevated tracks -- are precisely the socially marginal speakers whose word deflates as the deliberation proceeds, so the machinery of reasonable doubt runs on discrediting them. Is the film, then, a dramatization of testimonial injustice, or of the necessity of credibility-deflation, and can Fricker's virtue survive the demands of a criminal standard of proof, given her own insistence that the virtue must not collapse into credulity?

Work through the specific pressure points rather than summarizing the plot: Juror 10's racist rant as the film's picture of identity-prejudicial credibility deficit, and the other jurors' mere distancing from it as a repair that leaves the credibility economy intact; the defendant's own word, which never enters the room except at second hand, so that the knower whose testimony matters most is structurally absent and the slum-kid script crowds out his meaning; and Juror 3's certainty, which is rooted in his estranged son rather than in any social identity, so that the film may dramatize a non-identity distortion of credibility that Fricker's restriction to identity prejudice cannot quite name. Say what the knife demonstration, the old man's failed fifteen-second walk, the eyeglasses reveal, and the torn photograph each show about how credibility is actually redistributed -- and whether Juror 5's and Juror 11's experiential authority is testimony as evidence or something else.

Be faithful in both directions: don't reduce the film to an illustration of the theory, and don't stretch the theory to fit the film. Where the reading strains, say so, and end by saying what the film shows about the ethics of belief that Fricker's argument alone does not. No citations needed; I know the literature.
