Two philosophers have spent two years arguing about Pascal's mugging: the stranger who offers to avert an enormous amount of suffering with a vanishingly small probability if you hand over your wallet. They agree that the mugging is a serious problem and that the standard dismissal, on the grounds that the probability is too small to bother with, is inadequate as it stands. They disagree about where the defect lies. The first holds that unrestricted expected-utility maximization is a defective rule of choice and that the repair belongs in the theory of decision: some structural constraint on how risks of enormous magnitude may enter a decision. The second holds that the rule is fine and that the defect is upstream, in the credences and descriptions the mugger exploits: there is no determinate probability to maximize over, so the mugger's offer is not a counterexample to a rule of choice but to a fantasy of complete belief.

Draft the joint statement these two could sign. It should contain:

1. A numbered section of claims both accept: about the structure of the mugging; about what any adequate response must not do — in particular, it must not cap small probabilities in a way that would also forbid cheap precautions against unlikely catastrophes; and about which proposed responses are dead ends.

2. An exact statement of each remaining disagreement, given in two versions, each written so that the side it describes would accept the wording as its own.

3. The cruxes. For each, name the case or argument both parties agree bears on it, and record each party's prediction in advance. Include at least the following:
   - The location of the defect, rule versus input, tested by a pair both parties accept as a shared fixture. U: a stranger with no track record and no describable mechanism says that for $1 he will refrain from causing 10^18 person-years of severe pain, an outcome that will otherwise occur with probability 1 in 10^12. V: a publicly documented device — a radioactive source and a well-understood detector, its distribution published and independently checked — will, with probability 1 in 10^12, avert 10^18 person-years of severe pain, and one attempt costs $1. The two offers have the same expected value per dollar; the only difference is whether the conditional claim is vouched for by a described mechanism. Does any constraint that licenses V and forbids U earn its keep, and is it a constraint on choice or on credence? State what each side predicts.
   - Escalation: the same stranger returns with probability 1 in 10^100 and 10^200 person-years; and the many-muggers case, in which a crowd of strangers each press a competing claim and the agent cannot satisfy them all.
   - Whether the correct target is the value side, unbounded utility, rather than the rule or the credence; each side states what it predicts about whether the other's repair collapses into this one.
   - Whether a repair to choice must also constrain belief, and not only action.

4. A third proposal, floated during the collaboration and owned by neither side: before an action may be selected on expected-value grounds, the agent must be able to specify a procedure that would in principle confirm or disconfirm the outcome-claim, and the credence must be derivable from a distribution the agent would endorse as well-defined. Each side says whether this is its own view in disguise, a fudge, or a genuine third option, and what it predicts about the proposal's verdicts on U and V.

5. A closing section on which part of the dispute, if any, is merely verbal — for instance a disagreement about what the word rational is being asked to mean here — and on what evidence or argument would actually move each side.

Write the document as a working text, with no attributions and no references; every claim should stand on the argument given for it. Where you cannot find a formulation both sides would accept, say so, and say why.
