I'm in a pretty informal ethics reading group with some friends and we just did the bit of Nozick where he rolls out the experience machine. I think I get the basic move — you wouldn't plug in, so pleasure, or experience more generally, can't be all that matters — but our discussion kind of spiraled and now I'm stuck in a way I can't untangle on my own. The wrinkle is that Nozick also tosses in two more of these things: a transformation machine that turns you into whatever kind of person you'd like to be, and a result machine that makes the things you'd experience yourself doing actually have their effects out in the real world. One of the guys in the group said these are basically the same argument stated three times over, and that taken together they're a complete refutation of hedonism, and honestly I nodded along at the time, but now I want to double check that before I repeat it to anyone.

So my question is roughly this. Given that we've known since 1974 that hedonism about well-being is dead — the machine settles that, and the other two machines settle that it isn't just about your mental states either, it's about actually being someone and actually doing things — why do so many philosophers still bother defending hedonism? Is it just stubbornness or professional inertia, or do they think the thought experiment somehow doesn't land? And if the machines really do land, doesn't that mean the objective-list view, where things like knowledge, achievement, and deep relationships are good for you whether or not they feel good, is pretty much proven? I feel like I'm maybe skipping a step there but I can't see where. The annoying thing is that my argument feels airtight to me, and yet smart people keep disagreeing about this, which makes me suspect the airtightness is an illusion. I'd be happy to be told I'm confused, I just want to know exactly where.
