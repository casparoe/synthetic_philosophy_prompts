There's a line going round my department that epistemicism and supervaluationism about vagueness are basically two notations for the same thing. Under epistemicism a borderline case is determinately in or out and we simply can't find out which; under supervaluationism there's no determinate fact to find out, and 'she is liable' comes out not true rather than false. So the story goes, nothing anyone could *do* turns on the choice, and the whole dispute is pure semantics. I want to argue that's wrong, and that the two come apart the moment you ask what a decision-maker should do with a borderline case — a tax rule turning on whether a change of use was 'substantial', a criminal statute turning on whether someone was 'reckless', a hiring policy about 'relevent experience'. My hunch is that the two views give different answers to the question *what is the official doing when she resolves the borderline case*. On the epistemicist picture she's guessing at a fact that exists but can't be known, which raises the question whether punishing someone on the basis of an unknowable fact can be just at all. On the supervaluationist picture there's nothing to guess at, so the standards governing her decision — consistency, predictability, treating like cases alike — have to come from somewhere else, and 'she got it wrong' means something different.

What I want from you: state the question so it has a determinate answer, explain why the 'it's just semantics' line doesn't settle it, sketch what I'd actually do month by month, say what a result would look like, and be honest about what would count as the project failing (e.g. if every divergence I find turns out to follow from some general principle that you shouldn't convict people of things the law doesn't determine, then my interesting claim is dead and I should be able to say so rather than fudge it).
