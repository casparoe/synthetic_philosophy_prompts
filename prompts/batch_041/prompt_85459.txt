I want to pressure-test a position I've been defending, and I'd like it stated back to me with its commitments made explicit. Call it the deflationary view of heritability: heritability coefficients, as estimated by twin and family designs, are not merely limited in scope but are the wrong kind of quantity for any causal or interventional question. As I hold it, the view has four parts. First, a heritability coefficient is a variance ratio indexed to a particular population living under a particular distribution of environments, so there is no invariant value of it to be right or wrong about — the same trait can have different coefficients in different places, and the coefficient itself can be raised or lowered by changing the spread of environments without altering any causal pathway. Second, the standard decomposition presupposes that genetic and environmental contributions are additive and independent, so in the presence of gene-environment interaction or gene-environment correlation the estimate is a biased parameter rather than a tolerable approximation. Third, it follows that even a heritability of one within a group licenses no inference whatsoever about the causes of differences between groups. Fourth, the honest replacement is explicitly causal designs — random assignment, cross-fostering, naturally occurring perturbations, instrument-based designs — and the coefficient should be treated as a descriptive index of the current variance budget at best.

What would count against this? I want the defeaters ranked, from those that would be fatal to those that would be merely awkward, and I want them separated into two piles: results or arguments that a defender of the deflationary view would themselves have to concede count against them, versus things only critics would press. Say what the view predicts that its rivals do not — I take the core predictions to be that the coefficients should fail to transport across environments and across time, and that interventions designed on the assumption that the coefficients are causally informative should underdeliver relative to their projected effect sizes — and tell me how risky those predictions really are. I am especially interested in whether the view's central move can be turned against it: the observation that genetic variance estimates from the additive model sit well below estimates from direct molecular methods is often read as evidence that the model is misspecified, but it can also be read as evidence that the additive model simply undercounts genetic variance through imperfect measurement, which would leave the coefficients more innocent than I claim. Which reading is better supported, and does it matter for the view?

Finally, do the honest accounting on insulation. A defender can always say of any inconvenient estimate that it was confounded or that the environment has since shifted, and I want to know what would distinguish that from a view that has been qualified into unfalsifiability, and whether unfalsifiability here is actually a defect. I would be most moved by a demonstration that the coefficients are stable across environmental shifts and track the effects of interventions; I would be least moved by the reminder that heritability is not destiny. If the view survives everything you can throw at it, say so, and tell me what I should conclude from that.
