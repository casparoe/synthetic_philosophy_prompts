Please help me with a passage in Frederick Douglass's 1860 Glasgow speech on the American Constitution. Douglass is answering an abolitionist lecturer who had attacked him personally, and he writes:

They are men who thoroughly understand the principle, that they who are not for us are against us, but who unfortunately have failed to learn that they who are not against us are on our part.

The sentence looks as though it should be self-undermining. "Not for us, therefore against us" and "not against us, therefore on our part" are contrapositives of each other, so anyone who grants the first has already granted the second, and Douglass's complaint that his critics "understand" the one while having "failed to learn" the other would be incoherent. I want to know whether there is a principled reading on which the two are not simply contrapositives — for instance, if "for" and "against" shift their objects between the clauses, or if the first clause concerns allegiance while the second concerns standing to be treated as a fellow-worker. I also want that reading tied to the substance of the speech: Douglass maintains that the Constitution is anti-slavery while conceding that sincere and able people read it as pro-slavery, and he is willing to make common cause with people whose readings and politics he rejects. What principle of interpretation, or of alliance, is doing the work here, and can it be stated in a way that does not collapse into either "anyone who is not an enemy is a friend" or "anyone who is not a friend is an enemy"?
