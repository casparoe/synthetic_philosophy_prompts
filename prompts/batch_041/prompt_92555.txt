I work on the ethics/policy side of a small startup that builds an AI phone receptionist -- it answers calls for dental offices and salons, books and reschedules appointments, that kind of thing -- and I've somehow ended up owning our disclosure policy before we launch, which is turning out to be way harder than I thought. We had a two-hour argument about it last week: does the bot have to say up front that it's a bot, or can it just talk naturally and answer honestly if someone asks? Half the team says the second option is obviously fine because we never assert anything false. The other half says it's creepy and dishonest anyway. My aunt is a dental receptionist and she says she'd be furious if she found out she'd been talking to a machine, so that's my sample of one. So I've been going back to that Google Duplex demo from I/O in 2018, where the assistant called a salon and booked a haircut, and it did the little filler noises and the deliberate pauses to sound like a person -- the blog post about it is weirdly candid that the disfluencies were added on purpose -- and then there was a big public fight about whether it should have announced itself. Google's line was basically that transparency matters and they were working out the right way to signal it, but the demo itself didn't signal anything. I want to use that as my test case.

So analyze Duplex through the philosophy of lying and deception, and tell me where the standard machinery lands and where it breaks. Be precise about what the system actually did: did it assert anything false, or did it just not disturb a presumption that was already there -- on a phone call you assume the other party is a person, and the design was optimized to keep that assumption comfortable rather than to correct it. I want the difference between deceiving someone by assertion, deceiving them by implicature or by exploiting a default they can't see, and merely failing to correct a false belief, since the whole case seems to sit in that third bucket or on the border of the second, and I suspect the usual definition -- the one that wants a false assertion plus an intention to produce a false belief -- isn't going to help much here, partly because nothing was asserted and partly because the intent lives in a design team rather than in the system. Then push on it. Is there a separate duty of self-disclosure for AI that doesn't just reduce to the duty not to deceive, and if there is, where does it come from? The obvious objection is that humans on the phone don't announce they're human, and call centre staff read scripts that are practically engineered to make you think you're talking to a person, and nobody calls that lying, so either the AI case is genuinely different or we're holding the machine to a standard we don't hold the humans it's imitating -- if it's different, say exactly what the difference-maker is, and whether the distributed-intent point is doing the work. Also, if we go with the ask-only-if-asked policy, is that the narrow-answer move where you tell the truth and let the other person draw the wrong conclusion, and is that better or worse than just lying? Finally, if the honest answer is that no clean notion of dishonesty attaches to the system itself, and that what we're really evaluating is the interaction and the deployment, say so plainly -- I'd like to know whether that's a real conclusion or just a dodge, because it would mean the concept of honesty for AI systems isn't a property of AI systems at all, which I think is a bigger concession than most people writing about this want to make. And if there are eval transcripts where models just flat-out say false things, contrast those with Duplex: do the two cases need the same theory, or does forcing them into one definition make the definition useless?
