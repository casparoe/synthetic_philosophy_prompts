I have been building small programs that solve analogy puzzles in deliberately tiny domains — the kind where you are shown a short letter string changing into another, such as abc becoming abd, and asked what ijk becomes; or where one row of three shapes changes into a second row and you must produce the next change — and I keep running into a phenomenon that I can describe in detail but cannot name. I am hoping you can tell me whether there is an established concept for it and, more importantly, whether my description cuts at a joint or smears several different things together. Assume I know some cognitive science and philosophy of mind, but not this particular literature.

Here is what I mean. In these puzzles, the vocabulary I use to describe the material does not stay fixed across the solution. In the letter-string case, a letter functions at one moment as a particular individual (this a, the one at the front), at another as a member of a class (a vowel, a letter that precedes others), at another as a position in a sequence (the first slot), and at another as something defined purely relationally (whatever stands to j as b stands to a). The relation that takes me from one element to the next is sometimes alphabetical succession, sometimes positional succession, sometimes something closer to sameness of role. None of these readings is announced or settled in advance; each gets fixed, if at all, by the demands of the mapping I am trying to build, and often it is revised midway, after which the earlier parts of the description are quietly re-read so that they still fit.

Three features of this are what make me doubt it is ordinary ambiguity. First, the shift is not an error or a last resort: the puzzle is solvable only because the descriptions can shift, and a solver that holds its vocabulary fixed either fails outright or produces a flat-footed answer. Second, the shift is driven from outside the element being re-described — what makes me re-see b as a role rather than a letter is that the target string contains no b, so the mapping forces the source to be re-described. Third, and this is the part I cannot get a grip on, there is no fact of the matter afterwards about what the concepts were doing. If I write down the reading I used, the writing is either a tidy fiction, presenting as fixed what was actually negotiated, or an incoherent one, listing incompatible readings for the same occurrence. Two people who solve the same puzzle correctly give different accounts of what the letters were doing, and the same person solving it twice may give different accounts.

The same thing happens to me in a second toy domain, a puzzle in which a few colored blocks can be moved and stacked and the goal is to make the arrangement match a picture: a block is at one moment a rigid object with a position, at another the top of a tower, at another merely a marker of a place, and the whole description reorganizes itself once I see that the goal is to match a shape rather than to reach a location. Although I care about the toy cases, I suspect the same thing is happening when a word's sense drifts across a long argument while every local use remains defensible.

What I want to know is whether there is a term of art for this. Several things nearby do not seem to be quite it. It is not plain ambiguity or homonymy, because the readings are not all available in advance; they are manufactured in the course of the task. It is not the general vagueness of concepts, because at each moment the description feels perfectly definite. It is not quite the idea that one theory's concepts must be re-described before they can be lined up with another theory's, because there is no second theory here and no fixed original. It is not a perceptual Gestalt switch either, since nothing about the stimulus changes when I re-read the letters, and I can apparently hold both readings at once without one displacing the other. And it is not metaphor, since nothing is carried over from another domain; the new reading is drawn from the same material. If some term covers exactly this, I want to know what it is and what it was coined to do. If nothing does, I would rather you coin a term than tell me it is a special case of something broader.

I do not need a reading list or citations — name the ideas and the fields they come from and I will track down the references myself. Please organize the answer as follows.

1. Nearest established concepts (about 300 words). The two or three closest terms or concepts you know of, each with the field it comes from, its definition in your own words, and the specific respect in which it fails to cover what I have described.

2. What my description is not (about 250 words). The distinctions I have drawn above, corrected if I have drawn them wrongly — in particular, tell me if one of my near-misses is in fact the right home and I have simply misdescribed it.

3. The phenomenon, carved (about 400 words). A definition, the features you take to be essential rather than incidental, and an account of what my examples get wrong about the phenomenon I am gesturing at. Say whether you think this is one thing or a family of things, and whether it is specific to tasks that require invention rather than mere application.

4. Boundary cases (about 250 words). Three or four test cases, drawn from any domain you like, each with a verdict and a sentence of justification.

5. Name and verdict (about 150 words). Whether an existing term suffices or a new one is needed, your proposed term if any, a one-sentence definition of it, and the nearest thing it must not be confused with.
