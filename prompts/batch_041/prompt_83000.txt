I'm setting the exam for my third-year metaphysics of the social world module and I need one big essay question on generations — Boomers, Gen Z, all that — and I want it to be a seperate thing from the usual. Every year half the class answers with the social-construction-versus-natural-kinds dance and the other half just tells me about marketing labels, and neither answer tells me whether the student actually thinks generations are real. So I've decided to put a test into the question itself and make them run it. They've had the usual social-ontology diet (Searle, Hacking, that crowd) so they'll know the moves; the point is that the question shouldn't be answerable by reciting any of them.

Here's the test, which I've been calling the two-stage reality check, and I'd like it to appear in the question roughly as I've written it here:

Stage one — substitution. Take any explanation that appeals to a generation ('Gen X is more cynical about institutions because of what they saw growing up', etc.) and rewrite it so the generation label is replaced by the bare demographic facts: birth years, age at the time of the relevant event, period effects. If the explanation survives the rewrite intact, the generation was doing no explanatory work. If it collapses, say precisely what was lost.

Stage two — boundary. Now re-cut the birth years: move every boundary five years earlier, or split a single generation in two. If the same explanations come out the other side attached to different people, ask whether the original boundaries were discovered or stipulated.

Then the essay question itself: after both stages, does anything survive, and is what survives enough to make generations real kinds, or only convenient shorthand? I want them to give the generation-realist their strongest shot — the realist says generations aren't defined by birth years at all but by having lived through a common formative episode, so membership is fixed by history rather than by demography — and then say whether that reply actually passes the two tests or just relocates the arbitrariness from the choice of dates to the choice of which episode counts. And I'd like a third thing squeezed in: whether the kind might be real only because the label makes people behave generationally, i.e. whether we've made the kind rather than found it, and whether that tells for the realist or against.

Practical bits: 90 minutes, closed book, and I want a model answer of about 750 words. The marking scheme should be out of 20, with five or six must-hit moves, the marks each carries, and for each one a line on how students typically lose marks on it — I've noticed they're definately weakest on stage two, they treat 'the boundaries could have been drawn otherwise' as if it settled the question, so build in a note on that.

Please write it all in British English, the external examiner is fussy about 'analyse' and 'recognise' and that sort of thing. Don't bother citing anyone, they'll have the reading list.
