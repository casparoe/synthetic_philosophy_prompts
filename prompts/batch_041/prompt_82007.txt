Last night I had a long argument with a friend who is a law student and an obsessive designer of self-amending board games, and he kept repeating, as if it settled the matter, that "a rule can always turn on itself." His conclusion was that a constitution can never truly entrench anything: any amendment clause can be used to destroy the very rule that authorizes it, and the notion of an unamendable basic norm is a fiction. I think there is something in the point, but I also suspect he was sliding between several different claims, some of them trivial, some substantive, and at least one of them false. He gave two puzzles that I cannot get out of my head: a rule that says it may never be amended, and a rule that says all rules are amendable, including itself. Can a later rule change the first? Does the second make itself unamendable by its own content, or does it authorize its own abolition?

Please lay out the distinct precise theses that the one-liner could be expressing. I would like a menu rather than a single interpretation: for each reading, say what it commits you to, whether it is a platitude, a substantive and defensible claim, or simply false, and whether it would actually support his conclusion that nothing can be entrenched. I am especially interested in the difference between the logical point about self-referential paradox, the legal point about an amendment power that applies to itself, the game-theoretic point that in a self-amending game no rule is stable because the players can always propose changes, and the more radical conceptual point that all normative authority is ultimately groundless. Please also say which reading people who repeat this line probably mean, and which reading would be needed for the argument he was actually making. I am not after citations or a reading list; I want the conceptual map and your verdicts on each branch.
