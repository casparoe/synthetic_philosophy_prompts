I'm a novelist, about halfway through a book about a kid being raised in the wreckage of everything, and I keep circling back to The Road — so I'd like you to read that novel through Mencius and Xunzi on human nature and tell me which one it actually vindicates.

Start with the bottom line in your first sentence (whose account the book backs, or whether it ducks the question), then give me the reasoning. No throat-clearing, don't restate what I asked.

The thing I keep chewing on is this. The textbook version of the pair is: Mencius says we come out good — the child about to fall down a well, the four sprouts of compassion and shame and deference and right-and-wrong, all of it needing to be watered and extended — and Xunzi says we come out bad, that goodness is artifice, something the sage kings cooked up and then pressed into us with ritual, like steaming a straight plank and bending it into a wheel. Sure. But both of them clearly think you have to work at it. Mencius needs the sprouts cultivated, Xunzi needs the whole workshop. So maybe the real fight isn't about whether effort is required at all — maybe it's about which way the arrow runs. Is the boy's kindness something that survived the world, or something his father built into him by telling him the same story over and over until it took?

So take the scenes and give me a verdict on each. The boy insisting they feed the old man. The boy falling apart when his father makes the thief strip and takes everything. The boy who keeps wanting to go back and be kind when the safe move is to walk on. And the father's litany about the two of them being the good guys and carrying the fire — is that a Xunzian ritual, a made-up thing repeated until it becomes second nature, or is it just water on a Mencian sprout that was there the whole time? Which reading makes better sense of the fact that the father, the one who does all the ritualizing, is also the one going hollow inside, while the boy's goodness seems to cost him rather than come naturally to him?

Then be honest about where the novel resists both of them. My suspicion is that it dramatizes something neither philosopher has room for: that being good is neither a seed you inherit nor a technique you're trained into, but a fragile inheritance you can lose, and that the boy's decency is only legible against the background of a world where nothing enforces it anymore. Xunzi at least has sages and rituals and names worth keeping — in this book all of that is gone and the kid is still kind, which either breaks Xunzi or proves Mencius, and I can't tell which. If you think the book is just Mencius with a bleaker setting, say so and say why. And tell me what the novel gets that the two of them arguing it out in the abstract can't — because that's the part I need for my own book.
