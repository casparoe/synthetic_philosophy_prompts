Alright, I need help with a document. Two people in my reading group have been arguing for about a year now about whether an ant colony has a mind, and I want to make them write down what they'd both sign instead of going another round. So: draft the joint statement of an adverserial collaboration between them.

Quick setup so you know the case. A colony of ordinary ants, a hundred thousand workers, no leader, no central anything. Individually they're tiny and pretty dumb: follow pheremone gradients, bump into each other, drag stuff around. Collectively the colony does things that look deliberate. It picks the better of two potential nest sites and moves the whole colony there. It adjusts how much it forages. It reacts differently to a raid than to a flood. It keeps the same habits and the same address after every single ant in it has been replaced by a younger one. It seems to know where the good food is for weeks. There's no place in it where it all comes together — the deciding is spread across thousands of bodies and, arguably, across the nest itself and the chemical trails on the ground, which do some of the remembering. It's the kind of entity you can't help naming. Aunt Hillary, if you know the one I mean.

Side A (colony realists): the colony really has a mind, in whatever sense you and I do. Aunt Hillary is a subject — she can be surprised, she can be in a good mood, she has beliefs about where the food is, and there's something it's like to be her. The reason you can't find her mind in any one ant is exactly the reason you can't find your mind in any one neuron. Colony-level talk isn't a convenience; it's picking out a real thing with real states.

Side B (deflationists): no subject, nobody home. Yes the colony solves problems, and yes colony-talk compresses what a hundred thousand ants are doing, but compression is all it is. Nothing happens at the colony level that isn't happening at the ant level. The colony doesn't believe or want or feel anything; if you want the real story, look at the ants.

What I want is the document, not a dialogue and not a verdict on who's right.

1. A numbered list of agreed claims, and make it bite. What both sides accept about what the colony demonstrably does; what the fight is not about; and any shared conditional that squeezes both sides. Here's one I think they both have to sign: whatever the ants' ignorance of their own signals shows about the colony, it shows the same thing about you and your neurons, since your neurons don't know anything either. Say whether that's right, what it costs each side if it is, and if one side can wriggle out, how.

2. Where they actually part, each position written the way its own side would want it written, no strawmen. My hunch is the real fault line isn't the vague one about whether colonies are minds, it's whether the colony has states with content that no ant has and that aren't just a summary of ant states. But you tell me where it is. And if some of the year-long fight turns out to be about words rather than about minds, say which part and why.

3. The cruxes — the sub-questions where settling one would move at least one side — with, for each, the case or argument both sides agree bears on it and what each side predicts in advance. Two you should consider, though you're not bound to them. (a) Prediction test: is there a colony-level variable that does real predictive work and can't be swapped out for a distribution over ant-level states without losing anything? (b) Turnover: every ant replaced, colony keeps its habits — the realist wants that to be one persisting subject the way your body is one persisting subject despite cell turnover, the deflationist wants it to be a river, not a person, and both should say what they'd expect to find that would tell them apart. And don't duck the one that looks sharpest to me: the ants may well be conscious and your neurons aren't (or might not be), so this system might be built out of little subjects rather than out of non-subjects. Does that make Aunt Hillary one mind, a hundred thousand minds, or a different kind of thing? I want both sides forced to say something there, since it's the one place the colony and the brain genuinely come apart.

4. Put the remaining disagreements in a table if that's cleaner than prose.

Ground rules for the draft: don't let either side win by definition — no defining mind as whatever a brain does, and no defining it as whatever has a central place where it all comes together. Don't pad the agreed claims with stuff nobody disputes. And no bibliography or reading list, we're not doing that.
