Ok, so I keep going back and fourth on the "scale argument" for caring about insects. The rough idea: we're very unsure whether insects feel anything — suppose each one has only a small chance of being sentient — but there are so staggeringly many of them (I vaguely recall estimates around a quintillion alive at any moment) that even a tiny probability times that number yields an expected amount of suffering that swamps almost anything else we care about. I want you to argue that this argument is weaker than it looks. My main suspicion is the way it treats each insect as an independent lottery ticket: if sentience is really a fact about a whole lineage or a kind of nervous system, so that either the entire clade is sentient or none of it is, then the probabilities don't multiply out and the arithmetic changes completely. Build the strongest version of that objection you can, throw in whatever else you think helps, but also be honest about where the scale argument still has teeth even if you're right. How confident are you in the conclusion, and what's the one point you're least sure about? Please write in British English.
