I'm a philosophy grad student (epistemology and phil of mind, mostly) who's gotten slowly sucked into the AI alignment literature, and I keep tripping over HCH and factored cognition whenever anyone wants to talk about idealized deliberation. I think I have the rough picture: you take a question, have a human break it into smaller sub-questions, answer those by consulting copies of themselves or by training a model to imitate that move, and recurse until the pieces are small enough to just answer; factored cognition is the broader claim that sophisticated reasoning can be decomposed into lots of small, mostly independent tasks, and HCH is one way of cashing out what the limit of that process looks like. What I don't want is a summary of the proposals — I can read those. What I want is for you to explain the conceptual issue of whether this is genuinely an idealization of deliberation, or something else wearing an idealization's clothes. You should write this like a philosopher of mind who also knows the alignment stuff well, and please start by brainstorming out loud: list the candidate relationships HCH might stand in to human deliberation (idealization, abstraction, approximation, operationalization, stipulative definition, something else) and say which distinctions actually do work here, before you commit to an answer. Then give me the explanation proper.

Some things I want you to actually address, because they're where I get stuck. First, what kind of idealization is it? The classic ideal reasoner in formal epistemology strips away resource limits — logically omniscient, perfectly coherent credences — and people have complained both that no real agent can be that and that the resulting ideal may not even be the right normative target. HCH seems to go the other direction: it builds boundedness in at every node, since each subagent is a finite human and the sub-tasks have to be small enough to be tractable. So is there a principled line between an idealization that removes a limitation and one that installs the limitation as load-bearing structure, and which side is HCH on? Second, the decomposition problem. If a question only factors a certain way given a particular ontology or framing, then the tree's verdict depends on the decomposition, and it looks like there might be questions where no decomposition into mostly independent sub-tasks preserves the answer — the kind where you have to see the whole thing differently rather than chip at it. Is that a real limit or does it just mean the tree is allowed to contain a node that says reframe the question, in which case is anything left of the claim? Third, the normativity bit. Suppose HCH would say P. Why is that a reason for me to believe P? If HCH is an amplified version of one human, it inherits that human's blind spots, and amplification might just as happily amplify the errors. Compare the ideal-advisor tradition in ethics or wide reflective equilibrium, where the idealization is supposed to earn authority by subtracting distorting influences — here nothing is subtracted, so where would the authority come from? And fourth, who exactly is the deliberator in factored cognition? Is the tree one agent or a society, and does treating it as an idealized single deliberator smuggle in a unity-of-agency assumption that the construction itself is supposed to be neutral about?

Ground it with a small toy example — pick some modest reasoning task and walk through how the tree would handle it versus how an actual person does, so I can see where the two come apart. Then land on a verdict: what deliberation does this capture, what does it leave out, and which of the standard idealization worries apply and which don't. Don't cite anything, I'm not looking for a reading list, and don't hedge by saying different authors use the terms differently — I want your reconstruction and your argument for it.
