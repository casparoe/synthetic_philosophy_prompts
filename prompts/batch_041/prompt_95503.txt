My dog died in February, after fourteen years, and what has stayed with me is not the grief but its selectivity: I have kept fish, and never once felt that a fish's death was a death in the same sense, and I cannot name the fact about the dog, as distinct from the fact about me, that made the difference. In the same term my students have started asking, more often and more seriously, whether a large language model could be a moral patient, and I have noticed that I answer the fish question and the model question with the same improvised gestures. I would like to stop lecturing them about criteria and instead run a session in which they arrive at their verdicts under conditions that make the arbitrariness of those verdicts visible to them.

The setting: an upper-level undergraduate seminar on philosophy of mind and ethics, eighteen students, one ninety-minute meeting, chairs movable into two clusters. They have read two or three papers on animal sentience and have no neuroscience at all. The session falls after a unit on other minds and before a unit on moral status. Nothing may require equipment beyond paper, envelopes, and a whiteboard.

What I want them to come away seeing is twofold. First, that the question of which computations matter morally is underdetermined by everything we ordinarily know about a being, because our evidence arrives in two streams — what a system does, and how it is built — and our moral verdicts are fixed almost entirely by the first while the philosophical arguments turn on the second. Second, that the verdict may be doing something other than tracking a fact about the system: that what we call the boundary of sentience may be partly a fact about the attributor. I do not want them to leave with a criterion. I want them to leave unable to say confidently which of the two streams their own verdicts were reading.

Here is the structure I have in mind; build it out, and change it if you see a better route to those two experiences. Two panels, each given a different dossier on the same eight systems. The behavioural panel gets transcripts, response profiles, and outcomes: what each system does when poked, deprived, rewarded, separated, or given a choice. The mechanism panel gets only architecture descriptions: how signals are routed, whether processing is recurrent, whether there is integration across subsystems, whether some signal functions as a valence or reward-prediction term, whether the system models its own states, and whether noxious input is evaluated centrally or handled by a local reflex arc. Neither panel sees the other's dossier. Each panel then assigns every system a verdict on a four-point scale — no welfare relevance; welfare-relevant but no interests; moral patient; moral patient with a life to be considered — with a confidence and a one-line reason.

Before anything is exchanged, each panel writes the three questions it would most want answered about the other dossier, and what it would do with each answer. Collect these and read them aloud; they are the session's real data about what the students take the criterion to be. Then allow a limited trade: each panel may buy answers to at most two of the other panel's questions, from a small budget that also buys other things you design, such as the right to revise a verdict or to abstain. What they spend on is a second and sharper revelation.

Reconvene and reveal the cases in deliberately constructed pairs: two systems whose behaviour is identical but whose mechanisms differ, a reflex arc against a centralised evaluative loop producing the same avoidance; two whose mechanisms are identical but whose provenance differs, the same computation in a selected lineage versus in an artifact built to mimic one, or the same system presented once as a companion and once as a diagnostic instrument; one that has the machinery but shows none of the behaviour, so that the behavioural panel has already misjudged it, as a prey animal concealing injury or a patient who cannot report; one that shows all the behaviour with a scripted policy behind it; and one with only some of the markers, forcing the group to decide whether the verdict is graded or thresholded. Close with a single whole-group vote on one system in which I vary nothing but the framing — who made it, what it is for, whether they have interacted with it, whether they are told it was designed to be loved — and let them watch the vote move.

The mechanism cards should carry the specific criteria I want weighed, in plain language a student with no neuroscience can use: recurrent processing and feedback; integration of information across subsystems; a global broadcast; a valence signal modulating learning; a self-model; central versus peripheral evaluation of noxious input; and the behavioural markers usually treated as proxies, such as motivational trade-offs, self-administration of analgesics, wound-guarding, and the capacity to trade present pain against future benefit. The session should make vivid that these are not one criterion, that they do not sort the eight systems the same way, and that sentience may come in degrees along several independent axes rather than at a single threshold, so that the boundary is a decision about where to cut rather than a discovery.

For the debrief, map what happened onto the philosophy: where verdicts moved when information was purchased, and what that says about the underdetermination of sentience attribution; the identical-behaviour pair, and the supervenience question it raises; the identical-mechanism pair, and the possibility that moral status is partly constituted by our relations and practices of recognition rather than by the computation; the purchase round, as a record of their revealed criterion against their stated one; the misjudged case, and the asymmetry of the two errors, since under-attribution is invisible and over-attribution is not; and the framing round, as the gap between the question of what it is like, if anything, to be the system and the question of what we may do to it.

Give me a minute-by-minute timeline for the ninety minutes, the case cards written out so I can photocopy them, and a facilitator's script for the debrief. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle each failure. Be frank about what the exercise cannot reproduce — I expect it cannot deliver the phenomenology, cannot settle which criterion is right, and cannot reproduce the way these questions are actually settled outside a seminar room, by institutional incentives, legal categories, and cost — and tell me plainly which parts of the argument the facilitator has to supply rather than letting the activity generate. Do not let any of the named criteria appear before the debrief, and give me no reading list; the session must stand on its own.
