I hold the common prior assumption in a strong, normative form: it is a constraint of rationality rather than a modelling convenience, so two agents who have conditionalized on the same information cannot rationally disagree, and any apparent disagreement must be traced to different information, different models, or error. I would like to know what counts against it, ranked from decisive to merely awkward. I am interested in the formal results that genuinely bite — the no-trade theorem, given how much profitable speculative trade there is; the persistent-disagreement results for agents who share a prior but not a model; the failure of agreement once information is non-partitional or agents are unaware of what they do not know — and, separately, in the case for thinking the assumption is unfalsifiable rather than false, since any pattern of disagreement can be rationalized by some assignment of priors. Say which of these a defender would concede as a real defeater rather than dismiss as a scope restriction. I am also tempted by a stronger, intrapersonal version: that a rational agent's credences must be a martingale, so that she cannot expect her own future credence to differ from her present one except by expecting new information. If that is the right way to state the assumption, then forgetting, self-locating belief, and anticipated distortions of one's own judgement are counterexamples to it. Are they counterexamples, or only cases the assumption was never meant to cover — and if the latter, what is left of it?
