Ok so I'm 68, spent my whole career as a biochemist (mostly protein work, nothing famous), retired two years ago and have been reading philosophy ever since — podcasts, a couple of the big textbooks, my nephew's old seminar reader. The group I argue with on Thursdays keep telling me to just write my positions down so they can shoot at them, so here's the list, and I want you to tell me if it actually hangs together or if I've been helping myself to something.

(1) I'm a consequintialist. The right act is the one with the best consequences, and I'll say flatly that consequences means welfare, and welfare means people getting what they want (preference satisfaction). I have no patience for acts that are "inherently wrong" regardless of what they do to anyone.

(2) I ran controlled experiments for forty years. My reflex on any policy question is: run the trial, measure the outcome, then argue. If a consideration can't in principle be measured, I get very suspicious of it.

(3) I think there are preferences that shouldn't count. If someone only wants something because they're desperate — if the desperation is the only reason the want exists — I don't think we owe it any respect. It's not really their preference, it's the situation talking.

(4) On kidneys: I'd keep the current ban over the libertarian alternative of legalize-it-and-let-the-price-float. Not because of the body count — I'll concede the ban probably costs lives. It's that a kidney shouldn't have a price on it. Some things get degraded by being bought and sold, and I find that thought genuinely compelling even though I can't put a number on the degradation.

(5) But I'm completely fine with paying plasma donors, and with paying healthy volunteers for phase-1 drug trials. And I think Uber should be allowed to surge-price at 2am.

(6) On the other hand, I think price gouging on bottled water in a flooded city should be illegal, no question.

(7) Here's a scheme I came up with that I actually like: legalize kidney sales, but the state is the only buyer, at a fixed price, and you get paid in a lifetime credit against future health-insurance premiums instead of cash. Nobody's "bought", they're just compensated for a loss, and the price is set by the state so desperation doesn't set it.

So: which of these actually conflict, which ones only look like they do, and where's the real trouble? I'd rather you rank the problems by how bad they are than list everything as equally fatal — and for each one, tell me what has to give and what giving it up would actually cost me.
