I have been trying to get clear on the ownership of ideas, and there is a pattern in intellectual property law that I cannot make sense of, though I suspect it is not simply a mistake. Four regimes inside the same legal system take the same event -- two people, working independently, producing the same thing -- and give it four incompatible answers. Patent law treats independent invention as no defense at all: if you are second to file, you infringe, and the fact that you never saw the first inventor's work is irrelevant, apart from narrow prior-user rights. Copyright treats independent creation as a complete defense: if you wrote the same novel without ever encountering mine, you have infringed nothing. Trade secret law expressly permits both independent discovery and reverse engineering, and prohibits only the breach of a confidence. Trademark does not care about copying in the first place; you may copy a name or a logo freely so long as consumers are not confused, with a narrow exception for famous marks that I would rather you address than ignore. I can state each rule; what I want is the philosophy underneath. Each regime looks to be operating with a different theory of what is wrong with taking someone's idea, and perhaps a different theory of what an idea is such that it can be taken at all.

So reconstruct the implicit theory of ownership that each of the four presupposes, and tell me which of them the practice actually requires. For each regime, distinguish what the rule strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with -- I would guess, for instance, that patent's indifference to independent invention is compatible both with a natural-rights view on which the patent is an object one may not trespass on and with a purely forward-looking incentive view, but I want to know whether that is right and where the two would come apart. Then say whether the four are jointly consistent. My instinct is that they are, and that the differences track something about the cost of detecting independent creation in each domain, or about how much of the value lies in the expression as against the information conveyed. But I cannot tell whether that is a real theory or a rationalization of whatever the drafters happened to do, and I would like you to say which: if it is a theory, state it; if it is rationalization, identify which regime is doing the genuine philosophical work and which are historical accidents wearing philosophical clothes.

One further case, because it is the one I find hardest. In academic writing the norms run the other way again. A scholar who publishes a result that someone else has already published is expected to cite it, and the excuse that one never read that paper and arrived at the result independently is treated as at best a partial mitigation and often not even that, whereas a poet who independently writes a line that already exists in another poem owes nothing to anyone. I take it that the citation norm is not a property claim at all but something else -- a norm about the integrity of the scholarly record, or about credit functioning as evidence of priority -- and I would like you to say what view of authorship and of intellectual contribution it presupposes, and whether that view is the same one copyright presupposes or a rival to it. What I want out of the answer, concretely: the implicit theory per regime, the strict-presupposition versus mere-fit distinction for each, a verdict on joint consistency, and an account of what would have to change in the other regimes if any one of these theories were taken as canonical and exported to the rest.
