I've been reading LessWrong and the Alignment Forum for about a decade, and I keep hitting the same wall in the recurring argument over whether reinforcement learning agents can suffer. The exchange swings between "it's a scalar and a gradient, there is nothing there to hurt" and "if the functional profile matches ours, withholding the word is chauvinism," and the two moves never meet. Before I take a side, I want the specification first: what would a theory of valence have to deliver in order to settle whether an agent's reward signal can constitute suffering — or to show that the question is malformed rather than merely hard?

Give me the requirements list: the cases it must get right, the distinctions it can't collapse, the empirical findings it can't contradict, each with the consideration that makes it a requirement rather than a prejudice. The cases I care about: an agent whose reward is negative for states that represent its own damage; an agent that only learns a policy avoiding such states, with nothing in it that marks them as bad; a tabular agent versus one whose reward is itself the output of a learned model; an agent whose negative reward is momentary but dominated in expectation by a larger positive reward for pressing on; and, on the biological side, dissociation cases such as pain asymbolia, where the sensation is still reported but no longer troubles the patient, and blindsight-style cases where information is present without the felt character. The distinctions I don't want collapsed: a signal that represents that something is bad versus a signal that is bad for the agent; a reward that is instrumentally bad because it leads to worse outcomes versus one that is non-instrumentally bad; a signal globally broadcast through the system versus one that modulates a single pathway; and valence as a functional role versus valence as requiring consciousness of a specifically biological kind. Take seriously, too, the point that an agent's reward is not necessarily the thing it ends up optimizing, since that seems to cut against reading the reward function as a hedonic state at all.

Then mark which requirements are non-negotiable and which are commitments of a particular camp, say whether some subset of them cannot be jointly satisfied, and identify the single requirement that does the most to exclude candidate answers. If you think the question is malformed, show me where the malformation lives rather than answering around it. Finally, tell me what would change your mind: which argument or consideration would make you drop or demote a requirement, and where your reasoning is most likely to be wrong.
