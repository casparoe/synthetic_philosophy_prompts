Write a joke about the cosmological argument. The punchline should turn on a real feature of the arguments rather than on generic theism-versus-atheism material, and it should be funny to someone who has actually spent time on these arguments; a reader who knows the literature should not be able to tell that the writer has only read a summary. Please avoid the two jokes everyone has already heard: the gotcha that asks who caused God, and its mirror image, in which the theist wins by definition.

The contrast I would most like the joke to live in is the difference between the kalam version and the contingency (Leibnizian, principle-of-sufficient-reason) version. The kalam argument needs the universe to have had a temporal beginning, and what its causal premise delivers is a cause of the universe's coming to be: a first cause that need not be conceived as doing anything thereafter. The contingency argument needs no beginning at all, since a universe that has always existed is still a contingent one, but precisely for that reason its conclusion is a cause of the universe's existence rather than only of its coming to exist, and so it demands an explanatory entity with a more continuous role. The image I want the humor to exploit, without stating it flatly, is that the first picture licenses something like a contractor who finishes the job and leaves the site, while the second requires something more like whatever is holding the building up right now, so that a universe with no beginning is no less dependent than one that started. If you would rather land the punchline on some other genuine feature of these arguments — the possibility of an actually infinite past, the question whether beginning to exist means having a first moment or being preceded by a time at which the thing did not exist, the Grim Reaper style arguments against an infinite past, the suggestion that the causal premise is embarrassed by indeterministic quantum events — that is fine, provided the joke still turns on the content rather than on the general shape of cosmological arguments.

After the joke, add a brief note of a few sentences: say where it takes liberties with the arguments or leans on a contested reading, and identify the premise or interpretive choice the punchline actually depends on — that is, what would have to be true for the joke to be fair to the arguments, and what a critic who rejected that reading would say has gone wrong. If that choice turns out to be indefensible, say whether the joke dies or merely needs rewording.
