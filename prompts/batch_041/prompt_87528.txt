My oldest friend and I spent most of last night arguing about pain asymbolia, and I want to convert that argument into the essay question on the final exam for the upper-level philosophy of mind seminar I teach. She holds that the syndrome settles the matter: if a patient can report that her hand hurts while showing none of the usual aversion to it — no withdrawal, no guarding, no analgesia-seeking, no distress — then the badness of pain plainly is not intrinsic to the pain but is contributed by something else. I spent two hours insisting that this does not follow, and eventually realised we were talking past each other because neither of us had said what we meant by the badness of pain being intrinsic. That is precisely the confusion I want my students forced to work through, and it is not a confusion they can escape by reciting the textbook division between the sensory and the affective dimensions of pain.

Please write the question itself, a model answer of roughly 600 words, and a marking scheme.

The question should be answerable in fifty minutes and worth 20 marks. Its crux should be the badness claim rather than the physiology: students must say what it would take for the badness of pain to be intrinsic to it, what it would take for the badness to be relational or functional instead, and then say exactly which of those the asymbolia case supports. In particular, they must distinguish the claim that pain's badness is not intrinsic from the weaker claim that pain and its badness can come apart; many of my students will run these together, and the marking scheme should make that conflation cost them.

Build a second case into the question, a mirror of the first. This patient shows the full behavioural and autonomic profile of severe pain — guarding, withdrawal, analgesia-seeking, a stress response — yet reports that the sensation is not in the least unpleasant, calls it a mere sensation, and is genuinely puzzled by her own behaviour. Ask whether that case is coherent at all, and what its coherence or incoherence implies about whether the badness of pain consists in how the sensation feels or in the role it plays in the person's motivational economy. Students should have to commit to a verdict rather than survey the options.

Keep the empirical details open. Tell students they may stipulate whatever clinical details they need, so long as they flag the assumptions they are relying on, and that no names, studies, or references are expected: I am testing reasoning, not recall of a particular case report. The model answer should take a definite line. The marking scheme should name the specific moves a strong answer must make, with the marks each is worth out of 20, and for each move a line on the mistake that most often costs students it; then a short note on what separates an A answer from a B one.
