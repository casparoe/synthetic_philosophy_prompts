Municipal speech regulation in my city has settled into a package of rules that look incoherent when read side by side. Marches and amplified rallies are governed by route restrictions and decibel caps, defended on the ground that the rules target noise and traffic rather than the message; the city is barred from asking whether the marchers' message will anger the neighborhood, and when a hostile crowd gathers, the police are expected to protect the marchers rather than ask them to disperse. Street solicitation, by contrast, is restricted near cash machines, school gates, and traffic lanes, on the theory that solicitation produces intimidation, obstruction, and accidents. And threats are punished outright, including speech that a listener reasonably experiences as intimidation, whatever the speaker intended.

What philosophical commitments does this package presuppose? More precisely: does it rest on a distinction between harm that arrives through an audience's uptake of a message -- anger, offense, revised beliefs, a boycott that costs its target money -- and harm that arrives through the mechanism of delivery, such as noise, blocked sidewalks, or the fear produced by a person standing too close? If so, can that distinction survive the fact that much speech is designed to produce the first kind of harm, so that the message's success and its injury are the same event? Whom does the package treat as owning a listener's reaction: the speaker or the listener? Reconstruct the implicit theory of harm and of responsibility for reactions, distinguish what the practice strictly requires from what it merely fits comfortably with, identify where it is underdetermined among rival theories -- a Mill-style harm principle, an autonomy or truth-tracking view that assigns the audience responsibility for its own responses, a dignity-based view of the sort that underwrites hate-speech law elsewhere, or a straightforward balancing of interests -- and say what would have to change in the ordinance book if the implicit theory were replaced by one of the rivals, and which commitments, if the package cannot be made fully consistent, deserve to survive.
