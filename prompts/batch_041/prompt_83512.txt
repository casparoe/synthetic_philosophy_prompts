Next month I'm the visiting philosopher at the math department's philosophy-of-math reading group, and I need a Q&A prep sheet for the talk.

Here's the setup. The room is almost entirely analysts — functional analysis, a couple of PDE people — plus one or two set theorists and a physicist who drifts in, and as far as I can tell not one person with a philosophy degree. They only invited me because they spent a whole session arguing about Thomson's lamp and someone said, half joking, that they should go ask a philosopher. So the audience is smart, but it's smart in the way people are smart who think a limit is a limit and a sequence is a sequence, and they'll probly hear most of what I say as hand-waving unless I'm careful.

What I'm defending: the lamp isnt really a paradox about infinity. The description fixes the toggling schedule — infinitely many flips, all completed by t = 2 — but it doesn't fix the lamp's state at t = 2. I want to argue that's not a defect of the puzzle and not evidence that supertasks are impossible; it's a supervenience gap. If there's a fact about on-or-off at t = 2, it has to supervene on something, and the description gives it nothing to supervene on. Then the ambitious move: I claim every stock supertask "paradox" (the lamp, the shrinking walker, the marble-and-vase, the reversal cases) is that same supervenience gap in different clothing, and that's why they all feel like cheats rather than contradictions.

The talk is 45 minutes with 30 of Q&A, so this lives or dies in the question period.

Give me the prep sheet: the objections I'll actually get from this room — in their vocabulary, phrased the way an analyst or a set theorist would actually put it, not the way another philosopher would — ordered by how much damage they do, with a roughly two-minute reply to each and a note on where that reply is soft. Tell me what to concede up front to buy credibility, and which single reply has to land or the whole thing falls apart. And include the one question I'm hoping nobody asks.
