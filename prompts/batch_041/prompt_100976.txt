I teach a graduate seminar on risk, regulation, and environmental ethics, and I am assembling a handout for our unit on the precautionary principle. I would like you to construct a decision tree — equivalently, a taxonomy in which each branching node is labeled by the question that separates it from its siblings — that situates the main positions on the precautionary principle within a single logical space, and that my students can use to sort themselves by what they actually think rather than by the label they prefer.

My students will already know the textbook contrast between a weak, permissive reading (on which uncertainty is not by itself a reason to postpone cost-effective protective measures) and a strong reading that reverses the burden of proof, so the tree must not bottom out there. Organize it instead around the four distinctions I care about.

First, the trigger — what epistemic situation is supposed to license precaution at all. Separate, at minimum, the mere possibility of harm, a plausible but unquantified threat, scientific uncertainty about whether a causal link exists, outright ignorance of the mechanism, and irreversibility of the prospective harm.

Second, the response — what precaution, once triggered, actually demands. Distinguish the mere permission to regulate from an obligation to regulate, and from a genuine shifting of the burden of proof onto the proponent of the activity; where a burden is shifted, distinguish an evidentiary burden (produce the studies) from a demonstrative burden (establish safety). Also separate a requirement to consider alternatives from a requirement to abstain.

Third, the scope — whether the principle governs only environmental and health hazards or every domain of decision under uncertainty, whether it is confined to irreversible or catastrophic harms or reaches any significant harm, and whether it applies only to novel technologies.

Fourth, the status of the principle — a substantive decision rule, a merely procedural requirement on how decisions are made, an interpretive presumption that governs how existing evidence is weighed, or a heuristic or disposition of the decision-maker rather than a rule at all.

Build the branches out of the logical space first, then place the actual positions in it. I want leaves for: weak precaution; strong precaution with a reversed burden of proof; a purely procedural version that imposes no substantive outcome; the anti-precautionary position that decisions under uncertainty should simply maximize expected value; a version that treats precaution as a maximin or worst-case rule rather than an expected-value rule; a character- or virtue-based reading on which precaution is a disposition of the regulator rather than a principle; and the meta-level position that the principle is either incoherent or self-defeating (for instance, that it licenses inaction whose own risks are uncertain). Add any further leaves the space generates.

Drive the branches with concrete cases, not abstractions. Use at least these, and phrase each node as a question a student could answer without already knowing which position it leads to: (i) a self-replicating engineered organism released into a watershed, where the probability of catastrophe is unknown but the harm would be reversible within a decade; (ii) a widely used chemical whose low-dose, cumulative, delayed harms are suspected while the mechanism remains wholly unknown; (iii) a proposed precaution whose own implementation carries a risk comparable to the one it addresses; (iv) a hazard whose only victims would be members of future generations, who cannot consent; (v) a case in which the probability of harm can be bounded but its magnitude cannot, or the reverse; (vi) a case in which what is uncertain is not the size of a known harm but whether any harm exists.

Finally, flag any pair of leaves the tree cannot genuinely separate, and note the combinations of answers that no one occupies, saying briefly in each case whether the gap reflects an incoherent view, an unattractive one, or merely an unexplored one. Keep the tree to roughly eight to twelve leaves. Where two leaves differ only in terminology, say so rather than multiplying positions.
