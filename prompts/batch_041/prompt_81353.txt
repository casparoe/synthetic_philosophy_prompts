My friend and I had the same arguement for the third time last night and I want to figure out whether we're even disagreeing about anything. It's about conspiracy theories. She thinks "conspiracy theory" is a slur dressed up as a category — that it just means an explanation saying powerful people secretly coordinated to do something, and there's nothing epistemically wrong with that kind of explanation as such. Her examples: Watergate was a conspiracy, the tobacco companies really did collude to bury the research, the phone companies really did hand over the records. On her view you take each case on its own evidence and the label adds nothing; using it to dismiss something is just begging the question. I think there's a real family of beliefs that's worse than their evidence alone would suggest — the ones where missing evidence becomes proof of a cover-up, where nothing could ever count against them, where they explain everything and predict nothing, where every loose end the official story can't tie up gets counted as a point in their favor. Those really are a kind of thing, and the word names it. She says I'm just defining the bad ones as conspiracy theories and the true ones as something else. I say she's pretending that a hunch you can test is the same as a machine for never being wrong. Round and round.

My hunch, and I want you to tell me if it's right, is that this isn't actually a disagreement about conspiracy theories at all. Maybe it's a fight about how to use a word, so we're each right about our own vocabulary and there's nothing to settle. Or maybe it's really an empirical question about base rates that neither of us has bothered to answer — whether beliefs with that self-sealing shape are actually less likely to be true, or whether it just feels that way. Or maybe we mean different things by evidence and that's where the real fight is hiding.

So diagnose it. Is this a substantive disagreement, a verbal one, two people answering different questions under one heading, or a standoff over starting intuitions neither of us can argue for? Use what each side actually says, not a survey of who's written what. And tell me what would count as progress here — a distinction, some data, a change in the question — or whether the honest answer is that nothing will move it, and why. Also tell me which of my examples, if any, are actually doing work for the other side rather than mine, because I have a suspicion at least one of them is.
