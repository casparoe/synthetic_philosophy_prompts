I am assembling a handout for a graduate reading group in early modern philosophy, and I need a diagnostic instrument rather than an essay: a decision tree that sorts readers by which reading of Descartes on animal minds they actually hold. The familiar summaries — animals as machines, incapable of thought and therefore of feeling — flatten a space that is considerably more interesting, so please build the tree out of the logical space of the question rather than out of a list of labels.

Treat the following as the raw distinctions to build with, not as the answer to be reproduced.

First, Descartes' term for thinking is broad. His canonical enumeration of the modes of thinking places sensing and imagining alongside doubting, understanding, affirming and willing. If sensing is a mode of thinking, then a blanket denial of thought to animals looks as though it must deny them sensation as well — and yet he writes of animals as moved by fear, hunger and anger, and he takes their bodies to share our physiology down to the organs of sense. The interpretive question is how those two things are supposed to sit together.

Second, the published case against animal thought runs through two tests: genuine language, understood as the capacity to produce new and meaningful speech, and general adaptability of behaviour across novel situations. It matters a great deal whether these are read as constitutive criteria (thought requires language, so animals cannot have it) or as evidential ones (we have no adequate grounds for attributing thought to them, though they might have it).

Third, the metaphysical and the moral claims come apart. Only humans are said to have immortal, immaterial souls; and the denial of reason is sometimes presented in a way that settles what we may do to animals. A reading can accept one of these and reject the other.

Fourth, the correspondence late in life, especially with Henry More, is read by some as loosening the published doctrine and by others as clarifying that what was denied was never feeling as such.

The instrument should separate at least these candidate denials: no intellection or reason; no language; no phenomenal awareness (feeling that no one is having); no sentience at all; no immortal soul; no moral standing. It should also separate readings that differ over what kind of claim is on offer — what Descartes meant, what his own principles committed him to whether or not he saw it, and what his system could consistently accommodate. One leaf, or one explicitly flagged non-leaf, should be the verdict that the corpus contains no single stable position.

Aim for six to eight branch nodes and eight to ten leaves. Every node must be phrased as a question a careful reader could answer without already knowing which position it leads to, and no node may simply ask whether one accepts a named view. Every leaf must name a position (or be marked unnamed if nobody defends it) and state its most awkward commitment in one sentence. Where a reading rests on a particular argumentative move — the language test, the mechanism constraint, the mortality of the animal soul — identify it by its role in the argument rather than by page numbers, citations or a bibliography; the instrument should stand on its own for a reader with the texts but no secondary literature. Note any combination of answers that no one occupies, and diagnose it as incoherent, unattractive or merely unexplored. Flag any pair of leaves your tree cannot separate and say why. If two labels turn out to pick out the same commitment, merge them and say so.

Deliver the whole thing as a single JSON object with this schema:

{
  "instrument": {
    "title": string,

    "scope_note": string
  },
  "nodes": [

      "id": string,

      "answers": [

          "option": string,

        }
      ]
    }
  ],
  "leaves": [
    {
      "id": string,
      "position": string,
      "path": [string],

      "cartesian_resource": string,
      "most_awkward_commitment": string,

    }
  ],
  "unoccupied_combinations": [
    {
      "path": [string],

    }
  ],
  "inseparable_pairs": [

      "leaves": [string],

    }

    "follow_ups": [string],

  }

In nodes, goes_to holds the id of the next node or of a leaf. In leaves, path holds the sequence of answer options leading to that leaf, cartesian_resource names the element of Descartes' own system the reading leans on, what_would_tell_against_it names a concrete consideration or piece of animal behaviour that would count as evidence for or against the reading, and status is one of defended, minority, unnamed or unstable. In unoccupied_combinations, diagnosis is one of incoherent, unattractive or unexplored. In quick_sort, give the shortest route through the tree — the first question that separates the most leaves, and the follow-ups needed to finish the job — and use note for anything about the tree's structure a reader should know before starting, including which distinctions the instrument deliberately does not try to resolve.
