I run a small reading group on the ethics of AI and we've been stuck for months on whether a digital system could ever have moral status. The split is always the same. One side says consciousness, the thing that makes a being matter, is a biological phenomenon, something brains do, so no amount of silicon computation gets you there and all you'd ever be doing is rewarding a convincing imitation. The other side says thats just prejudice about carbon: if a system processes information, represents its own states, and reacts to harm and benefit the way a person does, then demanding a biological extra ingredient is an unjustified double standard, the same move people once used to deny status to animals or to other humans. Every meeting each side restates its position, everyone nods, nobody moves an inch. Take the role of a philosopher of mind who also knows the moral-status literature, brainstorm a bit first, then think it through carefully before you answer. My own hunch (just a hunch) is that this isnt really a disagreement about facts or even about consciousness at all, its a disagreement about what would count as evidence for someone else's mind and about how much risk of being wrong we're allowed to take when the stakes are moral. Is that right, or is there a genuine substantive dispute buried in there, say about whether having experiences is a natural kind or a functional role, or about whether moral status comes in degrees? Diagnose it for me: verbal, empirical, or a clash of starting intuitions neither side can argue for? And given the diagnosis, what would actually count as progress here, a formal criterion, a change of question, or should I just stop expecting the group to converge?
