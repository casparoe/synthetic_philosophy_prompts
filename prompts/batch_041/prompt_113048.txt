I'm a TA for an upper-level seminar on moral pscyhology, and we're spending two weeks on P.F. Strawson's reactive attitudes. The bit I want students to really feel is the difference between the participant stance—where you resent someone, demand something of them, feel indignant on someone else's behalf, and expect them to answer for what they did—and the objective stance, where you treat a person as something to be managed, explained, treated, or pitied. The standard examples are children, the very ill, and people we've decided are not fully agents. But I want a story that pushes on the idea that the objective attitude can become a general policy, almost a spiritual discipline, and that it might look like maturity right up until it becomes a way of never being vulnerable to anyone. I've been kicking around a setting: a small coastal town with a volunteer radio watch, a woman who has spent years training herself to explain everyone's behavior rather than react to it, and a night when a distress call comes in that she doesn't pass along. But use that only if it helps; I care more about the philosophical pressure than the plot.

Please write me a short story, maybe 2,000–3,000 words. You're a philosopher and a literary fiction writer, so think carefully about the scenes before you start: brainstorm three or four concrete situations where withholding reactive attitudes first looks like strength or compassion and then curdles into something else, then pick the one that lets the concept show up in action rather than in dialogue. Don't have anyone lecture about Strawson or use the terms 'participant stance' or 'reactive attitudes' in the prose. Instead, let the reader feel the difference between treating someone as a participant in a relationship—someone who can be resented, forgiven, demanded of—and treating them as an object to be managed. Include at least one scene where another character refuses the objective attitude and the protagonist finds that refusal intolerable, and include an omission (a failure to act) that the protagonist wants to excuse by saying she didn't cause anything, while someone else insists the failure is still hers. Don't resolve it neatly. End on a moment where a reactive attitude either breaks through or fails to, and make that ending ambivalent.

After the story, add a short note (150 words max) for me, not for students, mapping two or three scenes onto the Strawsonian ideas and flagging one place where the story risks making the view too easy or too one-sided. No citations, no reading lists, no references to real texts in the story. I'm trying to get them to see why the objective attitude is not just coldness—it can be kind, therapeutic, and protective—and why that makes it so tempting. If you need to invent a small institutional detail (a logbook, a duty roster, a town meeting), go ahead.
