Hey — second-year MA student here, and I've been stuck on something from my metaphysics seminar for about three weeks. The whole seminar is on grounding: whether there's a relation of "in virtue of" holding between facts, whether every fact is grounded in something more basic, whether reality bottoms out in fundamental facts. I wrote this argument at 2am and I still can't see what's wrong with it, but it says grounding isn't a relation at all, which would make most of the seminar loose talk, so something's got to be off. Every line still looks like a truism to me. Here it is.

(1) Grounding is hyperintensional — necessary equivalents can differ in what grounds them, or in whether they're grounded at all. The fact that Socrates exists grounds the fact that {Socrates} exists. That second fact is necessarily equivalent to the fact that {Socrates} exists and 2+2=4 (no world where one holds and the other doesn't), but the conjunction has an arithmetic conjunct to answer for, so it's grounds aren't just Socrates. Or: say the fact that 2+2=4 is fundamental. Then the necessarily equivalent fact that 2+2=4 and (2+2=4 or dogs bark) isn't fundamental, since a conjunction is grounded in its conjuncts. (2) A relation holds between its relata because of how those relata are — get the relata and you've got the relational fact, and swapping in an exactly similar relatum can't change whether the relation holds. (3) Two necessarily equivalent facts are exactly alike in every respect that a relation between facts could latch onto. There's nothing left over for it to go on, because a fact just is a bit of the world's modal profile. (4) So if grounding were a relation between facts, necessary equivalents couldn't differ in what grounds them. (5) By (1) they can. (6) So grounding isn't a relation; grounding-talk is more like a sentential operator, a fancy way of writing "because." (7) And if there's no grounding relation, then nothing is ungrounded in any robust sense either, so "fundamental" stops being a feature of the world and becomes a feature of our descriptions of it.

I want to keep (1) — it's the one thing the whole seminar agrees on, and it's what makes grounding interesting to me — and I'm not attached to (7). My friend says the problem is the jump from (6) to (7), which I'm happy to give up if it saves the rest.
