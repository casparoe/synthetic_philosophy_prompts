I am a novelist. My last book was set on a shrimp farm and narrated in part from inside the pond, and the research for it turned me into something I did not set out to become — a person with convictions about the welfare of decapod crustaceans. Next month I have been asked to open a symposium on aquatic animal health run by a fisheries science institute, and to spend the rest of the afternoon in conversation with the room. I would like a prep sheet for the question period, not a defense of the talk.

The thesis I will put on the table is roughly this: that the crabs, lobsters, and above all the farmed shrimp are serious candidates for sentience, and that they are owed a precautionary but graded welfare standard rather than the benefit of our doubt; that the practice I find hardest to defend is the routine removal of the eyestalks of female broodstock to force them to spawn, and, close behind it, the slaughter of crustaceans and fish with no stunning step at all; that a nervous system distributed through the body rather than gathered into a brain does not by itself defeat the possibility of a unified experience, though it does change what we should expect the evidence to look like; and that fiction is a legitimate instrument for taking such animals seriously when the behavioral evidence is ambiguous and every inference risks being read as anthropomorphism.

The audience is mostly aquaculture biologists and industry veterinarians, for whom welfare means growth, health, and mortality, and who are professionally wary of the word sentience because it seems untestable; a handful of producers who will hear a threat to their margins; and two philosophers of animal welfare invited as respondents, who will likely think I have not gone far enough and that my crustacean focus is a sentimental distraction from fish. Give me the eight or ten objections this room will actually raise, ordered by how likely and how damaging they are, each phrased the way that audience would phrase it — the biologists' demand for a mechanism, the producers' demand for a number, the philosophers' charge of squeamishness — each with a two-minute reply and an honest note on where the reply is soft. Tell me what to concede at the outset and what to hold. Include the question I am hoping nobody asks. And give me a committed answer: take a definite position on which objections have real force and which I should refuse, and tell me where you think I am wrong rather than leaving the verdict to my values.
