I write a column on science and ideas, and I've spent the past several weeks trying to get clear on a problem that surfaces every time I talk to people who build reasoning systems: how a finite agent should reason about claims in logic and mathematics that it has not yet proved or refuted. The standard apparatus for rational belief — Dutch books, conditionalization, expected utility — assumes logical omniscience. It treats the agent as certain of every logical truth, so its credence in a sentence and in its negation already sum to one whether or not the agent has any proof, and its probabilities over a conjecture are degenerate from the start. For an ideal reasoner that is unobjectionable; for anything made of finite computation it is close to useless, since the agent cannot survey the whole space of consequences at once. What I want to understand is the shape of a better account, one on which an agent can hold a genuine, middling credence in an open conjecture, or in the proposition that some particular program halts, without first settling the question — and can do so without thereby committing to a set of beliefs that a patient bookmaker could bleed dry. I gather there is a line of work, sometimes called logical induction, in which a computable process assigns probabilities to logical sentences over time and is meant to be immune to a class of traders; but I don't want the construction. I want the target.

So I'm asking you to write the desiderata: the requirements any theory of logical uncertainty would have to meet to count as adequate, each with the case or consideration that motivates it. I want, at minimum, the situations it must handle — an unresolved conjecture, a statement independent of the axioms, a sentence that refers to the agent's own future credence or to its own provability — the distinctions it must not collapse (between what is unprovable-and-false and what is merely unproven; between the agent's uncertainty about a fact and the world's indeterminacy; between internal coherence and calibration against the truth), and the constraints it cannot violate (avoiding a Dutch book, remaining computable, agreeing with the probability axioms in the limit on the questions it can eventually settle). Where you think some of these requirements pull against one another and cannot all be satisfied at once, say so plainly, and tell me which you would give up.

A last thing, since I'll be writing this up for a general readership rather than for a seminar. I don't need you to rank the existing contenders against the list — the omniscient Bayesian, the view that simply assigns probability one to what is provable and zero to what is not, and any account that splits the difference by calling every undecided statement a coin flip. I'm after the criteria themselves, and your judgment on which of them are genuine demands and which are inherited habits of the probabilistic tradition; if one requirement turns out to be doing most of the work of separating good accounts from bad ones, I'd like to know that too. A table is fine if it keeps the list legible.
