The issue is whether there is a morally significant difference between lying and misleading. The usual distinction is that lying involves asserting something one believes to be false, whereas misleading involves getting someone to form a false belief without asserting a falsehood—through implicature, selective omission, misleading emphasis, or the arrangement of true facts. But I am not satisfied with the usual ways of cashing out the moral difference. Some people say lying is worse because it violates a norm of assertion or a tacit promise of truthfulness. Others say misleading can be just as bad, or worse, because it manipulates the hearer's evidence while preserving the speaker's deniability. I want to understand the underlying conceptual issue: is the moral difference between lying and misleading grounded in the act of assertion itself, in the intention to deceive, in the violation of trust, or in the impairment of the hearer's epistemic agency? I am especially interested in what I have been calling the deniability premium: the advantage a speaker gains by misleading rather than lying, because she can later insist that every sentence she uttered was true. Does that premium explain why misleading is sometimes worse than lying, or does it show only that misleading is a more effective form of deception?
