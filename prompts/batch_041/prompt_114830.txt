I'm working on a little project about Nietzsches essay on history -- you know, the monumental / antiquarian / critical thing, history in the service of life rather than as a pile of facts. I want to push the scheme past anything he could've seen, so I need you to predict what he'd have made of some stuff from the last couple of decades. Dont worry about citing him or hunting down passages, I'm not asking for a bibliography -- I care about the reasoning.

Here's the material. Some of it is half-invented, so take it as described rather than as something you might already have opinions about.

1. Ambient automated nostalgia. An app that every day resurfaces one of your own old posts or photos from exactly N years ago, alongside a "on this day in your town" feed assembled from scanned local newspapers, plus a genealogy service that pings you whenever it finds a new record about a great-grandparent. No effort, no ritual, just constant low-grade exposure to your own past and your town's past, tuned to keep you scrolling.

2. Statue removals and street renamings. City councils pulling down monuments to figures who were widely admired in their day and often forgotten since, on the grounds that the city shouldn't keep honouring them.

3. Automated biography. A model that writes short confident biographies of ordinary people -- your great-grandmother, some random 19th-century blacksmith -- out of census records, newspaper fragments and other people's uploaded family trees, then extracts "life lessons" from each and serves one up as a daily card.

4. A chatbot trained on basically everything digitized that will, on request, argue any past position in the voice of somebody who held it -- the abolitionist, the slaver, the duellist, the pacifist -- fluently, with nothing in the output indicating which side is which.

Questions:

(a) For each of the four, which of his three modes does it look like, which does it actually serve, and where does it fit badly? My suspicion is that the modes are about what the past is *for* rather than about which archive you happen to be using, so the same practice might land in two different modes depending on the person's relation to it -- tell me if that's right or if I'm mangling him.

(b) Does he need a fourth mode? If the feed-optimized, machine-curated past is genuinely new, name the mode and say what it does that monumental, antiquarian and critical can't. If it isnt new, say which of the old three swallows it.

(c) His whole point was that life needs forgetting as much as remembering, and that an excess of history is a disease. What does he do with the fact that forgetting is now the thing you have to engineer deliberately (deletion requests, disappearing messages) while remembering is the default because storage is basically free? Is that just the 19th-century disease getting worse, or does the direction of the problem reverse?

(d) Which of the four is the hardest case for his typology, and which is the easiest?

(e) Be explicit about where you're reading this off him and where you're extrapolating -- I'd much rather have a flagged guess than a confident synthesis.

(f) What would change your mind about your answer to (b)?

Keep it reasonably tight, no need for a huge essay. Last line exactly in this form, nothing after it:
\answer{<mode he'd back for the 2020s, or 'none of the three'> | <the case he'd call most life-destroying>}
