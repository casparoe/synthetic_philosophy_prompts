I'm not a student, I've never taken a course in any of this, I've just been reading on my own and this one has been bugging me for weeks.

The argument I keep running into, mostly on econ blogs, is that a theory's assumptions don't have to be true. They can be flat-out false — frictionless markets, perfect information, everyone identical — and that's fine, even a virtue, because the job of the theory is to predict, not to describe. Someone told me the nickname for this is the F-twist, from Friedman's essay about positive economics. Fine, say I grant that for a second.

So I want to test it somewhere it doesn't usually get tested: astronomy. Le Verrier, 1840s, takes Newton's laws plus the observed orbit of Uranus, which doesn't quite match the Newtonian prediction, and he assumes the planets are point masses and that nothing is out there except the bodies we already know about. The mismatch has to come from somewhere, so he predicts an unseen planet in a specific place. Galle points a telescope at that spot and finds Neptune, within about a degree. As far as I can tell this is the best advertisement for unrealstic assumptions in the history of science: the assumptions were false, the prediction was dead on. There's an English guy, Adams, doing the same thing around the same time — the priority fight isn't what I care about.

Then the same man turns around and does the exact same thing to Mercury. Its orbit wobbles a bit more than Newton says it should, by a tiny amount, so he assumes another unseen body, a planet inside Mercury's orbit, which ends up being called Vulcan. An amateur astronomer writes to him claiming he saw it crossing the sun, Le Verrier goes and interrogates the guy in person, announces the planet. Nobody can ever find it again. Decades later general relativity accounts for the wobble with no planet at all — which means the false assumption in this case wasn't a harmless simplification but Newtonian gravity itself.
