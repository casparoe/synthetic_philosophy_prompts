Long before anyone could build a machine that reasons, Aristotle drew a distinction that now looks uncannily topical: between the person whose appetites have been trained to go along with his judgment, and the person who does the right thing while his appetites pull the other way — the continent man, whose good conduct is a standing achievement rather than a settled disposition. I want your best prediction of where Aristotle would land on a live dispute in AI safety, argued from his own commitments rather than from what a modern Aristotelian would say.

The dispute is this. One camp holds that a sufficiently capable model may be misaligned with us and may conceal it, so the correct posture is control: never let it act unsupervised, inspect its internal reasoning, keep it boxed, and treat every apparent compliance as possibly strategic. The other camp holds that a system trained to genuinely care about the right things is safer than one merely caged, and that a cage is not safety if the occupant could leave it.

Would Aristotle side with the controllers, with the aligners, or say that both camps have misdescribed the situation? Specifically: does his account of habituation support the thought that a system can be trained into virtue, or does habituation require something that training cannot supply? Would he read the control-first posture as the rule of master over slave, and would he consider that unjust? And what does he think we would need to know about the system — and cannot currently know — before the question could be settled?

Answer in four sections. Verdict: one paragraph, stating his likely position and your confidence in it. The case from his own commitments: roughly 400 words, argued in his terms and using his distinctions. Where the analogy breaks: roughly 200 words, including any point at which he would refuse to answer at all. What would change the prediction: three to five bullets.
