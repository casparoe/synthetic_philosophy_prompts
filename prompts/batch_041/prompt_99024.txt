I am a software engineer with no formal training in philosophy, and I am trying to think through a problem that has become both personal and professional. I would rather you reason the thing through with me than hand me a summary of the usual positions.

The personal side. Three weeks ago my uncle collapsed at home and has been in an ICU ever since. He is on a ventilator, and he is also on an external circuit: a machine outside his body oxygenates his blood and drives his circulation, so his own heart is not doing the pumping. Neurological exams and an imaging study show no blood flow to the brainstem and no brainstem reflexes; the neurologist says he will never breathe on his own or regain consciousness. A more sensitive scan, however, picked up intermittent electrical activity in a small patch of cortex. The neurologist says that activity is not enough to support any experience, but it is not zero. On the strength of the brainstem findings, he has certified my uncle dead. The hospital has recorded a time of death, and because my uncle was a registered organ donor, the procurement team has been notified and is preparing to retrieve his organs while the machine keeps them perfused. My aunt is adamant that he is alive: his chest is warm, the monitor still shows a blood pressure, and she believes that switching the machine off would be killing him rather than letting him go. A cousin who works as an ICU nurse in a neighboring state says her hospital would not treat this as death at all — there, he would count as dead only after the machine was withdrawn and his circulation stopped by itself, and only after some waiting period; and she adds that even then there is a live dispute about how long the wait should be. The family cannot agree, and the hospital is pressing us to consent to the withdrawal-and-retrieval sequence within the day.

The professional side is why I cannot stop turning this over. I write software for hospitals, and a product team has asked me to implement a single boolean, "patient is deceased," that downstream systems will read. The spec tells me to defer to "the clinical standard." It has become obvious to me that there is no single standard — the answer seems to depend on which of several different thresholds you pick, and those thresholds can disagree in exactly the kind of case my uncle is in. I want to know whether there is a principled way to choose among them, or whether this is ultimately a convention we impose and could impose differently.

What I would like from you: tell me which threshold you think should govern and why; how my uncle's case comes out under each of the competing thresholds; the strongest argument against your preferred view; and what, concretely, I should say to my aunt and to the product team.

Then finish with a structured summary as a JSON object with exactly these fields:

- "case_outcomes": array of objects, each with "criterion" (string), "outcome" (one of "alive", "dead", "indeterminate"), and "rationale" (string) — one entry for each threshold you distinguish, and there should be at least three.
- "recommended_verdict": one of "alive", "dead", "indeterminate" — your bottom line for my uncle.
- "strongest_objection": string — the best case against your verdict, in two or three sentences.
- "advice_to_aunt": string — a short, honest explanation I could give her.
- "product_advice": string — what the boolean should be replaced with, if anything.
- "confidence": one of "low", "medium", "high".
