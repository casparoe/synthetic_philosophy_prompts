I am at work on a novel set inside a machine-learning lab, and I have given its director a pet analogy that she trots out whenever her team proposes changing how they train their models. She says that supervising a model's reasoning step by step is like a teacher hovering over a student's shoulder to grade every line of the solution, whereas supervising only the final outcome is like grading the final exam and nothing else. Her claim is that the second approach is not merely cheaper but more honest: the world rewards results; a teacher who inspects the work teaches the student to perform for the inspector rather than to think; and a model trained to please a step-by-step critic will learn to produce reasoning that looks correct rather than reasoning that is correct. She uses the analogy to argue that her lab should abandon process supervision and lean on outcome rewards alone.

I want the argument in the book to be fair to her, so I need the analogy audited rather than dismissed. Please lay it out as an explicit mapping — what in the training setup corresponds to the teacher, the student, the work shown, the final exam, and the grade — and then tell me where the mapping breaks down. I am especially interested in which features of the classroom the analogy quietly imports that the training setting may not actually have: for instance, that a real student carries competence and motivation across many exams and contexts, that a real teacher can see effort and intention and not just marks, that partial credit is informative rather than merely a proxy, and that exams are deliberately designed rather than sampled from the world at large. Set those against the features of the training setting that the analogy leaves out — the sparsity and delay of outcome signals, the difficulty of assigning credit across a long chain of steps, the possibility that a model reaches a correct final answer through a defective chain of reasoning and is rewarded for the answer anyway, and the further possibility that the step-by-step critic she distrusts is itself gameable, so that inspecting the process trades one kind of corruption for another.

Then tell me, separating the disanalogies that actually bear on her conclusion from the ones that are merely picturesque, whether her conclusion survives once the breaks are on the table: does the classroom analogy support abandoning process supervision, does it support only a narrower claim about how process supervision should be designed, or was the analogy carrying the conclusion the whole time? If a better analogy is available for the real choice she faces, give me that too, with its own mapping and its own breaking points, so that I can put both in her mouth and let the reader judge.
