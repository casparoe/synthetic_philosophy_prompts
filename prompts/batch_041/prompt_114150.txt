I'm a third-year student in a course on decision theory and rational choice, and I've volunteered to open next week's tutorial with a case study. We spent the first fortnight on expected value and the standard expected-utility apparatus, and then a guest session introduced the Kelly criterion — the rule that says stake the fraction of your wealth that maximizes the expected value of the logarithm of your final wealth, which for a simple bet that pays b per unit staked with probability p and loses the stake otherwise comes out to p − (1 − p)/b — along with the stronger claim, sometimes called geometric or growth-optimal rationality, that maximizing the growth rate of your wealth is not one preference among several but a requirement of rationality. The class split along predictable lines: the economists think the growth argument is decisive, the philosophers think Kelly is just log utility wearing a lab coat, and neither side got anywhere in the seminar. I would like you to build me a case that puts the dispute on the table in its sharpest form, and I want the verdicts to fall out of the theories as applied to the case rather than be stipulated in the setup.

Construct a thought experiment to the following specifications. (1) It must be an ordinary, realistic decision for one person — savings, a job, a house, a small business — with no casinos, no lotteries, no borrowing, no exotic instruments, and no infinite credit; the decision should be a single choice of how much of the person's wealth to expose to one risk. (2) The opportunity must have strictly positive expected money value, so that a risk-neutral expected-value maximizer facing the same choice wants the largest stake available, which without borrowing means everything. (3) The worst outcome must not be ruin or anything close to it: in the bad branch the person keeps a substantial share of what they had, so that the growth argument cannot be won merely by pointing at the absorbing state of zero. (4) The growth-optimal, or Kelly, stake must be strictly interior — not zero, not everything. (5) The all-in stake must have negative expected log growth, so that the person who put everything in would, by the Kelly criterion, be making a losing proposition even though its expected value is positive. (6) The numbers must be round enough to check by hand, and you should show the arithmetic.

After the scenario, work through the following in order. First, state the verdict of each of four decision-makers on the same case: a risk-neutral expected-value maximizer; a Kelly or growth-optimal bettor; a standard expected-utility maximizer with a concave utility function of your choosing; and a fractional-Kelly bettor who stakes some fixed multiple of the growth-optimal fraction. Be precise about where the middle two coincide and where they come apart, and about which of the four disagreements is doing the real work. Second, identify the feature of the case that generates the divergence — my own suspicion is that it is the multiplicative way the payoffs compound rather than anything about the particular numbers — and say what happens if that feature is removed: what the case looks like when the payoffs are additive rather than proportional, and what it looks like when the decision genuinely happens once and never again. Third, and this is the part I care about most, tell me whether the divergence in this case is a disagreement about rationality or only about risk tolerance, and if it is only about risk tolerance for a one-shot decision, say what would have to be added to make it more than that — repetition, a long fixed horizon, a population of independent agents each facing the same choice, or a distinction between what happens on average across many parallel agents and what happens over time to a single one (the ergodicity point, as I understand it) — and whether any of those additions is legitimate rather than question-begging. Fourth, give the strongest objection available to someone who thinks the Kelly criterion is merely a concave utility function in disguise, and say whether the case survives it. I want a case on which a defender of expected-value maximization has to work, not one they can wave away.
