I spent thirty years designing experiments, and I learned that the hard judgments are rarely about the data; they are about who gets to make the call. Since retiring I have been reading political philosophy, and I have been trying to work out what I actually believe about deciding things by lottery. I would like you to tell me what principle, if any, my verdicts add up to.

A criminal jury of twelve, drawn by lot from the community, convicting someone of a serious crime: the draw is not a regrettable necessity but part of what makes the conviction legitimate. I would not swap it for a panel of legal experts.

A citizens' panel of a hundred and fifty people, drawn by lot, meeting for a year to recommend changes to the electoral system, with the recommendations then put to a referendum: legitimate, and I find it attractive.

A funding agency with money for twenty grants and two hundred proposals that its reviewers judge equally excellent, deciding by lottery which twenty to fund: fine, and probably better than pretending to rank them.

A lottery among all applicants for a limited number of resettlement places, where the applicants are equally deserving: fair.

The last intensive-care bed, wanted by two patients who will both die without it and both recover with it: a lottery seems to me the right way to decide. But when the two patients differ in prognosis, so that one is considerably more likely to benefit, a lottery feels like an evasion.

A draft lottery deciding who is called up for a war I take to be unjust: the draw itself seems fair to me, though it does not make the call-up just.

A randomly selected citizen, with no particular training, chairing the central bank and setting interest rates: absurd.

A randomly selected panel deciding whether a researcher committed misconduct and should have a paper retracted: also no, though I cannot immediately say why this differs from the jury.

A legislature chosen entirely by lot, replacing elections: I want to say no, and I notice that this sits badly with my approval of the citizens' panel.

And one case I suspect is a separate question: I approve of deliberately balancing the citizens' panel by age, sex, region and education, but if someone proposed balancing a jury so that its demographic profile matched the defendant's, I would object.

My first attempt at a principle was that the lottery is appropriate when the decision concerns our common life and inappropriate when it calls for knowledge that not everyone has. That handles the central bank and the retraction panel, but it condemns the jury as well, and it would wave the lottocratic legislature through. A second attempt: the lottery may be used to break a tie among options that further deliberation cannot separate, and to constitute a body whose authority derives from its being a fair sample of those bound by its decision. The first clause handles the funding agency and the two patients with equal prognoses; the second seems to handle the jury, the citizens' panel, and — awkwardly — the legislature.

What I would like is a principle, or a small set of candidates, stated precisely enough that I could apply it to a new case without asking you again. For each clause, tell me which of my verdicts forces it. Then tell me what the principle predicts for cases I have not mentioned — in particular a lottery used to select the members of a body that may only advise and never decide; a lottery over policies rather than over people; and a lottery used to distribute a burden that I already think should not be imposed at all. Finally, tell me honestly which of my verdicts the best principle cannot keep, and why I should give that one up.
