For the better part of two years a reading group I'm in has been circling the Dennett–Caruso exchange on free will, desert, and punishment without anyone's position so much as bending, and I've come to think the interesting question is not who wins but why the thing doesn't move. The shape of it is this. Dennett, as I read him, grants determinism and then argues that the freedom worth wanting is the freedom of an agent who is responsive to reasons, whose behavior can be shaped by norms and persuasion; on that basis he wants to keep a robust notion of responsibility, blame, and desert, and to say that punishing someone can be justified without any contra-causal gap in the causal order. Caruso grants the same metaphysics and draws the opposite conclusion: once you give up the idea that the wrongdoer could have done otherwise in the libertarian sense, nothing remains that could make her intrinsically deserving of suffering, and the honest response is to stop talking about desert and run our response to wrongdoing the way we run public health — quarantine the dangerous, treat the treatable, and stop pretending the suffering we inflict is owed to anyone. On the surface that's a head-on contradiction about whether desert survives, and yet both men seem to accept the same physics, the same sociology, and even, as far as I can tell, the same prediction about which penal policies actually work.

My working hypothesis — and I want it treated as a hypothesis, not a premise — is that this is a verbal dispute of a non-trivial kind. I've spent enough years in the rationalist corners of the internet that carving a disagreement into its verbal and substantive parts is practically a reflex, which is exactly why I don't trust my own carving here: it feels too cheap. The hypothesis is that Dennett is answering the question what justifies us in blaming and punishing, and his answer is a package of facts about reason-responsiveness and social function; Caruso is answering the question whether any agent ever deserves to suffer in and for itself, and his answer is no. If those are the two questions, then both answers can be true at once and the clash is an artifact of the single word desert doing double duty. One distinction I've been trying to use to pry it open is between desert as fittingness and desert as entitlement — between the claim that suffering is a fitting response to wrongdoing, in the way that gratitude is a fitting response to kindness, and the claim that the wrongdoer has forfeited a claim against being made to suffer. Caruso plainly denies the first; it's much less clear that he denies the second, and it's not clear at all which one Dennett needs. And underneath both is a question I care about more than the taxonomy: do our actual blaming and punishing practices presuppose basic desert, so that if Caruso is right, Dennett's reconstruction is not a rescue of the practice but a replacement of it — a different practice wearing the old name? If that's the crux, then the dispute is neither plainly verbal nor plainly substantive but a disagreement about what our concepts are tracking, which is the sort of thing that can look like a stalemate for a century.

What I want from you is a diagnosis, not a verdict and not a survey — I've read the positions and don't need the compatibilism/incompatibilism taxonomy recited back to me. Tell me what kind of disagreement this actually is: whether my verbal-dispute hypothesis survives contact with what each side says, or whether a substantive disagreement about the conditions of desert is hiding under the verbal one; whether the two of them are answering different questions under one heading, and if so which question each is really answering; whether some hidden empirical or formal claim — about the efficacy of punishment, about the function of the reactive attitudes, about what it takes for a practice to presuppose a proposition — is silently doing the work; and whether the fittingness/entitlement distinction dissolves the impasse, relocates it, or turns out to be another way of stating it. Then, given your diagnosis, say what would count as progress — a distinction, a change of question, an argument that one side's question is the only one in the neighborhood worth asking — or argue that none should be expected, and why. I'd rather have a sharp answer that commits than a survey that lists the escape routes.
