I've been reading rationalist blogs and forums for the better part of a decade, and game theory plus a little causal modeling is the water I swim in: players and payoffs, equilibria, common knowledge, costly signaling, coordination and Schelling points, mechanism design, interventions and do-operators. I'm a competent amateur with that toolkit — I can follow a signaling model or a causal diagram and I can usually tell when someone is dressing a story up as a theorem, but I've never done any of it professionally, and I flinch whenever someone says moral progress, because my instinct is to ask which parameter moved.

There's a historical dispute I only half-understand, and I want it restated in my vocabulary, systematically, with an honest account of what gets lost. The dispute is about why norms like chattel slavery and aristocratic dueling collapsed. Three candidate explanations get offered. First, argument: the moral case against the practice was made, and eventually it convinced people. Second, interest: the economic-interest thesis usually credited to Williams, on which abolition was a cutting of losses — the institution stopped paying, or stopped paying enough to cover its political costs, and the moral case was at best a rationalization that came along for the ride; the economic premise of that thesis is itself contested by later historians, which I take to be part of the point. Third, honor: Appiah's line, that in these cases the arguments were already on the table for a century before anything changed, and what actually moved was honor — the practice went from being something a gentleman could not decline to something a gentleman could not be caught doing.

For each of the three, give me the most faithful restatement you can in the vocabulary above: the players, the action sets, the payoffs, the beliefs, the common knowledge, the signal and what it indexes. Then the residue report, and I want you to adjudicate between three candidates rather than just listing them. (a) Direction of change: my framework can tell me which arrangement is stable, never that the new one is better, so is the realist's claim that abolition was a discovery rather than a switch inexpressible here, or merely unproven? (b) Honor's first-personal grip: the duellist who cannot decline is not maximizing esteem, he is constituted by the practice, and I suspect my payoff slot cannot hold that without circularity. (c) Discontinuity: a moral revolution sounds like a change in the game itself, or in the vocabulary in which actions can be described at all, whereas my framework takes the action space and its descriptions as given and moves parameters inside them.

Finally, my own amateur attempt, to be corrected: model the duel as a costly-signaling equilibrium in which the signal is willingness to bear mortal risk, and treat the collapse as a change in what the signal indexes, the same act flipping from proving you are a gentleman to proving you are a barbarian. Tell me whether that is a translation of the honor story or a substitution for it, and if it is a substitution, what it quietly threw away.

Say how confident you are in each claim you make, and single out the point you are least sure of. I would rather have a hedged answer with the soft spots marked than a confident one.
