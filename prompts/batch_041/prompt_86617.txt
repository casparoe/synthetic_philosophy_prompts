I've been trying to get my head around this thing called the Frege-Geach problem for months now. I'm not a philosopher at all, I just read blogs and encyclopedia entries until my eyes glaze over. The way I've got it: if you think saying 'lying is wrong' is more like booing than like stating a fact -- you're not describing the world, you're letting out an attitude -- then you're in trouble the second the same words turn up inside a bigger sentence. In 'if lying is wrong then telling my sister it's fine to lie is wrong', nobody is booing anything, the words are just sitting there as a condition. And yet the argument lying is wrong / if lying is wrong then telling my sister it's fine is wrong / so telling my sister it's fine is wrong is obviously valid, and validity has to do with meaning, so 'lying is wrong' must mean the same thing in both spots. If the meaning is the attitude expressed, and no attitude is expressed in the conditional, it looks like it can't. So far so bad.

I've collected the fixes I keep running into and I want you to sort them into tiers by how well they actually deal with this -- S/A/B/C/D, or whatever tiers you like, with a sentence or two for each one saying why it landed there, and please be honest if the top tier comes out empty. Roughly: (1) the two-level attitudes one, where the embedded sentence expresses a higher-order attitude, like disapproval of combining a tolerance for lying with a tolerance for the thing in the consequent (Blackburn, I think?); (2) the 'just give it ordinary truth-conditional semantics over special worlds' one, where 'wrong' gets a meaning in terms of worlds that carry a moral parameter or a complete set of norms, so it composes like any other predicate and the expressivist part is only a story about what attitude you express by saying it (Gibbard, maybe); (3) the hybrid one where the sentence has two pieces, a belief about what some ideal code or ideal advisor would prescribe plus a desire-like endorsement of that, and the belief piece is what does the embedding work; (4) the minimalism-about-truth dodge, where truth-talk is so thin that you can let these sentences be plain true or false and it costs you nothing, so the problem dissolves rather than gets solved; (5) the 'it's all pragmatics' move, where the sentence has whatever content it has and the expressivism is about what you're *doing* when you utter it -- endorsing, prescribing, pushing -- not about its meaning; (6) the old imperatives comparison, where 'wrong' is basically like 'don't' and you just need a logic for commands that embeds the way ordinary sentences do.

The one I half came up with on the bus, which I'd like included in the ranking and harshly marked down if it deserves it: treat 'wrong' as an operator on a whole sentence, like 'it must be that' or 'it ought to be that', give it a normal modal-logic semantics with possible worlds, and then shove all the expressing-an-attitude stuff into the pragmatics of asserting the operator-sentence out loud. Does that get anywhere at all, or is it just option 2 or option 5 with extra steps? And while you're at it, tell me if I've mangled any of the six, and whether there's a move I've completely missed that would land in the top tier -- I dont want to find out later that everyone solved this in 2011 and I'm arguing with a ghost.
