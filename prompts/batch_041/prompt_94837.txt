I'm a third-year PhD student in political theory, and I've just spent a fortnight at my partner's family home in Singapore. Her uncle, a retired civil servant, spent most of the last dinner trying to get a straight answer out of me about what I actually study. When I said I work on what makes political authority legitimate, he took it as a challenge and made a case I couldn't answer at the table.

His case runs roughly like this. His parents' generation lived through riots and strikes; his children have never seen a demonstration turn violent. Housing, schools, medicine and the streets all work, and they work because one party has been able to plan in decades rather than in electoral cycles. And people know it — nobody he knows wants the alternative. So what exactly is missing? If a government delivers what people want and the people accept it, it is legitimate; and the further stamp of approval I keep reaching for — a justification that doesn't run through what people actually get and actually accept — is a philosopher's fiction. He was careful not to rest on the fact that people believe it: he said the belief is a symptom, not the ground.

He also offered two analogies I couldn't dispose of quickly. You don't choose your family or your language, and no one thinks you are therefore bound illegitimately, so why should the fact that we never chose this government count against it? And if a firm keeps its workers safe, pays them well, and they stay, you don't ask whether the boss has the right to manage, so why is the state held to a different standard?

My instinct is that something is wrong here, but I can't say what without seeming to beg the question. I'd like you to treat this as an argument and adjudicate it. Which premise is carrying the weight, and what would I have to deny to resist the conclusion? If delivery and acceptance do justify the arrangement, is that justification unconditional, or does it depend on conditions that could lapse — and if they lapse, does the authority lapse with them? Does it matter whether the question is asked from inside the arrangement or from outside it? And do his two analogies survive contact? Give me a verdict rather than a survey of positions; I know the literature well enough to situate a view, and I don't need a reading list — I need the structure of the argument, where it holds and where it breaks, and what, if anything, I can say to him at the next dinner that isn't just a restatement of my prior.
