For several months a reading group I belong to has been arguing about whether machine translation systems understand anything, and we have made no progress whatever. I would like a diagnosis of the impasse, not a verdict on the question. The critique of the field associated with Hofstadter is where we started, but we have long since stopped talking about him and are now just talking past each other.

The two positions, as they are actually argued in the group, are these. The skeptics hold that a system which maps strings to strings by statistical or neural machinery, with no representation of what the words are about and no grasp of the situations the sentences describe, is not translating in any sense that requires understanding; at best it emits text that a competent reader can use as a translation. Their opponents reply that the only evidence anyone has ever had for understanding, in a human or in a machine, is a behavioral profile — producing the right output in the right circumstances, generalizing to novel cases, answering questions about what was said — and that the skeptics, when pressed, always retreat to a further demand (real intentionality, grounding, a particular causal history, consciousness) that is either unavailable for human speakers too or is simply asserted as the difference. Both sides accept the same phenomena, so far as I can tell; they cannot agree on what those phenomena show. Neither side has moved in a year.

I have a hypothesis about the diagnosis, and I would like you to test it rather than adopt it. I suspect the two sides are not really disagreeing about the systems but about what the word 'understanding' is for — whether it names a natural kind of internal state or a functional role — so that the dispute is verbal in the philosophers' sense, but with the twist that the verbal dispute is worth having because the choice of usage tracks a real difference in what we want the concept to do. I am not confident in this, and I would rather be told I am wrong, with reasons, than have it confirmed.

To keep the diagnosis from floating free of the systems, here is a distinction a colleague in computational linguistics proposed, which I would like you to press on. She says these systems may understand at the level of the sentence without understanding at the level of the discourse: given a single sentence, the system can be said to have grasped what it says, but it has no representation of who is speaking to whom, of what has already been said, of what the speaker is doing in saying it, or of what would follow. She offers an operational test — understanding a sentence is being able to answer questions about the situation it describes — and concedes that by that test the systems pass locally and fail globally. I want to know whether her distinction is doing real work or is a way of conceding the skeptics' point while keeping the word.

Two cases in particular, because they are where the argument actually sticks:
(i) a translation that is correct but reached for the wrong reasons — an idiom rendered correctly because that exact phrase pair was memorized, the system failing on a novel idiom that means the same thing;
(ii) a sentence whose correct translation depends on information in the surrounding text rather than in the sentence itself, the kind of thing a human translator resolves by knowing what the passage is about.

Please structure your reply in exactly these sections, in this order:

1. The diagnosis in brief — one paragraph, under 100 words, saying what kind of disagreement this is and what would move it.
2. The impasse, restated — 150-200 words. State the disagreement as sharply as you can, so that it becomes visible what would have to be shown for either side to be forced to concede.
3. What each side is actually committed to — 250-350 words. For each camp: the claims it must accept, the claims it need not accept, and where it is most vulnerable.
4. Diagnosis — 400-600 words. The substance. Classify the disagreement — verbal, factual, a difference in standards of evidence, two camps answering different questions under one heading, or bedrock intuitions that neither side can argue for — and say whether any hidden empirical or formal question is doing the work. Address my hypothesis directly, and say what the sentence-versus-discourse distinction and the two cases above reveal about the structure of the dispute.
5. What would count as progress — 200-300 words. Given your diagnosis, name what would actually move this: a distinction, a test, a formal result, an experiment, or a change of question. If nothing should be expected to move it, argue that instead and say why.
6. Where I am likely wrong — under 150 words. The weakest point in the framing I have given you, stated plainly.
