I retired from physical chemistry about three years ago and have been filling the time with moral philosophy, which has been a humbling experience — forty years of being the person in the room who knew the answer does not prepare you for reading people who disagree with each other for a living. Right now I'm stuck on the "separateness of persons" objection to utilitarianism, and I have a feeling my confusion is actually the interesting part of the topic rather than a sign that I've missed a chapter. So I'm asking you as someone who works on normative ethics and has taught this for years — but please pitch it to a reader who knows what a utility function is and doesn't need the history.

The objection, as it reaches me in every summary I read, goes like this: utilitarianism just totals up everyone's pleasures and pains, and so treats society as if it were one person with one life to live; but persons are separate, each of us is a distinct individual whose life is his own, and therefore a loss to one cannot be canceled out by a gain to another. I can recite that in my sleep. What I can't do is say what work the word "separate" is doing in it. What I want from you is an explanation of the conceptual issue underneath the slogan, not a survey of who said what about it. First: what is the strongest version of the objection — is the complaint that aggregation is a mistake because it presupposes a standpoint no individual actually occupies, or that it licenses treating some people as means to others' ends, or that it violates a constraint on how burdens and benefits may be traded off? Those sound like different complaints to me, and I want to know which one the separateness language is really carrying. Second, and this is what definately nags at me most: a person's life isn't a point either. Any ordinary utilitarian calculation will also make someone's earlier self suffer for the sake of her later self — I worked hard for years for a comfortable retirement — so if aggregation across distinct people is objectionable because it treats many lives as one, why isn't aggregation across the distinct stages of a single life equally objectionable? If there's an asymmetry, I want the argument for it rather than the assertion, because from where I sit the objection looks like it has to say that the very same arithmetic is fine within a life and forbidden across lives, and that needs defending. Third, a diagnostic question: if the objection really comes down to "you can't justify imposing a loss on A for the sake of a gain to B", tell me whether that's a distinct objection to utilitarianism at all, or just a distributive complaint that any theory with a trade-off rule has to answer — the things I've been reading seem to treat those as one objection and I suspect they aren't.

Before you start writing, take a minute to lay out for yourself the candidate readings of "separateness" and decide which one you can actually defend, rather than walking me through all of them and leaving the choice to me. I'd also like you to keep apart what is genuinely settled among people who work on this from what is contested, and where you're giving me one camp's framing, say so rather than presenting it as the consensus — I have no way of telling the difference on my own, and that is precisely what wastes my time. Write it in British English, and finish with a single line reading exactly "Bottom line: <one sentence>" that gives me the crux in a sentence.
