Question 4 (20 marks). Max Black's universe, containing nothing but two exactly similar spheres of chemically pure iron, is the standard test case against the identity of indiscernibles. Use the following stipulated vocabulary throughout, rather than the labels used in the literature.

(i) Two objects are indiscernible just in case they share every purely qualitative property, intrinsic or relational. A property counts as purely qualitative only if it can be specified without mentioning any particular object: 'being a mile from some sphere' qualifies, 'being a mile from sphere A' does not.
(ii) Two objects are weakly discernible just in case they stand to one another in some irreflexive relation — a relation that nothing can bear to itself — whether or not that relation is symmetric.
(iii) The unrestricted identity of indiscernibles says that no two distinct objects differ in any respect at all, properties that mention particular objects included.

(a) State the identity of indiscernibles in the unrestricted and in the purely qualitative readings, and say what is wrong with the unrestricted reading. (5)
(b) Say which of (i) and (ii) apply to the two spheres in Black's universe, and whether the fact that they are weakly discernible gives any reason to think that the purely qualitative reading is false. (8)
(c) If Black's universe is possible, what follows about the modal status of the purely qualitative reading, and what would have to be established for that consequence to follow? (7)

The script reads as follows.

(a) The identity of indiscernibles is the converse of the indiscernibility of identicals. The latter says that if a is identical with b then everything true of a is true of b; the former says that if everything true of a is true of b then a is identical with b. Together they make up Leibniz's Law, the biconditional. Nobody disputes the first half. The second comes in the two readings the question distinguishes. Unrestricted, it says that no two distinct objects differ in any respect at all; that reading is empty, since each object has the property of being identical with itself and not with the other, so two distinct objects differ in that respect automatically. The purely qualitative reading is the interesting one: if two objects share every property that can be specified without mentioning a particular object, there are not two objects but one.

(b) In Black's universe there are exactly two spheres, the same in diameter, temperature, colour and surface texture, arranged symmetrically about the centre of the universe. Nothing in a purely qualitative description of the world distinguishes one from the other: each is the same distance from the centre, each is at a distance from a sphere, each has the same composition. So the spheres are indiscernible in sense (i). They are also weakly discernible in sense (ii), since each stands in the irreflexive relation 'is at a distance from' to the other, and nothing is at a distance from itself. This is what lets us say that the universe contains two spheres rather than one, and it is also why the case does not refute the purely qualitative reading: the spheres do not, after all, share all their properties. Each sphere is at a distance from the other and no sphere is at a distance from itself, so the two of them satisfy different predicates. The purely qualitative reading would be threatened only if the spheres were alike in every respect, and Black's description of the case does not give us that.

(c) If the universe is possible, then the purely qualitative reading is not a necessary truth but a contingent one. It holds of most ordinary objects — two coins or two leaves always differ in some qualitative way — but Black's universe is a case in which it fails, so its truth depends on how things happen to be arranged rather than on logic. What has to be established for this to follow is that the universe is genuinely possible and not merely imaginable; it is easy to describe a contradictory situation without noticing the contradiction. Since I can find no contradiction in the two-sphere description and can imagine the case without difficulty, I take the possibility claim to be secure. Black's own conclusion is the modest one: the identity of indiscernibles, if true at all, must rest on something other than logic — on the nature of space, or on the kind of reason Leibniz appealed to when he argued that God would have no reason to create two indiscernible things.
