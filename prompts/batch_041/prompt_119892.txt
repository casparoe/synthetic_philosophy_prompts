I am grading the midterm for an applied-ethics course, and this unit has become unexpectedly personal: a close friend has spent the past year arranging a surrogacy and an egg-donation procedure across two jurisdictions, one of which prohibits what the other permits, and I have been the person she calls to talk it through. I would like a careful second opinion on one exam answer before I return it.

The question read:

Several European states prohibit surrogacy outright, while several others permit it only where nothing beyond expenses is paid. The United States has no federal regime, and its states run the gamut from a fully commercial market to outright prohibition. Note also that some jurisdictions which permit sperm donation prohibit egg donation.

In about a page, do three things. First, state the strongest objection to commercial surrogacy and the strongest objection to prohibiting it, and say precisely what each claims is wrong. Second, treat the sperm/egg asymmetry as evidence: what does it reveal about the rationale that must actually be doing the work in these prohibitions? Third, assess whether prohibition protects the parties it is meant to protect. No citations — argue from the concepts.

The student wrote:

The central objection to commercial surrogacy is that it commodities women and children: paying a woman for the use of her body treats her reproductive capacity as a thing to be bought, and treats the child as a product made to order. This is the same objection as the charge that surrogacy exploits women, since a market in reproduction will predictably draw in poor women who have no better options, and a transaction driven by economic desperation is by definition a commodifying one. Prohibitionists conclude that the only way to prevent commodification is to ban the practice.

Against this, the strongest objection to prohibition is that it does not stop the practice, only relocates it. Because intended parents can travel, a ban in one country shifts demand to permissive jurisdictions, where the surrogate is typically poorer and less well protected than she would have been at home. A ban may therefore worsen the position of the very women it claims to protect.

The sperm/egg asymmetry shows that the real rationale for prohibition is the welfare of the child rather than the treatment of the woman. If the objection were to the commodification of gametes as such, the law would prohibit paid sperm donation too; since it does not, the law must be aimed at something else, and the most plausible candidate is the child's interest in being conceived within a family rather than to order. This is confirmed by the fact that jurisdictions prohibiting egg donation also tend to restrict surrogacy, since both produce a child whose origins are split between providers.

I conclude that prohibition fails on its own terms: it neither reduces the number of such arrangements nor improves the position of the women who enter them, and its stated rationale of protecting the child is not one that a ban can deliver.

I am marking out of 20, weighting conceptual precision more heavily than coverage. Please give me a grade and then the specific comments that justify it: separate what the answer gets right from what it gets wrong, name any distinction it collapses or any step that does not follow, and tell me which of the errors, if any, is load-bearing for the overall argument rather than merely local. I would rather have three precise comments than a general impression.
