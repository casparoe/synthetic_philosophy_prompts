I am in my third year of a philosophy degree and writing an essay for a seminar on intentionality. We have spent the past few weeks on Searle's Chinese room and on his exchange with Dennett over the two-bitser — the vending-machine device that reliably sorts genuine coins from slugs, which Searle treats as a case of merely derived, observer-relative intentionality, in contrast with the intrinsic kind that our own coin-recognising exhibits, and which Dennett (if I have followed the dialectic correctly) treats as showing that the original/derived distinction is not a distinction in kind but a matter of the ancestry of the content, and that in any case we have no way of identifying content except by whether the intentional stance predicts well. My tutor has asked us to test a position of this sort against a historical case rather than against one more thought experiment, and the case I have chosen is Clever Hans. Please take the following as given rather than looking it up. In the years around 1900, a horse in Berlin owned by a gymnasium mathematics teacher named Wilhelm von Osten appeared to add, subtract, multiply, divide, tell the time and keep a calendar, answering by tapping his hoof. A commission of thirteen convened in 1904, with Carl Stumpf among its members, examined the horse and concluded that no trickery was involved. Then Pfungst, Stumpf's assistant, ran the controlled trials — blinders on and off, questioners other than the owner, questioners who did and did not already know the answer — and found that Hans answered correctly about nine times in ten when he could see a questioner who knew the answer and hardly ever otherwise, and that the questioner's posture and facial tension changed as the taps approached the right number and released at the correct tap. Von Osten appears to have been entirely sincere, the cues appear to have been involuntary, and there is no reason to think the horse could do arithmetic.

What I want is an analysis that runs in both directions, not a summary of the two views. Take each in turn and say what it must say about Hans. On Searle's account the horse is a two-bitser with legs: the appearance of arithmetic is content we confer on a bit of conditioning, the trainer's expectations are doing the work, and nothing in the horse's head has the content twenty-seven. On Dennett's account the ascription fails, but not because some ingredient is missing — it fails because the intentional stance stops earning its keep, since the hoof-taps are better predicted from the questioner's posture than from any beliefs about numbers. Then tell me where the case embarrasses both. The content twenty-seven is not present anywhere in the episode: not in von Osten's intentions, since he was trying to test the horse and not to tell it the answer; not in the intentions of any designer, since nobody designed the horse's cue-reading; and not in any selection history of the sort that is supposed to fix the content of a frog's fly-detector, since what the horse is doing is learning, in real time, to read the involuntary behaviour of a particular man. If derived intentionality means content borrowed from a mind that already has it, Hans does not look like a case of derived intentionality at all, and the two-bitser analogy breaks down exactly where the designer's intention was supposed to supply the content. Nor does the debunking remove the mentality so much as relocate it: reading postural micro-cues in real time is a genuine perceptual and social achievement. So the question becomes whether either position can say that the content of the horse's state is fixed by a learning history rather than by an intention or by selection, or whether the case demands a third notion of content that neither supplies.

Finally, I want the methodological afterlife brought in, because I suspect it cuts against both of them. After Pfungst, the Clever Hans effect becomes a standing reason for blind and double-blind procedures in animal cognition research, and that norm seems to presuppose something quite strong — that content can travel from experimenter to subject without anyone intending to transmit it and without either party representing what is transmitted. Say whether that presupposition is coherent, which of the two views can accommodate it, and what it implies for the way we now describe machines whose competence comes from training rather than from anyone's plan. Argue to a verdict rather than surveying the literature; I do not need citations or a reading list, I can find those myself, and if I have the two-bitser example or Dennett's reply wrong, correct me in passing rather than making that the subject of the essay.
