I'm a fourth-year PhD student in philosophy of mind, and I've been roped into writing the take-home midterm for the grad consciousness seminar this term because the professor is away at a workshop that week. I want one essay question on heterophenomenology, and I specifically want to avoid the version where students just recite that Dennett tells you to bracket the metaphysics and treat the reports as data, then coast to an A. The crux I want them to actually feel is whether the method can deliver on its own promise of neutrality — and I think the cleanest way to force that is to make them sit with a tension the method seems to carry: it needs the subject's reports to be authoritative about the content of what's reported (otherwise there's no material to build the world-as-the-subject-takes-it-to-be out of), while at the same time denying those same reports any authority about the nature of the states doing the reporting. So the question should make them say what heterophenomenology is actually committed to and whether that commitment is stable, or whether it quietly imports a deflationary / illusionist picture of consciousness — or, the other horn, whether a genuinely neutral version of it collapses into ordinary third-person cognitive psychology of report-generation and stops being a method for consciousness at all. I also want them to fold in the unreliable-introspection and confabulation material and say what it does to the whole treating-reports-as-a-text analogy: if the text is partly confabulated, is that a problem for the heterophenomenologist or actually a resource for them?

Please write the essay question itself (pitched at roughly 90 minutes of writing), a model answer of about 700 words, and a marking scheme with something like five must-hit moves, the marks each is worth (total 20), and one line per move on how students typically blow it. Two things I care about: keep the question self-contained, since they won't have the library open and I don't want it to hinge on anyone remembering a particular paper or who said what when; and in the model answer and the marking notes, keep what's genuinely settled separate from what's actually contested — for instance, don't write as though everyone who knows the area agrees about what the deflationary reading is or whether Dennett really meant it, because that's exactly one of the live disputes, and the neutrality claim is itself contested rather than a fixed premise.
