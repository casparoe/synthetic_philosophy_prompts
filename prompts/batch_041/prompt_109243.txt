Write a dialogue of roughly 1,400 words between two fictional interlocutors who have been appointed as external advisers to a working group revising a psychiatric diagnostic manual. They are arguing before drafting their joint note on whether a particular category should be retained, and their disagreement is about what follows from the fact that classified people often come to resemble their classification.

Give the two characters the following positions, and make both of them competent, informed and serious:

The first is a psychiatric epidemiologist who holds that looping effects, properly understood, are not an embarrassment to classification but one of the mechanisms by which a category can earn its keep. On this view, when a label and the people it picks out settle into a stable relationship over time, that stability is evidence that the category is carving something at a joint, not evidence that it has manufactured its own object. The epidemiologist should be able to explain why this is more than wishful thinking, and should have an account of how you would tell a category that converges on a coherent syndrome from one that merely converges on a convenient stereotype.

The second is a philosopher of science who holds that a category stabilised by looping is warranted only locally, and only for as long as the loop keeps running. The philosopher's central claim is that the relevant difference is not between social and natural kinds — plenty of natural kinds are shaped by human practice — but between loops that close through shared causes and loops that close through shared descriptions. Only the former yield inductive warrant that travels outside the classifying community, and psychiatric categories often cannot be shown to be of the former sort. The philosopher should be prepared to say what follows for practice, and should not retreat into the claim that all classification is arbitrary.

The dialogue must contain genuine back-and-forth: each turn should engage with the specific claim made in the previous turn rather than restating a position. Neither character should be a strawman, and neither should win. Include at least one moment where one of them concedes something that materially shifts the argument, and at least one moment where they consider whether their dispute is merely verbal, before explaining why it is not — or leaving it genuinely open.

Put the following distinctions to work in the dialogue. The first two are standard fare and may be treated as shared background; the remaining three are to be introduced as the characters' own analytical tools, and should be presented as proposals under dispute rather than as established results:

- the difference between interactive kinds, whose members are aware of how they are classified, and kinds whose members are not;
- the point that looping can run against the grain, with diagnosed people deliberately refusing the profile the category assigns them, and the question of what that resistance shows;
- looping latency: the lag between a label's introduction and any change in the people labelled, and the suggestion that short latency indicates a category that was already tracking something while long latency indicates one that is doing the tracking's work itself — together with the counter-argument that latency may reflect nothing more than the speed at which institutions transmit labels;
- the distinction between synchronic and diachronic inductive warrant: whether looping threatens only our ability to generalise across populations, or also our ability to generalise from the population we studied to the same population a decade later;
- the point that the instruments of measurement — structured interviews, rating scales, screening questionnaires — are themselves calibrated on populations already shaped by earlier rounds of the loop, so that the measurement may be reading back the history of the category rather than the features of the patient.

Neither character may cite a study, appeal to a literature, name a researcher, or give a date. Historical episodes may be alluded to in general terms, but without attribution or chronology. Where a concrete case is needed, prefer a hypothetical that the characters explicitly mark as hypothetical, and construct it in enough detail that the argument can actually turn on it. The characters may refer to the idea of looping effects, but should not quote anyone or attribute particular formulations to particular authors.

Close the dialogue with the two of them disagreeing not about whether looping effects occur, but about what evidence would settle their disagreement — and let it be clear why each thinks the other's proposed evidence would be question-begging.

Write in British English and in a formal register. Use speech labels for the two characters and keep stage directions sparse.
