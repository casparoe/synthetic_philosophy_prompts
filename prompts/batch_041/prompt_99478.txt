I have been reading about the problem-solving courts that many jurisdictions now run—the so-called drug courts, in which a person convicted of a drug offense is placed under the direct and repeated supervision of a single judge rather than simply sent away. The judge meets the participant every week or two, reviews their drug tests, praises them when they stay clean, and imposes escalating sanctions when they relapse; compliance is rewarded, non-compliance is met with consequences, and the whole thing is described as treatment rather than punishment. What I cannot make sense of is that the same courtroom seems to speak two incompatible languages at once. When the participant does well, the judge sounds like a moralist: she tells them they are finally taking responsibility for their life, that they have chosen to change, that they should be proud. When they relapse, the judge is disappointed, sometimes angry, and asks for an account. Yet the entire apparatus rests on a clinical picture of addiction as a chronic, relapsing condition with a substantial biological component—a condition the participant did not choose to have—and the sanctions are calibrated less to desert than to shaping a learned response, in the way one shapes the behavior of anyone whose conduct is to be managed rather than answered.

I would like you to reconstruct what this practice presupposes. Strawson, in his essay on freedom and resentment, drew a distinction between the participant attitude—the stance in which we resent, blame, forgive, and demand accounts, treating a person as a fellow member of the moral community—and the objective attitude, in which we regard someone as an object to be predicted, managed, and trained, as we do a small child or a person in the grip of a serious psychological abnormality. Does the drug court presuppose that the addict is a proper target of the participant attitude, of the objective attitude, or of some hybrid; and if hybrid, is the mixture coherent, or is it merely institutional improvisation that would survive under almost any theory? Does the practice require a compatibilist conception of responsibility—roughly, that the participant is answerable because their conduct is responsive to reasons, even though the addiction is not their fault—or does it instead presuppose that attributions of responsibility are a matter of what it is useful to say rather than what is true? And is the court's switching between the two registers a principled response to the participant's fluctuating capacity, in the way we blame a person more when they are most themselves and excuse them when they are not, or does that switching reveal the practice as underdetermined, such that a purely clinical institution and a purely retributive one could each run it without altering a single procedure? Please separate what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with, and say what would have to change if the presupposed view were replaced by a rival.
