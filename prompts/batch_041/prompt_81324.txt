For several weeks now, a colleague in our decision-theory reading group has been pressing an analogy that I find both attractive and suspicious, and I would like you to audit it rather than adjudicate the underlying dispute. Her line is that the expected value of a gamble is just its long-run average payoff, and equivalently the price at which the gamble would be fair, so that when a gamble has no expected value this is not a defect in our theory of rational choice but a fact about the gamble — much as a lottery with no ticket price is not something we are failing to price. She then produces the Pasadena game: a fair coin is tossed until it lands heads, and if heads first appears on the nth toss the player receives 2^n/n dollars when n is odd and pays 2^n/n dollars when n is even. Each outcome has probability 2^−n, so the signed terms run 1, −1/2, 1/3, −1/4, and so on; the series converges when summed in order of increasing n, yet the positive terms alone diverge and the negative terms alone diverge. The game is thus perfectly well-defined — the coin, the stopping rule, and the payoff schedule are beyond reproach — while no expectation exists in the sense that would license a fair price. Her conclusion is that the case is no counterexample to expected-utility theory, since a theory is entitled to silence where a quantity fails to exist.

What I want from you is a full audit of the analogy, written in the spirit of a referee report that would also be useful to graduate students. Begin with the mapping: state explicitly what corresponds to what — the long-run average, the fair price, the well-definedness of the gamble, the failure of the limit, and the alleged silence of the theory — and say which of these correspondences are load-bearing and which are decoration. Then the failures of the mapping. Where does the long-run-average gloss misdescribe the situation, given that in the St. Petersburg case the expectation is infinite while the empirical averages of actual plays creep upward without settling, and given that in the Pasadena case the partial sums of the payoff series depend on the order in which the outcomes are summed? Does the failure of a limit here resemble having no value at all, or does it instead resemble something else — a quantity that is not well-defined, a quantity that is infinite, or an object whose value depends on how it is decomposed? Next, what the analogy quietly imports: which assumptions about the supervenience of a gamble's value on its payoff distribution, about the order-independence or linearity of value, and about the difference between a theory's silence and a theory's verdict are smuggled in by the fair-price picture. Finally, the verdict I actually care about: does her conclusion survive once the disanalogies are on the table, and if it does not, what would be a better analogy for what is happening here? I would like at least one candidate replacement, with its own limits stated candidly. Please write in American English, and do not give me reading lists, citations, or bibliography — I want the conceptual audit itself.
