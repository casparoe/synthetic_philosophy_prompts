At a departmental workshop last week, a colleague ended a dispute about a proposed policy — one that would impose a modest but genuine burden on a small number of people in order to secure a large aggregate benefit for a much larger group — by declaring flatly that "the numbers don't count." I am a doctoral student working on contractualism, and I suspect the phrase is being asked to carry more argumentative weight than any single thesis can bear. I would like a systematic inventory of what that one-liner could actually be claiming.

Please present the readings as a menu, ordered from the innocuous to the radical. For each, state the precise claim, classify it as a platitude, as a substantive and defensible position, or as simply false, and indicate what would have to be true for it to hold. At a minimum I want the following possibilities separated:

1. A bookkeeping reading, on which the slogan denies only that numbers enter as an independent term in a sum, leaving open that they matter in every other way.
2. An evidentiary reading, on which numbers may legitimately shift one's confidence or the burden of justification but never determine the verdict.
3. A complaint-magnitude reading, on which numbers matter only through the magnitude of individual complaints, so that any number of small complaints can never jointly outweigh a single large one.
4. A homogeneity reading, on which numbers can matter, but only when the complaints at issue are of the same kind and magnitude, so that aggregation across heterogeneous complaints is blocked.
5. A persons-not-arithmetic reading, on which the slogan is a claim about the separateness of persons rather than about sums, denying that there is any standpoint from which benefits and burdens are pooled.
6. The strong reading, on which numbers are wholly irrelevant to permissibility, whatever the consequences.

For each reading, say which one my colleague would have to have meant for his dismissal to be warranted, whether that reading is one he could actually defend, and which reading the people who habitually repeat the phrase most likely intend. I am especially interested in whether the most defensible reading can be turned against the contractualist framework it is usually recruited to support: contractualism appears to need some account of why it matters that many people each lodge a complaint rather than one, and I want to know whether the slogan illuminates that need or merely restates the conclusion — and whether it can be squared with the idea that an act is wrong when it is disallowed by principles that no one could reasonably reject. Finally, please test the readings against a pair of cases: one in which a great many people each face a small but genuine complaint, and one in which a single person faces a very serious complaint. Say which readings treat the two as commensurable and which do not, and be explicit about where the slogan is doing independent philosophical work and where it is only announcing a verdict.
