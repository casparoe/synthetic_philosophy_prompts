After years away from it, I have recently begun to pray again, largely because someone close to me is seriously ill, and I have found myself unsure what I am actually asking for. I have settled — provisionally — on a particular understanding of divine omnipotence, and I want to work out where it leaves the practice of petitionary prayer.

The understanding is this. Omnipotence is not the capacity to do absolutely anything whatever, but the capacity to do anything that is genuinely a thing to be done: any state of affairs whose description does not collapse into contradiction. On this reading, a stone so heavy that even an omnipotent being could not lift it resembles the largest prime number — not a task that outruns a power, but a phrase that fails to pick out any object at all, so that the inability to produce one is no more a limitation than the inability to draw a square circle. I take this to be the traditional reply rather than a concession of weakness, though I suspect it may be doing less work than it appears to.

Two worries nag at me. The first is circularity: if what counts as a contradiction is itself somehow up to God, the restriction threatens to become empty; if it is not up to God, then something constrains God from outside, which the view was meant to avoid. The second is that the restriction may license too much, letting any failure be redescribed after the fact as a contradiction. I would like you to keep both worries in view as you go.

Please set out the most important implications of this view for how I should pray. Address at least the following: prayers about events already past; prayers for outcomes that might be impossible in the sense above; prayers that another person choose or act differently; and prayers for healing that, if granted, would look like the suspension of ordinary natural regularities. I also want to know what the view implies about whether prayers that seem unanswered should count as evidence against God's power or goodness, and about the difference between God being unable to grant something and God being able but declining. Tell me which of these implications matters most, and why. End by saying whether any of it should change the way I actually pray.
