I'm designing a two-hour workshop for a mixed group of about twenty graduate students — half of them from ecology and evolutionary biology, half from philosophy of science — and the session is on the species problem. I will not assign any reading beforehand, so everything has to be built in the room. What I want from you is not the explanation itself but a plan for teaching it: the order in which the pieces must come, the single case that carries the whole session, the misconceptions to expect and exactly where in the sequence each gets headed off, and how I will know at the end that they have understood the problem rather than merely heard about it.

Constraints on the plan. First, the dependency order has to be argued rather than asserted: tell me which distinctions must already be in place before the question "how many species are there here?" can even be well formed. My own guess is that the difference between species as a rank and species as a particular segment of a lineage, and the difference between a criterion for sorting things and a definition of what is being sorted, both have to land early, and that the audience cannot feel the force of the problem until they have watched the same population sorted two ways under two defensible criteria. If that guess is wrong, say so and put the real first move first.

Second, the carrying example. I don't want the usual textbook menagerie — no named ring species, no finches, no bacteria. I want to build the case on a slide from stipulations alone, so that nothing in the session depends on facts anyone has to remember: seven populations of a freshwater snail along a river, numbered from downstream, each adjacent pair interbreeding where they meet, the first and seventh never meeting, a narrow hybrid zone between the third and fourth, two populations that are morphologically indistinguishable but will not interbreed in the lab, and two that look strikingly different and interbreed freely. Tell me what the example must have, and what it must deliberately lack, for the session to work; whether my stipulations give it that; and what you would change. Then tell me why this beats the standard examples pedagogically, and where it would mislead if I lean on it too hard.

Third, the misconceptions, in the order they will actually arise, with the precise point in the sequence at which each should be headed off rather than merely corrected. I expect at least these: that "species" is a convenient label with nothing in nature answering to it; that the trouble is that we have not yet found the right definition and that one day we will; that if species are not natural kinds then conservation listings are arbitrary; that interbreeding is the definition of a species rather than one operational test among several; that the problem is purely verbal and therefore doesn't matter; and the opposite error, that it is purely metaphysical and so has no bearing on what an agency should do on Monday. Tell me which of these are genuine misconceptions rather than positions someone may hold after understanding the problem, where that distinction matters, and which half of the room is likely to arrive holding which.

Fourth, one question I can put to the room at the end that would separate someone who has understood the problem from someone who has merely learned the vocabulary.

Fifth, what I should explicitly refuse to cover in two hours, and how to say so without making the omission look like evasion.

Finally, don't hand me a menu. Take a definite position on the order, on the example, and on the underlying question — whether the species problem is a genuine problem, a knot of several genuine problems, or one that dissolves once the questions are separated — and give your reasons, including what would have to be true for your own position to be wrong. No citations and no reading suggestions; I need a plan I can teach from cold.
