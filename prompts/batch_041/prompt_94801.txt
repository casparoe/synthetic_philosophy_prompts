I'm a novelist, about a third of the way into a book set in a farming village of my own invention — late prehistoric in its technology, but deliberately not identified with any real site or culture, since I would rather invent than misattribute. Some way in, I understood that the thing I actually need is not facts about the period but the reasoning that carries anyone from mute objects to the beliefs and institutions that produced them, because everything my characters believe has to reach a reader through nothing but what they leave behind. So when a friend who teaches in an MFA program asked me to take one session of her seminar, I proposed exactly that subject: how archaeologists, and their critics, argue from objects to minds — from a scatter of refuse to a rule, a rank, a rite, an office — and where those arguments are strong and where they merely sound strong. The students will be fiction writers with no archaeology; the session is two hours with slides; and I do not want the lecture, I want the plan for giving it. Take the role of a methodologically careful archaeologist who has read enough philosophy of the social sciences to distrust his or her own inferences, and think the whole thing through before you commit to an answer — open with a short brainstorm of the possible readings of my example below, then give me the plan.

What has to be in place before the question "what did they believe?" is even well formed for these students, and in what order must the pieces come so that each one lands on ground already prepared? Which single example should carry the two hours, and what must it have and lack? I have one I would like you to test: a settlement of some twenty households in which every house has a small stamped clay token sealed into its grain store; one larger building, rebuilt many times on the same footprint, contains hundreds of the same tokens, none of them used; the stamp motif changes once, in a single season, simultaneously across all the houses; and in the one house that keeps its tokens in a different room from everyone else, there is also the settlement's only imported stone. Tell me what can and cannot be inferred from that — a central authority, redistribution, a household rite, a merely scribal habit, something I have not thought of — and whether it can bear the weight I am putting on it, or whether you would replace it and why. Then map the misconceptions they will arrive with or form, and the exact point in the sequence where each should be headed off rather than merely flagged. I expect "no temple means no religion," "if it has no obvious use it must be ritual," and a general faith that a close enough analogy to some living society settles the matter; I would like the ones I have not named as well, including whatever they will pick up from me, since a novelist's instinct is to convert every inference into a motive. Give me the question to ask at the end that would show me whether they have the logic rather than the vocabulary, and tell me what I should openly refuse to cover at this level, with the reason stated out loud.

Finally, I would like you to be candid about your own footing: say how confident you are in each of your main claims, and single out the one point you are least sure of and what would move it. No reading lists or citations, please — I have no way to check them in the room and neither will they; if an idea needs a name, give me the idea and let the name go.
