I'm putting together a little collection of short stories that each stage a philosophical puzzle, and the one I'm stuck on is the statue and the clay. You know the setup: a sculptor makes a statue out of a lump of bronze, and now it looks like there are two objects — the statue and the lump — in the exact same place, made of the same stuff, but with different properties, since the lump could get squashed back into a ball and survive that while the statue couldn't. I want a story where that actually matters to people, not just a thought experiment in a seminar room.

Here's the scenario I've been kicking around, use it or something close to it. A small foundry town has a bronze war memorial — a soldier on a plinth, unveiled in 1921, commemorating a war the town would honestly rather forget. Over the decades the thing has been through hell: shell damage in the 40s patched by welding on new bronze, a bad 70s restoration that stripped the original patina, and now the council want to melt the whole figure down and recast it from the same metal, because it's corroded and, frankly, they want it looking new for the centenary. The wrinkle: the original donor's will left the statue to the town, and the descendents turn up to argue that if you melt it down and pour it again, whatever's standing there afterward is a replica — a new lump of bronze — and the thing that was left to the town doesn't exist anymore. Meanwhile the foundry foreman, a bit of an amatuer philosopher, keeps insisting there was never one object there to begin with: there's the bronze, there's the shape, there's the statue, there's the statue-minus-the-1948-patch, and on it goes.

Write me the short story. I want it doing two jobs at once: being a real story, with characters and a conflict and a scene where somebody actually has to decide something, and genuinely staging the metaphysics — the constitution view, where the statue and the lump are two distinct things that coincide, against the view that says that's just nonsense and there's one object under different descriptions. Don't have the characters lecture each other; let the argument fall out of the stakes. And please don't give me Theseus's ship with a new coat of paint — the question I care about is coincidence, not gradual replacement, so keep the patching and recasting in the background where it belongs.

At the end, tack on a short note, a paragraph or two, about where the story takes liberties with the philosophy or leans on a contested reading — like whether "two objects in one place" is really the going version of the constitution view or a caricature of it, and whether the will-and-court framing actually tracks the metaphysical question or just muddies it. And if you think this setup can't pull off both jobs, or that I've rigged the scenario so the story is bound to come out one way, just tell me straight rather than writing around it.
