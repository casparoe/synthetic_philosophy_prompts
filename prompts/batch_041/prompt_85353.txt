I'm a software engineer by training, with no formal background in philosophy, and I sit as the non-scientist member on my university's animal care and use committee. Next Thursday we vote on a protocol that has quietly turned into a policy decision. A lab wants to run avoidance-learning experiments on bumblebees: foragers are trained to trade a sugar reward against landing on a heated platform, and a second cohort is offered a choice between plain and analgesic-laced feeders after exposure to the heat. Our institution's welfare policy assigns every taxon to one of three protection tiers — full review with mandatory anaesthesia and analgesia, review plus a monitoring plan, or notification only — and instructs us to set the tier by counting how many of four "indicators" are documented for the taxon: (i) motivational trade-offs between a noxious stimulus and a valued reward; (ii) self-administration of an analgesic when injured; (iii) flexible avoidance learning that generalises beyond the training context; and (iv) modulation of the noxious response by the animal's overall state, such as hunger or stress. Three or four indicators means the top tier, one or two the middle, none the bottom, "unless the committee resolves otherwise." Bees are not currently listed at any tier, and the count for them is genuinely ambiguous: each of the four indicators is contested in the material I've been handed, and nobody on the committee agrees whether "contested" should be scored as present or absent.

I'd like you to act as an expert in animal cognition and the philosophy of mind and draft the short written opinion I'll circulate before the vote. Please begin by laying out the live options before committing to one, and then recommend a specific tier for the protocol, the conditions (if any) that should attach to approval, and a date by which the committee should revisit the classification — I need a bottom line the chair can put to a vote, not a survey. The strongest objection on one side comes from our physiologist, who argues that these indicators are all behavioural proxies that any learning animal would pass, so the scheme measures learning rather than suffering and will eventually tier everything with a nervous system; the strongest objection on the other comes from our graduate student representative, who argues that the indicators were written so that uncertainty always counts against protection, and that the honest response to doubt about whether a creature can suffer is to extend protection rather than withhold it. Give both objections their due rather than simply landing on the cautious answer.

Finally, please take the underlying conceptual questions seriously, because I keep getting tangled in them and I suspect the committee does too. Is "sentience" the sort of thing that can be distributed across taxa by a checklist at all, or does the graded-tier scheme risk a category error — treating a yes-or-no question about a creature's inner life as a matter of degree? Does the fact that all our evidence here is indirect and behavioural make the case for bee sentience weak, or merely make it like every other case, including the human one? And when the evidence is genuinely unresolved, should that push the tier up or down — is the asymmetry between the costs of a false positive and a false negative the decisive consideration, or does that way of thinking smuggle in a moral premise the committee has never agreed to? I won't have time to do any reading before Thursday, so please make the memo self-contained and skip the bibliography; argue it in your own words.
