I teach philosophy at a mid-sized state university, and something happened last month that I can't shake. A former student of mine -- bright guy, went into consulting straight out of college, by his mid-thirties he's pulling down more than I'll earn in my whole career -- came back to campus and asked me, over terrible coffee, whether he'd wasted his life. He's healthy, he's got a partner he loves, money is a non-issue. But he says he feels like he spent fifteen years climbing a ladder and only now noticed it's leaning against the wrong wall, and he wanted me to tell him what to do with himself now. I panicked and gave him some mush about work-life balance, and he left visibly disappointed, which is fair.

What I keep reaching for and can't quite get out is Keynes. Around 1930 he wrote this essay, Economic Possibilities for Our Grandchildren, predicting that within about a century the brute problem of scarcity -- having to work in order to survive -- would basically be licked, and that we'd then face our real and permanent problem, which isn't making a living but figuring out how to use the freedom: how to fill the hours, how to live well instead of just continuing to accumulate. He was withering about the love of money as a possession, treating it as a kind of semi-pathological hang-up rather than a virtue, and he guessed we'd probably botch the transition and keep clinging to greed and overwork for generations. He thought that once people were free of necessity the things that mattered were stuff like friendship, art, beauty, and using your time wisely. I'm flattening it, I know, but thats the shape of what I want to bring to this kid.

So here's what I'd like from you. Put on your philosopher hat -- think of yourself as someone who really knows both Keynes's moral vision and the contemporary philosophy of well-being, and go slow and careful here, because I do not want to hand him a Victorian sermon or a LinkedIn platitude. Start by brainstorming what the question he's actually asking even is; my hunch is it isn't what job should I take but something about what makes a life go well once the money is handled, and I suspect Keynes is only one input among several. Then give me the strongest steel-manned version of that Keynesian picture -- where it's genuinely right, and where it's too optimistic, paternalistic, or just wrong (I'm skeptical of anyone who tells a 35-year-old to quit and paint). Finally, draft the actual thing I should say to him, in my voice, concrete and non-preachy, that takes his real situation seriously rather than lecturing him. And please flag the one piece of advice I'll be tempted to give that would probably do more harm than good.
