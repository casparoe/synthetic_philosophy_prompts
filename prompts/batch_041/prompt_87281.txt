I am a features writer who covers philosophy and the behavioral sciences, and I have spent much of this year reporting on a running dispute among value theorists over whether "better than" is transitive. The dispute usually surfaces through the family of cases people call spectrum arguments: a chain of options running from a small number of people living very well to an enormous number of people whose lives are only barely worth living, arranged so that each adjacent step looks like an improvement while the far end of the chain looks plainly worse than the beginning. I have interviewed enough people on both sides by now that I know the shape of the standard replies, so what I need from you is not a survey of the debate but a sustained argument.

The position I would like you to defend is one I have been hearing from a researcher I have been following, who works out of what I can only describe as a comparison-class account of betterness. On this view, a judgment that one thing is better than another is never made in a vacuum. It is always made relative to a set of alternatives that are genuinely available to the agent, a menu, in her idiom, and the standard of comparison is partly fixed by what is on that menu. Transitivity, on this account, holds within any single menu, because within a fixed menu there is a stable background against which options can be ranked; but it need not hold across menus, and the spectrum arguments are, on her reading, chains in which each link is evaluated against a different background, so that the appearance of contradiction is an artifact of sliding from one comparison class to another. I would like you to make the strongest case for this position that can be made. Assume a reader who knows the standard moves and will be annoyed by hand-waving: explain what work the notions of a menu and a comparison class are doing, why shifting from one to another is not simply a change of subject, and how the account handles the steps in the spectrum that seem to involve no menu at all, the ones where we are asked to compare two states of the world and nothing else. Take seriously the objection I find most interesting, namely that if betterness is transitive only relative to a menu, then it is no longer clear what the word "better" is tracking, and the spectrum argument's conclusion, that the far end of the chain is worse than the start, loses the very relation it was trading on. Say what survives of our ordinary talk of one option being better than another under this account, and what you take to be genuinely lost by adopting it.

Please write this in American English, in continuous prose of roughly 900 to 1,200 words, with no citations, bibliography, or review of who has said what; I want the argument itself, developed in your own voice, not a map of the literature.
