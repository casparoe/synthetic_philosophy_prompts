Two philosophers of biology are the sole panelists at a small, invitation-only workshop on whether biological function-talk is inescapably historical. The first defends an etiological (selection-history) account, on which a trait's function is fixed by the effect for which it was selected or that explains its persistence. The second defends a causal-role (systemic) account, on which a trait's function is the contribution it currently makes to some capacity of the system containing it, with history playing no essential role in the ascription. The organizer has ruled that the two will spend the session working through the same concrete cases rather than trading programmatic statements, and that neither may cite published literature or appeal to the authority of named authors; they must argue from the accounts themselves and from the cases as described. (The rule is deliberate: the workshop is meant to test whether either account can be defended from the ground up.)

The three cases:

1. Sticky hairs. A marine crustacean bears hairs on its feeding limbs that, in the ancestral population, filtered plankton from the water. In a newly founded population, a mutation has made the hairs adhesive; they now entangle and kill the drifting larvae of a competing species, and the population's survival has improved markedly. Does the hair now have the function of killing competitors' larvae, or is the killing merely a side effect of a trait whose function remains filtration? Does the answer change for the filtration role, which itself has not changed?

2. The obsolete keel. An island bird, flightless for many generations, retains a sternal keel. In its flying ancestors the keel anchored the flight muscles; today it anchors nothing and is a byproduct of a developmental constraint, mildly costly because it competes for calcium. Does the keel have a function? If not, what is the causal-role theorist to say about the many traits in physiology and medicine that are routinely called functional although they no longer contribute to survival?

3. The second vessel. A small worm has two contractile vessels. The second is a duplication that was fixed by drift in a very small population; only afterward did it begin to assist circulation, and it now does so in a way that marginally improves survival. At the time the duplication spread, there was no selection for circulation. Does the second vessel have the function of assisting circulation? The etiological theorist owes an account of when a contribution becomes a function; the causal-role theorist owes an account of whether the vessel was functional even before it contributed anything, and of what distinguishes a function from a mere effect.

Write the dialogue. Requirements:

- Both speakers are equally competent; neither is a strawman. Each must engage the other's strongest formulation of their position, and must respond to the specific points actually made rather than restating their own view.
- Each speaker must at some point concede something specific — a case that sits badly with their account, or a cost they are willing to pay — and must say what they give up in conceding it.
- They must reach a provisional verdict on each of the three cases, even if the verdict is that the two accounts deliver different answers.
- They must explicitly address whether their disagreement is substantive or merely terminological (two different senses of one word), and attempt to settle that question rather than leaving it hanging.
- No citations, no named literature, no invented quotations. Roughly 1,000-1,500 words.

After the dialogue, provide a structured summary as a single JSON object, with exactly these fields:

- "case_verdicts": an array of three objects, one per case, each with "case" (short name), "etiological_verdict" (one or two sentences), "causal_role_verdict" (one or two sentences), and "status" (one of "agreement", "terminological", "substantive").
- "hardest_case": an object with "case", "speaker" (which account found it hardest), and "why".
- "concessions": an array of objects with "speaker" and "point_conceded".
- "disagreement_type": one of "substantive", "terminological", or "mixed", with a one-sentence justification.
- "unresolved_question": the single question the dialogue leaves most pressing, stated in one sentence.
