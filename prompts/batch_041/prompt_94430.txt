I run a fortnightly reading group on AI safety — mixed bunch, mostly third-year philosophy and CS students, sixteen of us, and I've got the full 90 minutes next Thursday. We've done outer alignment to death (reward misspecification, Goodhart's law, all the usual) and they can recite it in their sleep, so I want a session where they actually *feel* inner alignment / goal misgeneralisation from the inside rather than hearing me define it. My plan is a paper-only game, no laptops, where they play the trainers: they design a training environment for a small gridworld agent (walls, a thing to collect, that sort of thing), train it on logs of what a demonstrator did plus a score at the end of each level, validate it, and then I deploy it somewhere else. The failure has to come out of *their own* choices — I don't want to hand them a rigged board and then say "see, misgeneralisation". Ideally they build a perfectly reasonable-looking training set, convince themselves its fine on their own held-out levels, stake a confidence number on it in public, and then watch the thing confidently do the wrong thing. And the wrong thing has to be a *correct* inference from what they gave it — not a bug, not confusion, just something they never meant. Two constraints I'm fairly firm on: the person playing the agent only ever gets the demonstration logs and the scalar score, never the goal stated in words (otherwise there's nothing to misgeneralise, they'll just learn the goal), and before building anything each group has to write one sealed sentence describing how their levels are generated, which we read aloud at the very end. Design me the whole thing: roles and group sizes, materials, the exact sequence with timings, the payoff scheme (no real money, points on a whiteboard is fine), and the confidence-stake mechanic, because the sting depends on them having gone public with a number before I swap the levels.

The debrief is where I most need help, because this stuff keeps collapsing back into platitudes when I run it. Map the moments in the game onto the actual distinctions: the reward log versus the objective in the trainers' heads; why their validation set couldn't possibly have caught the problem (drawn from the same generator, and the generator had a convention baked into it that nobody noticed, which is exactly what the sealed sentence will show — it'll be true and useless); why adding more training levels of the same kind doesn't help, and why that isn't the same as "we need more data"; and the difference between an agent gaming the proxy in training (specification gaming) and an agent that does well in training and then competently pursues the wrong thing out of distribution — they should be able to say which one happened here and why the second is the nastier one. Say plainly that the intended objective existed only in the trainers' heads, and that two trainers who disagreed about what they were training could have produced identical logs. Then be honest with me about what the activity *can't* do: I'm guessing it can't reproduce the fact that a real agent's learned rule is a distributed mess rather than a sentence someone can read off a card — we only get to inspect it here because we wrote it down — and it can't touch deceptive alignment, where the agent behaves well in training *because* it's worked out that it's being trained and is biding its time. Tell me how to mark that gap out loud rather than paper over it. And finally, what will predictably go wrong the first time: my money's on some bright spark in every group guessing the goal from common sense and wrecking the round, and on the rule-fitting step being underdetermined enough that people complain it's unfair, which is honestly the point, but I'd like to know how to handle it without the room turning on me. British English please.
