I've somehow ended up giving the little intro talk before our film club's screening of Amadeus (the Forman film) next month, and I've definately decided to do it through Schopenhauer's philosophy of music rather than the usual genius-versus-mediocrity angle. Can you give me a proper reading of the film in those terms? British English, please.

The bit of the theory I want to hang it on: for Schopenhauer the other arts — painting, sculpture, poetry — are copies of the Ideas, and the Ideas are the will's objectifications at their various grades, but music doesn't work like that at all. It skips the Ideas and is a direct copy of the Will itself, the thing in itself, which is why it can move us without going through concepts or images, and why he thinks the composer gives voice to the inner essence of the world in a language his own reason can't follow. That's my paraphrase, by the way, not his words — I don't want to be reciting him from memory and getting it wrong. He then gets more specific: melody is the life of the will, its desires and delays and satisfactions and sorrows; the bass and the harmony belong to the lower grades of the will; and music expresses joy, sadness and pain themselves — the essence of them — rather than the composer's own joy or grief, and without the actual pain.

So where does Amadeus actually dramatise any of that, and where is it just doing the religious-miracle thing? I keep coming back to the scene where Salieri first hears Mozart's music — the wind serenade — and is poleaxed by it, and the scene where he leafs through the manuscripts and can't find a single correction, and the night he takes down the Requiem from the dying Mozart. What is the film doing with Salieri's envy? On Schopenhauer's picture the composer is basically a conduit for something entirely impersonal, so there's nothing about the man to envy, and yet the whole film turns on the unbearable fact that God speaks through a vulgar boy. Is that a problem for Schopenhauer, or does the film simply have a different metaphysics?

Also, for Schopenhauer aesthetic contemplation is meant to quiet the will for a while and give you a holiday from wanting; Salieri's music-madness makes him want more, not less. Is that the film being a drama about envy and nothing more, or is it a real objection?

Where does the film resist the reading, and where does Schopenhauer come off worse? The Requiem is the obvious test case: the film clearly wants it to be Mozart writing his own death mass, which is exactly the individual, autobiographical view of music Schopenhauer denies. And Salieri's closing boast that he's the patron saint of mediocrities everywhere — does the film have a theory of who gets to hear the Will, does Schopenhauer have one, and do the two agree? Where they don't, say so straight rather than fudging it. Finish by telling me what the film shows about music that his theory can't account for — something the argument on its own doesn't get you. And please don't quote dialogue at me, I haven't got the script in front of me and I'd rather not have misquotes; scenes and plot are fine.
