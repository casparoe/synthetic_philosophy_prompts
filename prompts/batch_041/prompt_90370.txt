Im in a second-year MA program and I run paradox night for our theory reading group -- we did Newcomb, then the surprise exam, and next thursday it's the centipede game and the backward induction argument, the one that always ends with two people shouting at each other across the table. I'm on for the ten minute opener and I want to do a poem instead of slides, because I think the reason the thing feels like a trap is structural and not a matter of the numbers, and a poem can do structure in a way a slide cant. So: six stanzas, one per decision point in the standard six node version, pot doubles every time both players pass, payoffs arranged so that at every single node taking beats passing and getting taken from a node later. But print the stanzas in reverse of the order they happen -- first stanza is the very last node, last stanza is the very first move. Read it top to bottom and you're doing what the backward induction does, unwinding from the end; read it bottom to top and you get the actual game, two people handing a doubling pot back and forth and neither one taking. It should survive both readings. Plain voice, one player talking to the other, alternating speakers, 25ish lines, rhyme only if it doesn't come out sounding like a greeting card.

The bit I care most about is the refrain. Every stanza starts with the same line, and it should grow by one level of nesting as you go down the page -- "I know that you know," then "I know that you know that I know," and so on -- because the knowledge you need gets deeper the closer you get to the first move, not shallower: to take at node one you need to know what she'll do at node two, and to know that she knows what you'd do at three, and so on down the line. I had this backwards in my notes for a week so please dont copy me. And in the last stanza, the first move, I want the regress to fail to close -- the line shouldn't be able to finish itself, it should just run out, because the whole premise is that you can count on her counting on you counting on her, and that tower has no bottom.

The other thing, the one my group actually fights about: the argument seems to eat its own premises. If she's really the kind of reasoner who takes at her first chance, then the fact that she's still sitting across from you at node four is evidence that she isn't, which is evidence you should pass, which means... and meanwhile the lab version is that real people pass four or five times and then take near the end, which is the one thing the theory doesn't predict and can't just wave off. Get that self-undermining feeling into the poem somewhere without turning it into a lecture, and if the first move at the bottom of the page can stay genuinely open, let it. Accurate enough that nobody in the room can say the poem got the game wrong; I don't mind if they argue about whether it got the philosophy wrong.
