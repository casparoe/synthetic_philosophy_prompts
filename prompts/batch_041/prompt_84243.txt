ok so before I commit to any account of verbal disputes — the whole "we're not actually disagreeing, we just mean different things by X" move — I want the checklist first. I keep noticing that every account I like just happens to make the disputes I find boring come out verbal and the ones I care about come out substantive, which is a red flag, so I'd rather see the spec before I look at anyone's theory.

So: what does a theory of verbal disputes have to deliver to count as adequate? Not a survey of who said what — the requirements list. For each requirement, the case or consideration that puts it on the list, and whether you think it's genuinely non-negotiable or just a prejudice of one camp. If some subset of the requirements can't be satisfied together, thats the most interesting thing you could tell me — say so explicitly and tell me which one you'd drop.

Things I keep bumping into and want the list to handle:

(a) my friend Ravi's proposal that a dispute counts as verbal only if both parties' claims can be restated in a vocabulary neither of them would say already takes sides, and the disagreement then evaporates. He thinks this fixes the circularity in the usual test (would they still disagree once they agreed on all the facts?); I think it just relocates it, and that whoever gets to decide what "takes sides" wins by fiat.

(b) the case where two people agree about every actual case but split on the counterfactuals — whether a brain in a vat could know anything, say.

(c) the same fight coming out verbal in one language and substantive in another, depending on what each language forces you to decide.

(d) the residue problem: "it's just a verbal dispute" always seems to leave something behind — fine, then which word should we use, and for what purpose? I don't see how any account that ends at "so nothing is at stake" survives that.

(e) the case that got me into this: whether tables and chairs really exist alongside their particles, where the two sides seem to agree about everything observable, and yet nobody involved thinks they're just talking about words.

(f) the theory has to say something about whether the fight between the deflationists and the people who think ontological disputes are dead serious is itself verbal, without sawing off the branch it's sitting on.

(g) it has to be usable by someone inside the dispute, not just a god's-eye observer — I'm not going to settle whether free will talk is verbal by first settling the metaphysics of meaning.

At the end, tell me which single requirement does the most damage — the one that quietly kills the most popular accounts. Skip the scorecard of who said what, I'll do that part myself.
