I'm a retired marine biologist — forty years on invertebrate sensory physiology, mostly crabs and their bizzare eyes — and since I stopped working I've been reading philosophy with a lot more enthusiasm than skill. For the last few months I keep running into the idea that consciousness is a user illusion, the mind being like the desktop on a computer: the icons are perfectly usable, but there's no little folder living inside the screen. I met the comparison years ago in some pop-science book and took it as a metaphor, and now I find people apparently mean it literally-ish, and I can't work out which of the views on offer is the one that says I was wrong for forty years when I assumed there was something it is like to be a crab, as opposed to the ones that say I was right but confused about what I was right about. Every summary I find collapses into either consciousness isn't real, get over it, or the hard problem is hard, and neither of those tells me what to do with the dentist's chair.

What I'd like from you is a decision tree — built out of concrete cases and plain claims, not jargon; my eyes glaze over at things like the transparency of the self-model — that starts from the one thing I'm actually sure of, namely that when I'm in the chair with a root canal, something is going on, and I am not lying to the dentist or misreading my own report, and leads to the positions on what that something is. Every branch should be a question a person could answer without already knowing where it sends them. Cases I want on the tree, at least these: the root canal; a blindsight patient who can guess which way a line is tilted but insists he sees nothing; a split-brain patient whose two hands fight over the same object; the video where you count basketball passes and completely miss the gorilla, which I gather is really an argument about how much of the scene is actually in front of us at any moment; a lucid dreamer; and an animal with no self-model at all, if the views say anything about what its mental life is like. Two things I think get lost in the summaries: first, the gorilla and change-blindness business seems like a different kind of illusion claim from the qualia-are-a-fiction business, and I suspect the handouts online either seperate them badly or lump them together, so give that its own axis. Second, I want at least one branch on what the illusion is supposed to be an illusion of — a property (there's no feel there), a boundary (where the feel stops), or a unity (that it's all one experience) — because those seem to come apart and I've never seen them pulled apart. And I'd like a leaf for the position where the illusion is real but there's nobody there to be fooled, since I can't tell whether that's a serious option or just a slogan.

Please give it to me as a JSON object so I can paste it into my notes, with these fields: tree (a list of branch nodes, each with a node_id, the question, an answer_options list where every option carries the answer text and the id of the branch or leaf it leads to, and a one-line note on why that question is the thing that seperates the positions); leaves (each with a leaf_id, a short plain-words label for the position, the path of answers that gets you there, its core claim in one sentence, its most awkward commitment in one sentence, occupied true or false, and a line on what sort of reasoning lands there); unoccupied (paths through the tree that match no position anyone holds, each with a verdict of incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored, plus a sentence of why); indistinguishable_pairs (any two leaves your tree can't really pry apart, with a note on what would be needed to seperate them); cases (each case from my list with a line on which branch it tests); and my_landing, since I'm sure about the root canal and inclined to think a crab has some kind of inner life — tell me which leaf that combination drops me into and what it costs me to stay there. Skip philosopher names, book titles and dates entirely; I can't check them and I'd rather not have guesses dressed up as facts. Just label the positions in plain words, and mark a leaf unoccupied if you aren't confident anyone holds it.
