ok, novelist here, two thirds through a book, and I need a poem for it — I'm not a poet, hence the ask. Setup: my narrator, a woman in her thirties, finds out she was conceived with a donor egg, and that there were six embryos from that batch and only one got implanted. The other five are still in a freezer in another state. She keeps calling them her siblings; her partner thinks thats a category error, and that's what sets her off.

The poem is what she writes in that chapter, and it should be addressed to the one who would've been here instead of her. What I want it to be about is the necessity-of-origin view: you're essentially tied to the actual sperm and egg you came from, so if either one had been different there's no you having a different life, there's just a different person — and that different person would be her sibling, not her. She badly wants to be able to say the frozen ones are versions of her. The poem should keep failing to let her say it.

Make it a sestina. The form is already doing half the philosophy — six end-words rotating through every permutation, which is exactly the recombination she's been fantasizing about, and then an envoi where all six have to fit into three lines and only one person comes out. Pick end-words that carry the argument (egg, other, same, only, never, me, or whatever earns its place), and if one of them is me, every stanza is forced to end there and she has to mean something different by it each time. Dont let her resolve it: no consolation, no we're all stardust, no tidy turn where she loves the freezer babies and feels whole. And if she tries out the thought that she could've been born to different parents, get the modal part right — she's in other possible worlds, but only the ones where it's that same egg and that same sperm; the other worlds have other people in them and those people don't get to be her. I know a strict sestina is 39 lines; if the envoi lands I won't count.
