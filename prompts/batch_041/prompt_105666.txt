So I'm in this political theory seminar on populism and I keep getting into the same argument with a friend who's a real believer -- she thinks "the will of the people" names an actual thing, a single thing, and that any politician who defies it is betraying the people, full stop. I keep wanting to say the phrase is doing no work, but I realize I don't actually have a spec for what a good theory of popular will would have to do, so I can't tell whether the phrase is empty or whether my friend just has a bad theory of it. Before I read another account (Rousseau's general will, the minimalist "democracy is just whatever the voters decide" line, the constructivist idea that the people only exists because someone appeals to it), I want the requirements list first.

Give me the desiderata: what any adequate theory of "the will of the people" has to deliver. Which cases it has to get right and why each one actually bites -- a majority voting to strip the minority's rights permanently; a population where every individual is perfectly consistent but the majority-preferences cycle, or come out jointly inconsistent (each person coherent, the group not); the same people asked the same question twice with the options relabeled or reordered and answering differently; a founding generation whose "will" a constitution is supposed to bind later majorities with; and the question of who even counts as part of the people to begin with, given that the boundary can't itself be drawn democratically without going in a circle. Which distinctions it can't be allowed to collapse (actual will vs. the will people "really" have or would have if better informed; a single unitary will vs. an aggregate of many; the will of the people vs. the will of a majority vs. the will of whoever bothered to show up; a momentary mood vs. a settled will). And which formal results it has to stay consistent with -- I know there's the Condorcet cycling thing and Arrow's theorem and something about groups being inconsistent even when every member is consistent, and I want the list to say what those actually rule out rather than just name-checking them.

Then flag the part I most want: whether some of these requirements are jointly unsatisfiable, and if so which ones. My suspicion is that "faithful to what people actually will" and "a single consistent rational will that can ground legitimate authority" can't both be had, but tell me if that's wrong or too cheap. And say which single requirement does the most work excluding candidate theories. Structure it as: "The list" (~600-700 words), where each requirement gets its motivating case and a line on whether its non-negotiable or just one camp's preference; "The clash" (~250 words), on which requirements can't be jointly met and which one is doing the heavy lifting; and "Where the obvious contenders land" (~200 words) -- Rousseau, the minimalist proceduralist, and the constructivist, a few sentences each.
