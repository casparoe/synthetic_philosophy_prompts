I've spent the past several months reporting on a small group of researchers who want to change how institutions reach hard factual judgments, and I am now writing the feature. Their proposal is easy to state and, I think, underexamined. Take a question that no single person could settle in a working day. Break it into sub-questions, each narrow enough that a competent but strictly bounded reasoner — one with a fixed budget of time and attention — could answer it alone. Farm the sub-questions out, then recombine the answers into a verdict on the original question. The distinctive part is how they close the loop. A reasoner who takes on a sub-question is not a humbler kind of thing than the reasoner at the top; it is the same kind of bounded agent — in the limit, one person with a fixed afternoon — and it may in turn decompose its own question and consult other agents of that same kind. The whole arrangement is therefore defined by its own self-application, and whatever competence the system displays at the top is nothing over and above the competence of the bounded agents at the bottom plus whatever the decomposition and the recombination contribute. They have a name for it — factored cognition — but the name matters less than the shape of the thing, and they present it as an idealization of deliberation as such: not a machine anyone might build, but a purified picture of what deliberation already is whenever it works, whether in one head or in a committee.

My editor wants 1,500 words, and I want the piece to be honest about what the proposal implies rather than what its authors hope it implies. So give me the most important implications of this idealization, understood as an account of what deliberation is and what it can be. I am not asking whether the proposal is technically feasible, and I am not asking whether it is a good idea; I want the consequences that follow if one takes the picture seriously as a description of the thing deliberation is.

Please structure the response as follows.

Summary — roughly 120 words. State the idealization back to me in the plainest terms you can, then name the one implication you think I should build the feature around, and say why that one rather than the others.

The implications, most important first — roughly 800 words. For each, give the implication, the step in the idealization it descends from, and what it would mean for an institution that took it seriously. I would rather have five implications that are genuinely distinct than ten that restate one another; say explicitly where one implication presupposes another.

Where the idealization gives out — roughly 300 words. The picture treats decomposition, recombination and checking as though they were free and faithful. Tell me which parts of deliberation it cannot describe even in principle, and whether those parts are marginal or central. Do not soften this into a list of engineering obstacles; I want to know what the idealization is an idealization of, and what it leaves out by construction.

The line to lead with — roughly 100 words. The sentence or two that a reader who remembers nothing else should take away, and the reason it is the honest one rather than the most exciting one.

No reading lists, please; I need the argument, not the literature.
