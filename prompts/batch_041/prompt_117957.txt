Ok so I just spent three hours arguing with a friend of mine (law student, insufferable) about juries and appellate courts, and I need an outside oppinion before I lose my mind. Take the role of a philosopher of law who also actually knows the social choice literature, and please think carefully before you answer — I'd rather you start by brainstorming the different things these practices *could* be committed to, lay out the candidates, and then tell me which one you actually think it is and why. Don't bother with citations or reading lists; I don't care who said what first, I want the argument.

Here's what set it off. Two things courts do that seem to pull in opposite directions.

First: a criminal jury normally has to be unanimous on the verdict, but it isn't asked to agree on its reasons, and it generally isn't supposed to explain itself at all. We get "guilty" and that's it. So: total agreement demanded on the conclusion, complete indifference to agreement on the premises. Twelve jurors, twelve incompatible stories, all fine.

Second: in a civil case the judge can hand the jury a list of narrow factual questions instead — was there a contract, did the defendant breach it, were the damages foreseeable — and the verdict just gets assembled out of the answers. Now it's the reverse: we demand agreement on the premises, and the conclusion is arithmetic.

Then she hits me with this and I can't stop thinking about it. Three judges on a panel. The rule is the plaintiff wins only if (i) there was a valid contract and (ii) the defendant breached it. Judge A: contract yes, breach yes, so plaintiff wins. Judge B: contract yes, breach no, so defendant wins. Judge C: contract no, breach yes, so defendant wins. Vote on the outcome: 2–1 to dismiss. Vote issue by issue: 2–1 that there was a contract, 2–1 that there was a breach. Same three judges, same three opinions, and the court reaches opposite results depending only on whether you count conclusions or count reasons. And it isn't hypothetical — courts really do both, they vote on the disposition, and then separately we try to figure out what "the court held," which gets genuinely strange when the court splits 4–2–3 and no five people agree on a single sentence of reasoning. There's apparently some rule that lower courts should follow the narrowest opinion among the judges who agreed on the outcome, which means the law can effectively be set by an opinion a majority of the court rejected. Meanwhile the whole system is obsessed with consistency — precedent, like cases alike, one law for everyone — except that a jury is explicitly allowed to be twelve inconsistent people who happen to agree on a verdict.

So: what does all of this presuppose? I want you to reconstruct the implicit theory, not tell me what the cases say. In particular:

- Does the practice need to assume the group is a single rational agent with logically consistent beliefs? Or is it enough that the *outcome* is consistent — you can't both convict and acquit the same defendant? Or does it assume nothing about group minds at all, and this is all just a bookkeeping convention for producing a decision?
- When a panel votes only on the bottom line, is that treating reasons as private and the verdict as the only public thing? Or is it treating the verdict as the thing with authority and the reasons as decoration? Are those actually different, and does the practice need to pick?
- Is unanimity in the jury doing legitimacy work (we accept it because you all agreed) while the fractured-opinion mess is doing correctness work (we accept it because that's the law, agreement or not)? Can one system coherently hold both at once, or is one of them a fiction the other one quietly exposes?
- What does it presuppose about whether a group can have a will or a judgment at all, as opposed to a tally of individual ones? If a court "holds" something no majority believes, in what sense did the court hold it?
- Which parts are strictly presupposed, which parts would survive if we swapped the underlying picture, and where is the practice merely underdetermined — i.e. several incompatible pictures would license exactly what we do? I suspect it's underdetermined in more places than my friend thinks, and I want to know which ones.
- And what would actually have to change if we committed to one picture — say, that group judgments are only ever defined over the bottom line, or only ever over the reasons? Would we have to stop talking about "the holding" of a fractured court? Would juries have to start giving reasons?

Give me a real answer, not a survey of positions. If you think the honest answer is "the practice is a mess that presupposes nothing coherent," say that, and then tell me what the least incoherent repair looks like.
