I'm a journalist on the science-and-ideas beat, and I keep bumping into a line that people pin on Kierkegaard: the crowd is untruth. Latest sighting was a panel I was moderating on trust in journalism and social media, where a philosopher on the panel used it to wave away a question about whether a large, well-informed readership could act as a check on a bad newspaper. His move was: Kierkegaard showed that crowds are never in the truth, only single individuals are, so wisdom-of-the-crowd reasoning is a category error. I didn't push back becuase I couldn't have said why it felt off, but I'd like to be able to next time.

So: what could that one-liner actually be claiming? I want the menu — the distinct precise theses the phrase could be expressing, and for each one whether it's a platitude, a substantive and defensible claim, or just false, plus whether it would actually support what the panellist did with it.

Things I suspect are in the mix but can't sort out myself: (a) the ordinary statistical claim that majorities are often wrong; (b) something about the crowd as a moral category — that in a crowd the individual stops being answerable, so untruth isn't about false beliefs at all; (c) something about the public as an abstraction rather than a real set of people, which I think is roughly his point in that 1846 review where he goes after the press and the present age; (d) something about levelling, the press, and passionless reflection draining the meaning out of ethical concepts; (e) something theological about Christianity, Christendom, and the single individual before God; (f) something local and biographical — he'd just been torn apart by a satirical Copenhagen paper, and this could be a wounded man's line about the mob rather than a doctrine.

I don't want the standard encyclopedia gloss, I want the readings separated and graded. In particular: does any reading of the line license "crowds can't be right about anything" or "a readership can't be a check on a paper"? If the strongest reading is the moral or religious one, is the panellist's epistemic use a straight equivocation? And what would Kierkegaard say about an actual assembled crowd versus the dispersed readership of a newspaper — is that the distinction the panellist was quietly ignoring? Also worth knowing whether the slogan belongs to the same layer of his authorship as the review, or whether it's from later, more polemical material where his own voice is harder to isolate from the pseudonyms and the posthumous editing.

British English please. At the end, tell me how confident you are in what you've claimed and single out the point you're least sure of — if a reading depends on a contested translation, or on how much of the late material is really his own voice, put it there rather than asserting it flatly. And don't feel obliged to make it come out tidy; if two of the readings are genuinely hard to prise apart, just say so.
