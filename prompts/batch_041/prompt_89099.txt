everyone in the foreign-policy podcasts i listen to keeps saying the US and China are caught in the "Thucydides trap" — a rising power scares the incumbent and war follows more or less automatically, the way Athens and Sparta slid into it. my uncle, who actually read the book, reckons they're mangling it: he says the interesting bit is that Thucydides calls the truest cause the growth of Athenian power and the fear it put into Sparta, but the openly stated complaints were all about third parties — Corinth, Megara, Corcyra, Potidaea — and the two main powers weren't even the ones pushing hardest. so can you audit the analogy properly? give me the correspondence table first: who's Athens, who's Sparta, who's Corinth, what plays the Thirty Years Peace, whats playing the Megarian decree. then the places it strains — nukes, deep economic interdependence, no shared Greek religious world or rules of hoplite warfare, no oracle to consult. separate the disanalogies that actually matter for what people are using it for from the ones that dont. and then the real question: does the conclusion the analogy gets used to license — that war is structurally likely and leaders are basically passengers — survive once the disanalogies are on the table, or was the analogy carrying the whole argument? if its a bad analogy, what would be a better one for the actual situation, or should people just drop it?
