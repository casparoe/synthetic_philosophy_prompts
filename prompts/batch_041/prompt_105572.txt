I am revising a paper on the transitivity of the betterness relation, and I have become convinced that the standoff in this literature is an artefact of our never having written down what a theory of betterness is actually being asked to deliver. I would like you to write that specification for me. Let the object of the theory be a single relation — 'at least as good as', together with the comparatives definable from it — over a domain that includes ordinary goods (lives, health, works of art, distributions of resources) and the mixed, many-dimensional items that make the familiar continuum arguments run: sequences in which each item appears to improve on its predecessor while the last item is plainly worse than the first, comparisons between a small number of flourishing lives and an enormous number of lives that are barely worth living, and pairs of items that seem to be neither better nor worse nor exactly equal. Write out the desiderata such a theory must meet: the clear cases it has to get right, the arguments it must either block or absorb, the distinctions it may not collapse, the formal properties it needs if it is to be usable in deliberation and in policy comparison, and the constraints on the theory itself — that betterness supervene on non-evaluative features, that no cutoff or threshold be left unexplained, that verdicts be stable under irrelevant redescription. Behind each requirement put the case or argument that earns it a place on the list; a requirement with no case behind it is a prejudice, and I would rather have a short list than a long one.

Two things I want especially. First, flag any subset of the requirements that cannot be had together, with the proof or the counterexample in outline. I suspect that transitivity, the rejection of any threshold, and the verdict that every step in the continuum is an improvement cannot all survive, but I want to know whether that is right, and what else goes overboard with it. Second, tell me which single requirement does the most to exclude the extant contenders, and whether it excludes them for a good reason or merely legislates the intuition of one camp. I am not asking for a scorecard of the theories — I will do that myself once I have the specification — but I would like a sentence or two at the end on what the list leaves standing. One further demand, which I care about and which I have not seen treated cleanly: the theory has to be usable, so say whether it can deliver at least an acyclic choice function over options even where the full betterness relation fails to be transitive, or whether that middle position is unstable, and what it costs to hold. I press this because a colleague in the department argues that we need only enough structure to choose, not a total ordering of value, and I have never known how to answer her.

Finally, since I intend to circulate this to my graduate seminar, be explicit about the weakest joint in your own reasoning. Say which of the requirements you would surrender first if forced, and what argument or counterexample would actually move you to surrender it.
