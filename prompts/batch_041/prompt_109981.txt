I'm a third-year philosophy student currently taking a seminar on decision-making under moral uncertainty, and my term paper has been selected for the end-of-term work-in-progress workshop in two weeks. I need to prepare for the question period, and I would like you to draft my Q&A prep sheet.

The paper defends a position I call rule-level aggregation. When I am uncertain which moral theory is correct, I argue that I should not aggregate the theories' verdicts act by act; I should aggregate at the level of decision rules. Each theory is treated as ranking the candidate rules for my situation by its own lights, and I adopt the rule that fares best across the theories, weighted by my credences. The selling point is that the procedure never requires comparing value magnitudes across theories: its inputs are only each theory's ordinal ranking of the candidate rules, together with my credences, so the familiar objection that intertheoretic comparisons of value are ill-defined is supposed to be disarmed before it starts. My running example is a case in which act-level aggregation returns different answers depending on how each theory's value function is normalized, while rule-level aggregation returns the same rule under every admissible normalization; the two theories agree on the ranking of the acts available to me but disagree sharply about how much worse the worst option is, which is precisely what makes normalization matter. I claim the rule-level procedure is invariant to that.

The audience is a departmental workshop, and I know roughly who is coming:

- Two formal types. One thinks "moral uncertainty" is ordinary decision theory applied to an unusual state space; he will want to know what my aggregation rule maximizes, whether it satisfies any representation theorem, and what happens when the candidate rule set is individuated differently. The other works on measurement and will go straight for whether ordinal rankings of rules can generate a determinate choice at all.
- Three normative ethicists, all robust realists, who suspect the entire problem is an artifact of bad framing: either I am uncertain about non-moral facts, or I should simply do better moral philosophy.
- One Kantian who will say that treating theories as inputs to a maximizing procedure subordinates the categorical to the prudential, and that "aggregating across theories" presupposes a standpoint no agent can actually occupy.
- A sharp graduate student who has read more of the literature than I have and will ask the question that actually breaks the paper.

Include the objection I am hoping nobody asks; I have a suspicion about which one it is, but I would rather you tell me. Roughly 1,200 words, and please do not include citations or attribute positions to named authors — I cannot check references, and the replies should not depend on them.
