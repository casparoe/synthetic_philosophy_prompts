Write an essay on the following controversy in epistemology, and reach a defended conclusion.

Consider an agent—call her Ana—who has just completed a long, careful derivation and is highly confident in its conclusion. She then receives strong evidence that she belongs to a small subgroup of people in whom this kind of multi-step derivation tends to go subtly wrong, even when it feels perfectly sound from the inside. The evidence about the subgroup is itself solid and was gathered independently of her present derivation. Nothing in her actual first-order reasoning is shown to contain an error; the higher-order evidence simply impugns the faculty she used.

This is the locus of a live dispute. On one side, the conciliationist holds that Ana's confidence must be lowered roughly to the level the higher-order evidence warrants, because the very process she relied on is what the evidence calls into question. On the other side, the steadfast or self-trust view holds that first-order and higher-order evidence occupy different levels, so that Ana may, at least sometimes, retain her first-order credence; a related line holds that the higher-order evidence is in a certain sense idle, since it can only move her if she trusts the very faculty it purports to discredit.

Your essay should do the following. First, state the controversy precisely enough that the disagreement is not merely verbal—say what each side is and is not committed to. Second, weigh the strongest considerations on each side, including at least one that turns on how the case looks when the higher-order evidence is itself the product of the same kind of reasoning it purports to discredit, and at least one that turns on what it would take for Ana's situation to count as a case of epistemic akrasia—that is, of believing something while believing that so believing is irrational. Third, consider whether the verdict changes if Ana's first-order evidence is not merely strong but conclusive, or if the higher-order evidence bears not on her particular reasoning but only on her general reliability as a reasoner.

Reach a verdict: state what Ana should believe, or what a general rule should say about cases like hers, and defend it against the most serious objection you have raised. Do not end on a noncommittal note; if the correct answer is a qualified one, state the qualification explicitly and defend it.
