You are a philosopher of history with genuine expertise in two adjacent fields — the metaphysics of teleology, and the historiography of moral and political change — and I would like you to answer in that capacity. Please think carefully and slowly before writing; I care more about the structure of the answer than about its verdict, and I would rather see your reasoning than your conclusion.

I am a retired evolutionary biologist. For thirty-five years my working creed was that apparent purpose in nature can be explained without a purposer: you account for the fit between organism and environment by selection over variation, and the appearance of striving falls out as a consequence rather than operating as a cause. Now that I have retired I have been reading philosophy of history, and I keep meeting a claim with the same shape as the one I spent my career denying in biology — that the arc of history bends toward justice. My former colleagues would call that teleology. The charitable reading is that it is a teleonomic claim: a claim about a mechanism that generates direction without anyone intending the direction. I want to know whether the charitable reading can actually be made good, and I want a number attached to it.

Proceed as follows.

First, before any numbers, spend a few sentences mapping the terrain. What would have to be true for the arc claim to be a substantive empirical thesis rather than a slogan? What are the candidate mechanisms — selection among institutions, ratchets that make reversals progressively costlier, the sheer expense of maintaining domination without consent, the growth and diffusion of knowledge, something else? And can justice, or the relevant notion of arbitrariness, be specified precisely enough for the thesis to have a truth value at all? If you judge the question as posed to be ill-posed, say so plainly, then state the nearest well-posed version you can formulate.

Second, give me your credence in this proposition, which I have tried to state so that it could be false: there exists a mechanism operating on human societies across spans of centuries that biases institutional change toward reducing the arbitrary exercise of power over persons, and the bias is strong enough that the long-run expected direction of change is toward such reduction, even though particular centuries run the other way.

Third, decompose that number. Assign a rough probability and a one-line justification to each of at least these sub-questions, plus anything I have left out: (a) that at least one such mechanism exists, and is not an artifact of our having chosen the last three centuries as our sample; (b) that, conditional on its existing, it survives the class of shocks that have previously reset institutions — collapse, conquest, epidemic, technological disruption; (c) that the relevant notion of justice or of arbitrariness can be fixed non-circularly, rather than being read back off whichever outcomes we happen to approve; (d) that a historical record consisting of one trajectory could in principle discriminate the directional thesis from its rivals — drift, cyclicity, plain contingency — rather than every rival accommodating the same evidence. Then combine the terms explicitly and tell me which one your final number is most sensitive to.

Fourth, tell me what would count against the thesis: name a century-scale development that a defender must classify as temporary reversal rather than refutation, and say candidly whether that classification is doing honest work or is unfalsifiability wearing a lab coat. Then say which piece of evidence or which argument would move your number most, and in which direction.

Finally, two people I argue with about this. One puts the proposition at about 0.75 and says the direction is real but slow and bloodily uneven; the other puts it at about 0.1 and says it is Whig history with good public relations. Give me your own number and then tell me where their disagreement most likely actually lives — whether it is a disagreement about mechanisms, about the specification of justice, about what the historical record can support, or about something more basic. I am not asking for a reading list, and please do not pad the answer with names, titles, or dates: I have no way to check them and they are not the point.
