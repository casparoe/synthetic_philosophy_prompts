My dad turns 70 in March and I've got to give the toast, and I keep putting off writing it because I can't work out how to be honest in it.

Background: he spent basically his whole working life running money, four decades of it, and he is a sincere beleiver in the efficient-markets picture. Not as a pose. He'll tell you that if you think you know something the price doesn't, then you're wrong about one of those two things, and he means it, and he has said it to me in the car on the way home from me telling him about some stock tip. He has also, over those four decades, beaten the index he was measured against, and he refuses to take any credit for it. He says he got lucky, or that he was just being paid to hold things nobody else wanted to hold for a while.

So here's the knot I'm in. There's the result that says if it's common knowledge that everyone's rational and everyone starts from the same picture, then differences in information alone can't produce any trade at all — you'd look at the price, infer what the other guy must know, and walk away. And there's the other one, that if prices were already perfectly informative nobody would spend a cent digging anything up, so the information would stop flowing and prices couldn't stay informative. Which means a man with a forty-year edge is either a counterexample to the theory or a man who was never trading on information in the first place. And then there's a third thing nagging at me, the bit about the price being a summary of what everybody knows and nobody in particular knows — because if that's right, there's nothing there for a person to be good at, only a process to stay out of the way of.

The room: about half his old colleagues from the desk, who will hear every clause of this and know exactly which horn of which dilemma I've just impaled myself on. The other half is family, including my mum and my aunt, who want it warm and short and will have no idea what I'm on about if I start explaining why it's strange to praise a man for something that supposedly can't happen.

Write me the toast. Four minutes, give or take. I want it to actually commit to a reading of what his record means instead of waffling, and I want it to be a real toast about a real person rather than a lecture with his name stapled to the front. If the honest reading turns out to be that what I admire about him has nothing to do with markets at all, fine — but then say that out loud. And at the end, tell me which reading the toast you wrote is actually committed to, and what I'd have been saying instead if I'd gone with the others, because I want to know what I'm signing up for before I stand up.
