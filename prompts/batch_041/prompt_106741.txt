I'm the managing editor of a small math journal that publishes constructive and computational mathematics — maybe fifteen submissions a month, two referees each. Our referee pool is a mess: a couple of old-school constructivists, several type theorists, and a lot of ordinary analysts and number theorists who use classical logic all day and genuinely don't know what the fuss is about. Every few weeks I get a report that says, in effect, "this proof uses excluded middle, not constructive," and then the author writes me a furious letter pointing out that the theorem itself is perfectly fine constructively, or that the referee's own proof of the same lemma is worse. I want to kill this with a form.

The house rule I want the form to enforce: authors declare an "axiom budget" in the abstract — the nonconstructive principles their proof actually leans on, drawn from a short list we publish. The referee's job isn't to rule on which principles are respectable, it's to audit the budget: nothing used that isn't declared, nothing declared that isn't used. Though some of the real philosophical fun is deciding what goes on that list in the first place, so say something about that too.

So write me the referee form. The questions in the order a referee should ask them, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops with a verdict (budget honest / under-declared / over-declared / not constructive in any useful sense), and where it hands the thing back to me or the author instead of settling it. Include a short list of the telltale moves that mean a proof has quietly spent money it never declared, and a little table of the usual suspect principles with what each one costs you — I want to be able to tell an author "your theorem is equivalent to X" instead of "your proof is bad."

Then run the form on three submissions: (1) a classical proof that every program in some class halts, via a compactness argument, where the author claims the result is "constructive" because a program exists; (2) a proof of a fixed-point or intermediate-value type theorem that declares no extra principles but leans on the fan theorem in an intermediate lemma; (3) a paper I personally like whose main theorem needs Markov's principle, submitted by someone who thinks Markov's principle is a scandal.

Referees get about 45 minutes and most of them are not constructivists, a few are hostile. Tell me which parts of the form are judgment calls dressed up as procedure, and where the whole thing will misfire. I'd rather know now then get another angry letter.
