I am a doctoral candidate in philosophy, with some training in international law, applying for a one-year dissertation-completion fellowship, and I need you to write the three-page prospectus. The topic is the naming of atrocities, but I want to steer clear of the two exhausted framings — that the legal definition of genocide is too narrow and ought to be expanded, and that the concept is so contested as to be useless — because both assume the real task is to discover the single correct definition. My half-formed idea is that naming an atrocity is not one act but several, and that the perennial impasse comes from forcing a single definition to serve jobs whose success conditions are incompatible. Roughly: a jurisdictional job (triggering courts, obligations, and institutional response), which wants a thin, bright-line, administrable concept; a recognitional job (affirming to victims and to history what was done to them), which wants a thick, context-sensitive concept; an explanatory job (picking out a distinctive social process, as Lemkin's original concept tried to do by folding in cultural and economic destruction), which wants a concept that carves social reality rather than legal procedure; and a mobilizational job (rallying publics and states), which wants a concept whose application is vivid and hard to dispute. I want to treat the concept as an object of conceptual engineering rather than of definitional discovery — not what genocide means, but which concept of atrocity we should have, given the distinct things naming is for — and then to ask when a naming dispute is substantive and when it is merely verbal.

The proposal should state the question precisely enough that it has a determinate answer; explain why the standard positions (the narrowness critique, the contestation critique, and the various proposals to expand the concept by adding protected groups, acts, or categories such as cultural destruction or ecocide) leave it open, and why the familiar verdict that this is an essentially contested concept does not by itself settle anything; set out the method — a decomposition of the naming roles, a criterion for sorting substantive from merely verbal disputes, and a test of both against a set of hard cases; say what a finished result looks like and what would count as the project failing; and give a realistic twelve-month plan with the deliverable at the end. The hard cases I have in mind: mass killing aimed at a group constituted by political affiliation, which the Genocide Convention's protected-group list leaves out; deliberate destruction of a group's language and institutions without mass killing; death by blockade, displacement, or engineered deprivation, where intent to destroy is contested; and violence against a group whose very boundaries are contested or constructed. Do not supply a bibliography or citations — refer to positions and arguments by description and I will add the references myself. You may invent illustrative cases, but do not attribute specific claims to named authors.
