I teach a final-year undergrad seminar that's half anthropology, half history — deep history, the long view of how people lived — and I'm writing the final. I need one essay question for it. 90 minutes, closed book, no reading list printed on the paper.

The topic I want it on is the "was the agricultural revolution a mistake?" debate. Trouble is, anyone who's read the pop-science version can hand me the same essay: foragers worked less, ate better, were healthier; farming brought hierarchy and misery; so yes, mistake. I get that essay about forty times and it grades the same forty times. The question has to be one you *cant* answer by reciting that.

What I want the question to make them confront, roughly:

1. "Affluent" is doing two jobs at once. You can be well-off by producing a lot, or by wanting very little — and once you notice that, the claim that foragers were affluent stops being a purely empirical claim about calories and work-hours and becomes partly a claim about what people wanted. I want them to say what's left to argue about once you fix which sense you mean, and what evidence would actually bear on it.

2. "Mistake" needs a subject. Nobody sat down and chose agriculture; it seems to have come together out of thousands of small choices over generations, unevenly, with people going back to foraging in some places. So is calling it a mistake a claim about a decision that was never made, or a shorthand verdict on a trajectory? Either answer is fine but they have to pick one and live with it — including saying what the comparison baseline is (compared to what, exactly, and for whom).

3. The inequality part isn't automatic. Storable food is supposed to be what let some people accumulate and others get bossed around — but storage by itself doesn't do that, and I want them to say what else has to be true. And I'd like them to notice that the "was it a mistake" verdict and the "did it cause inequality" verdict can come apart: one could go either way while the other doesn't.

To keep it out of the regurgitation zone, I want at least one move in the question to turn on something they won't have gotten from the pop version. Good candidates: the two roads to affluence thing from point 1; or the distinction between foragers who eat what they collect within a day or two (nothing piles up, nothing to defend) and foragers who store food for months (now there's a larder, and a larder is a thing someone can take) — and what that does to the standard story about where hierarchy comes from. Pick whichever you can build the sharper question around.

Now write me the deliverable: the essay question itself, then a model answer of about 700 words, then a marking scheme. For the marking scheme, give me five must-hit points with the marks each is worth, total 20, and for each one a one-line note on the move students typically make instead that costs them the marks. Make it so an A answer and a B answer are distinguishable by named moves, not by length or polish. Also — the model answer shouldn't lean on names or dates. I care whether they can make the moves, not whether they can cite anyone, and I don't want the exam rewarding a reading list I haven't assigned.

Last thing: tell me how confident you are in the model answer overall, and flag the single claim in it you're least sure about. I'd rather find out now than while I'm marking.
