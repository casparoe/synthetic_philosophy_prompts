Ok so this started as an argument with my sister (she's an ICU nurse) and I've been unable to let it go for weeks. Here's the pattern. Whenever something bad happens and people start asking what caused it, the honest answer is always a giant sprawling story — the disease, the staffing levels, the fact the hospital is built where it is, the patient's age, the weather, all of it. But what actually gets named as "the cause" is nearly always the one thing in that story that was out of the ordinary. My sisters example: a patient deteriorates overnight while the on-call doctor is covering three wards (which happens a couple of times a year), and the review concludes that the doctor not being at the bedside caused the death. Nobody calls the disease the cause, even though it was doing its work the whole time and would of killed him under any staffing arrangement. But then on a different night, same understaffing, nobody dies, and now people say the short-staffing "almost caused" a death — nothing actually happened, and we still hand it causal language. And notice that when the unusual thing is a non-event — a guard who wasn't at his post, a checklist nobody filled in, a scan nobody ordered — we're perfectly willing to call the absence a cause, and often we're angrier about it than we'd be about somebody who actively did the same amount of damage. But other non-events we flatly refuse to treat that way: if I forget to water my neighbours plants while she's away and they die, I don't think anyone, me included, would say I caused their death, even though "I didn't water them" seems like exactly the same kind of fact as "the guard wasn't at his post."

So: is there a name for this? I did one semester of philosophy so I know about half the vocabulary, which is honestly making it worse. I'm pretty sure it isn't just post hoc reasoning, and it isn't just blame, because the causal talk and the blame talk come apart — sometimes we say "she caused it but it's not her fault" and sometimes "she didn't cause it but she should be ashamed." What I want is the concept, or the set of competing concepts, not just a label. In particular: (a) is there a term for the general thing, that our causal verdicts seem to track what was abnormal or unexpected rather than what was necessary or sufficient; and (b) is the special case where the abnormal thing is an absence treated as its own separate problem with its own literature, or is it just a sub-case of the general one? And (c) what is the actual disagreement here, because I keep getting told two things that can't both be true — that an absence can't cause anything at all, because there's nothing there to do any causing, and that if absences can cause things then I am the cause of every death I failed to prevent, everywhere, forever.

If there's no single name for the whole thing, coin one, but then tell me what it excludes: how does it differ from just "the thing we could have changed," and from the cynical view that we only pick out causes for practical reasons, to know who to sue or who to fire.
