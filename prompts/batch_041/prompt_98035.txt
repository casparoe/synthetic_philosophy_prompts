EXAM QUESTION

Consider the following "two-step" account of state consciousness:

(1) A mental state S is conscious just in case the subject also has a higher-order representation R whose content is that she is now in S.
(2) R must be produced by a mechanism that is reliably causally sensitive to S, but R need not itself be conscious. The account is offered as an account of state consciousness only, and makes no claim about which creatures are conscious.

(a) Explain how this account handles the familiar transitivity intuition — that a state's being conscious consists in the subject's being, in some suitable way, aware of it.
(b) Suppose a subject forms R, with the content "I am in pain," through a process that is causally sensitive to her anxiety rather than to any pain; no pain is present. By the lights of (1) and (2), is a state of hers conscious? Assess whether the account gives the right verdict here, and say what, if anything, the case shows about the account.
(c) Does the account answer the hard problem of consciousness? Explain.

STUDENT'S ANSWER

"The transitivity intuition says that for a mental state to be conscious, the subject must be aware of it — the state must be represented to the subject in some way. The two-step account captures this: condition (1) is the transitivity principle, and it distinguishes the target state S from the higher-order representation R. S is the state that is made conscious; R is the state that does the making. So far so good.

The account avoids the standard regress. If a state is conscious whenever a higher-order representation of it exists, one might worry that R is itself a mental state and therefore needs its own higher-order representation, and so on forever. The account blocks this by requiring R to be unconscious (condition 2), so R never needs a representation of its own.

On the confabulation case in (b): R has the right content — 'I am in pain' — and it is produced by a mechanism that tracks something in the subject, namely her anxiety. The mechanism is causally sensitive to a state, and (1) asks only that the subject have a higher-order representation whose content is that she is in S. Since the subject has R, the account counts S as conscious. Some philosophers would call this a misfire, but the account is entitled to say that the subject is conscious of a pain; that is just what it is for a state to be conscious, on this view. The case therefore does not threaten the account; if anything it shows that the account can handle misrepresentation.

Finally, the account answers the hard problem. The hard problem is why a state feels like anything at all. On this account, S is conscious precisely because the subject represents it to herself; the higher-order representation is what gives the state its felt character. So the account explains why there is something it is like to be in S, which is exactly what the hard problem demands."
