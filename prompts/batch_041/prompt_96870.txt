I'm a doctoral student in the Scottish Enlightenment, and I keep losing the same argument at departmental lunches. A friend of mine who works in education policy is fond of claiming Adam Smith for the school-choice side: Smith, he says, would obviously have welcomed vouchers, state-funded education savings accounts, charter schools, and the legal right of parents to educate their children at home, because he distrusted monopolies, thought competition made institutions answer to the people they served, and was withering about the endowed universities of his own day, where, as he saw it, professors with comfortable fixed incomes had no reason to teach well. Another friend, who teaches in a public high school, claims Smith for her side just as confidently. She points out that Smith held that the division of labour leaves the common people stupid and ignorant, that he therefore wanted the state to ensure that ordinary people were educated at public expense, that he was willing to make basic instruction effectively compulsory, and that he was deeply suspicious of the reformer who imagines he can arrange a whole society as easily as one moves the pieces on a chessboard. Both of them quote Smith at me, and I have begun to suspect that neither has really asked what Smith would have made of institutions he never saw.

What I'd like from you is a careful reconstruction rather than a verdict borrowed from one camp. Take the modern landscape as it actually is -- means-tested and universal vouchers, education savings accounts that families can spend on tutoring or online courses, charter schools admitted by lottery, homeschooling now routinely supported by packaged online curricula and parent cooperatives, and the ordinary public district school that remains the default for most children -- and ask what Smith, reasoning from his own commitments, would have thought of each. Be specific about which of his premises do the work: his account of how self-interest responds to incentives, his insistence that the state has a duty to keep the common people from being stupefied by their work, his unease about concentrated state power over opinion, his assumption that parents are the natural guardians of their children's education. Where those commitments pull in opposite directions -- as I think they must over homeschooling, which is at once the most extreme exercise of parental liberty and the most complete withdrawal from any public guarantee of the child's education -- say so plainly rather than forcing a consistent line. And be honest about the places where Smith's texts simply underdetermine the answer, so that several positions are compatible with his views and my two friends are each half right. I am not asking for a reading list or for citations, and I don't want you to invent quotations from works you cannot consult; I want the argument itself. Please write it as continuous prose, the way one would write a long letter to a colleague -- no headings, no bullet points, no bold or italicized labels, no tables.
