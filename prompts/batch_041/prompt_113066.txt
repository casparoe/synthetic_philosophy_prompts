Here's the thing I can't stop chewing on. A while back I got interested in how long "hegemonic orders" last — the stretches where one power more or less sets the rules for everybody else. I compiled 22 of them. I've stripped the names off, deliberately: I don't want you reasoning from whatever you remember about Rome or Britain or the Mongols, I want you reasoning from the numbers. And I know you can't verify the list and can't look anything up — that's fine, treat it as the data.

Years each one lasted, start of clear dominance to end of it:

9, 12, 23, 31, 44, 58, 67, 71, 88, 96, 112, 130, 154, 187, 240, 320, 410

Those 17 are finished. The other five haven't ended, so all I've got is how long they've been running so far: 18, 26, 41, 63, and 95. That last one is the current order, the one we're living inside. It's sitting right about in the middle of that first list, which is the part that keeps me up at night.

My question: what's your credence that this current one ends within the next 25 years? Call "ends" = a consensus among historians a generation from now that it was over. I've gone back and forth with myself and landed at about 0.25, but I don't trust it, and I don't trust it in a specific way — I can't tell if the wobble is in the data or in what I'm even asking.

I've read enough to know this is the reference class problem and that there's a literature, so don't just hand me the literature. I know about the outside view and the base rate fallacy and the turkey. What I want is your number, taken apart. A few things I specifically want you to not skip over:

- Which reference class you're actually using, why that one and not the obvious runner-up, and whether the answer moves much if you switch.
- What you're assuming about the clock. Do these things age — does the hazard of ending go up the longer one runs — or is it memoryless, or does it have a fat tail where the old ones are the durable ones? These give very different answers and I want to see you pick.
- What to do about the five that are still running. I keep suspecting my own list is biased by the fact that I'm sitting here in the middle of one of them, but I can't articulate exactly how.
- Whether a number about a single unrepeatable event is even a real thing, or just a way of keeping score. In my old job a 95% interval was a claim about a procedure. Here you're supposed to hand me a number about one event that will happen once and never again. Tell me honestly whether you think that's coherent.
