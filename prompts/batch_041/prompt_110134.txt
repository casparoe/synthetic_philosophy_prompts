I'm a retired field ecologist, 68, and since I hung up my boots I've been reading a lot of philosphy, mostly ethics and decision theory. I keep getting stuck on something that looks like a small administrative detail but I suspect is not.

Here's the practice. Health systems and insurers in a lot of countries decide whether to pay for a treatment by comparing its cost per quality-adjusted life-year, or QALY, against a threshold. A QALY is meant to be one year of life at full health, and the threshold is meant to say how much the system is willing to pay for one. So a drug that gives a thousand people one extra QALY each at $30,000 per QALY gets funded; one that gives one person a hundred QALYs at $300,000 per QALY does not. That is the official story, anyway. But then the same systems routinely make exceptions. A public campaign for one identifiable patient, a child with a rare disease, a local hero, and suddenly the money appears, often at hundreds of thousands or millions per QALY. There is a name for this I came across, the rule of rescue. The same systems also pour money into neonatal intensive care and some cancer drugs that look terrible by their own threshold. So the practice is: use QALYs for the general rule, but let identifiable life-and-death cases break the rule.

My question: what philosophical commitments does this two-part practice actually presuppose? I gather Harsanyi's aggregation theorem says that if you accept certain expected-utility axioms and a Pareto condition, the social ranking has to be a weighted sum of individual utilities, which sounds like the QALY rule. Does the rule-of-rescue exception presuppose that Harsanyi's theorem is false, or that its axioms don't apply to the social decision, or just that the system is inconsistent? Does it presuppose that there is something morally special about identifiable persons, or about rescue as opposed to prevention, or about deontological constraints that can't be reduced to summing QALYs? Which rival views does the practice quietly rule out? And which parts of it are actually underdetermined, in the sense that several different philosophical theories would license the same behavior?

I want a committed answer, not a menu. If you think the practice is straightforwardly incoherent, say so and defend it. If you think it can be made coherent, pick the interpretation you think is best and argue for it. Don't tell me there are many views and leave it at that. Take a definate position.
