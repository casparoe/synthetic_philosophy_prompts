I'm giving the keynote at our local history and philosophy society's annual dinner — about eighty people, half of them academic historians who'll notice if I fudge anything, and half ordinary members who come for the wine and the argument. The dinner this year lands on the anniversary of Thermidor, and they've asked me to speak to the old question of whether revolutions devour their children. I want to answer it honestly as someone who thinks the whole thing was contingency all the way down — no class logic, no ideology unfolding on schedule, just choices that could have gone otherwise — but there are people in that room whose families lost someone in the Terror, so I cant just be clever about it.

The speech has to be built around a notion I've been chewing on and want you to take seriously rather than name-drop: "legitmacy debt". The thought is that a revolution which founds a new order on an act no authority could have authorised incurs a debt it can only repay by repeating the act, which is why each wave of leaders ends up consuming the last — and the story that revolutions *always* devour their children is itself part of how the debt gets serviced. I want that doing real structural work in what the speech can and can't say, not sitting on top as decoration.

Act as an expert rhetorician and a historian of political thought. Think carefully before you write, and start by brainstorming what this speech must avoid saying given the contingency view — then write it, roughly 800 words. It should read as something actually spoken (rhythm, not an essay), land for the historians without losing everyone else, and leave the room with the debt idea rather than with a reading list. British English throughout.
