I'm writing an ideas-section piece about the theory that keeps showing up everywhere — the handicap principle, costly signaling, whatever the right name is. Biologists use it for peacock tails, economists use it for college degrees (is that the Spence thing?), and there's a whole cottage industry now explaining fasting, tithing, Ironmans and $40,000 watches with it. Here's my problem. The way I've got it, the whole point is that a signal is only beleivable if it costs the signaller something: the tail is a burden, the degree is four years you'll never get back, the initiation is genuinely painful. And it's beleivable precisely because a low-quality signaller couldn't afford to fake it. So doesn't it follow that the more wasteful the signal, the more we should trust it? The guy who burns the most money on a wedding is the most genuinely comitted, the religion that demands the most agonizing initiation has the most sincere members, the company that blows the most on a Super Bowl ad must have the best product. A friend of mine who does behavioral ecology says no, that's not it at all, but when I pressed her she just said "it's differential" and changed the subject, and honestly that sounded like a dodge. What am I missing? The whole spine of my piece is "waste is a form of honesty" and I'd rather find out now than from the letters column.
