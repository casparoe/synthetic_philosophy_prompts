Two colleagues of mine, Nadia and Paul, spent most of a long train ride arguing over a proposal Nadia had sketched in a seminar, and I have been turning it over ever since without being able to settle it. I would like you to adjudicate it move by move rather than simply declare a winner, and to say at the end what each of them is missing.

Nadia's proposal was this. Before each general election, every citizen who wants to vote answers a short, publicly published set of questions — not about policies or parties, but about basic matters of fact and reasoning of the sort any attentive person could master in an afternoon (for instance, what a filibuster is, or what a base rate is, or how a marginal tax rate differs from an average one). Those who pass are placed on the voting roll; those who do not may retake the test as often as they like, at no cost, and may vote in local and primary elections in the meantime. Crucially, the questions would be written and periodically revised not by experts or officials but by a citizens' assembly selected by lot, and the passing bar would be set deliberately low — she guessed it would exclude perhaps a tenth of adults.

Paul's first objection was the demographic objection. However minimal the test and however neutrally it is authored, he said, it will be passed at different rates by different groups — the better-educated, the more leisured, the native speakers — so the electorate that emerges will be skewed along exactly the lines that already divide us, and a skewed electorate produces skewed policy. That is disqualifying, he said, whatever the test's content.

Nadia's reply: that argument proves too much. Every selection rule skews the electorate somehow — turnout skews by class and age, and we do not regard universal suffrage as unjust for that reason; jury pools skew too, and we correct for it rather than abolish juries. If the bar really is low and free to retake, the skew may be small, and small skews are the price of every procedure we already accept.

Paul: juries are a bad analogy, because jurors are summoned rather than volunteering, and the stakes are a particular defendant's liberty, which justifies screening for competence; voting is a right of membership, and we do not condition rights of membership on passing tests — we abolished literacy tests precisely because they were the instrument by which a group was excluded, and no amount of redesign changes what a test at the entrance to the franchise is for.

Nadia: the history is a reason for caution, not a proof that no filter can ever be legitimate. The old tests were discriminatory by design and were paired with unlimited discretion in the registrar's office; hers are lottery-authored, published in advance, and mechanical. She said Paul was treating any test as though it inherited the sins of the worst test.

Then Paul changed his ground. Grant the demographics, he said; the deeper trouble is that you cannot specify competence without smuggling in a picture of the good reasoner, and a picture of the good reasoner is a picture of the good citizen, and whoever writes the test decides, in advance and invisibly, whose way of thinking counts. A citizens' assembly is not neutral; it is just a different author of the same smuggling operation.

Nadia: neutrality is a mirage on both sides. Universal suffrage also embeds a conception — that preferences count equally however uninformed they are — and if embedding a conception were fatal, it would be fatal to Paul's position too. The question is which conception is defensible, not whether a conception exists.

Paul's last move: even if the accuracy gains were real, the filter changes the character of the polity. It tells those excluded, in the most public way possible, that their voice is withheld because they are not good enough, and that message corrodes the mutual respect that makes a democracy a democracy — a cost that outweighs any gain in the quality of decisions.

Nadia's last move: that is an expressive objection wearing ethical clothes. A single vote in a national election is already nearly powerless, and we do not think telling citizens that degrades them; and if competence requirements degraded people, driving tests and professional licensing would degrade them too. Paul answered that a driving licence is not a badge of membership in the political community and does not decide who belongs — which, Nadia said, is exactly the point she had been making all along about the difference between having a say and having a weighted say, and there the ride ended.

Please go through the exchange and rate each of the moves — Nadia's proposal, Paul's demographic objection, Nadia's reply, Paul's jury reply, Nadia's history reply, Paul's smuggling objection, Nadia's symmetry reply, Paul's expressive objection, and the two closing moves — from clearly mistaken to clearly right, justifying each rating briefly. Then say who has the better of the argument overall, and identify what each of them is missing that neither saw.
