For thirty years I did experimental work on magnetic materials; since retiring I have been sitting in on a graduate philosophy seminar, mostly in order to be confused in public. Last term we spent an afternoon on the donkey that starves between two identical bales of hay — the case usually labelled Buridan's ass — and two of the regulars, Ines and Ravi, fell into an argument that ran well past the end of the hour. I took notes at the time because I wanted to think it over afterwards, and I still cannot decide who came out ahead. Here is the exchange as I recorded it.

(1) Ines said the case proves nothing because it cannot happen. No physical agent is ever exactly balanced; there is always some asymmetry — a habit, a faint preference, a fluctuation in the nervous system — and the moment you allow any asymmetry at all, the donkey eats. The puzzle looks deep only because we are asked to imagine an agent that cannot exist.

(2) Ravi said that concedes the interesting question rather than answering it. The question is what rationality demands of an agent whose reasons for the two bales are exactly equal. If acting rationally means acting on the balance of reasons, and the balance is exactly zero, then either both acts are licensed or neither is. Ines's nudge makes rationality idle precisely where we wanted it to be decisive.

(3) Ines said Ravi was assuming that rationality must be a function from reasons to a single action. Nothing requires that. A tie means both options are admissible; rationality forbids choosing a worse option, but it does not always single out a best one. The donkey can eat either bale and be perfectly rational, whichever it does. If it starves, the fault lies in its procedure — deliberation that never terminated — not in a violated requirement, since starving is plainly worse than either bale.

(4) Ravi said that last clause gives the game away. In the case as described there are only two options, so starving is not one of them; it is what happens when no option is selected. And if nothing except the reasons can determine the choice, then with the reasons exactly equal whatever eventually moves the donkey is not the balance of reasons, and its motion is an event rather than an action. So either the donkey is not an agent at all, or the tie makes action impossible. He added that she cannot treat starvation as a defect of a procedure while denying that the donkey ever had a third alternative to choose.

(5) Ines said: grant that the description was impoverished. At every moment the donkey does have a third alternative, namely to go on deliberating, and that alternative is dominated — it costs time and, in the limit, costs everything. Restore it and the tie between the bales stops mattering, because starving is worse than either. A finite creature that must spend time deciding will therefore not starve, without anyone ever having to break the tie. Whether it ends at the left bale or the right remains arbitrary, and arbitrary is not the same as irrational.

(6) Ravi said that only moves the problem. You still need the donkey to take the left bale while nothing favours the left bale, and taking either is not something an agent can do; it can only take one. Whatever actually produces the motion is therefore not the balance of reasons, so the final step of the rational act is not itself rational. Calling the leftover arbitrary-but-not-irrational concedes that rational agency runs out just before action begins — and if that is tolerable here, it is tolerable everywhere, since any choice can be redrawn as a tie with an arbitrary tiebreaker bolted on.

I should add that I tentatively offered an analogy from my old field — that a magnet cooled through its ordering temperature must pick a direction although the equations are perfectly indifferent to which — and both of them seized on it in support of opposite conclusions, so I dropped it.

What I want from you is not a general essay on the puzzle but an adjudication of these six moves as they were actually made. Rate each one on a fixed five-point scale — clearly wrong, mostly wrong, mixed, mostly right, clearly right — and justify each rating in two or three sentences, saying what is sound in the move and where it overreaches. Then identify the single hinge: the one move such that, if it falls, the rest of that person's case falls with it, and explain why it is the hinge rather than the others. Then say what both of them are missing, if anything — a distinction, an option, or a premise that neither has noticed. Finally, tell me whether the symmetry-breaking analogy is doing any real work here or whether it is a red herring that both parties were able to exploit only because it is vague.
