In What Is Art? Tolstoy advances an account of art that is, in outline, familiar: art is a human activity in which one person, having experienced a feeling, deliberately transmits it to others by means of external signs, and the others come to share the feeling. What is less often examined closely is the machinery by which he separates genuine art from counterfeit art, and in particular his claim that how far a work succeeds in communicating its feeling depends on three conditions: the individuality of the feeling transmitted, the clarity with which it is transmitted, and the sincerity of the artist, with sincerity assigned by far the greatest weight. Counterfeit art, on this view, arises when artists imitate earlier art or manufacture effects designed to please, and it is marked by the absence of sincerity. Tolstoy then draws a social conclusion: because art is the communication of feeling, its function is to unite people, and the art of an age is to be judged in light of the religious understanding characteristic of that age.

I would like help working through a specific difficulty in this argument. Please treat what precedes as a paraphrase of Tolstoy's position rather than as his exact wording, and reason about the position itself rather than about the paraphrase.

The difficulty is this: sincerity is a property of the artist's relation to the feeling, whereas success in communicating is a property of the audience's response, yet Tolstoy treats the first as the chief determinant of the second. I want to understand how that inference is supposed to run, and whether it can be made to run at all. In your answer, address the following. First, is sincerity meant as a psychological fact about the artist, as an observable feature of the work, or as a normative verdict about the work, and does the argument need it to be one of these rather than another? Second, is the account circular? If a work counts as sincere only because it moves us, and it moves us because it is sincere, the criterion appears to turn in a circle; say whether there is a way out. Third, can a work satisfy the sincerity condition and still fail to communicate, for instance because the feeling is too idiosyncratic, because it is conveyed obscurely, or because the audience is badly positioned, and if so, what does that do to the ranking of the three conditions? Fourth, is sincerity better modeled as a threshold that a work either meets or fails, or as a scalar with diminishing returns, and which model fits Tolstoy's examples of counterfeit art? Finally, does the conclusion that art unites humankind follow from the communication thesis, or is it imported from Tolstoy's religious views and then presented as a consequence of the definition?

Please avoid summarizing Tolstoy's reputation, his verdicts on particular composers and writers, or the standard charge that he was a censorious moralist. I am interested in the internal structure of the criterion and in whether it can bear the weight he places on it.
