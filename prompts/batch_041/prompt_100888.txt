I am a retired experimental physicist who, since leaving the lab, has been reading political philosophy and social choice with more enthusiasm than system. I have been asked to give the after-dinner address at the annual meeting of our national scientific academy. The timing is awkward: tomorrow the membership votes on whether the academy should issue a public statement endorsing a particular direction in climate intervention research. I do not intend to tell anyone how to vote, and I do not want to deliver a lecture on democratic theory. I want a speech that uses the Condorcet jury theorem as its actual spine—not as a decorative reference—to say something honest about when a large body of experts can be trusted to track the truth and when it cannot.

The audience is mixed. There are physical scientists who know probability but not social choice; there are social scientists and philosophers who will notice if I misuse the theorem; there are administrators and donors who will glaze over at technicalities. Write the speech, about eight to ten minutes spoken, in a register that is warm and a little self-deprecating but formally polished. It should be built around the theorem, not around my biography.

Make it in the weeds in the following specific way. Introduce and use a distinction I have not seen put quite this way: between a vote that is a measurement and a vote that is a decision. A measurement vote presupposes a fact of the matter and treats the vote as an instrument for estimating it; a decision vote constitutes the outcome rather than discovering it. The Condorcet theorem is about measurement votes, and most real institutional votes are hybrids. Let that distinction organize the speech.

The speech should also distinguish the aggregation of beliefs from the aggregation of preferences, and explain why the theorem applies to the former and not the latter. It should take seriously the independence assumption and the competence assumption, and should say what happens when votes are correlated—through faction, through social influence, through shared training, or through the fact that different voters are answering slightly different questions. It should address the problem that majority voting on a set of logically connected propositions can yield an inconsistent collective judgment even when each individual is consistent. It should mention strategic voting and the difference between sincere and sophisticated voting, and it should say why the theorem’s optimistic result depends on a kind of sincerity that real institutions rarely guarantee. It should also consider the objection that experts are not a random sample of independent truth-trackers but a community with shared paradigms, and that this cuts both ways.

I would like the speech to end with a practical, non-utopian proposal for how the academy should treat its own votes—something like requiring the membership to be explicit about whether a given vote is a measurement vote, a decision vote, or a hybrid, and about which assumptions are doing the work. Do not cite any sources, do not give a reading list, and do not name-drop. I want the ideas to do the work. The speech should not resolve the climate intervention question; it should give the audience a way to think about the vote without pretending that the theorem settles it.
