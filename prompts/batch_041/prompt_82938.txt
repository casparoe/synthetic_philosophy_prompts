After a career in physiology I have spent several years reading philosophy of mind, and before I commit anything to paper I want an audit of where I have landed on pain. My commitments are these. (1) Pain is at bottom a perceptual state: it represents actual or threatened damage to the body, and in this respect it is continuous with the other senses. (2) The hurtfulness of a pain is not a separable coating laid over a neutral sensation but is internal to it; a state that did not hurt would not be a pain, whatever else it might be. (3) Pain is also essentially motivational: to be in pain is already to be moved to protect the injured part and to want the state to end. (4) I count the patients called asymbolic — who, after certain brain injuries, feel and localize pain and describe it in ordinary terms, yet are untroubled by it and make no protective response — as genuinely in pain, not as misreporting some non-painful state. (5) I take the badness of pain to lie in how it feels rather than in any attitude we adopt toward it; its badness is intrinsic to the experience and not conferred on it by desire or dislike.

Please tell me whether these five hang together. I expect some of them to be in genuine conflict and others only to appear so, and I would rather you separate the two than flag everything. Order the real conflicts by severity, and for each say which commitment must give way and what the revision costs. Then tell me which single revision would clear up the most, and finally what would change your mind: which consideration, if I accepted it, ought to move you to a different verdict, and where your own reasoning is most likely to be mistaken.
