I am a novelist — literary fiction, chiefly, with a recurring weakness for characters standing at the brink of an irreversible decision — and I have spent the better part of a year circling a problem I can only half articulate. In the philosophical literature on what is called transformative experience, there is a position, most closely associated with L. A. Paul's book of that name, according to which certain choices — whether to have a child, whether to accept a vampire's offer, whether to become a surgeon or a soldier — are doubly opaque. They are epistemically transformative, in that one cannot know in advance what the experience will be like; and they are personally transformative, in that undergoing them changes what one values, so that the person who emerges on the far side is not straightforwardly the person now deliberating. On that view, the ordinary machinery of deciding — weighing outcomes by how much one wants them — is supposed to break down, because one cannot assign a value to an outcome one cannot imagine, and because one's present preferences are no reliable guide to one's future ones. I grasp the shape of the argument, but I understand it only as a spectator; my real fluency is in the novelist's vocabulary — character and character arc, point of view, the gap between what the narrator knows and what the character knows, showing and telling, foreshadowing, subtext, the reliable and the unreliable narrator, and the distinction between the order of the story and the order of its telling.

What I should like you to do is restate that position as faithfully as you can in the vocabulary I actually work in, treating my craft terms as a genuine language rather than as ornament, and without smuggling the philosophers' assumptions in under new names. Then give me the residue report, which is the part I care about most: which claims of the position survive the translation intact, which survive only in a distorted form, and which cannot be said at all in the novelist's vocabulary — for instance, whether the decision-theoretic scaffolding (preferences, credences, expected value) has any counterpart in what a novelist means by a character's desire or will, and whether the very notion of an arc quietly presupposes a persisting subject that the position is at pains to deny.

Finally, tell me what you make of the residue: does it expose a genuine gap in the novelist's toolkit, or does it suggest that the philosophical problem is an artefact of a particular standpoint — forward-looking, first-personal, deliberative — which fiction is free to abandon, say by letting the narrator speak from the far side of the transformation? I ask because I suspect my novel keeps failing for a reason the philosophers have already named, and I want to know whether my instinct — that a novel can do what the decider cannot — is a real solution or a sleight of hand.
