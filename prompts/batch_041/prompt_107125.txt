I've been arguing alot with a friend in a stats reading group about the principle of indifference — the rule that says when you've got no reason to favor one possibility over another, split your credence evenly — and I've realized I have pretty firm gut verdicts on a whole pile of cases but no clue what single rule, if any, generates them. So here they are, roughly in order of how sure I am. A fair-looking six-sided die about to be rolled and hidden under a cup: 1/6 a face, obviously fine, and honestly I don't even feel like this is "indifference," the die is just symmetric. A coin I'm about to flip, hidden: 1/2 a side, fine. A spinner — a needle on a pivot, spun — the chance it stops in some arc is proportional to the arc's angle, fine. Now: a machine I know nothing about that's going to output a real number between 0 and 1. A friend says the chance it's below 1/2 is 1/2. I say there's no fact of the matter — "between 0 and 1" doesn't pin down a distribution, and whatever number I gave would be made up. Same verdict for a factory that makes cubes where all I know is the side is somewhere between 0 and 2 cm: chance the side is under 1 cm? He says 1/2 (uniform over side), I say underdetermined; then he says fine, uniform over volume, so it's 1/8, and I want to say both are equally arbitrary. And a chord drawn at random in a circle, chance it's longer than the side of the inscribed equilateral triangle — I've seen the three answers (1/3, 1/2, 1/4) and my verdict is the bare question has no answer until you say how the chord is drawn. Except here's what confuses me: if you say "pick two points independently and uniformly on the circumference and connect them," that's a perfectly good way to draw a chord, it gives 1/3, and I'm totally fine with that question having that answer. So it's not the continuity or the infinity that bothers me.

The case that really trips me up is this pair. A visibly bent coin, no symmetry I can point to, about to be flipped: I feel no pull whatsoever toward 1/2. But two sealed envelopes, one holds a prize and one doesn't, and I'm handed one at random: 1/2 feels right, even though I can't see any symmetry in the envelopes themselves, only in the procedure that handed me one. So my first stab at a principle is: indifference is licensed when there's a procedure whose symmetries I can point to — a spin, a shuffle, a random draw from a well-mixed pool — and not when I'm just staring at a parameter space or a set of hypotheses with no such procedure. On that rule the die, spinner, and envelope cases come out fine, and the machine, cube, and bare-chord cases come out underdetermined.

But two things bother me about it. First, the fair-looking die: I called it fine and the reason is the die's shape, not any procedure — I could be handed a die with no story at all about how it'll be rolled, and I'd still say 1/6. So is shape-symmetry a "procedure"? Second, the cube factory: someone could describe a procedure — pour molten metal into a mold whose size is set by a genuinely random process — that makes the side length uniform, and then I'd be fine with 1/2; and a different procedure that makes the volume uniform, and then I'd be fine with 1/8. So the procedure rule seems to make the answer turn on facts about the world the person asking "what's the chance the side is under 1 cm" never mentioned — which makes me think the cube question really is answerable once you fix the procedure, and my "underdetermined" verdict was only ever about the bare question. So: give me a principle, or a short list of rival principles, that gets my verdicts, including the ones I'm wavering on, and tell me what each says about (a) a shuffled deck of cards versus (b) a hypothesis space like "this coin is either fair or double-headed," where I feel 1/2 is plainly wrong, and (c) the case I keep flip-flopping on — a friend's random triangle, three points picked independently and uniformly on a circle, asking whether the triangle is acute, where I can't decide whether to just accept whatever the uniform procedure spits out. If some of my verdicts have to go, say which, and be honest about whether the principle is doing real work or is just a fancy redescription of "I know it when I see it."
