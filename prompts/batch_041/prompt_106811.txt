I have been reading a popular book on rationality, and it gives the following account of Aumann's agreement theorem. "Aumann's agreement theorem is, at bottom, the claim that two rational agents who share the same information cannot agree to disagree. If each of them is rational, and if each knows the other's opinion, then the two must end up holding the same belief; the result is sometimes given the informal name of the 'no-disagreement theorem.' Where you find two people who are both competent and both sincere and who nonetheless persist in disagreeing — about a scientific question, say, or about whether a defendant is guilty — the theorem tells you that one of them must be reasoning badly, or else that they simply have not compared notes thoroughly enough. In the limit, it implies that open dialogue between rational agents converges: if the parties keep exchanging views and updating on what they hear, they will eventually see eye to eye, and any disagreement that survives that process is evidence that at least one of the parties is being irrational. The one escape hatch is the theorem's assumption that the agents begin from a common prior, the same set of initial probabilities over the possible states of the world. Once you drop that assumption, disagreement becomes possible again, and because the common prior looks like an assumption about how people happen to be wired rather than a requirement of rationality, many theorists treat the whole result as an artifact and conclude that it tells us very little about real disagreement."
