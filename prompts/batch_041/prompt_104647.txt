I spent thirty-five years as a bench chemist and retired four years ago. Since then I have been reading philosophy in earnest, and somewhat to my surprise I now sit on my former institution's research-integrity panel. We meet monthly, we handle perhaps a dozen cases a year, and our findings follow people for the rest of their careers, so every ruling has to survive an appeal read by administrators and clinicians who have no background in logic and who will see only our written rationale.

Our recurring trouble is a single word: knew. The panel slides between two senses without noticing. In the first sense, an author knew something only if it was actually present in the material they held — a notebook entry, a calibration file, an email, a co-author's data sheet. In the second sense, an author knew whatever follows from what they held, whether or not anyone ever drew the inference, and this second sense is the one that the standard treatment of knowledge in epistemic logic appears to license: knowledge closed under known implication, everything entailed by what you know counted as known, and every logical truth known to everyone. As a working scientist that strikes me as plainly false. I have known many things whose obvious consequences I failed to see for years, and I have held two beliefs whose incompatibility I noticed only when a student pointed it out. But the panel cannot simply discard the second sense either, because some failures to draw an inference are precisely what we ought to be censuring — the group that never noticed its own two datasets flatly contradicted each other, the author whose central figure depends on measurements taken during a drift documented in a different section of the same record.

Please write me a decision procedure the panel can actually run, in order, for deciding when it is fair to attribute knowledge of a consequence to a person who never entertained that consequence, and when the attribution is really a stipulation about what they ought to have known rather than a claim about their epistemic state. The procedure must be executable by non-philosophers in under twenty minutes per case, must state what counts as a sufficient answer at each step, must say where the chain of reasoning is too long or too technical for the attribution to stand, and must mark clearly the points where a value judgment about culpability is being smuggled in under the guise of a factual finding about belief. It should also handle the awkward case in which the person demonstrably lacked the concepts needed to formulate the consequence at all, and the case in which the person held an inconsistency and had no procedure for repairing it.

Then run it on three cases: a laboratory whose records separately contain a calibration drift and a set of measurements taken during that drift, where nobody ever joined the two; a senior author who co-signed a paper whose conclusion is the opposite of one in their own earlier paper, in a different context, years before; and a junior researcher whose failure turns on a statistical distinction they had never been taught. At least one of these should come out in a way the panel will find uncomfortable, and I want that discomfort named rather than smoothed over. Finish by telling me what the procedure cannot do, and which of our cases it will simply get wrong.
