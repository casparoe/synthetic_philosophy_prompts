You are an expert in metaethics and the philosophy of taste, with a working knowledge of the psychology of bias. Think carefully, and begin by listing the candidate readings of the slogan before you evaluate any of them.

At a departmental meeting about how our admissions committee reads applications, a senior colleague shut down the discussion with "there's no accounting for taste." His point was that his strong preference for a certain profile of applicant is not something he can be asked to defend or revise, and that treating it as a bias is a category mistake. I suspect the proverb is doing work it cannot support, but I could not produce the disambiguation in the room.

Give me the menu: the distinct precise claims "there's no accounting for taste" could be making, from the trivial to the radical — the gustatory platitude; the claim that taste-attitudes are not truth-apt; the claim that they are not under voluntary control and so immune to demand (and what that owes to "ought implies can"); the response-dependent reading on which a taste-attitude is made correct by how suitable judges would respond; the liberal claim that tastes are simply not to be interfered with; and thoroughgoing relativism about whole evaluative frameworks. Lay these out as a list. For each, say whether it is trivial, substantive and defensible, or false, and whether it survives the shift of object from flavors to persons — a preference for a certain kind of colleague seems to have a track record of getting things right or wrong, and a bias is normally identified by the agent's own second-order standards rather than by anyone else's.

Then say which reading would actually have to be true for my colleague's move to work, and whether any reading makes the preference/bias distinction collapse rather than protect it. State your confidence in each claim and single out the point you are least sure of.
