Roughly, the view she keeps defending is that the only thing that ultimately matters is the absence of craving and of suffering — that pleasure, joy, achievement, and love matter not in themselves but only insofar as they relieve or forestall craving and pain. She calls it tranquilism, and she insists it is not just ordinary hedonism, because she thinks a life of steady, low-key contentment with no craving at all is better than a life of intense highs and lows, even when the second contains more total pleasure. I keep feeling that something must be wrong with this, but every time I push back she has an answer, and I can't tell whether the view is subtle or just slippery.

Please act as a professional philosopher who specializes in axiology — the theory of what is intrinsically good — and write a dialogue between two equally competent interlocutors: one defending the tranquilist position described above, the other defending the rival view that positive states such as pleasure, engagement, and achievement are intrinsically good and not merely the removal of craving. Neither side should be a strawman: each should get the strongest version of its case, and they should genuinely respond to one another's specific points rather than talking past each other. Before you write, think it through to yourself: what is the most damaging objection to tranquilism, and what is the best available reply for a tranquilist? Then build the dialogue around that exchange. I am especially stuck on one puzzle. If only the absence of craving matters, then it seems to follow that someone in dreamless sleep or a coma — where, presumably, no craving occurs — is faring as well as a serene meditator, and perhaps even that ceasing to exist is no worse than living contentedly. I want to watch the tranquilist try to handle that consequence, and the opponent press on whether the attempted reply is actually stable or quietly reintroduces the very positive goods the tranquilist claims to reject. Please write in polished prose and avoid handing me a reading list, citations, or references to specific authors — I can't look anything up right now, and what I really want is the argument itself.
