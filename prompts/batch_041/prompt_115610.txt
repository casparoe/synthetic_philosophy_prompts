Alright, so a bit of background: I'm a retired physical chemist -- kinetics, mostly -- and since I hung up the lab coat I've been reading philosophy of science, and I've gone and talked myself into a position I'd like you to attack.

The position: I now think the so-called Principal Principle isn't just good advice, it's what makes 'objective chance' mean anything at all. Roughly: if you know the objective chance of some event is p, and you have no other relevant information, your credence in that event has to be p. Not should, has to -- if you know the chance and still don't defer, you're just not using the word chance the way everyone else is. Back when I was fitting rate constants this felt trivially true. If the model says the reaction goes through in a given second with probability 0.3 and I've got nothing else to go on, then 0.3 is my degree of belief, full stop. I want to say that's the whole content of 'objective': the chance is the thing it's rational to defer to, and whatever isn't deferrable was never a chance.

So what counts against it? I don't want the standard textbook objections read out to me -- I want to know which ones should actually move someone who thinks deference is constitutive of chance, ranked from fatal to merely awkward. What does the position predict that its rivals don't, and what would we have to observe for it to be wrong?

Heres the case that nags me at night. An oracle tells me, truly, that the objective chance of X tomorrow is 1/2 -- but I also know that the oracle telling me this is itself one of the causes of X. Do I still have to set my credence to 1/2? My gut says no, and if no, then deference was never the constitutive rule I wanted it to be, just a rule that applies when you've been careful about your evidence. Is that right, or is there a principled way to keep the deference and quarantine the oracle?

Related worry, from my old day job: in statistical mechanics we sling probabilities around constantly, and if the underlying dynamics is deterministic then those chances are arguably just our ignorance. If that's the right reading, does my position collapse, or does it just quietly stop applying to those cases? And be honest with me about whether the constitutive version has made itself unfalsifiable -- if it has, is that a defect, or is that just what a constitutive claim is supposed to do?

What would persuade me most is a clean case where deferring to a known chance is demonstrably the wrong thing to do and the defender can only escape by saying well that was never really a chance. If the defender can always say that, tell me so, and tell me whether I should mind.

Hard limit: 700 words. Lead with the single strongest defeater and your verdict on whether it's fatal, then work down. Don't pad it with the usual name-dropping.
