Last week I spent the better part of an evening arguing with an old friend about conscience, and we ended exactly where we began, which is why I am writing to you. The dispute started with a colleague of hers who has asked to be excused from part of his job on grounds he calls moral, and it widened into a general fight about whether the line between a moral commitment and a mere preference survives any scrutiny at all. Her position was the deflationary one: that what we call a conviction is usually a preference that has learned to demand respect, and that the solemnity we attach to conscience is a trick we play on ourselves. Mine was that the distinction must be real, because we plainly do accommodate some refusals and not others, and because if we could not draw the line our institutions would have no principled way to proceed. But when she asked me for the test itself — the thing a neutral third party could actually apply to a hard case — I had nothing. I could only redescribe my conclusion in louder words. I suspect you can do better, and I now need it done properly, because the question has become part of my job.

I chair the accommodation and ethics panel at a mid-sized teaching hospital. Twice a month, sometimes more, we receive a request from a member of staff to be excused from some part of their duties on grounds they describe as conscience: assisting in a procedure, dispensing a medication, taking part in a particular kind of end-of-life care, being assigned to a particular patient. Nearly as often we receive the mirror-image complaint — that a colleague's refusal is not conscience at all but squeamishness, distaste, or prejudice with a moral story wrapped around it, and that the hospital is dignifying a bias by calling it a conviction. The panel is three people, none of us trained in philosophy, and we have to produce written reasons. We have about ten days, sometimes less when a patient's care is waiting, and we cannot always move the requesting staff member to another role or another shift. What we do not have, and what I am asking you for, is any procedure. At present we decide by impression, and I have watched the same facts produce opposite outcomes depending on who happened to be sitting on the panel that week.

Please write me a decision procedure we can actually run. I want the questions in the order we should ask them; for each, what would count as a sufficient answer and what would not; the point, if there is one, at which the procedure returns a flat no and we stop; and the point at which it has to hand the decision back to the panel because what remains is a judgment of value rather than a question of fact. Translate whatever moral psychology and metaethics have genuinely settled about the difference between a preference, a commitment, and a bias into questions a nurse or an administrator can put to a colleague and get an answer to. I am thinking of the distinctions I have half-absorbed and never learned to use: between an attitude a person endorses on reflection and one that merely happens to them; between a preference that tracks reasons and one that is a bare aversion looking for a justification after the fact; between a commitment that would survive the removal of the person's unrelated dislikes and one that is quietly doing their work for them; between what someone would still want under conditions less hostile to wanting it; and between a refusal that costs the refuser something and one that costs only the person refused. I do not want a reading list and I do not want citations — I want the usable version, with the places where the simplification distorts the underlying idea marked as such, and an honest note on which steps are judgment calls dressed up as procedure.

Then run the procedure, in full, on three cases. First: a nurse who asks to be excused from assisting in a procedure she says she cannot in conscience take part in, who is otherwise willing to trade shifts, and whose refusal delays nothing. Second: a senior clinician who says he would rather not be assigned to patients with a particular history, and who describes his reluctance as a matter of moral conviction, though he cannot say what the conviction is beyond the discomfort. Third, and I care most about this one: a request to be excused from a duty that the panel, and I, find morally repellent, where the person's stated grounds are coherent, his sincerity is not in doubt, and excusing him would shift the burden onto a colleague who has already asked to be spared. Tell me which way the procedure sends each, and be willing to send at least one of them where I will not want it to go: I would rather learn now that the procedure tells me to accommodate something I find contemptible, or to refuse something I find reasonable, than discover it the first time a real case lands on my desk. Where the procedure cannot decide, say so explicitly, and tell me what the panel is then actually deciding — a disagreement about facts, about the weight of a value, or about how much institutional burden we are prepared to absorb. Finally, tell me where this procedure will misfire: the cases it will get confidently wrong, and the kind of person it will penalize for being inarticulate about their own commitments.
