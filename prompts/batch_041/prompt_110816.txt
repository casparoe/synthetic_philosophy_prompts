A friend and I spent most of last night arguing about split-brain patients — people whose two cerebral hemispheres have been surgically disconnected from one another — and about what, if anything, such cases reveal about the unity of consciousness. My friend's line was simple: once the connecting fibres are cut, the most economical description of the situation is that two streams of experience now run where one ran before, and since experience is always someone's experience, there are two subjects sharing a single skull. I resisted this, but I could not get my resistance into a form I was satisfied with. Please make the case for the following position as forcefully and rigorously as you can.

The position. Questions about how many minds are present in a split-brain patient have no determinate answer, and not merely because our evidence is limited or our concepts coarse. The count is genuinely unsettled: being a single subject is a matter of the degree to which a system's states are integrated, and where integration is partial there is no fact of the matter about how many subjects there are — not a fact we cannot discover, but no fact at all. To bring this out, consider a dial that continuously varies the bandwidth of the connection between the hemispheres, from complete severance at one end to an intact commissure at the other. My claim is that no setting of the dial marks a sharp transition from one subject to two, and that no setting is privileged as the point at which the truth about the number of subjects becomes definite. It follows that even the ordinary intact brain is only determinately one subject by courtesy: a special case at one end of a continuum rather than the standard against which every other case is measured.

In making the argument, engage at least with the objections that seem to me the most serious. First, that the position is incoherent, because subjects are precisely the entities that must be countable — any experience either belongs to a subject or it does not, and there is no middle state to occupy. Second, that the indeterminacy on offer is merely representational: our concept of a subject may be vague while the underlying facts remain perfectly determinate, so that the position collapses into a claim about language rather than about minds. Third, that the position proves too much, since the same reasoning would license the conclusion that ordinary people contain many minds, or that a person is not determinately one subject even across a single uneventful afternoon. Fourth, that it undermines first-person authority and our ordinary practice of ascribing actions, since a system cannot be held responsible, or even addressed, if it is indeterminate how many agents it contains.

Finally, say what difference the position should make in practice: how we ought to describe split-brain patients to themselves, to their families, and in research reports, and whether anything about the ethics of commissurotomy — or of any future procedure that only partially disconnects a brain — turns on it. Reach a verdict and defend it; do not end by announcing that the question is hard. Write the piece as a continuous argument rather than a survey, and do not worry about naming who said what.
