Suppose we fix the claim precisely enough to be testable: strong cognitive penetration occurs just in case there is at least one confirmed case in which a subject's belief, concept, or expectation changes which properties her perceptual experience represents — its content — as opposed to merely changing her attention, her interpretation, her confidence, or her verbal report.

Give me your credence that strong cognitive penetration occurs, and decompose it. I want separate probabilities for at least: (a) that content is determinate enough here for the claim to have a truth value at all, rather than collapsing into what the subject would say or how she would act; (b) that the usual candidates — memory colour, the perceptual discrimination advantages of expertise, ambiguous-figure flips — survive the deflationary readings on which they are really perceptual learning, response bias, or a shift in decision criterion; (c) that the top-down influence in such cases is genuinely cognitive rather than sourced in other perceptual states, i.e. that perceptual-perceptual penetration is a distinct phenomenon and not the whole story; and (d) that attention, which most parties already concede to be perceptual, does not exhaust the effect.

Then say which term dominates the product, whether your number would move if strong penetration were weakened to the perceptual-perceptual variety, and which single experimental result would shift your credence most.
