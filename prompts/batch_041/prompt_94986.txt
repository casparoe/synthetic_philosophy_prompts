My oldest friend and I have just spent a long weekend arguing, more acrimoniously than either of us would have liked, about whether moral compromise is a form of trade. My position is that it is: when two people who hold different values have to settle on something they will both live with, there is often a package of mutual concessions that leaves each of them better off by their own lights than digging in would have, and that this is precisely why compromise is neither capitulation nor the mere splitting of a difference. His position is that this is a category error: values do not come with exchange rates, so a concession is not a cost that can be set against a benefit, and the whole language of gains from trade quietly presupposes the common currency whose absence is the very thing at issue. We broke off unable to state the other's view without caricaturing it, which usually means a real disagreement is tangled up with a verbal one, and I would rather find out which is which than keep scoring points.

I would like you to draft the joint statement the two of us could both put our names to. Set out what we can affirm together about compromise, about value, and about what people are actually doing when they settle a dispute; then state, in wording that each side would accept as a fair rendering of its own view, exactly where we still differ; then identify the cruxes — the sub-questions such that settling one would move at least one of us — and, for each, the case, argument or piece of evidence we would both agree bears on it, together with what each side predicts about how it comes out. Two things kept surfacing in the argument and I want the statement to take a position on them rather than skirt them. The first is that most of what we call compromise is compromise about joint action under persisting disagreement rather than about belief or value itself, and I suspect the exchange view is at its strongest there while his objection bites hardest on the idea of trading values directly. The second is my claim that a compromise can be worth making as insurance against being wrong — I concede on a point I may be mistaken about in return for his conceding on one he may be mistaken about — to which he replied that insurance requires a probability and a measurable loss, and a measurable loss is exactly what he denies we have here. Do not simply report what various schools say; I want your own drafting, and I want you to commit: come down on which of our disputes is genuine and which was about words, and give your reasons for the verdicts you reach.

Write it as continuous prose, the way a joint open letter or op-ed would read — no headings, no numbered lists, no bullet points, no bold text and no tables. Use British English throughout.
