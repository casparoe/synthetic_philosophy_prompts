I lead a fifty-minute discussion section for a third-year undergraduate course in philosophy of biology. The students are mostly biology majors taking the course as an elective, and they have had exactly one lecture on the topic. That lecture introduced the contrast between an etiological account of biological function, on which a trait's function is the effect for which it was selected, and a causal-role account, on which a trait's function is the contribution it makes to a capacity of the system that contains it. The whole lecture ran on the heart — the heart's function is to pump, a heart can malfunction — and the students came away believing that the two accounts are competing definitions of one word, so that the disagreement is terminological and can be settled by deciding which usage is tidier.

I want the section to dislodge that belief. Do not explain either account to me; plan the explanation.

1. What must be in place before the students can see that the two accounts are answering different questions rather than giving different answers to the same question? Give the order in which the components have to be introduced, with the dependencies made explicit — what must be understood before what, and why that order rather than the reverse.

2. The heart has been spent and I want a different carrying example. My candidate is this case, which I can put on the board in three sentences: a made-up beetle whose wing case carries a lattice of ridges; the ridges are a byproduct of how the case hardens, every individual of the species has them and no heritable variation in them has ever existed, so nothing has ever been selected for them; in the beetle's habitat the ridges condense dew and channel it to its mouth, and a beetle whose ridges are scraped away dies of thirst. Tell me whether this case can carry the session, what it would have to have and lack in order to do so, and — if it cannot carry the session by itself — what it must be paired with, where in the sequence the foil belongs, and what that foil must accomplish that the beetle cannot.

3. The misconceptions I should expect, in the order the students will actually form them, each with the exact point in the sequence where it should be headed off. I already anticipate that they will conclude the etiological account is refuted by the beetle, since it denies a life-saving trait a function, and that they will read the causal-role account as the bare claim that "function" means current usefulness. Tell me which further misconceptions I am not anticipating, and where each one enters.

4. One closing question I can put to the room that would show whether they have grasped the distinction rather than memorized the slogans.

5. What I should decline to take up at this level, and say so explicitly. I have in mind the relativity of function to a beneficiary or a level — whose capacity, whose good — but tell me whether that is the right thing to cut or precisely the wrong one.
