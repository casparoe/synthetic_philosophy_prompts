I'm working on a seminar paper about vagueness, and a friend and I have been going back and forth about whether the split between epistemicism and supervaluationism is as deep as it's usually presented. My rough grasp: the epistemicist says a vague predicate has a fully precise extension, so there is a fact about whether any given borderline item falls under it, but that fact is in principle unknowable; the supervaluationist says there is no such fact, so a borderline predication is neither true nor false, even though it comes out true (supertrue) that some cutoff or other exists. What I want from you is not another restatement of that contrast but a concrete example of a conceptual issue that actually pulls the two views apart — a specific case, worked through, in which they issue different verdicts about the same sentence, together with an account of why the difference matters rather than being a mere bookkeeping dispute.

To keep us away from the grains-of-sand sorites that everyone reaches for, let me hand you a predicate from a domain I actually know something about. In software, people argue about whether some observed behavior is "a bug" or "a feature," and both labels are plainly vague: there are behaviors that sit in the disputed middle, and behaviors that some people insist are neither. Suppose we treat "bug" and "feature" as a contrast pair — a behavior is a bug if it's a defect, a feature if it's intended or at least tolerated — and let a particular behavior be one of these middling cases. Give me an example of a conceptual issue here, ideally pinned to a particular sentence or inference about that behavior (a negation, a disjunction, a conditional, or a claim that some first such behavior exists), that epistemicism and supervaluationism would handle differently; then say which treatment you find more defensible and on what grounds. If the way I've framed this smuggles in a false dichotomy, mischaracterizes one of the two positions, or if the bug/feature case turns out not to separate them at all, I'd rather you say so plainly than manufacture an example to fit the request — a clean answer that papers over a malformed question is worth less to me than being told the question needs reworking.
