I'm presenting at our game theory reading group next Thursday and I want a Q&A prep sheet. The room: mostly applied micro people who run lab experiments and care about whether a change in assumptions moves an equilibrium prediction, two philosophers of economics who read the formal literature closely, and one logician who does proof theory and will not let a hand-wavy definition slide. Roughly twelve people, and this crowd treats common knowledge as a harmless idealization you tack on at the end of a model, so they're going to be irritated that I'm trying to make it carry the whole load.

The thesis I'm defending: in the standard examples everyone uses (coordinated attack, backward induction in long finite games, the usual stories about why coordination can fail when everyone knows the relevant facts), common knowledge of rationality is doing almost none of the work. What's actually doing the work is common knowledge of the *state space* — of what each player treats as the set of relevant contingencies, i.e. which features of the situation they're even partitioning on. Once you make the state space explicit, you can drop common knowledge of rationality in most of these cases without losing the prediction, and the coordination failures people attribute to missing common knowledge are really failures of a shared state space. So my claim is that the interesting epistemic condition was never rationality, it was relevance.

Give me the 7-8 objections I will actually get from this room, ordered by how much damage they do (not just how likely), each phrased the way one of these three groups would actually put it — the experimentalists will push on whether any of this is testable or whether I've just changed the words, the phil-of-econ people will ask whether I've merely relabeled the problem, the logician will go straight at my definition of the state space and at whether common knowledge of it is even well defined when the state space itself is what's in question. For each objection: a reply I can give in about two minutes, and an honest note on where that reply is soft. Tell me which ones I should just concede up front to save time, and which single objection I have to survive for the paper to stand. And finish with the one question I'm hoping nobody in the room asks.
