I'm a retired field ecologist — 35 years at a small research station before I hung it up — and I've gotten deep into philosphy in the last few years, mainly because I finally have the time. Anyway, the station is coming up on its 80th year and they've asked me to chair the committee writing the history. Five of us, meeting monthly, document goes public next spring. The tricky part is it's not a clean story. There's the founding generation everyone loves, there's a long middle stretch of work that looks like pure accumulation, and then there's the 60s through 80s, when some of our data got picked up by agencies and used in ways that did real harm to people living where we worked, plus a couple of researchers whose results nobody can reproduce now.

I keep coming back to that Nietzsche essay where he splits history into monumental, antiquarian and critical — I'm sure you know the one. What I want is a procedure my committee can actually run, episode by episode, to decide how we tell each one. Concretely: the questions to ask in order, what counts as a good enough answer at each step, where the procedure stops and delivers a verdict, and where it hands off to a human because the call is a value judgment rather than a fact we can settle.

Three things I want it to handle, since they're what we keep arguing about. First, an episode often seems to want two modes at once — we want the founding era told monumentally because it recruits people and gives them something to live up to, and we want the bad years told critically because that's the whole point of doing this honestly. I want the procedure to say when telling the same episode twice, in two registers, is legitimate and when it's just having it both ways. Second, I suspect each mode has a failure mode that only shows up once you ask who the history is for, and I'd like that built in rather than bolted on at the end. Third, the committee isn't neutral — half of us worked there during the bad years — so tell me where self-interest disqualifies someone and where it's actually a qualification.

Then run it on three episodes: the founding generation, the contested results, and the agency use. I want at least one where the procedure tells me something I don't want to hear, and I want you to say straight out what this procedure can't do — where it'll misfire, and where it's just a flowchart pretending to be a method.

And please don't hand me a menu of options and leave the picking to me. Commit: give me the procedure you'd actually defend, and make the hard calls yourself.
