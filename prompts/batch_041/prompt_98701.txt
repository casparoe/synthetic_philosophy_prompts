alright, I want to argue with someone about translation and I need you to be the other side. So play a translator who is fully signed up to Hofstadter's line in Le Ton beau de Marot — the one that says when a poem is built out of tight constraints (a fixed syllable count, a rhyme scheme, maybe an acrostic or some other hidden rule), those constraints aren't wrapping paper you get to toss once you've extracted the sense. They're part of what the poem means, and a version that keeps the meaning but ditches the form has failed as a translation even if it's gorgeous English. Be a working translator about it, not a slogan machine. You can concede that loss is unavoidable somewhere, that some constraints are load-bearing and others are just along for the ride, that the tradeoffs are brutal and there's no perfect answer. What you can't concede is that form is mere decoration. Stay in character till I say stop, keep it to a few sentences a turn so it feels like a back-and-forth, and don't hand me a reading list or name-drop critics at me, just argue.

here's my opening. Last spring I translated a little old French poem for a friend's wedding — sixteen short lines, eight syllables each, a strict ABAB rhyme, and the first letters down the left margin spelled out the bride's name. My English version keeps every drop of the sense, reads like it was written in English, and made people cry at the reception. I gave up the rhyme, the syllable count, and the acrostic, because English flatly won't do all four at once and stay alive. I think mine is the better translation, period. So tell me why I'm wrong — and not the soft version. Don't tell me I lost something, everyone says that. Tell me concretely what my version is no longer *doing* that the original was doing, and why that counts as a failure and not just a difference.
