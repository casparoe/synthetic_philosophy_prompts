I've got a half-formed idea that I need to turn into a two-page proposal for a one-year fellowship, and I'm too close to it to tell whether its a real project or just a clever-sounding reframe. So please write the proposal for me.

The topic is middle knowledge -- the Molinist claim that God knows, prior to any decree, what any possible free creature would freely do in any circumstances. The standard objection, as I understand it (I'll add the citations myself, don't worry about them): those counterfactuals have to be true before anything is decreed, but it's hard to say what makes them true, since there's nothing in the creature yet -- it hasn't been created -- and nothing in God's will, because the whole point is that the choice is free and so isn't settled by God. My hunch is that this framing is a red herring and the real trouble is upstream, in the semantics rather than the metaphysics. Counterfactuals are true when the consequent holds in the most similar worlds, but if the creature's choice is genuinely undetermined then for any full specification of its circumstances there are worlds equally similar to the actual world in which it chooses differently, and the semantics has nothing to pick out. On that reading, the Molinist's counterfactuals of freedom quietly need an assumption of counterfactual definiteness -- the same thing that shows up as a hidden assumption in arguments about Bell's theorem, where people notice you can't assume an unmeasured particle would still have had a definite spin in the direction nobody checked. I don't know if that analogy is deep or just cute, and I don't know whether the objection, if it works, is fatal, or whether a Molinist can just swap in a semantics built on dispositions or propensities and carry on.

What I want from the proposal: get the question to the point where it has a determinate answer -- roughly, does the truth of a counterfactual of freedom require a fact about what an unactualized free creature would do, and can that fact be had without assuming determinism? Explain why the existing positions don't settle it. Say what the actual work looks like month by month -- and I want this to be philosophy with a test case in it, not abstract haggling, so use the Tyre and Sidon bit in the gospels (Jesus saying those towns would have repented if they'd seen what he did there) as the running example, since it's the case people keep reaching for. Tell me what a result looks like: either a version of middle knowledge that survives the objection, or a demonstration that the theological work Molinism is supposed to do -- God providentially ordering a world of free creatures without authoring their sin -- can be done without it, and say which of those outcomes would count as the project failing. And be honest about scope: one year, one person, no archives, and I'm not a biblical scholar, so tell me what to cut if it gets too big.
