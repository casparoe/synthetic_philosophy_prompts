The position I hold is this. Skolem's paradox is not a paradox. There appears to be a contradiction because a first-order theory of sets proves the existence of uncountable sets, and yet, if it has any model at all, it has a countable one. But the appearance dissolves once one is careful about the relationship between a theory, its models, and the language in which we state facts about both. To say that a set is uncountable is to say something relative to a model, with quantifiers ranging over that model's domain: inside the countable model there is no bijection between the supposedly uncountable set and the model's natural numbers, and from outside there is. The Löwenheim-Skolem theorem then tells us only that no first-order theory can pin down the intended interpretation of uncountability, which is just the familiar lesson about the expressive limits of first-order logic. Nothing further needs explaining, and the residual unease is the unease of someone who has not yet made the object-language/metalanguage distinction. Call this the dissolution view.
