I've been writing fiction for twenty-odd years now — five novels, a couple of story collections, and a translator in the one I'm finishing, an Argentine woman in her sixties re-translating a poet she's been living with for decades — so translation has been on my mind, and at dinner last month my brother-in-law, who works on machine translation, got going about Douglas Hofstadter's old complaint that these systems don't understand anything, that they just spit out statistically likely strings and that fluency is cheap. I got the gist and none of the machinery, and I've been chewing on it ever since. What I want from you is as faithful a restatement as you can manage of that position — the case that a system can turn out fluent translations without understanding what it's translating — in the vocabulary I actually work in: voice, register, subtext, what a line is doing underneath what it says, the difference between a sentence that sounds like something a person would say and a sentence that is a person saying something, the ear you develop for when a character's dialogue has gone false on you even though every word is grammatical and in character. Those are my technical terms, I'm fluent in them and hopeless in yours, so treat "statistical," "distribution," "training data" as words I don't own. If something can't be said in my language, don't let it back in through the window and call it a translation — find the nearest thing I'd actually say and flag that it's an approximation.

Then the part I actually care about, which is the residue. Tell me which of his claims come across cleanly into craft terms, which ones only survive if you distort them a little, and which have no counterpart at all in how I think about language — and then, for that last pile, tell me whose problem it is. Is there something in my vocabulary that simply can't see what he's pointing at? Or is his argument less substantial than it sounds once you strip the apparatus, i.e. is he just saying in math-flavored language what I'd say as "that line isn't true"? I'm suspicious of both answers, so don't just pick the flattering one, and if the translation only goes through because you've quietly imported some assumption of your own — that meaning just is a spread of probabilities over what comes next, or whatever — say so out loud rather than letting it ride.

And write it as plain prose, please — no headings, no numbered lists, no bullet points, no bold, no tables, just paragraphs, the way you'd write a long letter to somebody who asked you a real question. I read these things on my phone at night with the lights off, so it needs to flow and it needs to hold up if I stop in the middle of it and come back the next day.
