I have been developing a distinction in my seminar that I am no longer confident survives scrutiny, and I would like you to explain the conceptual issue it is meant to capture rather than to defend my terminology. We begin from a familiar thought: some commitments function as hinges. They are not the conclusions of any inquiry we could actually carry out, they are not open to doubt within the practice they make possible, and yet we do not arrive at them by first establishing them. Now suppose we separate two ways such a commitment might be held. A hinge is inherited when it is absorbed through upbringing, training, or immersion in a form of life, so that the question of whether to accept it never seriously arises for the person who holds it. A hinge is achieved when it is taken up as a deliberate methodological commitment: a researcher resolves to treat the uniformity of nature as settled for her purposes, or resolves to treat the consensus of some designated body of experts as beyond challenge in her reasoning, having first encountered reasons to doubt both.

What I want explained is whether this distinction marks a real difference in rational standing or collapses under pressure. The pressure comes from two directions. First, if an achieved hinge can be justified by reasons, then either the chain of reasons terminates somewhere, in which case we seem to be back at an inherited hinge, or it does not, in which case the commitment was never a hinge at all. Second, if an achieved hinge really is closed to doubt, it is hard to see how the considerations that motivated adopting it could ever suffice to dislodge it, which makes the adoption look like an arbitrary act rather than a rational one.

Please explain the issue as follows. First, state what is genuinely at stake in the distinction and why it is not merely terminological. Second, give the strongest case that achieved hinges are answerable to reasons in a way inherited hinges are not, and say what account of reason-responsiveness would have to be true for that case to work. Third, give the strongest case that the distinction collapses, and say what the collapse implies for how we should describe the certainty of ordinary inherited hinges. Finally, say which argument or consideration would change your mind about which side is right, and identify the point in your own reasoning where you would expect it to fail first.
