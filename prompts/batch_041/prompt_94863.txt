I've been carrying around a version of the Epicurean argument for some time now — not just the conclusion that death is nothing to us, since we and it never coexist, but what I take to be the principle underneath it, which I've been calling the Datability Requirement: a person is harmed only if there is some time in that person's life at which they are worse off than they would otherwise have been. On that principle the Epicurean conclusion falls out quickly — after death there is no such time, so death is not a harm to the one who dies, and the fact that a longer life would have contained more of what is good is a fact about a possible life rather than a deprivation suffered by anyone. What I like about stating it this way is that it isn't a claim about death specifically but a general claim about the grammar of harm, and it generates verdicts across a lot of territory — unknowing betrayals, goods that never arrive and are never missed, the asymmetry between our attitudes toward the time before our birth and the time after our death — which is exactly why I want it stress-tested rather than merely defended. I should say that I came to this from the rationalist blogs rather than from a seminar, so I would genuinely rather discover that a view I hold is circular or unfalsifiable than keep it around because it is consoling.

So: what would count against it? I am not asking for a reading list; I want a map of the exposure, ranked by how decisive each item should be for someone who currently holds the view. Which cases generate a verdict I would have to swallow and cannot — the person whose life's work is undone and their name ruined after they die, the life that is going well and is cut off in the middle, the project abandoned one step short of the result that would have vindicated it; which rival accounts of welfare, if true, would leave the Datability Requirement not false but question-begging, since any view on which the bearer of harm is a whole life rather than a time-slice of one simply denies my premise; which of the standard pressure points are genuine defeaters and which are just my own view's predictions being restated back at me; and what the view predicts that its rivals do not — where the deprivation account and the Datability Requirement issue different verdicts about the same case, what the case is, and whether there is any way for me to tell which verdict is right rather than merely which I find more bearable.

To be concrete about what would move me: I would give up the view if someone could show me a case in which I am forced to say that a person was harmed, and forced not by some general theory of welfare I have already accepted but because the judgment is inescapable on its own terms, and I suspect the strongest candidates are the posthumous undoing of a life and the death that arrives precisely because things were going well. I want to know whether my resistance to those cases is a considered judgment or the view quietly protecting itself. So rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, tell me which of them a defender of the Datability Requirement would themselves concede count against it rather than only which ones critics press, and finish with an honest verdict on whether the view risks anything at all — and if it turns out to be insulated by definition, whether that is a defect in a view of this kind or simply what a claim about the concept of harm is supposed to look like.
