I am a retired biochemist who, since leaving the bench, has taken up moral philosophy as a serious amateur; for the past eighteen months I have also served as a community member on our regional health authority's ethics committee. The committee is revising the mass-casualty triage annex to our crisis-standards-of-care plan, and I have been asked to draft the short written opinion I will circulate before Thursday's meeting. The draft annex states that any clinician taking part in triage must allocate strictly by the protocol's clinical criteria, must not let a personal relationship affect an allocation, and must declare to the incident commander any family, romantic, or close collegial relationship with a patient, so that the clinician can be reassigned. One member holds that this clause is not merely unworkable but ethically objectionable: it tells a clinician who is also a parent that if she is to save her own child she must do so under an impersonal permission to save one's own, and it treats loyalty itself as a contaminant to be declared and quarantined. Another member answers that the whole point of triage is to replace exactly this kind of partiality with a rule everyone can accept in advance, and that any exception will either be abused or corrode the trust the plan depends on. I need the committee's opinion, not a survey: a concrete recommendation — retain the clause, amend it in a specified way, or replace it — with a time frame (we have a regional drill in eight weeks and the annex goes to the board the month after), and a fair statement of the strongest consideration on the side I decide against.

The philosophical snag, as I understand it, is that a policy can be impartial as a public rule while the motive that actually moves a person is partial, so the real dispute is whether this clause legislates conduct or motivation. I have been reading around the objection that impartialist morality asks of the agent one thought more than love itself requires — that the fact that this is my child must be accompanied by the further thought that saving one's child is here permitted — and that this extra thought alienates the agent from the very commitment that gives her life its shape. I would like you to tell me where that objection bites in this concrete policy and where it does not, since a rule addressed to an incident commander may be doing something quite different from a rule addressed to a parent's conscience. Please take seriously the failure modes the dissenter worries about — the clinician who is the only person present able to assess her own child quickly, the family that has no clinician in it, the commander who must act in the first hour before anyone has declared anything — and say how the clause should handle each. For what it is worth, my own instinct is that a disclosure requirement is defensible while a mandatory recusal is not, but I would rather be shown that I am wrong than have it flattered.

I do not want citations, a reading list, or a tour of the literature. Write it as a decision memo my colleagues can vote on, in plain prose, with a clear bottom line and reasons they could actually respond to.
