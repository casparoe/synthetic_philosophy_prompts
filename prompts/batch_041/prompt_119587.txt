In a seminar on Machiavelli's political thought, a classmate tried to settle a long argument about the relationship between fortune and virtù by invoking the proverb that fortune favors the bold. His gloss was that Machiavelli thinks success in politics is mostly a matter of daring: the cautious prince is the one who loses, and virtù just is the willingness to act decisively when others hesitate. I suspect this is too simple, but I cannot articulate why, and I have to bring a position to office hours. Lay out the distinct precise claims that the proverb could be making when it is used in this context. What kind of favor is fortune supposed to grant, to what kind of boldness, and with what relation to virtù? Consider, for instance, whether it asserts a causal law, a probabilistic tendency, a piece of advice about how to interpret defeats, or something else entirely. For each reading, say whether it is a platitude, a substantive and defensible thesis, or something Machiavelli would reject, and explain why. Then tell me which reading my classmate probably meant, and whether that reading actually supports his conclusion that caution is the main vice in politics. I would especially like you to address the tension between the impetuous advice of The Prince and Machiavelli's praise of Fabius Maximus's delaying tactics in the Discourses, since that seems to cut against the proverb. Give me a menu I can bring to office hours, not a single interpretation.
