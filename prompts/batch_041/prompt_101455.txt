For most of my working life I was a physiologist; I came to philosophy only after retiring, and I am conscious that I am still an amateur at it. So I offer the following as a question from a beginner, though I believe it is a genuine one.

I have been reading about pain asymbolia, the syndrome in which patients — usually after damage to the parietal region on one side, sometimes reaching the insula — tell you, perfectly calmly, that the pinprick hurts. They can say where it is and how intense it is. But they do not withdraw, they do not grimace, and some of them appear mildly amused by the proceedings. Here, it seems to me, is a case in which the sensation is intact and the badness seems to have gone missing.

That case sits right at the centre of a dispute that I keep expecting to be settled and find is not. On one side are those who hold that the badness of pain is intrinsic to the experience itself — that to be in pain is already to be in a state that is bad for the one in it, and that any aversion, any desire to make it stop, is downstream of that badness rather than the source of it. On the other side are those who hold that the badness is not built into the sensation at all, but is a matter of the subject's attitude: pain is bad because and insofar as it is unwanted, and the sensory component and the aversive component are separable in principle and often in fact. Each side, as far as I can tell, treats the asymbolia cases as confirming its own view, and neither has moved in some time.

My working hypothesis — and I offer it as a hypothesis, to be discarded if it does not fit what you know — is that a good part of this exchange is verbal, turning on an unacknowledged disagreement about what is to count as pain in the first place: the raw sensory event, or the whole experience including its motivational character. If that is right, then once the term is fixed the remaining dispute may be a narrower and more tractable question about which of two things bears the badness. But I may be wrong; it may instead be a disagreement about which cases are data, or a hidden empirical question about what the relevant neural machinery is doing, or two camps answering different questions under one word.

I would like a diagnosis rather than a verdict, and I would rather have your reasoning than a survey. I cannot look anything up, so please do not give me citations or a reading list; argue from the cases and from what the positions themselves commit their holders to. Please structure your answer as follows.

The dispute, restated (about 200 words). Set out the two positions and the asymbolia evidence as you understand them, so that I can check your reconstruction against mine before reading further.

Where the disagreement actually lies (about 500 words). Give me your diagnosis. Is this verbal, substantive, or a case of two questions wearing one name? If there is a hidden empirical or formal question doing the work, identify it. If it comes down to starting intuitions that neither side can argue for, say so plainly and say which intuitions.

What each side must say about the cases (about 400 words). Take the asymbolia patients, and at least one other sort of case if you think it bears — congenital indifference to pain, or the ordinary experience of enduring a pain one has decided to tolerate — and say what each position is forced to claim about it. Where does a position have to deny the appearance, and where does it have to redescribe the case rather than explain it?

What would count as progress (about 300 words). Given your diagnosis, name the distinction, experiment, or change of question that would move things, and be honest if you think nothing will.

What the diagnosis leaves open (about 150 words). Say what your diagnosis does not settle and where you are least confident.
