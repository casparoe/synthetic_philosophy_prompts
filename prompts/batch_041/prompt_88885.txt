I'm setting the metaphysics final for my upper-level seminar (a few third-years, mostly fourth-years, two grad students auditing) and I want one long essay question on the identity of indiscernibles that they can't get through just by reciting Black's two spheres back at me. The crux I care about is formulation: make them state the principle so it comes out neither trivial nor obviously false -- i.e. they have to notice that if you count properties like "being identical with sphere A" it's true for free, and if you restrict it to qualitative properties the whole thing is up for grabs. Then make them say where the thought experiment actually gets its modal force, and whether being able to describe such a world is enough to make it a real possibility.

After that, hit them with a variant they can't have read about: at t1 the world contains two qualitatively identical iron spheres; at t2 the description of the world is exactly the same except the two labels are swapped -- no other matter, no absolute positions, nothing anywhere that could mark the difference. Is there a fact of the matter about whether they swapped or stayed put, and what does their answer commit them to about the principle and about haecceitism? And make them deal with a classmate who objects "but one of them is identical to A and the other isn't, so they're discernible after all."

90 minutes, 25 marks, hardest question on the paper. Give me the question as the students would see it, then a model answer of around 600 words, then a marking scheme: five or six must-hit moves with the marks for each, and for each one a line on the mistake that usually costs marks. I want an A answer to have the impure-property point, the modal point, and a definate line on the swap; a B does Black faithfully and then flounders. And don't ask them for references -- they can't look anything up in the exam.
