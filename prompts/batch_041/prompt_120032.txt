I'm a second-year PhD student in an RL lab with a side interest in philosophy of mind, and there's a pattern in our practice I can't square.

We routinely add penalty terms to the reward function for states we'd call aversive in an animal: a small negative reward for each timestep a walking agent's torso dips below a height threshold, a larger one for dropping the object, a very large one for letting a simulated patient's vitals cross a danger line. We tune the magnitudes, compare runs, and say the agent "learns to avoid" falling, "doesn't like" dropping things, that the penalty "teaches it to be careful." One postdoc went further and calibrated our fall penalty as a function of fall severity against the shape of the intensity–response curves that show up in rodent aversion studies, so that "its dislike of falling is at least on the same scale as a rat's dislike of a shock." He was half joking, but the calibration is real and the agent's behavior tracks it smoothly. Meanwhile the penalty is applied per timestep, so the very same fall is a serious matter to a patient agent and nearly nothing to an impatient one — which is an odd thing to say about a harm.

In the same breath, everyone insists the agent feels nothing: a reward is just a scalar, there's "nobody home," and asking whether it suffers is a category error.

I want an essay on what our practice presupposes and whether the two halves can be held together. What has to be true of a reward signal for it to count as a valence at all, and does the calibration move presuppose that it is one, or merely gesture at it? Where does the practice locate the badness of a negative reward — in the designer's intent, in the policy's causal role, in the signal's functional profile, in its relation to the agent's organization? And what would have to change if we took either half seriously at the expense of the other?

Skip the survey of AI-consciousness debates; I want an argument about what the engineering is committed to.
