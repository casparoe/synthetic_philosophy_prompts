I am a high school junior, and our philosophy club spends each spring on a unit about adversarial collaboration -- the practice in which two researchers who disagree publicly commit to a joint statement of what they agree on, what they still disagree about, and what evidence would move the dispute. Each of us has to produce one such document, and mine is due on the club's longest-running argument.

That argument is about Benjamin Libet's readiness-potential experiments and what, if anything, they show about free will. Two of our members have gone back and forth all year. The first holds that the experiments show the conscious intention to act is not what starts the action: the brain has already committed before the person is aware of deciding, so the feeling of willing is at best a narrator and at worst an illusion. The second holds that the experiments leave free will untouched, because they time an urge during an arbitrary, reasonless act, and free will is about whether an action flows from the agent's own reasons and character, not about when a feeling shows up on a clock.

Both members already accept the following, so please treat it as shared ground rather than something to litigate: that the readiness potential is detectable only after averaging many trials; that it builds for roughly half a second before the movement, while the reported moment of conscious intention comes only about two hundred milliseconds before it, so the neural build-up precedes the reported intention by a few hundred milliseconds; that single-trial prediction of the movement from the potential alone is weak, and that the potential does not by itself reveal which movement is coming; that participants who have already begun to build a potential can still call the movement off shortly before it happens; and that the reported moment of intention is itself a judgment, possibly reconstructed after the fact rather than read off directly.

What I need from you is the document these two could both sign. Please include, in this order: (1) a numbered list of claims both sides can affirm, substantive rather than truistic, and worded so that neither side has to abandon its position in order to sign; (2) a table of the genuine disagreements, each written in wording its own side would accept as fair, and for each one a note on what that side would have to count as evidence or argument against itself; (3) the cruxes -- the sub-questions such that settling one would move at least one side -- and for each crux a case or experiment both sides agree bears on it, with each side's prediction recorded in advance; and (4) a brief judgment on which of the disagreements, if any, are merely verbal, and why.

To keep this from collapsing into the usual rehash, please organize it around three questions instead of one: whether the conscious intention initiates the action, whether the neural build-up already specifies which action is coming, and whether the power to veto a prepared action counts as agency in the same sense as initiating one. For the crux you consider most decisive, use this case: a person whose motor cortex is being recorded while they deliberate over a choice that actually matters to them and turns on weighing reasons -- say, whether to report a friend for cheating -- rather than a choice between two identical buttons. Both sides must state what they predict and what result would embarrass them.

Finally, please do not simply lay out the options and leave the choice to me. Take a definite position: tell me which crux you think is real, which of the two members you find better supported by the evidence as it stands, and what would change your mind. I would rather have your committed judgment, with its reasons, than a neutral survey.
