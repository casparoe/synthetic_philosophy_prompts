I teach a graduate seminar on collective intentionality, and I want to build one session around a diagnosis rather than a survey. My students can recite the standard menu — shared intentions assembled out of individual intentions plus mutual responsiveness, primitive we-intentions, joint commitment, the we-mode — without being able to say what any of it is meant to do. So I plan to hand them my own verdicts on a run of cases and ask them to find the principle behind them. Before I do, I want to see how the exercise goes, which is what I am asking you for.

Here are my verdicts.

1. Two friends walk to lunch together, matching pace, one slowing when the other stops to tie a shoe. Joint action, and the paradigm of it.

2. Two strangers on a crowded sidewalk each drift to avoid a collision, each adjusting to the other, neither acknowledging the other. Not joint action — mere coordination.

3. Two drivers arrive at a four-way stop at the same moment. Each watches the other, each waits, each proceeds only once the other has clearly yielded. Not joint action, even though each is exquisitely responsive to the other and each knows the other is being responsive to him.

4. A jazz duo improvising: no score, no plan, no words, no prior agreement about what is coming. Each listens and answers. Joint action — to my ear as much a paradigm as case 1.

5. A flash mob whose members have never met, cued by a text message at 3:00 p.m. Joint action.

6. A market. Thousands of people pursuing private ends produce a highly ordered outcome that none of them intended as such. Not joint action, and not because of the numbers.

7. Two people carry a table upstairs. One is doing it because the other ordered him to, and would walk away if he could. Still a joint action, I think, though a miserable one.

Cases 3 and 4 are the pair I cannot get to sit still together, and cases 5 and 6 are a second such pair.

The principle I have been working with, which I suspect is too strong: an episode is a joint action just in case each participant intends the whole activity and not merely his own contribution — the content of each intention is a we, rather than an I plus a prediction about the other fellow. I believe this breaks somewhere, though I am not certain it breaks where I think it does.

What I want from you: (a) a principle, stated precisely enough that I could apply it to cases I have not listed, that captures as many of my verdicts as possible; (b) for each clause, the case that forces it, so I can see what I would lose by dropping it; (c) the verdicts your principle returns for two cases I have not given you — a person and a language model co-writing a paper, where the model has no intentions at all, and two correspondence chess players who never meet and who each suspect the other is a program; (d) an honest accounting of which of my seven verdicts your principle cannot accommodate, and whether you would rather keep the verdict or the principle; and (e) whether what you have offered is a criterion for joint action or only for shared intention, and whether it draws a line or a gradient. Do not survey the literature; I know where the usual positions stand. I want the principle, and the price of holding it.
