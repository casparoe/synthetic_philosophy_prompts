OK so I've ended up believing a fairly specific thing about where meaning lives, and I want you to try to knock it down. I got the germ of it from GEB originally, the record-and-grooves bit, but I've pushed it further than I think the book does.

The picture is roughly this. A message doesn't carry its meaning inside it the way a sealed jar carries a smell. What makes some physical thing a message is that its structure lines up — isomorphism-wise — with some structure out in the world, and that lining-up is the meaning. But alignment alone isn't enough: you also need the receiver to know how to use the thing at all. So I split any message into a frame part (the cues that tell you "this is a message, here's how to decode it") and an inner part (the content you get once you're actually decoding). The frame can be totally arbitrary — a groove pattern, a wax cylinder, whatever — and the inner message is parasitic on it. So meaning isn't in the signal and isn't in the head; it's in the relation, and the relation only exists when something can find the right isomorphism.

What I want from you is the strongest case against this, sorted by how much it should actually move me. In particular I'm bothered by these:

(a) the regress worry — if the frame message is what tells you how to read the inner message, what tells you how to read the frame? Does every message just push the decoding problem back one level, and is that fatal or just a shrug?

(b) accidental messages — a rock formation that happens to match the digits of pi, or tree rings that happen to encode a real historical event. The isomorphism is undeniably there, but it feels wrong to call it a message. Does my view have to call it one?

(c) multiple isomorphisms at once — if a single structure lines up equally well with two different things, what picks out the meaning, and is there any fact of the matter or is it just decoder-relative?

(d) the sneaky one — the possibility that "finding the right isomorphism" is itself just whatever the interpreter is disposed to do, in which case I've smuggled meaning back into the head through the side door.

Which of these are real defeaters and which does my view already have the resources to absorb? Dont go easy on (d); if the whole thing collapses into "meaning is whatever the interpreter does," I want you to say that plainly rather than rescue it for me. I'd also like to know what the view predicts that a boring "meaning is use" or "meaning is in the head" story doesn't — if there's nothing, that's telling. And if it turns out the position is insulated by qualifications and nothing could count against it, tell me that too, and whether that's a problem for a view of this kind.

Personally the thing that would bug me most is a case where the isomorphism is undeniably there but nothing like meaning is present — that would move me more than any regress.
