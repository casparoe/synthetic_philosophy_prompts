I'm a journalist who writes about science and ideas, and in three weeks I have to give the speech at the relaunch of a wooden schooner that my hometown has spent the last forty years restoring. Here is the situation I need help with. Over four decades, working afloat and one section at a time, the yard replaced every plank, frame and deck beam under a rule the society insists on: she was never to be taken out of service, so there was no day on which the vessel in the water was not a rigged, working schooner. The keel, the stem and the bell are original; the rest is new wood, cut and fitted by hand. Meanwhile the maritime museum down the street has taken the removed material — every original plank and frame, catalogued as it came off — and assembled it into a full hull, as closely as the surviving records allowed. That hull has no rig and cannot float; it sits in the boat hall as an object. There is a further awkwardness in the background: in the 1890s the vessel was lengthened by several feet, so even the "original" material already represents more than one ship.

The town has split over this. The society's press materials say the relaunch returns the original schooner to the water, and the donors' plaques say their gifts saved her; a local historian has published a letter saying that what is going into the water is a replica and that the real vessel is the hull in the museum. The shipwrights will be in the room, and so will the donors, and so will the historian.

Please write the speech: about eight minutes, delivered by me, harbor behind me, both camps in the front rows. I don't want a lecture on the riddle of the ship of Theseus; I want the question to do the structural work, shaping what I can and cannot honestly claim about continuity, so that the result is neither evasive nor insulting to either side. Tell me how to handle the hull in the museum — whether to name it at all, and how. I would also like to know whether there is a fact of the matter about whether she is the same ship, or whether that is a decision the town makes when it chooses what to put in the water and what to keep indoors. After the speech, add a short note to me, not for delivery, saying which view of the vessel's identity the speech commits me to, where its reasoning is weakest, and what argument or consideration would lead you to a different verdict on whether what is being relaunched is the original schooner; if the speech has to lean on a contested reading of the puzzle or on a liberty I've taken with the facts, flag that too.
