I'm a retired geophysicist — thirty-odd years doing seismic imaging before I retired and started reading philosophy in earnest, and I've gotten a bit obsessed with the Bayesian confirmation literature, especially the old evidence problem. Here's the view I've talked myself into, and I want you to try hard to break it. The way I see it, the old evidence problem is mostly an artifact of a bad idealization, namely treating a rational agent as a single frozen probability distribution that gets conditioned on facts. Real agents live in time. So when I say a fact is old evidence, what I really mean is that it already got baked into my credences at some earlier stage, and conditioning on it again is just double counting — no surprise there. But that doesn't mean the theory gets no credit. It means you have to ask the counterfactual question: back when the fact was genuinely up for grabs for me, would the theory have made it more likely than the rivals did? If yes, the theory earned its boost, even though I can't get the boost today. The machine-learning analogy is what convinced me. A model that fits its training data perfectly impresses nobody, but we still credit a model for having been built in a way that could have failed on held-out data, and we do evaluate it on data it never saw. So I want to say confirmation is fine as long as there was a live possibility of failure at some earlier time. I'm not claiming this is original, but I've probably garbled whatever the actual positions are, so don't just tell me who said what.

What I want is the exposure map. What would have to be true for my view to be wrong? Concretely: are there cases it simply cannot describe — say, a fact known to everyone since childhood, so there's no earlier stage where it was in doubt, or a theory that was cooked up specifically to explain the very fact in question, where the counterfactual likelihoods seem to get fixed after the fact? Does it collapse into one of the standard rivals once you push on it (I have a nagging feeling the whole thing might just be a relabeled version of some other move)? Are there formal results — about counterfactual credences, about what it even means to compare a likelihood at a time when the agent didn't have the theory yet — that would sink it rather than just make it awkward? And the thing I'm most worried about: have I defined confirmation so loosely that nothing could ever count against it, in which case is that actually a problem for a view of this kind, or is it fine for a view whose job is to describe rationality rather than predict anything?

Please sort the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, and be honest about which ones a committed defender of this sort of line would themselves concede and which they'd wave away. Use whatever real cases help — Mercury's perihelion is the one everybody reaches for, and in my own field the fit of the continents was known for a century before anyone took drift seriously, which I've always found a strange example to think about. I'd also like to know which defeaters would actually move me, as opposed to ones that only look decisive until you notice the defender has a cheap reply.
