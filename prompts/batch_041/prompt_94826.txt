I'm running a reading group on whether a social-insect colony can literally have a mind, and I want to show people what a really good exchange looks like. You're a philosopher of mind who also knows the social-insect literature well, so write me the dialogue.

Two characters, both sharp, both scientifically literate, neither a strawman. One thinks the colony is a genuine cognitive system: it senses, remembers, chooses, regulates, and has interests, and the fact that its memory is partly sitting in the world (pheromone trails that decay on their own schedule, nest architecture, traffic patterns) rather than sealed inside heads is no disqualification. The other thinks that's a category error or at best a handy shorthand, like saying a traffic jam wants to reach the bridge, and that whatever deserves the name cognition is going on inside individual ant brains, which arent exactly mental giants either.

Then walk them through these cases one at a time, and make each of them commit to what their view predicts about a case before the other responds:

1. Biologists wall a mature colony into two halves, each with brood, foragers, and enough workers to carry on. Both halves keep foraging and building. Two minds, one mind that lost half its substrate, or no mind either way?
2. A month later the wall comes down and the halves fuse back into one coordinated colony. Did the mind come back, or was there never one to come back?
3. In a different colony, every ant is swapped out one at a time over several weeks for an ant from another colony of the same species, until none of the originals remain, but the trails, the nest, and the traffic patterns are undisturbed. Is it the same cognitive system?
4. Two colonies of different species that can't interbreed get merged by an experimenter and end up sharing one nest and one trail network. One mind, two, or a mess?

Ground rules: real back-and-forth, they answer each other's actual points instead of talking past each other, no lectures, no bibliographies or citing papers at each other, and nobody wins by definitional fiat. Before writing, spend a few sentences thinking through the strongest version of each side, especially the deflationist's best reply to the point about cognition being spread into the environment, then write the dialogue. Somewhere around 900 to 1200 words, tight, no padding with history of the debate.
