I hold a strong form of the underdetermination thesis and want the geography of what it risks, not a survey of the literature. The thesis is this: for any theory rich enough to be worth holding, there exists a genuine rival — not a contrivance assembled to make a philosophical point — that is empirically equivalent to it in the strong sense of agreeing with it on every observation formulable in any vocabulary we could ever come to have, so that no possible evidence could discriminate between them. If that is right, the choice between such rivals must rest on considerations that are not evidential, and the rationality of science lies in the disciplined use of those considerations rather than in the tribunal of experience.

Two distinctions I want you to work with rather than restate. First, weak versus strong empirical equivalence: agreement on all observations we can now formulate versus agreement on all observations formulable in any possible future vocabulary. Second, transient versus persistent underdetermination: a rivalry that further evidence or conceptual innovation could in principle dissolve, versus one that no possible evidence could dissolve because the rivals diverge only in what they say about unobservable structure. My thesis concerns the strong and persistent variety.

What would count against it? Rank the defeaters by what they would cost me, and separate them according to what exactly they damage — the thesis's truth, its interest, or its status as a thesis at all. Consider at least the following, and add what I have missed.

(a) The triviality objection: equivalent rivals can be manufactured at will, by conjoining idle hypotheses, by recasting a theory so that it mentions only observational vocabulary, or by permuting its ontology. If bare existence is all the thesis needs, it is cheap and uninteresting; if it needs the rival to be equally well supported on every epistemic dimension, it becomes an empirical claim about the space of theories that nobody has established. Does the objection refute the thesis or merely relocate its interest?

(b) The equal-support requirement and the dilemma it generates. Either non-evidential virtues such as simplicity, unification, explanatory depth, fertility, and fit with background knowledge count toward rational choice, in which case underdetermination by evidence does not entail underdetermination by reasons and the thesis loses its skeptical sting; or they do not, in which case the thesis is secured by a stipulation that begs the question against its opponents. Is the dilemma fatal, or is it the correct location of the dispute?

(c) Intertranslatability and duality. If the rivals are systematically intertranslatable — as formulations related by a symmetry or a duality appear to be — they may be one theory described twice, and the thesis proves too little. If they are not intertranslatable, each alleged case must be established individually, and I want to know whether any survives scrutiny.

(d) The observable/unobservable divide on which strong equivalence depends is itself shifting and theory-relative, so the thesis may be ill-formed rather than false, which is a different and possibly worse kind of defeat.

(e) Whether the thesis is empirical at all: if it is a claim about the logical space of theories rather than about the world, what observations could refute it, and does that make it unfalsifiable in a way that matters?

(f) Its predictions. The thesis should predict perennial irresolution and an absence of convergence in mature science. Is that borne out, or does the eventual settlement of disputes that once looked evidentially hopeless — the nineteenth-century dispute over the reality of molecules is the obvious candidate — count against it?

(g) Generalization. Does the thesis apply with equal force to metaphysics, or does the choice among metaphysical readings of a physical theory have a different evidential standing?

Then tell me what a committed defender of the strong thesis would concede as counting against them, as opposed to what only critics say; whether the thesis has been qualified into insulation; and if it has, whether that is a defect or the unavoidable price of a thesis at this level of generality. What would move me most is either an actual episode in which a genuinely empirically equivalent rival was rationally abandoned, or an argument that the strong thesis is vacuous.
