A close friend of mine — someone whose honesty I have never had reason to question — recently told me, with medical records in hand, about an event her doctors could not explain and that she understands as a divine intervention. I do not know what to make of it, and my attempts to read up on the philosophical literature on testimony and miracles have left me frustrated: the two camps seem to talk past each other endlessly, one insisting that no testimony could ever make a miracle credible and the other insisting that this cannot be right, without either side ever conceding anything. I would like you to write the document that a genuine adversarial collaboration between them should produce.

Treat the two positions as follows. The first is the committed Humean: someone who holds that a miracle, understood as a violation of a law of nature brought about by a supernatural agent, is by definition so antecedently improbable that no merely human testimony — indeed no finite accumulation of it — could ever render belief in one rationally justified. The second is the committed anti-Humean: someone who holds that this is too strong, that testimony can in principle and sometimes in fact provide good reason to believe a miracle occurred, and that the Humean maxim smuggles in a conclusion it was supposed to establish.

The deliverable should be a joint statement both parties could sign in good faith. Open with a numbered section of agreed claims: what both accept about the nature of testimony, about how evidence bears on improbable hypotheses, about what a law of nature is and whether "violation" is even the right notion, and about the distinction between the claim that miracles never happen and the claim that one could never be justified in believing one did. Then give a disagreements table. For each row, state the crux — the sub-question such that settling it would move at least one side — followed by the precise formulation of each side's position, each phrased so that the party holding it would accept the wording as fair. For every crux, supply a probe that both sides agree would bear on it, and record each side's prediction about it in advance.

Two things I want built into the collaboration, because they seem to be where the usual exchange breaks down. First, require both parties to work within a shared probabilistic framework — a prior probability for the miraculous hypothesis, a likelihood for the testimony given that hypothesis versus given the mundane alternatives — so that "improbable" and "credible" become quantities each side is willing to plug into rather than slogans. Second, force them to confront a class of cases that the standard exchange tends to ignore: not a one-off reported event behind a single chain of testimony, but a repeatable, instrument-mediated anomaly — an occurrence that recurs under controlled observation and can be re-examined by skeptics — and, separately, a case of many independent witnesses with no common source. Each side should say what it predicts about such cases, and whether these cases fall inside or outside the scope of the original argument.

Finally, tell me which parts of the disagreement, if any, you judge to be merely verbal — a dispute about what the word "miracle" or "evidence" is to mean — and which are substantive. Be explicit where a formulation is genuinely contested rather than settled by definition. I am not asking you to adjudicate who is right; I am asking for the text the two of them could have written together, with the residual disagreement made exact.
