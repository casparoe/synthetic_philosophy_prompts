I'm a junior at a public high school and I run our school's philosophy club, which meets after school once a week for sixty minutes. Attendance runs between ten and fourteen students, mostly juniors and seniors; three or four of them will argue about anything, and the rest will sit silently unless the format gives them a specific reason to speak. I plan every session myself. I have no budget, a whiteboard, and a stack of index cards.

Last month we spent a whole meeting on the ship of Theseus and it went badly. Everyone had an intuition, nobody could say what would make one answer better than another, and by the end the loudest people were just repeating themselves more forcefully. I want our next session, on personal identity, to be different. I've read a summary of the teletransporter case that the philosopher Derek Parfit is known for, and I think there is a way to stage it so the group runs into the problem through their own decisions instead of trading opinions, but I can't work out the staging on my own.

Please act as an expert both in the philosophy of personal identity and in designing activities and games for teaching philosophy. Think carefully, and work in stages rather than jumping to a finished plan. Begin by brainstorming at least three structurally different formats — for example a hearing or tribunal, a role-play with assigned interests, a staged sequence of forced decisions with real stakes, or a simulation with a scorekeeper — and for each one say what it would make people feel and what it would leave untouched. Then commit to the one you think is strongest for my constraints and build it out. If you have to assume something about my group or my room, state the assumption instead of leaving it implicit.

What I want the group to run into: that the verdict "same person" feels obvious in a case with no gap and no duplicate, and equally obvious in a case of slow, piecemeal change, and then becomes unstable once those two cases are placed side by side — so that by the end they are genuinely unsure whether the question has a fact of the matter, and whether what they care about is identity at all or something narrower.

Build the activity around the following material, and add cases of the same kind if you need to, but keep these five.

The setup: a company called Meridian runs a teletransport service. The group sits as its Continuity Review Board.

Case 1, clean transit. A passenger is scanned in a booth, the body at the origin is destroyed painlessly, and a body is assembled at the destination four minutes later with all the passenger's memories, habits, and skills intact. Nobody disputes that the person who steps out is the passenger.

Case 2, stale scan. A passenger is scanned, but reconstruction is delayed eleven years because the destination station is still under construction. The original dies in a car accident three days after the scan. Eleven years later a body is assembled that remembers everything up to the scan and nothing after it.

Case 3, piecemeal migration. No transport. Over roughly twenty years a person's neurons are replaced one at a time with silicon units that do exactly what each neuron did, a few per day, with no loss of consciousness, no change in memory or personality, and nothing noticeable to their friends. At the end, no biological neurons remain.

Case 4, no destruction. Identical to Case 1 except that Meridian's older machines cannot destroy the original. A passenger is scanned, the original stays on Earth, and a copy is assembled on Mars. Both have the same memories up to the moment of scanning, both answer to the same name, and they begin diverging immediately.

Case 5, interrupted migration. Case 3, but halfway through, the replacement is suspended for six months and then resumed by a different company whose chips are functionally equivalent but built differently.

Two rules for the board, which I think are the point of the whole thing. First, every case must be ruled on twice, with no hedging and no "it depends": once for a life insurance payout (is the arriving or surviving person the same person as the one who departed or began?), and once for a living will (the document that says what medical treatment someone would want if they could no longer speak for themselves — does the instruction the original signed apply to whoever or whatever is now here?). Second, the votes are public and recorded on the board as they happen, so the group can see the pattern forming as it forms.

Please give me the rules and roles, the materials to prepare, and a minute-by-minute timeline for sixty minutes with at least fifteen minutes left for the debrief. The debrief should map specific moments in the activity onto the philosophical points, and should be the first time anyone hears Parfit's name or the technical vocabulary. Also tell me at least three things that will predictably go wrong the first time I run this and what to do in the moment, and be honest about which feature of the idea the activity cannot reproduce and that I will have to supply myself.

One last thing: I can't get books or articles, and I don't need a reading list — what I need is the activity itself, complete enough to run on a Wednesday afternoon.
