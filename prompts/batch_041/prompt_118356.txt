I am a high school student who helps run our school's artificial intelligence discussion club, and I would like help designing a session I can actually run next month. The club has about eighteen members, we meet for seventy minutes, and the room is an ordinary classroom with movable desks and a projector. Almost nobody in the club programs, so the activity has to work with paper, index cards, tape, and the people themselves.

The idea I want them to feel is inverse reinforcement learning: the problem of inferring what someone wants from watching what they do, rather than being told. I understand the basic picture — you observe a sequence of choices, you assume the person is acting sensibly given what they want and what they are able to do, and you try to recover the wants. What I want is for the session to end with the members uneasy about that last step rather than able to recite it. Specifically, I want them to reach the point where they see that the behaviour they watched is fully consistent with several incompatible goals, that their own favourite inference was shaped by an assumption they never noticed they were making, and that a system which reproduces the observed behaviour perfectly may still have learned something the person they watched would find absurd.

Here is a rough mechanism, which you should feel free to replace if you can think of something better. Several volunteers take turns walking a fixed route across the classroom floor from a start tile to a goal tile, with the floor marked out in tape squares. Each walker has been given the same private instruction, which the audience never sees, and the audience watches only the paths. The audience then writes down on a card what they think the walker wanted. Groups compare their inferences and predict, for a rearranged room, what a walker with that goal would do. I then change the room — move an obstacle, remove it, add a shortcut — and the volunteers walk again. Members whose inferred goal was wrong have to explain what in the original paths misled them, and I want their mistake to have been reasonable at the time.

A few things worry me and I would like you to solve them. First, I want the wrong inferences to be wrong in an interesting way rather than merely careless. My instinct is to plant, in the first round, a walker who is not pursuing a goal at all but avoiding something, or who is following a rule about which tiles may be stepped on, so that the audience naturally reads a preference where there is really a constraint. I am not sure how to arrange this so that it is fair rather than a trick, or how the debrief should point at the real distinction between what a person wants and what a person is able to do, and why those two look identical from the outside. Second, I want at least one round where two very different goals fit the observed paths equally well, and I want the members to discover this by defending rival inferences against each other rather than being told. Third, I want the members to notice that how sensible they assumed the walker to be changed what they inferred the goal was; I am not sure how to make that visible without simply announcing it. Fourth, I would like a final round that gives them something checkable: a case where they can see that an agent matching the observed behaviour exactly still does something the original walker would reject once the room changes.

Please give me the full design: setup and materials, the exact rules and what each role is told, a timeline for the seventy minutes, how inferences are recorded and compared so the disagreement is visible to everyone at once, and the debrief. In the debrief I would like each stage of the activity mapped onto a specific part of the problem — where the goal is underdetermined by the behaviour, where a constraint masquerades as a preference, and where matching behaviour and having the goal come apart — along with an honest note on which part of the idea the activity cannot reproduce and that I will have to supply myself.

Finally, tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this. I expect at least one of the planted cases to be spotted immediately by somebody, and I would rather plan for that than be surprised by it, so include what to do if the group catches it early and what to do if nobody does.
