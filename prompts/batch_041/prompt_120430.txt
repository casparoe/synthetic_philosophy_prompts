Before I read another account of how children come to know the grammar of their language, I want the specification itself: the desiderata that any adequate theory of first-language acquisition has to meet. Treat the theory as an empirical hypothesis about human children rather than as a conceptual scheme, so that the requirements are ones a theory could visibly fail. Do not give me citations, names of researchers, or reading lists; where a point would ordinarily rest on a particular study, describe the phenomenon in plain terms instead, and say what would have to be true of it for the requirement to have force.

For each desideratum, give me: the phenomenon or consideration that motivates it; what a theory that fails it gets wrong, with a concrete illustration of the failure where you can; and whether it is empirical, conceptual, or methodological — that is, whether a discovery could overturn it, or whether it is a condition on what counts as explaining anything at all. Also flag any item on your list that is vacuous, in the sense that no theory could fail it, and any that does no work beyond restating that a theory should fit the facts.

Cover at least these areas, and add others you think belong: the gap between what the child hears and what the child ends up knowing; the errors children make and, more tellingly, the errors they never make; the regularization of fragmentary input into a systematic language, including the case of children who receive no full model; infants' sensitivity to distributional regularities in artificial speech; the maturational window; and the general problem of how a finite body of data could fix an infinite competence.

Throughout, keep apart four things that 'innate' is used for: present before the relevant experience; not derived from the relevant evidence; species-typical; and specific to language rather than to cognition generally. A theory that runs any two of these together should be marked as failing a requirement, and you should say which.

Then three things I want argued rather than listed.

First, what does it take for an appeal to the poverty of the stimulus to be a real argument rather than a gesture? My suspicion is that it needs four parts — the generalization the child attains, the evidence available in the input, the class of learners for whom that evidence is supposed to be insufficient, and an argument that no learner in that class could get from the one to the other — and that most such appeals leave the learner class unspecified, which makes them unfalsifiable. Say whether that is the right way to state the requirement.

Second, the conflict. I expect some subset of the desiderata to be jointly unsatisfiable, and I want to know which. Two candidates I would like you to test: the demand that the theory posit no bias specific to language, held together with the demand that it explain convergence on the adult grammar from positive evidence alone; and the demand that a learner without negative evidence only ever narrow its hypothesis, held together with the well-attested overgeneralizations children in fact produce. For each, say exactly which requirements collide, whether the collision is a proof or a theorem about learning rather than a merely empirical tension, and what has to give.

Third, which single requirement does the most to exclude the theories people actually hold, and is that requirement defensible, or is it a prejudice of one camp in the dispute?

Prose is fine, though a table for the requirements themselves would be welcome.
