I'm a software engineer — backend infrastructure, mostly — with no formal training in philosophy. What I know about these debates I've picked up from papers and blog posts on my own time, and I'm aware that this means I'm likely to misjudge what's already been said. I'm putting together a short research proposal (roughly four pages) for a six-month, modestly funded independent project, and I'd like help turning a half-formed idea into something a reviewer would take seriously.

The idea is this. The alignment discussion I've read tends to be framed in worst-case terms: assume the system is optimizing against us, grant it arbitrary capability, and ask for a guarantee that holds no matter what. In my own field we went through a version of this and largely moved past it. Worst-case analysis of algorithms is often dominated by inputs that never occur, and the interesting work came from finding assumptions that are weak enough to be credible but strong enough to prove something — for example, analyzing performance under small random perturbations of a worst-case instance, or measuring an algorithm against the best possible algorithm for each individual instance rather than against a single global bound. There's also a strand where you're handed a predictor that is usually right but may be arbitrarily wrong, and you design for graceful degradation: good behavior when the predictor is good, no catastrophe when it isn't. My suspicion is that the worst-case versus average-case argument in alignment is stuck partly because both sides are arguing at the extremes, and that this middle ground — assumptions calibrated to be credible rather than either vacuous or heroic — has no real analogue in the alignment discussion yet.

Please write the proposal. I need the question stated precisely enough that it could come out either way; an account of why the existing worst-case and average-case positions don't already settle it; the approach I'd actually take, including what I would try to formalize and a month-by-month plan for six months; what a successful outcome would look like and what would count as the project failing; and a frank scope check on whether someone with my background can get anywhere on this in the time available.

Two things I especially want from the proposal. First, it should confront the obvious objection that my analogy is shallow — that an adversary which is a program is not the same kind of thing as an adversary which is a distribution over inputs, and that whatever plays the role of the perturbation or the predictor in the alignment case may not exist. If the transfer fails, the proposal should say so and propose the nearest thing that does work. Second, it should argue that the result, if it came, would matter to someone other than me.

Do not include a bibliography or a reading list. I'll handle citations myself; I'd rather the proposal argue about positions and assumptions than name them.
