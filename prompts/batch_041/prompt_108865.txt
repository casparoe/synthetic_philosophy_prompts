I'm a grad student prepping for a comp exam and I keep bouncing off Hume's Section VIII of the first Enquiry — "Of Liberty and Necessity." I can follow the local moves fine, but I can't shake the feeling that the whole thing is doing something sneakier than the usual "compatibilism about free will" reading. Here's the bit that's bugging me (from the 1777 posthumous edition text, Selby-Bigge's edition, which is what I've got open):

"Our idea, therefore, of necessity and causation arises entirely from the uniformity, observable in the operations of nature; where similar objects are constantly conjoined together, and the mind is determined by custom to infer the one from the appearance of the other. These two circumstances form the whole of that necessity, which we ascribe to matter. Beyond the constant conjunction of similar objects, and the consequent inference from one to the other, we have no notion of any necessity, or connexion.

If it appear, therefore, that all mankind have ever allowed, without any doubt or hesitation, that these two circumstances take place in the voluntary actions of men, and in the operations of mind; it must follow, that all mankind have ever agreed in the doctrine of necessity, and that they have hitherto disputed, merely for not understanding each other."

Then a page or two later he says: "It is universally acknowledged, that there is a great uniformity among the actions of men, in all nations and ages, and that human nature remains still the same, in its principles and operations."

Act as a Hume scholar with a strong background in early modern philosophy and in philosophy of history. Take a breath and brainstorm before you answer — I'd rather you lay out three or four candidate readings of what the argument is doing and then commit to one, than give me a tidy summary.

What I actually need help with:

1. Reconstruct the argument of the passage with every premise made explicit, including the ones Hume leaves as hand-waving. In particular, is the "great uniformity among the actions of men" supposed to be an empirical generalization, a regulative presupposition that historians and moralists can't help making, or something closer to a transcendental condition on our being able to ascribe any mental state to anyone at all? Give me the textual signals for and against each reading.

2. The bit I care most about: the move from uniformity of nature to uniformity of *human nature*, and then to the claim that everyone has always agreed about necessity and liberty and that the dispute "has hitherto turned merely upon words." That last claim seems to do a lot of work. Is it a genuine descriptive thesis about what people have believed, or is it a way of making one side's conceptual framework the default — so that anyone who denies necessity is, by Hume's lights, not really disagreeing but failing to understand their own words? If it's the latter, what does that do to the idea that we can ascribe the same beliefs and motives to people across radically different times and cultures? Is Hume's universalism a claim about the world, or a claim about the conditions of interpretation that quietly privileges the interpreter's own psychology?

3. Related: Hume says that if nature were constantly shifting, "inference and reasoning concerning the operations of nature would... be at an end." Does that make the uniformity claim self-sealing or unfalsifiable? Can I hold the uniformity of human nature as a heuristic without it collapsing into the claim that any apparent counterexample must be a misunderstanding or a failure of description?

4. Finally, tell me what a serious critic would say is the weakest joint in the argument, and whether the passage can be defended by reading the universalism as normative rather than descriptive — i.e., not "everyone does ascribe the same motives" but "we are licensed to ascribe the same motives, and here's why."
