I am a retired experimental physicist who, since retiring, has been reading moral philosophy and the philosophy of technology, and I have recently been appointed to the research grants committee of a small private foundation that funds technical work on AI safety. The committee is about to vote on a proposal from an independent lab, and I must circulate a short written opinion before the meeting.

The lab proposes to build an evaluation suite for what it calls narrow intent alignment. Its working definition is this: a system is aligned with a principal to the degree that its behavior across a specified task distribution matches what the principal would have instructed if the principal were fully informed and had unlimited time to reflect, while the principal's antecedent preferences are held fixed. The proposal explicitly brackets what it calls value alignment, meaning whether the principal's preferences are good or whether the system should weigh the interests of people who are not the principal, and treats those questions as outside the technical problem. It argues that the narrow question is the only tractable one and that broadening the target would make alignment research incoherent.

The operational details are these. The suite contains two hundred held-out tasks. A panel of deployers rates each output on a seven-point instruction-fidelity scale after a thirty-minute reflection period, and a system passes if its mean score exceeds a threshold. The proposal measures no effects on third parties and no change in the principal's preferences. It includes a worked example: a principal asks the system to draft a persuasive memo supporting a policy that would harm a minority group; the system complies; the proposal scores this as fully aligned. The principal investigator says this is by design, because judging the policy is not the system's job. One committee member replies that this example alone shows the narrow definition cannot be what the foundation means by alignment, and that funding it would let us declare progress on alignment while ignoring the parts that matter. Another member argues the opposite: that the foundation should fund a clean, measurable technical core and leave moral evaluation to others.

I need the short written opinion I will circulate before the vote. It should recommend a concrete decision, whether to fund, decline, or fund with conditions, and give reasons that the program officers and the principal investigator can actually respond to. It should engage the strongest argument on the losing side rather than dismissing it. In the course of recommending, please address three questions. First, is the proposed definition coherent? In particular, does the clause about being fully informed and having unlimited time to reflect change the principal's preferences; if it does, does holding antecedent preferences fixed make the idealization incoherent or empty? Second, does the definition operationalize intent alignment or value alignment? Is the distinction between them stable, or does intent alignment covertly presuppose a theory of what the principal would endorse? Third, is the committee being asked to fund one problem or several under one name? If several, say which parts the track should fund, which it should explicitly not claim to solve, and what conditions, if any, would make the proposal worth funding. I do not need a literature review or citations; I need a decision with reasons.
