Write a short story, of roughly 2,000–3,000 words, that takes Nietzsche's thought of eternal recurrence seriously as both philosophy and lived experience, and that does not simply restage the familiar scene of a demon asking whether you could will your life over again.

Build the story on a tension the standard retellings usually skate past. The thought has two faces. On one face it is a cosmological claim: if the world's possible configurations are finite and time is endless, then every configuration — including every particular life, down to its smallest detail — recurs, and recurs infinitely often. On the other face it is an existential test: the demon's question is addressed to you, is meant to reveal what you are, to separate those who can affirm their lives from those who cannot, and perhaps to change how a person lives once she has heard it. Let the cosmological reading be true within the story's world, and let the protagonist work out what becomes of the test. Two consequences are worth her attention. First, if the recurrence is exact, then everything a cycle contains — including her hearing the doctrine and whatever she does in response — occurs again in every cycle, so the thought cannot be acted upon, because the acting is already inside the loop; insight cannot alter the thing it is insight into. Second, if the recurrence is exact, the person who lives her life again is a duplicate rather than she herself: a separate token of the same type, standing to her in no causal or memorial relation whatever. Through the story, let her ask whether the demon's "you" survives either of these observations, and whether a thought that can change no one and address no one can still be the heaviest weight.

Do not deliver the doctrine as a lecture. Give the protagonist a life — work, a marriage or its absence, one specific afternoon — and let the philosophy show in what she notices and what she does. A shape you may take or discard: she is not a philosopher, but someone to whom the doctrine is explained by a person she loves, and her difficulty is not whether it is true but what to do with the fact that it is. If you use a narrator, you may let the narration register the loop — a scene told twice, or told as something remembered while it happens — but do not let the device become a puzzle that displaces the story. End where the story ends, not with a summary of positions.

Avoid the demon's speech as a set piece, any version of "so live each moment as if it would return," and any resolution that dissolves the tension by technicality — that the recurrence is only of events and not of persons, that it is merely approximate, or that memory carries across cycles. Keep the recurrence exact and impersonal, and see what is left.
