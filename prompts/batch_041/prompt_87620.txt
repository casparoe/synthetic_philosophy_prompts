I run a biweekly reading group that's mostly people who came in through LessWrong and Slate Star Codex and then drifted into actual game-theory papers, so they're sharp, but they've all read the same ten blog posts about tit-for-tat and they think they already know exactly how the iterated prisoner's dilemma goes. I want to break that smugness. About 16 people, 90 minutes, church basement with a whiteboard, a stack of index cards, and a printer I can use for handouts. Here's the thing I want them to feel in their bones instead of nodding at: reciprocity is a fair-weather machine. It works beautifully when everyone sees every move perfectly, and it turns to garbage the moment there's noise — one misread move, one slip, and two copycats spiral into mutual defection even though neither of them ever intended to defect. And the second half, which is the part I actually care about: the obvious fix is forgiveness, but forgiveness is a subsidy you're paying to defectors, so there's a real tradeoff here and no free lunch. I want them to run into both halves themselves, in that order, before I say anything.

So: design me a runnable in-class game for this session. You're a game designer who's also read the actual literature on cooperation under noise, not just the blog-post version — so I want the mechanics to be doing real work, not decoration. I'm imagining something like fixed pairs playing a known-length repeated PD where I secretly roll a die each round and flip a couple of moves before they're transmitted, so that a single accident poisons a copy-their-last-move pair; then a second phase where they get to rewrite their strategy cards and discover that the forgiveness rule they invent restores cooperation but also opens a door for an exploiter in the room. But don't just take my sketch — think it through carefully, and if a different structure gets them to the same two realizations more cleanly, use that. Include the rules, the payoff scheme (no real money), the noise mechanic, what I say and when, the repair phase, and a debrief that maps specific moments in the game onto the ideas: why copy-the-last-move is fragile under noise, what the forgiving and contrite variants buy you, why a win-stay/lose-shift-ish rule can beat it in a noisy world, and the frontier between correcting errors and rewarding exploiters. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle it, and be honest about which part of the idea the activity can't reproduce and that I'll have to supply myself.

Start with the bottom line — one sentence naming the game and what it makes people feel — then give me the full design. No preamble, no restating what I asked for. And brainstorm a couple of alternative noise mechanics before you commit to one, even if you only show me the winner.
