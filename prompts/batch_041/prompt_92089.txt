I'm building the last unit of a grad seminar I teach on explanation and prediction in history — it's cross-listed with the history department, and the final three weeks are on base rates, reference classes, and what people call the outside view. The running exercise has the students forecast a generic case instead of a named one: a mature hegemon whose share of world output has been sliding for about two decades, whose military commitments are still sized for the peak, whose currency is still the default settlement unit, but where the political class has begun to openly debate retrenchment. The question is whether it's still the top-ranked power twenty-five years out — you can cash out top-ranked however you like, just tell me what you're measuring. I want them choosing among competing reference classes instead of grabbing the first one that comes to mind, so I've fixed a menu and asked for weights across it (weights sum to 1; a class that shouldn't be used at all gets 0). The menu as it stands: (a) all great powers in the record, however you want to define great power — the blunt base rate; (b) great powers that at the same time held the reserve-currency position and a lead in the dominant military technology; (c) polities whose peacetime tax take sat below the cost of their standing military establishment for a sustained stretch; (d) a cluster that a clustering algorithm picks out of a large polity-year dataset by choosing its own features, where the cluster is genuinely predictive but none of its defining features are interpretable to any historian in the room; (e) powers that share the focal case's dominant religion, which does correlate with the outcome in the data; (f) the focal case treated as its own reference class, i.e. the inside view smuggled in as a class of one.

What I actually need from you is the answer key: rank these six from most to least weight, give me the numbers themselves so I can see the shape of the distribution and not just the order, and a sentence or two on why each lands where it does. Two things matter to me more than the ordering. First, whether the weighting move is even coherent — my own suspicion is that deciding how much to trust each class is already an inside-view judgment about which features are causally relevent, in which case the outside view isn't doing the work it's advertised to do, and I'd much rather you take a side on that than hand me the standard both-sides summary. Second, whether any of these should be excluded outright rather than merely down-weighted; (d) and (e) are what the students fight about, and I think they're fighting about different things without noticing. Commit — a ranking I can argue with in class is worth more to me then a survey of considerations. No citations or reading suggestions needed, just the reasoning.
