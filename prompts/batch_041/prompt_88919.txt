Background: we'd been reading about Goodhart's law, the idea that once you start optimizing some measure it stops tracking the thing you actually cared about. The framing in the seminar was that this is usually sold as a deep warning, both about metrics in institutions and about reward hacking in AI training, so nobody in the room was questioning whether it's important. Then Dana and Marcus started.

(1) Dana said the whole thing is basically a tautology. If a measure were a perfect stand-in for what you care about it wouldn't be a proxy, it'd be the thing itself; so saying a proxy degrades when you lean on it just means proxies aren't perfect, which is true by definition and predicts nothing. (2) Marcus said that's too quick, because the failure isn't random noise — it's systematic and it grows with how hard you push. Optimizing a proxy doesn't just add error, it makes you diverge from the goal in a predictable direction, and a lot of the time the divergence is caused by the targeting itself, not by some pre-existing imperfection. (3) Dana said fine, but then split it in two: case one, you pick among many options by a noisy proxy, so the top scorers are high partly by luck and the proxy looks worse up there — that's just a selection effect, plain statistics, no incentives needed; case two, people notice the metric and game it — that's about incentives and it's ancient news. And she said those two mechanisms have nothing to do with each other, so either the "law" is a triviality about statistics or it's just the observation that people game metrics, and neither is worth a seminar. (4) Marcus said they're not two things, they're the same thing: in both cases the proxy's validity was measured under one distribution and you're now acting to change the distribution, so the correlation you relied on doesn't survive. And he added that gaming isn't just people being naughty — any sufficiently strong optimizer, whether it's a human or a gradient descent, will find the cheapest path to the number, and the cheapest path usually isn't the intended one. (5) Dana came back that this is unfalsifiable: correlations estimated under one distribution don't transfer to another, sure, but that's true of all learning, not of measures, so it stops saying anything specific; and "the cheapest path" is a slogan unless you say what counts as strong and what counts as cheap. (6) Marcus said the substance is in the asymmetry — when the proxy is cheap to measure and the goal is expensive, the gap between them is exactly the space the optimizer lives in, and the wider that gap the faster the divergence, which is a real claim with conditions, not a tautology.
