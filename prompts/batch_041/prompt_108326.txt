In my philosophy of history seminar we have been discussing overdetermination: the idea that a major historical outcome — a revolution, a war, the collapse of a regime — often had several independent causes, any one of which would by itself have brought it about. My instructor described this as a serious problem for causal explanation in history, and I want to be sure I have drawn the right lesson from it. If the outcome was going to occur regardless of which of the candidate causes obtained, then it seems no single cause was necessary for it; and if no cause was necessary, it is hard to see in what sense any of them caused it. I am therefore inclined to conclude that overdetermination makes historical causal claims either false or merely pragmatic — we simply pick whichever redundant factor suits our narrative — and that the honest upshot is that history has no causes, only stories we tell. Am I right about this, or is there something in the very notion of causation that I am misconstruing?
