I write about AI research and the ideas underneath it, and I'm at work on a long piece about whether oversight by debate can solve the problem of supervising systems cleverer than the people judging them. Having read my way in, I find myself persuaded by the core of the proposal that Geoffrey Irving, Paul Christiano and Dario Amodei set out in 2018: two AI systems argue opposing sides of a question in short, turn-limited statements before a human judge who could never have answered the question unaided, on the theory that a debater committed to the truth enjoys a structural advantage over one committed to lying, since the honest side can always expose the dishonest side's error. The complexity-theoretic gloss is what did it for me — with a polynomial-time judge, optimal debate can in principle settle any question in PSPACE, whereas asking that judge to answer directly reaches only NP — and the early experiment they reported, in which debate lifted a weak image classifier's accuracy on a visual task from roughly 59 percent to roughly 89 percent, seemed to me a suggestive down payment on the theory. So the position I'm writing from is this: debate is not a stopgap but the most promising route to scalable oversight, and its central asymmetry — that truth has a structural advantage over deception — is a genuine feature of the setup rather than an artifact of toy problems.

What I want from you is a map of what would count against that.

I have one candidate already, and I'll state it in my own words, since it is what started my doubts: the obfuscated-arguments problem. A dishonest debater need not win the argument; it need only make the argument unaffordable. It can advance a step that looks simple, is cheap to assert, and is false, where the only way to expose the error is for the honest debater to carry out a computation too long or too costly to complete and then explain within the turn and time limits a judge can absorb. The honest debater has the truth and loses on the clock. If that is right, the structural advantage of truth is really an assumption about an asymmetry between finding a proof and checking one — an assumption that holds in the toy cases and may invert exactly where the questions get hard, which is the regime debate is meant to cover.

So: what else counts against the position, and how much? I want the defeaters ranked, with the ones that would be close to fatal separated from those that are merely awkward or that a defender can absorb without giving ground. Which theoretical results and which empirical findings would move it, and which have arguably already arrived? What would the view's own defenders concede as counting against them, as opposed to what only critics say? Does anything in the position predict something its rivals don't, such that we could catch it out — and if the claim that truth has a structural advantage has been qualified into something that no experiment could embarrass, tell me so plainly and say whether that is a defect in a view of this kind. I don't need a reading list; I need the shape of the case, and an honest verdict on how exposed the position actually is.
