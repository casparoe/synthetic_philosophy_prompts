Consider Alice and Bob, two coworkers deciding about an office party. The possible outcomes are: Alice attends, Bob attends, neither attends. Alice ranks them: neither > Alice attends > Bob attends. Bob ranks them: Alice attends > Bob attends > neither. I want to state the principle behind my verdicts on this and related cases.

My verdicts are:

1. Whether Alice attends should be settled by Alice’s preference between Alice attends and neither attends. Since she prefers neither, neither is better than Alice attends, even though Bob would prefer Alice attends.

2. Whether Bob attends should be settled by Bob’s preference between Bob attends and neither attends. Since he prefers Bob attends, Bob attends is better than neither, even though Alice would prefer neither.

3. Since both Alice and Bob prefer Alice attends to Bob attends, Alice attends is better than Bob attends.

4. If Alice’s preference for neither over Alice attends is not self-regarding—if she is indifferent to parties and wants neither only because she wants to keep Bob from attending—I am inclined to say her preference should not be decisive over her own attendance, or at least should not be allowed to block verdict 3. I am unsure which.

5. If Bob’s attending would materially affect Alice—she would have to cover his shift, or she would be unable to get home safely—then Alice’s preference against Bob attending should count in the social ranking, and Bob’s liberty does not automatically settle the matter. If Alice’s objection is mere disapproval of Bob’s partying, it should not count against Bob’s liberty.

6. If both Alice and Bob prefer neither attends to both attend, then neither is better than both, even though each might prefer to attend if the other did not.

Taken together, verdicts 1–3 form a cycle; I know this, and I want to know which verdict to give up or how to restate the principle so the cycle is broken without losing what motivates each verdict.

My tentative principle is: (a) each person is sovereign over the pair that differs only in whether they themselves attend; (b) the social ranking respects unanimous strict preference over any pair; (c) preferences that are purely meddlesome are excluded from (b) but not from (a). I suspect (a), (b), and (c) cannot all hold.

Please give me the principle, or a small set of candidate principles, that best captures these verdicts. State each candidate precisely enough that I can see which clause does the work and which verdict motivates it. Then say what the principle predicts for a case I have not listed: Alice’s preference about Bob’s attending is based neither on self-interest nor on disapproval, but on her belief that Bob will be miserable at the party. Does her preference count in the Pareto ranking, and does it affect Bob’s liberty? Finally, tell me honestly which of my verdicts the best principle cannot accommodate, and whether I should give up that verdict or add a qualification. I am not asking for a literature review, citations, or a bibliography; I want the principle and its consequences.
