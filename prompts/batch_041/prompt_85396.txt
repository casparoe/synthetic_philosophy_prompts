A friend of mine with a doctorate in history and I spent most of a long dinner arguing about whether "why Britain and not China?" — the old puzzle about why sustained industrial growth began where it did — is even a well-posed question. Her view is that it treats one outcome as the default and the other as a deviation needing explanation, that "Britain" and "China" are aggregates doing work no evidence can support, and that you cannot ask why something did not happen. I think she is too quick: contrastive questions are ordinary in history and often answerable. What I took from the argument is that we have no way of telling the well-posed ones from the ill-posed ones.

I chair the committee that approves comparative essay titles for our final-year history students. Each year we get about forty proposals of the form "why did X happen in P and not in Q?", we have one afternoon to vet them, most of us are not specialists in the regions concerned, the final list goes out under my name, and a rejection means writing to the teacher who proposed the title to explain it. Please write a procedure we can run on each title in a few minutes: the tests to apply in order, what counts as passing or failing each, what to do with a title that fails (reframe it, specify the contrast, split it, reject it), and where the procedure must stop and hand the matter to a person because it turns on historiographical or evaluative judgment rather than on whether the question is well formed. Ground it in whatever philosophy of science actually says about contrastive explanation and in what comparative history has learned about comparing large entities; I don't need a survey of the debate, I need something my committee can use. Then run it on three titles: "Why did sustained industrial growth begin in Britain rather than in China?"; "Why did Japan industrialize in the nineteenth century while the Ottoman Empire did not?"; and "Why did the Scientific Revolution happen in Europe and not in the Islamic world?" — the last is one I would be sorry to lose, so be explicit if the procedure comes out against it.

Tell me how confident you are in the procedure itself, and single out the step you are least sure of.
