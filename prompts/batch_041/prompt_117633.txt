I do cost-benefit work for a living and I know the consequentialist toolkit cold — welfare aggregation, expected value, ex ante vs ex post, act vs rule, side-constraints, moral luck, interpersonal comparability, the repugnant conclusion, all of it. A friend has been dragging me to a Buddhist study group and I keep having the same reaction: this karma business is consequentialism, they just won't come out and say it. Either confirm that for me or kill it.

What I want: restate the karma/merit system in my vocabulary, as faithfully as you can manage, and then give me the residue report. What carries over cleanly, what only carries over if you distort it, and what has no counterpart in my framework at all. For every piece of residue, tell me whether it's my framework that's missing something or whether the Buddhist claim was just doing something vaguer than it looks.

The bits that make me think this won't come out clean:

- The deed is the intention. Whatever else happens, the karma is the volition — not the act, not the fallout.
- But the payout is scaled by who it was aimed at. A gift to an animal, a gift to an ordinary decent person, and a gift to someone far along the path pay out at wildly different rates — same gift, same cost to me, same benefit to them. That's a multiplier with nothing to do with how much good actually gets done.
- And the valence is fixed by the roots: out of greed, hatred or delusion and it's bad; out of their absence and it's good. The good result is then supposed to follow as a matter of causal law. Nobody is weighing anything.
- Ripening needs conditions and can be delayed for lifetimes, and I'm told some karma just never ripens at all — it lapses, the conditions never come together. So there's no ex post total, no bookkeeper.
- Merit gets transferred. People dedicate it to dead relatives, to all beings. That's not the same thing as making anyone better off.
- And the whole thing runs on no-self, so I can't even ask whose welfare is being aggregated. The stream that collects the result isn't me in any sense my framework can use.
- Yet the precise working-out of the results of karma is on the list of things you're not supposed to ponder. There's a don't-compute clause sitting inside what looks like a calculus.
- One more, and I may be mangling it: I half remember a story about a ship captain who kills a bandit to stop him slaughtering a boatload of merchants, and the tradition treats that as the compassionate thing to do rather than a crime. If I've got that roughly right, use it — that's the one case where my framework seems to slot straight in, and I want to know why it still doesn't feel like my framework.

Then the verdict I actually care about. Is Buddhist ethics a species of consequentialism, or is it closer to a theory about the quality of mental states that happens to have a causal story about pleasant and unpleasant results bolted onto it? And if it's the second, what is the consequentialist getting wrong when he recruits karma for his side? Dont be diplomatic about it — I'd rather find out my instinct is cheap than get a both-sides answer.
