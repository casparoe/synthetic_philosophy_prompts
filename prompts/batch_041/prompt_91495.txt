I have been trying to formulate a position about the unilateralist's curse, and I would like a serious map of what would count against it. As I understand the curse, it arises when several agents each judge independently whether some action is net good, and any one of them acting is enough to bring the action about: even if the action is probably bad, it is likely that at least one agent will judge it good and act. The usual remedy is a norm of collective conservatism for high-stakes, irreversible actions, requiring something like consensus or a procedure that filters out the unilateral judgment. I want to hold a stronger version. I think the curse is not an artifact of independent, error-prone judgments but a structural feature of any collective whose members can act on their own. On my view it survives deliberation, evidence-sharing, and even a shared prior, because deliberation selects for the most confident and action-biased members, and because the mere option to act unilaterally changes the strategic environment in which evidence is interpreted. So I hold that collective conservatism is required whenever unilateral action is possible and the stakes are asymmetric, not only when judgments are independent. I teach a seminar on technology policy, and we keep returning to cases like a single lab releasing a self-spreading organism or a lone engineer publishing a dangerous design, which is where the intuition feels strongest to me.

What I want from you is a map of this position's exposure. What observations, arguments, formal results, or cases would count against it? Which would be decisive and which merely awkward? Which would its defenders themselves have to concede as counting against them, rather than only what critics would say? What does my stronger version predict that the standard independence-based version does not, and are those predictions actually borne out? I would find most persuasive a case where unilateral action is both predictable and clearly warranted, or a formal model showing that the curse disappears under conditions weaker than consensus. I also suspect the position is nearly unfalsifiable: any case where unilateral action goes well can be described as luck, and any case where it goes badly as confirmation. Is there a version of the position that is genuinely falsifiable, or is the retreat to asymmetric stakes doing all the work? Give me a ranked table with columns for the defeater, what it would show, how a defender would respond, and a verdict of fatal, serious, or merely awkward. Then close with a short paragraph on whether the position has been insulated into unfalsifiability and whether that is a problem for a view of this kind. Keep the whole answer under 650 words.
