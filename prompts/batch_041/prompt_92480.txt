A metaethics tutor of mine — who insists, whenever the subject arises, that the naturalistic fallacy and the is-ought gap are two different faults, though she has never quite explained how — has spent years issuing verdicts on whether arguments commit the latter, and I have finally begun writing them down. She rules that the fallacy is committed when an argument moves from 'human beings are naturally territorial' to 'we ought to defend our borders', or from 'every known society has punished murder' to 'we ought to punish murder', or from 'the strong have always ruled the weak' to 'the strong ought to rule'. She rules that no fallacy is committed when the argument moves from 'one ought to keep one's promises' to 'I ought to meet her', from 'one ought to relieve suffering that one can relieve' to 'I ought to relieve this suffering', or from 'if you want to be trusted, you ought to tell the truth; you want to be trusted' to 'you ought to tell the truth, given that you want to be trusted'. Her next case is this: 'Factory farming inflicts suffering on an enormous scale, and there are cheap alternatives; so, if you want to avoid causing suffering, you ought to give up factory-farmed meat.' What will she say, and by what rule? I am less interested in the verdict itself than in the rule she seems to be running, the other rules that fit her recorded verdicts just as well, what each of those predicts about the new case, which prediction you would bet on and why, and which further argument would separate the candidates.
