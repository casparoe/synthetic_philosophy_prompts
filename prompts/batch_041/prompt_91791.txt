I have a question that began as a metaphysical one and has ended up, to my surprise, practical, and I would rather have the tangle underneath it straightened out than have the practical half answered.

I am persuaded by the arguments that the past and the future are as real as the present, that there is no moving present moment, and that time's asymmetry is a matter of direction rather than of what exists. I had assumed that the matching view about ordinary objects is perdurantism: that a thing is never wholly present at any one moment, but exists as a four-dimensional whole whose temporal parts are spread across its history. The two views seemed to me to be of a piece. If no moment is privileged, then nothing can be wholly present at one, and the endurantist's object that is entirely itself at every instant it exists looks like a leftover from a tensed picture of the world.

When I take perdurantism seriously, though, I lose my grip on myself. If what exists now is one temporal part of me and what exists in ten years is another, then the later one seems not to be me at all but a distinct thing continuous with me, a descendant of a sort. And if that is right, my habit of giving up present comfort for the sake of that later thing starts to look less like prudence and more like a gift to a stranger, or a sentimental fiction of the kind we maintain about our descendants' interests.

Two things I should mention, since they are probably where my thinking went wrong. First, I have read that the standard argument for temporal parts is the problem of temporary intrinsics — that a thing cannot be both straight and bent, or both standing and sitting, unless the straightness and the bending belong to numerically distinct parts, one for each time — and that the rival endurantist maneuver of relativizing properties to times, so that a thing is bent at one time and straight at another, is usually treated as a verbal dodge rather than an answer. If that is so, the parts are not optional. Second, I have seen it said in passing that relativity makes endurantism untenable, since being wholly present at a time presupposes a simultaneity that physics has taken away. I do not know what to make of that either, and I suspect it is not doing the work I am using it for.

What I would like, in this order:

**The assumptions in my question** (roughly 300 words). Which of the things I take for granted in setting this up are actually true, and which I should abandon.

**The debate I think I am in** (roughly 400 words). What endurantists and perdurantists actually disagree about once the confusion is cleared up, and what the temporary-intrinsics argument and the relativity remark really contribute to it.

**Temporal parts and persons** (roughly 350 words). What perdurantism does and does not imply about personal identity and about what matters in survival.

**What survives of my practical question** (roughly 250 words). Whether there is still a genuine question about the status of concern for one's future self, and if so what it is.
