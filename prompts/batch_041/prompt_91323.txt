My confirmation-theory reading group has spent three weeks on an argument one of our members constructed, and we cannot agree on whether it refutes a principle or merely abuses one. The set-up: 'All ravens are black' is logically equivalent to its contrapositive, 'All non-black things are non-ravens.' Nicod's criterion holds that an observation of something that is both F and G confirms the generalization 'All F are G.' The equivalence condition holds that logically equivalent hypotheses are confirmed by the same observations. The argument runs:

(1) A black raven is something that is both a raven and black, so by Nicod's criterion it confirms 'All ravens are black.'
(2) A white shoe is something that is both non-black and a non-raven, so by Nicod's criterion it confirms 'All non-black things are non-ravens.'
(3) 'All non-black things are non-ravens' is logically equivalent to 'All ravens are black,' so by the equivalence condition the white shoe confirms 'All ravens are black' as well.
(4) Our member then adds a further principle she calls instance symmetry: since each observation is a single positive instance of its generalization, and the two generalizations are one and the same hypothesis, the shoe and the raven confirm 'All ravens are black' to the same degree.
(5) Hence a single white shoe should raise our confidence in 'All ravens are black' exactly as much as a single black raven does, and we could build up all of ornithology without ever examining a bird.
(6) That conclusion is absurd, so some step must be wrong.

Identify the step that fails, say what kind of error it is, and price the repairs: I would prefer to keep both Nicod's criterion and the equivalence condition if I can, so tell me which of the three principles I must surrender, what each option costs, and whether the absurdity in (5) is a genuine falsehood or merely an apparent one. If instead you think (1) through (5) are valid and only sound absurd, argue that, and say what our confidence in 'All ravens are black' should actually do when we observe a white shoe.
