I have been reading rationalist blogs and forums for about a decade, and I run a small reading group that has spent the past month on the rule-following paradox. Two members have been arguing about it for a week, and I would like a point-by-point adjudication of their exchange rather than a survey of the literature — no reading list, just who has the better of it where. What follows is my best reconstruction.

1. Dana: The skeptical argument is sound. Take anything that supposedly fixes what I meant by plus — a mental image, a formula, a disposition to answer — and it is equally consistent with a deviant function that agrees with addition on every sum I have ever computed and diverges afterward. Since meaning is supposed to determine use, and nothing determines it, there is no fact of the matter about what I meant.

2. Owen: The argument is self-undermining, and the skeptic cannot even state his hypothesis without cheating, since the deviant function has to be specified as agreeing with addition up to some point, which leans on the very meaning he is calling into question. A rival that can only be described in terms of what it rivals is not a genuine alternative.

3. Dana: That is a complaint about the skeptic's vocabulary, not about the argument. Restate it as: every fact about me is compatible with many continuations, and I cannot non-arbitrarily prefer one. That is a reductio on the determinacy of meaning, and it does not need meaning to be determinate in order to be valid.

4. Owen: Then the fact of the matter is social rather than individual: correctness is constituted by agreement in a community's practice, and a lone dissenter is wrong because we will not go along with him. That is not a surrender of the concept of correctness but a better description of it.

5. Dana: It relocates the problem instead of solving it. A community is finitely many finite creatures; whatever was wrong with my dispositions is wrong with theirs, and if the argument worked against me it works against us. Worse, it makes arithmetic a social fact: on this view a sufficiently unanimous community cannot be wrong about a sum, and a change in usage would change the truth.

6. Owen: You keep treating the community response as a rival fact that is supposed to compel the next step. It is a diagnosis instead. The demand for some fact about me that forces an application is incoherent, because any such fact would still have to be applied; it would not be met even by an infinite mind. You want the answer to sit in the territory, and correctness is not that kind of thing.

7. Dana: Then there is a fact of the matter with no community needed: among the rules compatible with my finite history, one is simplest, and that is what I meant. That is why the deviant function is not a live hypothesis — it is enormously more complex.

8. Owen: The shortest description is shortest in some description language, and you have to pick one; the standard invariance results get you agreement only up to a constant, and that constant can absorb any finite amount of data. Simplicity is something we bring, not something we find, so invoking it is one more practice rather than an escape from practice.

Neither will budge, and I am deliberately not telling you which of them I lean toward. Please assess the exchange move by move — which moves are clearly right, which are mistaken, and where each of them overreaches — then say what both are missing. If part of their disagreement is verbal, identify which part, and if one of them has a real advantage the other has not answered, say what it is.
