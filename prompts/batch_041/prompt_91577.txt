Late in a career in physical chemistry I began noticing a pattern that I suspect philosophy has a name for, though I have not seen it stated in the form I have in mind. Set a higher-level feature that plainly does explanatory work — the temperature of a sample, the acidity of a solution, the fitness of a trait — beside the lower-level facts that realize it. Fix the lower-level facts, and the higher-level feature stops adding anything: the coarse description is redundant once the fine one is in hand. Yet we go on explaining and intervening at the coarse level, and nobody concludes that temperature is causally idle, even though it is redundant in precisely this way. Some writers, however, seem to treat such redundancy as proof that a higher-level feature — consciousness, say — does no causal work; applied uniformly, the same argument would strip causation from most of the sciences. Is there an established name for the redundancy itself, and separately for the inference from redundancy to causal idleness? What are the nearest named ideas, and how does the received discussion carve the territory differently from my description? A bare label will not satisfy me: I want to know what the described thing is and is not, where its edges lie, and which of the nearby notions fits it best.
