I'm a philosophy graduate student, and I've spent the term arguing myself into a position that I now want someone to attack properly. I think the principle of alternative possibilities is false — that an agent can be morally responsible for what she does even though she could not have done otherwise — and I think cases of the Frankfurt type establish this: an agent deliberates and acts on his own steam, for reasons that are genuinely his, while a counterfactual intervener waits in the wings, prepared to force the same choice should the agent waver, and in the event never has to lift a finger. What I take from such cases is stronger than the bare denial of the principle. I take it that the ability to do otherwise was never the thing responsibility tracks; what matters is the actual sequence — whether the action issues from the agent's own reasons-responsive mechanism, whether she would have responded differently had her reasons been different — and that this actual-sequence condition is both necessary and sufficient for the kind of control that responsibility requires.

What I want from you is a map of what this view is exposed to. I don't need the objections I can recite in my sleep; I need the ones that should actually move me, ordered honestly from fatal to merely awkward. Tell me which of the familiar replies to the Frankfurt cases a defender of my view can legitimately dismiss and which he only pretends to dismiss — in particular, I have never been satisfied by the way the view handles cases where the intervener's trigger is something less than a full decision, or where the mechanism that produces the action is reasons-responsive only by luck. Test it against a case I have been constructing: the intervener is not infallible but merely highly reliable, having intervened correctly in nearly every trial while being known to misfire occasionally, and the agent in question happens to be one of the cases where he simply never needs to act. Does the case still do the work it is supposed to do? And does it matter whether it is the agent's own deliberation that settles what he does or whether it is some neural precursor that settles it, with the deliberation along for the ride? I would also like to know what my view predicts that its rivals don't, since that is the part I can never get straight.

Finally, I want a candid verdict on a worry that has been nagging at me: that my position, as I have stated it, has been qualified into something no evidence could touch. If the view amounts to saying that responsibility obtains whenever the actual sequence has a certain shape, and the shape is specified after the fact by whatever we are inclined to say about the case at hand, then it risks being true by construction. If you think that is what has happened, say so, and say whether it is a defect or simply what a view at this level of abstraction looks like; if you think the view still stakes something, tell me what observation or case would falsify it, and whether such a case could ever arise in a world like ours. Please write this as continuous prose, the way you would argue in a letter: no headings, no numbered lists, no bullet points, no tables. I want to follow the reasoning, not skim it.
