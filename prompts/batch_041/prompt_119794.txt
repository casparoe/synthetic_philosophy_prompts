I'm a software engineer with no philosophy training, and a colleague and I have been arguing about who is responsible when an autonomous system harms someone. I wrote our reasoning out to find where it breaks, and now I'm stuck. Here it is:

(1) A person is morally responsible for an outcome only if they had control over it — only if they could have done otherwise with respect to it.
(2) When an autonomous system harms someone, no human controls the specific harm: the developer never decided what it would do on this occasion, and the operator neither foresaw nor could have overridden this particular output.
(3) So no human had control over the outcome.
(4) The system itself is not a moral agent, so it cannot be blamed.
(5) Therefore no one is morally responsible for the harm.

(6) Nothing in (1)–(5) depends on the system being artificial. A driver whose attention lapses and causes a crash, or a company whose product harms people through the uncoordinated choices of individually blameless employees, is also a case where no single person controlled the specific outcome.
(7) In at least some of those cases someone is plainly responsible — the driver who chose to speed, the company that shipped the product.
(8) So (1) is false, and the "responsibility gap" for autonomous systems is an illusion: a fact about the resolution of our blame-assigning practices, not a hole in the world.

My colleague thinks (8) is right and that the whole debate rests on a confusion. I suspect the trouble is with (6) — that dragging in drivers and companies changes the subject — but I can't say why, and I don't know what I'd be giving up to fix it. Tell me which premise or step to reject and why it looked plausible, then price the alternatives: for each candidate repair, what does it cost, and does any of them leave a genuine, narrower gap behind?
