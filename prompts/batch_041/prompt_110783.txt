I'm in the middle of a graduate seminar on personal identity, and I've become frustrated with how the discussion keeps going: someone proposes a case, someone else reports that it intuitively is or isn't still the same person, and we never settle what the case was supposed to be testing. Before I read another theory, I want the specification. Write me the desiderata that any adequate theory of what matters in survival — not of personal identity as such, but of the prudential relation in virtue of which it is rational for a subject to anticipate a future experience as her own — has to satisfy. I want the requirements themselves, each paired with the case or consideration that motivates it, and an explicit verdict on which of them are genuine constraints on any theory and which are artifacts of one camp's prior commitments. Treat the following as the phenomena the theory has to accommodate, since these are the ones my seminar keeps fighting over: the ordinary teletransporter, in which my body is destroyed and a replica with my memories and dispositions is assembled elsewhere; a variant in which the replica is made but the original is left alive and well, followed by a second variant in which the original is destroyed a week later, after both have lived; hemispheric fission, in which each half of my brain is transplanted into a new body and both wake up claiming to be me; slow drift, in which nothing is ever copied but my memories, temperament, and projects are replaced piecemeal over years, so that the resulting person shares no one mental trait with me, though every step was ordinary; and finally a case in which a device can induce, on demand and between any two people whatsoever, the very relation I now bear to my future self. For each, the theory should tell me whether the subject survives, whether it matters, and whether it matters more or less.

Then the constraints that do not come from cases. The theory has to keep the question of what matters distinct from the question of what is identical, or else earn the right to run them together; it has to say what it licenses and what it would require us to revise in the practices that presuppose survival, including promising to oneself, advance directives, desert and punishment, and grief; it has to take a stand on whether the relation that matters admits of degrees and whether it can branch; and it has to be consistent with what we know about how psychological continuity is actually realized in creatures like us, where it comes in fits and starts and is interrupted by sleep, anesthesia, and injury without anyone supposing that a life has ended. Finally, tell me which of the desiderata cannot be jointly satisfied. My own suspicion is that the damage is done by the demand that survival be all-or-nothing together with the demand that it be rationally anticipable, but I want your version of the incompatibility, and what it costs to give up either horn. Then, in one short paragraph, how a psychological-continuity view, a brute-physical-continuity view, and Parfit's own treatment fare against the list. Keep the weight on the requirements; I will do the scoring myself.
