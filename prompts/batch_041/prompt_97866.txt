Years of reading rationalist blogs have left me fond of David Lewis's account of convention — a convention being a regularity in a population's behavior that everyone conforms to, expects everyone else to conform to, and prefers to conform to on condition that others do, because it resolves a recurring coordination problem; the regularity is arbitrary in that some other equilibrium would have served just as well, and all of this is common knowledge. A friend in my reading group has extracted the following argument from that account, and neither of us can tell whether it exposes a real gap or merely abuses the machinery.

(1) For a regularity to count as a Lewis convention, the relevant mutual expectations must be common knowledge in the population.
(2) Common knowledge of that kind can only be produced by some shared practice of attending to the same signals, precedents, or saliences.
(3) Any such shared practice is itself a regularity sustained by mutual expectation, and therefore a convention.
(4) So every convention presupposes a prior convention.
(5) There is no infinite regress of conventions, and any finite regress must begin somewhere.
(6) Therefore there is no first convention, and so no conventions at all.
(7) But language and traffic rules plainly are conventions, so something has gone wrong.

Which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost? I would most like to keep (1), since the account seems toothless without common knowledge, but I want to know what I am paying. If it helps, tell me whether the trouble lies in the argument itself or in a genuine bootstrapping gap that the later simulation literature on the evolution of conventions has to plug. Please answer in American English.
