I retired four years ago after a career in environmental toxicology, most of it inside a national risk-assessment agency, and I have spent the time since trying to give the precautionary principle a formulation I can actually defend. Next month I present that formulation at a small invitational workshop, and what I need from you is a question-period prep sheet, not reassurance.

My thesis, in brief: the precautionary principle is not a decision rule but a rule governing the allocation of a burden of argument. It is triggered only when three conditions hold together — the activity introduces something persistent, self-propagating, or effectively irreversible into a system that cannot be restarted; there is a mechanistically articulated pathway to serious harm rather than a bare statistical association; and the evidence about that pathway rests on models whose structural assumptions cannot be tested at the scale that matters, because the only experiment that would test them is the one under consideration. Where all three hold, the burden falls on the proponent of the activity to show that the harm is not merely possible. I hold that the principle is deliberately silent about how much precaution follows from a shift in the burden, and that this silence is a virtue rather than an evasion. I also insist that the asymmetry at the heart of the principle is not between acting and refraining but between errors that can be corrected and errors that cannot. My worked case is the release of a self-propagating engineered organism into a wild population.

The audience is mostly regulatory economists — cost–benefit practitioners who think in risk–risk tradeoffs and who regard my principle as an irrational refusal to price things — plus two philosophers of science who work on inductive risk and on values in science, and one lawyer from an agency's general counsel office who will care only about who decides. None of them is on my side, though none is hostile.

Give me the roughly eight objections I will actually face, ordered by how much damage they would do, each phrased the way that audience would phrase it and tagged with the camp it comes from, each with a two-minute reply and an honest note on where that reply is weakest. Tell me what to concede in my opening rather than under questioning, and close with the one question I am hoping nobody asks. I don't need a reading list; I need the shape of the fight.
