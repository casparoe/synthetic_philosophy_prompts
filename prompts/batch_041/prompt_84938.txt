A colleague in my lab sent round a precis of the free energy principle ahead of our reading group, and I want to know how much of it I can actually rely on before I open my mouth in front of people who work on this. It went, more or less: 'The free energy principle says the brain is a prediction machine that works to minimise prediction error. Perception is therefore controlled hallucination, meaning we never really perceive the world, only our own top-down predictions, which sensory input corrects only when they go wrong. The free energy being minimised is basically the thermodynamic free energy, so the theory amounts to the second law of thermodynamics applied to brains. A straightforward consequence is that the ideal agent should just sit in a dark room doing nothing, since that minimises surprise, and because nothing behaves like that the theory is refuted. So the free energy principle is either trivially true or unfalsifiable.'

Give me a claim-by-claim audit, as a numbered list of exactly five items, one per sentence above, each a sentence or two, with no intro or conclusion. For each one I want to know what a careful defender of the FEP would happily accept, what they'd wince at, and what is just wrong, and then whether the closing verdict follows from whatever survives the correction. I'm a phil of cog sci PhD student so don't dumb it down, but don't assume I've read the primary lit either. Please write in British English. And tell me where your own audit is most likely to fail, or which argument would definately change your mind about any of the five.
