I am a third-year philosophy student in a metaphysics seminar on dispositions, and I also sit on my university's student conduct board. After we find that an incident occurred, we decide whether it was out of character — a one-off under pressure — or whether it reveals something durable about the person, and that decision drives the sanction. I cannot state the rule we are actually applying, and for my final paper I want to argue that the practice presupposes a theory of dispositions. What has to be true of a character trait for one incident, or for a clean record, to count as evidence of it? Does our practice need the simple conditional analysis — a person has the trait just in case they would act that way were the situation to arise — or does it need the finkish and masked cases, in which the trait is present but its manifestation is blocked, or is destroyed by the very circumstances that would call it out? I suspect we conflate at least three different excuses (the trait was never there, it was there but masked, it was there and then lost), and I want to know which of them the practice can actually accommodate. The clean-record cases puzzle me most: reading the absence of misconduct as evidence of good character seems to assume that the relevant temptations arose and were resisted, and I cannot see how we would know that. Also tell me whether the practice requires traits to be intrinsic to the person, or whether it survives the thought that some dispositions are partly constituted by their circumstances — and what the board would have to stop doing if we took masking and finking seriously.
