I'm assembling a session for a small graduate reading group in philosophy of science on the problem of verisimilitude — what it could mean for one theory to be closer to the truth than another, and what to say when both are false. What I need is not an explanation of the debate but a diagnostic instrument: a decision tree that takes a reader from a few very general commitments down to a named position, constructed so that every branch question can be answered by someone who has never heard of any of the positions, and so that readers are sorted by what they are prepared to say rather than by which label they find congenial.

Build the tree from the logical space first — the questions and the combinations of answers they permit — and only then locate actual positions in that space. Every node must be a substantive question or a choice between two substantive claims, phrased in plain language; no node may ask, in effect, whether the reader accepts a particular named view.

The following positions must appear among the leaves, identified by their commitments rather than by anybody's name:

(a) the original content-based definition, on which one theory is at least as close to the truth as another when the first theory's true consequences include the second's and the first's false consequences are included among the second's;
(b) refined-content accounts that keep the consequence machinery but systematically exclude, discount, or reweight false consequences;
(c) possible-worlds likeness accounts, on which closeness is a matter of distance between the theory's worlds and the actual world, aggregated by some stated rule;
(d) accounts on which the target of comparison is not the actual world but a class of worlds — a nomic or nomological target — so that a theory is judged by which possibilities it rules out;
(e) epistemic or estimated truthlikeness, on which what is ranked is a theory's expected closeness given the available evidence rather than its actual closeness;
(f) deflationary views on which the notion is dispensable because something else does the work (empirical adequacy, predictive success, approximate truth, unification);
(g) views on which truthlikeness is a primitive relation that resists definition;
(h) structuralist accounts on which the relata are structures or models rather than sets of sentences.

Where a position has a standard name you may give it, but the leaf must stand on its description alone; if you are unsure of a name, write unnamed rather than guessing. I do not want citations, reading lists, or references to particular works — the instrument has to work for a reader with no library.

Treat the following as given, and make it bite: on the original content-based definition, no false theory comes out strictly closer to the truth than any other false theory, because a false theory's stock of true consequences cannot be enlarged without enlarging its stock of false consequences as well. Every leaf that retains the consequence machinery must say what it does about this; every leaf that abandons it must say what it substitutes.

Every leaf must record a verdict on each of the four cases below, or state explicitly that the account is silent:

- Case I. A language with three atomic claims, all three true. Compare three false theories: the first gets two of them right and one wrong; the second gets one right and two wrong; the third gets none right.
- Case II. A language with two atomic claims, both true. Compare a theory that gets the first right and the second wrong with a theory that gets the first wrong and the second right.
- Case III. The same false claim about a continuous magnitude, formulated twice: once directly and once as a long disjunction over a finite partition of that magnitude's range. Does the account rank the two formulations identically, and does it treat them as the same theory at all?
- Case IV. Two probabilistic hypotheses over the same three-claim space, neither of which assigns probability 1 to the truth: one concentrates a moderate amount of probability on the true complete description, the other spreads slightly less over the true description but keeps the remainder closer to it. Which is closer to the truth, or is the account silent?

Each leaf should also carry one sentence on its most awkward commitment and a status marker — defensible, refuted, minority, or unexplored. Finally, list the combinations of answers that no one occupies and say for each whether that is because the view is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored, and flag any pair of leaves your tree cannot separate, rather than papering over it.

Keep the tree to at most six branching levels and between seven and nine leaves. Return everything as a single JSON object with the following fields: nodes, an array of objects each having id, parent (null for the root), question, answers (an array of objects with answer_text and child_id), and separates (one sentence naming which leaves the branch divides); leaves, an array of objects each having id, position (a name or unnamed), path (the sequence of node ids and answers leading to it), core_commitment, handling_of_the_triviality, verdicts (an object with keys case_I, case_II, case_III, case_IV), language_invariance, most_awkward_commitment, and status; unoccupied_combinations, an array of objects each having combination, diagnosis, and explanation; and inseparable_pairs, an array of objects each having leaves and why.
