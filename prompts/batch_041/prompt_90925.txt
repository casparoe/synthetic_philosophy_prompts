Thirty-odd years in a physiology department (kidneys, mostly), and I've retired into philosophy, which is humbling in ways I didnt expect. Two things have collided and I'd like help sorting them out.

My back is a mess, so I've been going to a pain clinic, and the physio there keeps saying, as if it settles the matter, "hurt doesn't equal harm." I've now heard two other people at the clinic use the same phrase, so I take it it's a slogan in that trade. Meanwhile I've been reading about people who, after certain strokes or brain injuries, report that a burn or a pinprick hurts -- they'll agree that it hurts, they just don't mind it, aren't distressed, sometimes don't even pull away, and one account has a patient finding it mildly funny. Asymbolia, I think it's called.

What I want is a menu of the precise claims "hurt doesn't equal harm" could be making, laid out from the trivial end to the radical end: that the feeling and the tissue damage are seperate things; that the intensity of the pain doesn't track the amount of damage; that pain is something the brain constructs rather than a reading taken off the body; that pain is only bad because it warns us about harm. Which of those are straightforwardly true, which are platitudes in costume, which are contentious, which are false? And which one is she actually relying on when she tells me to stop worrying that I'm re-injuring myself? Then, reading by reading, tell me what the stroke patients do to it -- if a person can have pain and simply not be bothered by it, does that show the awfulness is a separate ingredient bolted on, or is that too quick?
