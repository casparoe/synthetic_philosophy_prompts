I'm a high school student in my school's philosophy club, and I've been asked to write the closing joke for this term's club zine. The theme is sphexishness, and I'd like your help, because I want the joke to be genuinely good rather than the sort of thing that gets a polite laugh because nobody wants to be the one who doesn't get it. I'm treating you as a professional philosopher with a working sense of humour and a real command of the philosophy of mind and the free will literature, so please bring that to bear, and please think carefully rather than going with the first idea that comes to you.

Here is the background, so that the joke can be built on the case itself rather than on a vague impression of it. The digger wasp of the genus Sphex digs a burrow, paralyses a cricket, drags it by the antennae to the entrance, leaves it on the threshold, and goes inside to inspect the burrow before hauling the prey in. If you nudge the cricket a few inches away while she is inside, she will come out, find it, drag it back to the threshold, and then inspect the burrow all over again from the start, even though she has just inspected it. Repeat the nudge and the whole routine repeats with it; by some accounts you can keep her at it more or less indefinitely, since the program seems to reset rather than to notice that anything is off. Jean-Henri Fabre wrote about this wasp, Dean Wooldridge used it in a book on the brain in the early nineteen sixties, and Daniel Dennett and Douglas Hofstadter made it a standard example of behaviour that looks purposeful while being purely mechanical; it is Hofstadter's word, sphexishness, that names the phenomenon. There is a complication you should know about too: a philosopher named Fred Keijzer has argued that the wasp is not as hopeless as the story suggests, since with enough repetitions she does eventually change what she does, and since re-inspecting a nest that something has disturbed may simply be a sensible precaution rather than a glitch. That complication seems to me like good material, but I'll leave it to you whether it goes into the joke or only into the explanation afterwards.

What I want is a joke about mechanical rigidity, flexibility, and what the difference has to do with free will, where the humour turns on the actual structure of the case rather than on the fact that wasps are small and insects are supposedly stupid. The loop, the reset, the fact that the experimenter is the flexible one, the suspicion that the wasp might be more flexible than the parable allows, the awkward position of the philosopher who sets up the same experiment forty times in a row in order to demonstrate that someone else is stuck in a rut: any of these could carry a punchline. What I don't want is a joke that would work just as well with the wasp swapped out for a robot, or that reduces to a complaint about how everyone is on autopilot, or that sounds like a stand-up routine about bugs. It should also be able to survive on its own for someone who has never heard of any of this, even if it lands harder for the people who have; a joke that needs a lecture before it can be told is not much of a closing joke for a zine.

Before you settle on anything, please brainstorm a few genuinely different angles and punchlines to yourself, at least three or four, and then commit to the one that is actually funniest rather than the one that is most educational. Give me the finished joke first, and only then a short explanation of why it works and which feature of the wasp case the punchline depends on, including a frank sentence or two about how much background a listener needs for it to land. If any detail you were about to use is one you are not confident about, drop it rather than guess, and do not put words in the mouths of the people I've named; paraphrase their views or leave them out.

Please write the whole thing in continuous prose, the way you would write an email or a letter, with no headings, no bullet points, no numbered lists, no bold or italic text, and no tables.
