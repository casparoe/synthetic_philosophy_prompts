Last night I lost an argument to a friend of mine — an anaesthetist who also teaches medical ethics — about whether the doctrine of double effect is a genuine moral principle or a flattering description of verdicts we reach on other grounds, and I have come away unable to say whether my own position is a coherent package. I would like an audit before I commit it to paper for a seminar. His charge, which I could not answer on the spot, was that the condition everyone quotes — that the harm be intended, or at least not intended as a means — never actually does any work in the cases I care about, because a proportionality judgement is quietly carrying the whole load; I think he is wrong, but I cannot articulate why.

Here, as honestly as I can state them, are the commitments I hold. (1) The distinction between harm intended as a means and harm foreseen but not intended marks a real moral difference, and the doctrine is the right shape for it. (2) I accept the familiar pair: diverting a runaway trolley onto a single track worker to save five is permissible, whereas killing one healthy patient to distribute his organs to five dying ones is not, and I take the doctrine to explain the difference rather than merely restate it. (3) A variant my friend put to me: the side track curves around and rejoins the main line, so that the diverted trolley fails to come back round to the five only because the one man's body brings it to a halt; on this version his death is not a byproduct of the diversion but the very thing that stops the trolley, and I still want to say the diversion is permissible. (4) A second case of his, drawn from the older medical casuistry: to deliver a woman whose fetus cannot pass through the birth canal, the surgeon must first collapse the fetus's skull, and the fetus dies as part of the manoeuvre that saves her; I want to say this is permissible too, though the death there is plainly a step in the plan. (5) When I speak of what is intended, I mean nothing about the agent's feelings, hopes, or preferences: I mean what his plan requires. The tactical bomber who wishes the civilians were elsewhere is not thereby excused, and the terror bomber who kills them to break morale is not condemned merely for enjoying it. (6) Whether an act is permissible is settled by the facts, not by the agent's evidence: an act can be wrong even when the agent has done everything a reasonable person could ask in working out what to do. (7) And I take the value of outcomes to be exhausted by how well off people are — I do not believe in agent-relative values, or that the world is worse because of who did what to whom, as distinct from how people fare.

What I need is not a survey and certainly not a reading list, but an audit. Tell me which of these pull against one another and how badly, ordered by severity rather than presented as equally bad; for each, say precisely what would have to give and what the cheapest repair would be. Be careful to separate the pairs that genuinely conflict from the pairs that only appear to, since I am fairly confident that at least one of my apparent inconsistencies is harmless, and I would rather not jettison something on a false alarm. Then finish by naming the single revision that would clean up the most at the least cost, and end with a final line in the form "Verdict: <the commitment to revise, in a short phrase>".
