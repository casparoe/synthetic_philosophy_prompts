I am applying for a one-year dissertation fellowship and need a two-page research proposal. My working idea is this. Discussions of whether reinforcement learning agents can suffer tend to stall on whether a scalar reward signal is the right kind of thing to bear valence at all, and I think the stall comes from a conflation. A reward signal can stand in at least three quite different relations to the system that carries it. It can be a mere causal lever: a scalar that multiplies a gradient and shapes which actions become more likely, and that is never itself represented anywhere in the agent. It can be a represented variable: something the agent predicts, models, or reads off, so that the system carries an internal state whose content is roughly how well things are going for it. Or it can be a felt quality. The first two are routinely treated as interchangeable, and the third is then either inferred from the first or dismissed because the first is plainly not enough. I suspect the middle grade—a reward signal that is genuinely represented rather than merely obeyed—is where the philosophical work has to be done, and that it does not come for free with the first: using a signal to steer learning and representing that signal as a value are different achievements, and a system can do the former without the latter. The question I want the proposal to take up is therefore: what exactly distinguishes a reward signal that is merely used from one that is represented as a value, and does the second, once present, supply everything a valence realist needs short of phenomenology, or is it still a further step away?

Turn this into the proposal. State the question so that it has a determinate answer; explain why the familiar positions—those that read valence straight off functional role, and those that deny it to anything whose states are not felt—leave it open rather than settling it; and set out the method, which I take to be conceptual analysis carried out against a small number of deliberately constructed cases rather than empirical work, together with the reason for preferring that to the obvious alternatives (comparative neurobiology, behavioural tests, or a survey of what practitioners say they mean by reward). For each case I construct, the proposal should commit me to saying which features of the architecture the argument actually depends on and which are decoration; the cases need not be exotic, and something as plain as a bandit agent whose reward enters only as a multiplicative factor on an update, set against one that learns and consults a value estimate, would do. Say what a result would look like, what would count as the project failing, and give a realistic month-by-month plan for a year, including what I would have in hand at the six-month mark. One outcome I want the proposal to own in advance: the distinction may turn out to be definable but inert, so that no case can be constructed in which it changes what we should say about the agent. If that is a live possibility, the proposal should say so and say what would be salvaged from the project if it happened.
