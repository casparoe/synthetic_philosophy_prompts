Ok, side project for the last chapter. Everyone knows the Hegel-and-war material — war as the moment a state gets tested, prolonged peace rotting a people, his beef with Kant's perpetual peace because he won't let anything sit above states and pass judgment on them. I've read all that a hundred times and I dont need it again. What I want is a prediction: had he lived long enough to see mutually assured destruction, would nuclear deterrence vindicate that picture or blow it up? My half-formed hunch is that MAD is the case he never imagined — the war-moment never actually arrives, it just stays alive forever as a threat, so a state's sovereignty remains pure potential and never gets cashed out. Which sounds a lot like the perpetual peace he hated, except armed to the teeth. Then the other half, which I think is the same puzzle from the other side: what does he do with a court that tries states and their leaders? He's pretty firm that nothing above a state gets to judge it, and that it's world history, not any bench, that delivers the verdict on states. So is something like the ICC a flat contradiction of him or some weird realization of his own idea? Dont hand me "he was a Prussian statist who liked war," I want the structure of the argument and an honest call on where it runs out. No citations, I'll add those myself.
