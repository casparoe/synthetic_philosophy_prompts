I'm a novelist and I've been ambushed. A friend of mine is doing a PhD in philosophy and he walked me through the following on a long drive, and I wrote it down on the back of a receipt. Now I can't get it out of my head, and I need someone to tell me which line to cut and what I'm paying when I cut it.

(1) Some things are made of other things. My desk is made of a top and legs, a book is made of pages, a person is made of cells. Take this as given; I need it.

(2) If some things compose something, then for any given bunch of things there's a fact of the matter: either they make up a whole or they don't. No "sort of."

(3) It can't be vague whether a bunch of things composes something. If it were vague, it would be vague whether the whole existed — and things don't "sort of" exist. Either there's an object there or there isn't. He hit this one hard and I definately couldn't find the gap.

(4) So there's a sharp, no-exceptions rule for when composition happens.

(5) Any rule of the form "they compose when they're fused / glued / stuck together / grown from one seed / working together as a unit" has borderline cases. Cloud, pile of sand, puddle, tumour, coral, slime mold, a person in a coma, a corporation. Whatever line you draw, I can describe a case one hair to the wrong side of it, and the line will look like a coin toss.

(6) A rule can't be both sharp and a coin toss. So the only sharp rules that aren't arbitrary are "always" and "never."

(7) "Never" is a non-starter — no desks, no books, no me. Just particles and nothing built out of them.

(8) So it's "always": any things you like compose something.

(9) Which gives me an object made of my left shoe and the moon. An object made of the mug on my desk, the Suez Canal, and one particular hydrogen atom in your body. Objects blinking in and out of existence every time a dust mote drifts past another. I can't write a novel set in that world, or at least I can't take it seriously.

I won't give up (1) and I absolutely won't give up (7), so the bad step is in there somewhere and I can't see it. A seperate friend says the real exit is to say the question is malformed, that "when do things compose" is a pseudo-question like "when does a heap become a heap" — but I can't tell if that's a real position or a shrug wearing a hat.

One more thing that might matter. I write fiction, so I already believe in things that aren't made of anything at all — my characters. If I'm relaxed about those, maybe the moon-and-shoe object isn't the horror I think it is, and my gut reaction to (9) is doing work it shouldn't.

When you answer: tell me which step to reject and what rejecting it costs me, and be straight about which parts of this are actually settled and which are still genuinely contested by the people who work on it. Don't sell me a contested view as the consensus — I'd rather know there are three camps shouting past each other than get a tidy answer that isn't one.
