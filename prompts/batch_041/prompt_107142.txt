I'm a third-year philosophy student taking a seminar on consciousness and the metaphysics of mind, and next week I have to present my term paper to our department's philosophy-of-mind reading group — a mixed crowd I know well enough to be genuinely nervous about. My paper argues that the long-running dispute over whether dreams are experiences — Malcolm's Wittgensteinian case that dreaming is not a form of awareness, and Dennett's functionalist case that dreams are not experiences occurring during sleep — is a false dilemma, because both sides quietly assume that "experience" names a single kind of occurrent state. I want to pull apart two things the literature keeps running together: phenomenal occurrence (there being something it is like for the dreamer at the time) and experiential ownership (the episode's status as an episode of the waking subject's experience, available to that subject as *theirs*). My verdict is deliberately split: dreams are experiences in the first sense, but the second sense is where Malcolm was tracking something real — the dream subject is, in a sense I want to make precise, a character constituted by the waking narrator at the moment of recall rather than a subject who was there all along. My working slogan is that dreams are experiences with retroactively constituted subjects, and I want to defend that against the people in the room.

The audience, precisely: one Wittgensteinian / ordinary-language philosopher who is sympathetic to Malcolm and allergic to any talk of "phenomenal properties"; one philosopher of cognitive science who works on predictive processing, thinks dreaming is just offline generative-model activity, and will say my distinction is empirically idle; one empirical sleep researcher who runs a dream-report lab and cares about REM versus NREM differences, lucid dreaming, and non-recall; and one phenomenologist in the Husserlian tradition who will insist on the lived temporality of the dream and on the dreaming subject as genuinely experiencing in the present tense. There will also be two or three first-year graduate students who ask whatever is easiest to ask.
