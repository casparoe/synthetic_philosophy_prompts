I have been reading the rationality blogs since the mid-2000s, and I now find myself running something that grew out of that scene: a discussion community of about four thousand people. The culture I inherited is the one you would expect — long arguments, steelmanning as a norm rather than a courtesy, an explicit rule against punishing people for the conclusions they reach, and a founding story about having once been the weird one in the room whose views were unwelcome.

For the past eighteen months a member I will call R has been pushing a single thesis. R argues that the body of research underpinning our community's most-repeated practical advice about sleep, attention, and habit formation has substantially failed to replicate, and that people who follow the advice would on average be better off ignoring it. R is careful, well-read, quotes the studies, marks their own uncertainty, and has said in public that a decisive counterargument would change their mind. I think R is probably wrong, and so do most of the senior people here — but none of us has produced the counterargument, and several of them have told me privately that they agree with parts of it and will not say so on the forum.

I have three decisions to make and I keep getting different answers.

First: R's twelve-thousand-word, heavily sourced, self-critical essay is the most-read post of the year. My instinct is that it stays up, whatever I think of its conclusion. Taking it down feels like the exact thing we were founded against.

Second: at an in-person meetup last month, R gave a five-minute version of the same thesis to about twenty people, half of them new, with no sources and no hedges, in response to someone who had just described a decision they were about to make. My instinct is that this should have been stopped, and that R should be told not to do it again. I cannot defend the difference. The content is identical; only the audience and the packaging changed. Either the packaging matters in a way I can articulate, or I am simply more comfortable with speech that requires effort to digest.

Third: R's essay was screenshotted and posted elsewhere with a hostile framing. Since then several members have asked me to remove it, on the grounds that it is now doing harm to the community — scaring off contributors, damaging our standing — and that the essay is the cause of that harm even though the essay itself has not changed. That argument strikes me as either obviously correct or obviously circular, and I cannot tell which.

Two things make this harder than I expected. Our norms forbid pile-ons and downvoting, so there is no cheap way for the group to register disagreement; the available responses to R are silence or moderation, and silence is what we are already doing. And the harm here runs in both directions: if R is right, then the advice is what harms people and my removal of R's essay is what keeps the harm going. I cannot size the two harms without deciding whether R is right, which is precisely the question I am told I must not decide. I am also aware that any rule I adopt to handle R will eventually be turned on someone I agree with, and that if I am honest I would be more tolerant of R if R's conclusion were one I liked.

What should I do about each of the three decisions, and what general rule lies behind them? I am particularly interested in whether the harm that licenses a restriction has to fall on identifiable people, or whether diffuse and norm-shifting harm counts; whether the audience-and-manner distinction can be drawn without collapsing into my personal taste in rhetoric; whether moderating a forum I own is itself a form of social pressure on the people in it or merely a decision about whom to associate with; and which of my three instincts, if any, I should give up.
