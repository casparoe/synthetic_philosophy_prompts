Write a short story set in a small port city that governs itself by prediction markets. It is the kind of arrangement sometimes called futarchy, but the story should never use that word, and it should not be a story about prediction markets in general. Everything turns on the institution's exact mechanics, so treat the following as fixed.

Every significant policy — a tariff, a harbor dredge, a school reform — is decided by a pair of conditional markets opened while the proposal is under debate. The first pays its holders according to the city's welfare index as measured two years after the policy takes effect. The second pays according to the same index as it would have stood two years on had the policy been rejected. The proposal passes if and only if the first price stands above the second. The index is an ordinary composite: wages, grain prices, years of schooling, reported contentment, and a few others.

The second market can never be settled by observation, because the branch it names never occurs. Its payout is fixed instead by a standing board of arbitrators, who convene two years after each decision and reconstruct the counterfactual from the actual course of events. Their working convention, refined over three generations, is to begin from what happened and adjust it by what the policy is judged to have contributed. The convention was never formally adopted, and each arbitrator can defend it in isolation.

Your story should turn on what that convention does over time, and on the incentive it creates: because the counterfactual is inferred from the observed present, a worse present licenses a worse counterfactual, and therefore a larger measured benefit for whatever was done. Nobody in the story needs to lie for this to happen; it should arise from defensible judgments made one at a time.

Tell it from the point of view of a mid-ranking participant — a newly seated arbitrator, a market analyst, a clerk to the board — who notices the pattern slowly and does not become a hero. The story should satisfy the following.

- Let the mechanism emerge from scenes and procedure. No character may summarize it, and no one may name any law, epigram, or principle about measures becoming targets.
- Include one full scene of the board at work, with the concrete texture of how an adjustment gets argued: what is cited, what is waved away, whose hesitation settles the room.
- Show the ordinary gaming of the index's components as background noise rather than as the engine of the plot.
- Give the story one recurring object or in-world phrase that carries the theme without stating it.
- Resist villains. The harm should be produced by a system working as designed and defended by people who are mostly right about their own small part of it.
- End without consolation and without a moral attached.
- 1,500 to 2,000 words, with a title, in American English.
