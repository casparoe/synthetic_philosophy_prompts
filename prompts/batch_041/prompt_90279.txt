I'm closing a reading-group session on the private language argument with a poem rather than a summary, and I'd like you to write it. The poem should be spoken by someone who keeps a written record of a recurring sensation of their own — a record no one else could ever read or check — and who is trying to make that record mean something. They are not confused about what they feel, and they are not deceiving themselves about what they are doing; whatever is wrong, if anything is, has to be visible in the writing rather than asserted about it.

The constraints are the substance of the request. The poem may use only words this person could plausibly have fixed for themselves in isolation: the demonstrative, the copula, the bare words of time and recurrence (now, again, still, today), words for the act of writing and for what is kept, a coined name for the sensation, and words of attitude (surely, perhaps, I think, I know). It may not use any word that names a quality, a kind of thing, or a resemblance, since every such word would already belong to other people. It may not use the vocabulary of the argument itself — nothing about privacy, language, rules, criteria, meaning, verification, beetles, boxes, or any philosopher — and it must not conclude anything about whether such a record can succeed. The reader should be left to draw that conclusion, or to resist it.

The shape: six stanzas, the first of six lines and each stanza after it one line shorter, so that the last stanza is a single line. The coined name should appear exactly once in every stanza, and the shrinking should leave the final stanza as nothing but the name. Rhyme and meter are not required; what matters is that the shortening feels like something closing down rather than a trick.

I want a poem that reads two ways at once. Read innocently, it should be a lyric — someone keeping faith with something that matters to them, and keeping it badly, in the way people do. Read by someone who knows the argument, it should be a demonstration of why the record cannot do the work its keeper needs from it. The demonstration must not be announced anywhere in the poem; if the poem explains itself, it has failed. If a philosopher of language winces at a line, I have failed; if they find the collapse in the place where it belongs, I have succeeded.

Please do not format the response: no headings, no bullet points, no bold or italics, no tables, nothing that looks like a document. Give me the poem, and if you want to add a word about your choices, let it be one short unbroken paragraph after it.
