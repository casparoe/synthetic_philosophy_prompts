I'm putting together a small set of writing prompts and I'd like your help drafting one of them. For this one, take on the role of someone who's taught Strawson's "Freedom and Resentment" for years and also writes comedy on the side. Before you write anything, think it through carefully and brainstorm a short list of the actual moving parts of Strawson's position -- the participant stance, resentment and gratitude and guilt as attitudes we hold toward each other, the objective attitude, the difference between an excuse and an exemption, and the fact that he treats the objective attitude as something you can adopt for a while but can't live inside permanently -- and then pick the ones that will actually carry the jokes. Don't just brainstorm and stop; the brainstorm is a step toward the piece.

Here's the piece I want: a parody employee handbook, in the deadpan register of a real corporate HR document, issued by a fictional company that has rolled out a "Non-Reactive Workplace" initiative. Everyone in the company is required to take the objective attitude toward everyone else, at all times. Write it as numbered clauses, roughly 700-900 words, with the kind of section headers a real handbook would have (Purpose and Scope, Expectations, Disciplinary Procedure, Frequently Asked Questions, etc.).

A few things I care about:

- The humor has to come from getting Strawson right, not from generic HR jokes. Someone who's read the essay should nod; someone who hasn't should still find it funny. A reader should not need to know the essay to laugh.
- The objective attitude -- seeing a colleague as something to be managed, handled, cured, or trained rather than as a party who can wrong you -- should be sold in the handbook as a perk, with straight-faced benefits language.
- Include a clause on exemptions: some categories of employee are outside the scope of the participant stance altogether. Let that be quietly uncomfortable.
- Include the carve-outs where the reactive attitudes are officially permitted -- a scheduled window, the holiday party, the exit interview -- and let it be clear between the lines that this is because the objective attitude can't actually be sustained. That tension should be the spine of the satire, not a throwaway gag.
- Work the excuse/exemption distinction into the disciplinary procedure: "I didn't mean to" gets you one kind of treatment, "that wasn't really him" gets you another.
- End with an FAQ that answers whether the policy depends on determinism being true, and have the answer be that it doesn't matter.
- No citations, no quotes from the essay, no references to any real company or real person. Paraphrase the ideas in your own words. And don't wink at the reader -- stay deadpan the whole way through.

Make it good enough that someone who teaches this stuff laughs and dosn't wince. Oh and please keep the clause numbers consistent, I got burned on that in a seperate piece last week.
