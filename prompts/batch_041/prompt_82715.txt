I am a doctoral student in philosophy working on the metaethical implications of Quine's naturalism. Please explain the conceptual issue that arises when one tries to reconcile Quine's holistic account of knowledge—on which our beliefs face the test of experience as a corporate body and no belief is in principle immune to revision—with his remarks suggesting that moral values are not straightforwardly answerable to empirical evidence and are not part of the true theory of the world. I am particularly interested in whether Quine can consistently hold that moral values are inculcated rather than discovered while also denying that they are merely emotive or arbitrary. Then consider the following modification: suppose we treat moral commitments as part of the web of belief but as governed by a different direction of fit, so that they are revised not by empirical recalcitrance but by social coordination. Does this preserve Quine's naturalism, or does it introduce a normative dimension that his austere naturalism cannot accommodate? Finally, explain what this implies for the status of moral disagreement: in what sense, if any, can two communities disagree substantively about values rather than merely express different inculcated attitudes, and how does that differ from disagreement in empirical belief?
