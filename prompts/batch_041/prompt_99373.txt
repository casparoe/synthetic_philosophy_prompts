Odd one, but this has been bugging me for months and I cant find the label for it.

The pattern: somebody does something good, and then it comes out that the *thought that makes it good* was actually running through their head while they did it — and somehow that makes the whole thing feel worse. And I dont mean they were secretly selfish. The reason they were consciously holding can be the correct one, the one you'd put in the justification if you had to defend the act. Its the fact that it was present in the deliberation at all that seems to thin the act out.

Three cases I keep coming back to. A woman pulls her kid out of a burning car and afterwards says she did it because it was her duty as a mother. A man keeps a friend's secret and explains that he did it because promise-keeping is a general social good. Someone gives away a big slice of their salary and says it maximizes expected welfare. In all three the stated ground is true and defensible — I'd argue for it if someone attacked the act — but as a description of what the person was *thinking while acting*, it makes me trust them a little less, or makes the act seem like it belonged to nobody in particular.

The wrinkle that stops me from filing this under "bad motives": sometimes I want the good thought to be there. If a total stranger drags my kid out of the car and says he did it because it was his duty, I'm thrilled. I dont want him to have been running on "kid, fire, go." So the spoiling looks relationship-relative, or relative to whether the act was supposed to flow from something already settled between the two people rather than from a consideration to be weighed. And it isnt just morality — a person who explains exactly *why* a joke is funny kills it, and I've watched someone talk themselves out of liking a painting by listing its merits. There the immediate thing and its articulated ground seem to fight each other, which feels like the same shape as the moral cases but clearly isn't a moral phenomenon.

So: does this have a name? If it does, what is it exactly, and where are its edges — is the spoiler the *content* of the thought, or the fact that it was one consideration among several that had to be weighed, or the fact that it was *articulated* at all? And if nothing established fits, coin a term and give me the boundary cases, especially the stranger-rescue one where I think we want the opposite verdict.

Bottom line first please: one sentence saying whether there's an established name (or that there isn't), then the carving. Under 350 words.
