In my department a colleague regularly settles historiographical disputes by observing that history is written by the victors. Most recently, they invoked it to argue that any account of a past event which has survived to the present must have served the interests of whoever prevailed; that the surviving documents are therefore less trustworthy than what they omit; and that the honest historian should aim at a 'counter-history' reconstructed from the standpoint of the defeated. I suspect that several quite different claims are being run together under that one sentence, and I want them separated.

Please set out the distinct precise theses that 'history is written by the victors' could be expressing, arranged from the nearly trivial to the most radical. For each, make explicit what it commits one to — about how testimony is produced, why records are preserved or lost, how archives are selected and read, how the relation between power and what counts as evidence is supposed to work, and whether there is a fact of the matter about the past that historiography can in principle recover or only rival constructions. Say for each reading whether it is a platitude, a substantive and defensible claim, or something probably false, and what would have to be true — or what kind of evidence would be needed — to establish or refute it. Where a reading is ambiguous, split it into finer readings rather than leaving it coarse.

Finally, tell me which of these readings, if any, actually licenses my colleague's inference — that survival implies complicity with power, that the silences are more reliable than the documents, and that counter-history is the honest method — and which readings leave that inference unsupported. I would like the result as a menu of readings with verdicts attached, rather than a single interpretation defended at length.
