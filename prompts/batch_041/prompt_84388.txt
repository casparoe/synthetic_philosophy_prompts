At a small workshop on decision theory last month, two participants — call them A and B — spent most of a dinner arguing about whether expected value maximization survives the objection that it is fanatical, and the rest of us could not agree on who had the better of it. I was a bystander and have no stake in either side. What I want from you is a point-by-point adjudication of the exchange as it actually went, not a summary of the surrounding literature; the substance matters to me and the attributions do not.

Both parties accepted the following two cases as fair descriptions of the problem.

Case 1. You may take $50,000 in cash, or a ticket that pays $100 billion with probability one in a million and nothing otherwise. The ticket's expected monetary value is $100,000, twice the sure sum.

Case 2. You may secure a certain, modest improvement in the lives of a few thousand people, or take an option that with probability one in a billion produces an improvement a quadrillion times as large and otherwise produces nothing. The option's expected value is a million times the certain improvement.

A's position. The two cases have the same structure and the same correct answer, and taking the values at face value, the answer in both is the gamble. Expected value maximization is the whole of practical rationality. Case 1 feels different only because we are averse to risk, and risk aversion is a preference — one that a concave utility function can already encode, so that a logarithmic utility of wealth will tell you to take the $50,000, in which case the question simply becomes why that curvature should survive into Case 2 — or else it is a mistake. Either way it is not evidence for a special rule that overrides expected value. And any concavity strong enough to save the modest option in Case 2 is strong enough to make the astronomical value practically irrelevant, which is a cap on value by another name.

B's position. She proposed what she called a floor constraint. Certain outcomes must be kept above a floor — for a person, the level below which a life goes badly wrong; for a policy, the level below which the moral losses are catastrophic — and no gain above the floor, however large or however probable, may be bought with any increase in the probability of falling below it. In Case 1 the ticket's losing branch is a floor breach and the $50,000 secures the floor, so the sure sum is required even though its expected value is half the ticket's; Case 2 has the same structure. The fanaticism objection, she said, is not an objection to her view but the proof that unconstrained expected value maximization is not a theory of rationality at all, but a machine for sacrificing everything to large numbers.

The exchange then went roughly as follows.

1. A: The floor constraint, stated absolutely, is fanaticism in the mirror image. It tells you to forgo any finite gain, however large and however probable, for any infinitesimal reduction in the chance of a floor breach — so it licenses spending your entire income, and then your children's, on a one-in-a-trillion reduction in the risk of a catastrophe. Stated with a threshold instead, it needs a number, and no number is defensible except by intuition. Worse, if the constraint attaches to acts rather than to outcome distributions, he can build a two-stage lottery that the constraint forbids at the second stage but that is identical in its reduced form to a single gamble the constraint permits; a rational agent should be indifferent to how the same prospect is described, so a rule that is not indifferent is not choosing among prospects at all.

2. B: Concavity cannot do the work in Case 2, and any bounded utility function is a cap in disguise — a refusal to take large numbers seriously. As for the pump, she accepts that her constraint is not a preference over outcome distributions; it is a constraint on acts. She denies that the reduction of compound lotteries — ranking a two-stage gamble as you rank the one-stage gamble with the same outcome distribution — is a requirement of rationality rather than a substantive axiom, and says the fanaticism argument is exactly what shows it is not compulsory.

3. A: If the reduction of compound lotteries and the sure-thing principle are both up for grabs, then expected value maximization is not the thing on trial; a package of axioms is, and B owes an account of which she keeps and what replaces the rest. A constraint that is not a preference gives no guidance when every available option breaches the floor, nor when the location of the floor is itself uncertain. And where it does give guidance, it is silent about the vast majority of decisions, which are trade-offs among goods that all sit above the floor.

4. B: Where every option breaches the floor she falls back on expected value among the survivors, so her view is expected value plus a constraint rather than a rival to it; where the floor is uncertain she applies the constraint to the probability of breach, which she concedes reintroduces a threshold and therefore a number. She also conceded that the asymmetry between the floor and everything above it is the part of her view she can least defend, and that she needs an argument for why it is principled rather than stipulated.

5. A: The many-shots version. An agent facing a long sequence of Case 2 offers should, on her view, decline every one, although the policy of accepting all of them has an astronomically higher expected value. B: a policy that breaches the floor with probability approaching one is not redeemed by an astronomical value in the branches where it does not breach it, and she denies that the value of an outcome may be multiplied by its probability without limit.

Please now do five things. (1) For each numbered move, say whether it is sound, partly sound, or mistaken, and why; where a move as I have reported it is weaker than the best version of it, supply the stronger version before judging it. (2) Say whether "expected value maximization plus a floor constraint" is a coherent position or an unstable one, and if it is coherent, what work the constraint does that a concave utility function could not. (3) Give a verdict on the two cases: which option is rationally required in each, and whether the answers must match. (4) Say which of A and B is closer to right overall, and what each of them is missing. (5) For each verdict, state how confident you are in it, and identify the single point in your adjudication that you are least sure of.
