I am not a philosopher, but I have been trying to think carefully about a decision that people around me describe as life-changing—whether to have a child, in my case, though the same issue seems to arise for moving to another country or taking up a demanding vocation. Whenever I try to lay out the pros and cons, someone says that you cannot know what it is like until you try it, and that therefore the deliberation is beside the point. I have also read a little about what philosophers call transformative experience, where a choice changes not just what you get but who you become and how you evaluate the options, and I suspect the saying is doing several different jobs at once.

What I want is a precise menu of the claims that one-liner could be making. At one end it might mean only that you lack first-person data before the fact, which seems true of almost everything and settles nothing; at the other it might mean that no amount of evidence and imagination can make the choice rationally assessable in advance, or that the person you are after the choice is the only one entitled to judge it, or that the value of the outcome is constituted by the choosing itself. For each reading, tell me whether it is trivial, substantive and defensible, or false, and say which reading the people who repeat the line most likely have in mind when they use it to wave away deliberation. I would rather you commit to a definite position than hand me a survey: tell me which reading you think is correct, which reading would actually support the advice to stop deliberating, and where the line between reasonable caution and self-defeating paralysis falls. Please answer as exactly five numbered items, each a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion.
