I am a retired experimental physiologist. For most of my career I studied the regulation of salt and water balance in vertebrates, and I trusted that the boundary between a working organism and a diseased one was simply there to be discovered. Since retiring I have been reading my way into the philosophy of medicine, and I have come to a position that I can state but not yet defend. I would like you to audit it.

Here is the position. A condition counts as a disease just in case two things are true of it. First, it must be a dysfunction: some component of the organism — an organ, a tissue, a cell, a regulatory mechanism — must fail to perform the function it was selected to perform over that organism's evolutionary history. On this clause I am a naturalist; I take dysfunction to be an objective, value-free biological fact, and I prefer a selectionist reading of function to a purely statistical one. Second, the dysfunction must be harmful: it must impair the organism's ability to pursue its own life interests. Here I break with the strict naturalists, since harm is plainly evaluative. But I also break with the normativists, because I hold that the relevant interests are fixed by the organism's own biology and not by social convention, clinical custom, or medical fashion. The upshot is a hybrid: the second clause is normative, but it is not socially constructed. What counts as harm is settled by the organism's constitution, not by the clinic or the culture.

Please act as a professional philosopher of medicine and of biology — someone at home in both the naturalism–normativism debate and the philosophy of biological function — and stress-test this position rather than defend it. Begin by brainstorming before you commit to anything: list the kinds of consideration that could bear against a view like mine, and only then sort them. Assume I already know the standard literature; I do not want a reading list, citations, or bibliography. Reason from the ideas.

I want the following.

1. What would count against the position. Which cases, arguments, or empirical developments should move me? Rank them, and be explicit about which would be decisive and which would be merely awkward or embarrassing. Separate what the position is committed to predicting or forbidding from what it merely happens to be compatible with.

2. What a defender would concede versus dismiss. For each defeater, say whether an honest proponent of my view would grant that it counts against them, or would wave it away — and whether that waving-away is legitimate or mere damage control.

3. The pressure points I should worry about most. In particular, work through these. The reference-class problem: if normal functioning is relative to a class, who fixes the class, and does my selectionist reading of function escape this problem or merely relocate it? The function concept: can selected-to-do survive the familiar objections from exaptation, from vestigial and vestigially repurposed traits, and from traits whose function is genuinely contested? The harm clause: are there dysfunctions that harm no one — aging, a lost faculty no one needs, myopia corrected before it matters — and harms that involve no dysfunction at all, such as grief, pregnancy, or conditions once classed as diseases and later declassified? And the boundary questions: can my view actually draw the distinctions between disease and illness, between disease and disability, and between treating a disease and enhancing a healthy organism — or does it collapse into one of the rival positions I am trying to steer between?

4. An honest verdict on insulation. Tell me whether the position is well insulated against refutation — whether I have defined the hard cases away rather than answered them — and whether, if so, that is a vice in a view of this kind or simply what a conceptual position is entitled to do.

I am most persuaded by arguments that turn on whether the biological norms are truly independent of our interests, and by cases where competent biologists and clinicians disagree about whether something is a disease; weight those accordingly. What I want in the end is the sharpest case against me, sorted by how much it should move me, and a clear statement of what is left standing.
