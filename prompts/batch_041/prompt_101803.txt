My housemate and a friend of hers argued all through dinner about whether our thoughts can cause anything, and neither gave ground. As best I can reconstruct it: (1) The friend said that every physical event already has a sufficient physical cause, and that a thought is not itself a physical event, so if a thought caused anything it would be an extra cause competing for an effect that is already fully accounted for — at best redundant, at worst ruled out. (2) My housemate replied that this only looks like a competition if you picture a cause as a push that occupies a single slot; if a cause is instead just something whose being different would make the effect different, then a thought can qualify as a cause while the physical story also qualifies, with no rivalry between them. (3) The friend countered that this changes the subject: on that view the thought counts as a cause only because it is fixed by the physical facts, so whatever difference-making it does is borrowed from them, and the physical cause is still doing all the work. (4) My housemate said the borrowing talk assumes the thought and the physical state are two distinct rivals, whereas if the thought is nothing over and above the physical state, there is one cause rather than two, and identity is not competition. (5) The friend said that this saves causation only by denying that anything distinctively mental is left; if the thought's causal contribution is just the physical one, its content is doing no work. I could not referee any of this. Please rate each of the five moves from clearly mistaken to clearly right, with a short justification for each, then say who has the stronger overall case and what both of them are missing.
