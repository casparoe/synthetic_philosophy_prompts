Dewey's move is to take the experimental method out of the lab and generalize it to everything, politics included. Inquiry for him is just problem-solving: you find yourself in a confused situation, you form a hypothesis, you act on it, you check the consequences, you revise. So democracy, on his account, is just the political version of that method — it's the best mechanism we have for aggregating information and testing policies, and he sees it as continuous with science (basically Popper's open society, a couple of decades early). The state should be a kind of laboratory. His reply to Lippmann is that the public isn't incompetent, it's that we haven't run the experiments properly yet — and once social inquiry is up and running, ordinary citizens should mostly defer to the people running the experiments, the same way you defer to an engineer about a bridge. He's also a pure instrumentalist about democracy: he doesn't think democracy is good in itself, only that it produces better outcomes, and he expects inquiry to converge on the right policies, which is why he treats persistent political disagreement as a sign that the experiment isn't finished.
