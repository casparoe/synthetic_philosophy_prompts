I'm a third-year student in a social choice and formal political theory course, and we have just finished the unit on the Gibbard–Satterthwaite theorem. In section, half the room kept paraphrasing the result as the claim that no voting rule is strategy-proof, and our instructor objected that this is a slogan rather than the theorem, since the theorem speaks only about resolute social choice functions over at least three alternatives that are onto and non-dictatorial. I would like to see the theorem actually bite in one small, fully specified case rather than in the abstract, and I would rather build that case than read another proof, so please construct it for me to the following specifications.

The rule I want you to use is one my study group invented, which I have not seen in the textbook: five voters choose among exactly three alternatives, A, B and C, each submitting a strict ranking; a voter's most-preferred alternative receives 2 points, her middle alternative 1 point, and her least-preferred alternative −1 point; the alternative with the highest total wins; and if two alternatives tie for the highest total, the winner is whichever of them comes first alphabetically. That last clause is only a convention to keep the rule single-valued, and I would like the profile you build to make it idle, i.e., no tie for the top score in either the honest or the manipulated tally. The rule is anonymous, neutral apart from the tie-break, onto (each of the three alternatives can win), and plainly not dictatorial, so Gibbard–Satterthwaite says it must be manipulable somewhere; your job is to exhibit the somewhere. Give me the five honest strict rankings, the honest tally and the honest winner, and then a manipulation with three properties: exactly one voter misreports; her misreport consists in swapping two alternatives that she ranks next to each other, so that the lie is as mild as a lie can be; and the honest winner is not her most-preferred alternative, while by misreporting she secures an outcome that is strictly better for her.

After the scenario, do the check. State the honest profile, tally and winner; name the manipulator, her misreport, the new tally and the new winner; and verify that the misreport is a genuine improvement. Then enumerate every voter who has a profitable misreport at the honest profile, with every profitable misreport available to each of them — I have a suspicion the answer will not be "exactly one," and I want the full set rather than the tidiest example. Finally, the analysis: confirm that the rule meets the hypotheses of the theorem and say what that guarantees about your case; say which feature of the case or of the rule is doing the work, in particular what happens if the scoring is changed to the ordinary Borda scores 2, 1, 0 on the same profile — does the honest winner change, and is the same misreport still profitable; say what happens if onto-ness is dropped by restricting the rule to two alternatives, and whether strategy-proofness becomes available; and say how a defender of this rule would try to defuse your case. Throughout, please separate what is settled textbook material from what is genuinely contested among people who work on this, since I have heard both that Gibbard–Satterthwaite shows something deep and troubling about democracy and that it shows almost nothing of the kind, and I want to know which part of that disagreement is mathematics and which is interpretation. Please give me the reasoning and the case itself, not a reading list or citations, since I have no way to look anything up right now.
