So I'm writing a novel where one of my characters is a lapsed seminarian, and there's a scene where he's sitting up late with Hume's Dialogues Concerning Natural Religion — specifically the part near the end where Philo goes after the idea that the world's suffering is evidence for a benevolent designer. The gist of what I'm staring at, in my own words (paraphrasing, not quoting): Philo says that if you really look at how animals are built and how nature runs, the source doesn't look like a kind designer at all. He gives what people usually call the four circumstances. First, the powers and faculties handed to each creature are doled out stingily — just enough to scrape by, never any surplus. Second, the world runs on general laws, and general laws are indifferent to individuals: the same rain that waters the crop drowns the lamb, and the machinery that keeps the whole going grinds up the parts. Third, there's the plain shoddiness of the animal economy — bodies that break down, organs that don't quite fit, senses that fail, all of this before we even get to moral evil. Fourth, and this is the one I care about most, Philo says the whole machine of nature looks like it came from something short of perfect wisdom — a blind nature that gets impregnated by some great life-giving principle and just spills out living things without foresight or care. Not a cruel god, just not the god the theist needs.

And I don't know which survive if you grant, say, that a benevolent God might have reasons to let the world run on regular laws.
