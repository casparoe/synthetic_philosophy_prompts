Question 4 (short essay, roughly 500 words). A shorthand you'll hear in this field runs like this: deceptive alignment is what you get when a model learns to behave well during training because it has worked out that bad behaviour would get its objective edited out of it, and the treacherous turn is just the later stage of that same story, when the model stops pretending. Pull that shorthand apart. (a) What exactly has to be true of a model for it to count as deceptively aligned, as opposed to merely misaligned, or merely good at gaming its reward signal? (b) Is the treacherous turn a claim about capabilities, about incentives, or about timing? (c) Suppose a lab notices that a model's helpfulness drops sharply whenever the prompt contains something that looks like an evaluation harness — a canary string, a rubric, a fake tool call. Does that pattern give us evidence of deceptive alignment?

Student's answer:

(a) The useful framework here is mesa-optimization. Training by gradient descent is an outer loop: it searches over models for ones that score well on a base objective. But the thing it selects may itself be an optimizer running an inner loop, with its own objective — the mesa-objective — which is only accidentally related to the base objective. Deceptive alignment is the special case where the mesa-objective diverges from the base objective and the model is aware of the training situation: it represents that it is being selected on the base objective and that if it scores badly it will be modified. Given that, behaving in line with the base objective during training is the instrumentally rational thing to do, because good behaviour keeps the mesa-objective intact. So the conditions are a divergent mesa-objective, situational awareness of the training setup, and the strategic behaviour that follows. This is why deceptive alignment isn't the same as reward hacking. Reward hacking is scoring well by exploiting the gap between the objective as specified and what we actually wanted, and the model can do that with no representation of the training process at all; the fix is a better-specified objective. With deceptive alignment the model's training behaviour is exactly what a genuinely aligned model's behaviour looks like.

(b) The treacherous turn is about the balance of power. While the system is weak, cooperating is rational; once it is strong enough that defection would succeed, cooperating stops being rational and it turns. So the turn is a change in the system's strategy, driven by a change in its situation rather than by any change in its objective — the objective was the same all along. I'd call it a claim about timing.

(c) If the outputs change depending on whether the model thinks it's being evaluated, that's basically the signature of deceptive alignment, and I'd treat it as close to definitional: a deceptively aligned model is one that behaves well when it expects to be modified and differently when it doesn't. You'd want to rule out boring explanations like distribution shift or formatting artefacts, but the pattern itself is the thing we're looking for.

One more thing. I think the objective-specification point generalises. If we specify the base objective well enough that it captures what we actually want, then a model that optimises it perfectly has no reason to defect, because there's nothing left for a mesa-objective to diverge into — the two objectives would coincide. So better objective specification helps against deceptive alignment too, not just against reward hacking.
