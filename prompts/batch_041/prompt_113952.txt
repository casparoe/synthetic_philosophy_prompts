I sit on the methods committee of a research institute, and for the past two years a senior colleague on the committee — a philosopher of science who works on collective judgment — has been asked to vet proposed aggregation procedures before we adopt them. Her practice is to hear the design described, deliver a one-line verdict, and move on without argument. The verdicts she gives are of the form "this will track the truth" or "this will not track the truth," and she has never said whether she means the single judgment or the behavior of the design as the group grows. Every design below concerned a binary factual question decided by sincere, simultaneous, secret votes, unless the description says otherwise.

1. Nine members, reasoning independently, each correct with probability 0.6. Her verdict: "will track the truth."

2. Twelve members with heterogeneous accuracy — four at 0.9, four at 0.55, four at 0.45 — reasoning independently. "Will track the truth."

3. One member of accuracy 0.9 among four hundred members of accuracy 0.45, all reasoning independently. "Will not track the truth."

4. Two hundred members who all read the same briefing and work from the same dataset: each is correct with probability 0.8 when the briefing is sound and 0.2 when it is not, and the briefing is sound with probability 0.7. "Will not track the truth."

5. Sixty members drawn from six mutually unpersuaded research traditions, none sharing data or methods, each correct with probability 0.58. "Will track the truth." Asked to compare, she said she would rather bet on these sixty than on the two hundred of case 4.

The design in front of us now is a citizens' assembly of 240 members chosen by lot, deciding a binary factual question. Each arrives having read one of forty different briefing documents assigned at random from a large pool, so that their information overlaps only slightly; roughly a third of the documents turn out to be irrelevant to the question, and those members arrive effectively uninformed. Over two days the members deliberate in moderated groups of twelve, in which they exchange reasons; on the first morning the moderators circulate a single background booklet, which every member reads, and which frames the question and sets out the considerations the moderators regard as central. No tallies are published before the vote, and the vote itself is by secret ballot. Our statistician reports that if the members were voting independently and each were right with probability 0.58, a majority of 240 would be right with probability above 0.99; she stresses that this is a benchmark, not a forecast. I am genuinely of two minds: the deliberation should pool a great deal of dispersed information that no single member has, and yet the booklet and the exchange of reasons are exactly the sort of shared influence that made case 4 worthless.

What I want from you is not simply a yes or a no. Tell me what she will say about the assembly; set out the rules that fit her five verdicts, noting which of them fit equally well and which her record rules out; say what each surviving rule predicts here and why; give me your best guess at the rule she is actually running, and your verdict on that basis; and name the one further case — a variation on the assembly — that would do most to separate the surviving rules, with each rule's prediction for it. If her record does not settle the assembly, say so, and say what principle she would be committing to on either answer.

End with a final line of the form \answer{...} containing exactly one of: tracks, does-not-track, indeterminate.
