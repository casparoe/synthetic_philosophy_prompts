I've been trying to draft a short essay on the idea of a free-floating rationale — the notion, which I believe comes from Dennett, that a piece of behavior can be explained by citing a reason for it, that it is worth doing X because X conduces to Y, even when nothing in the system represents that reason, weighs it, or could state it. The bee's dance, the stickleback's rush at anything red, the orchid that resembles a female wasp: we explain all of these by appeal to reasons, and the reasons can be correct or incorrect, and that assessability seems to be what makes them reasons rather than mere causes. My draft tries to press the idea in three steps, and I have a nagging feeling that at least one of them is wrong.

First, I distinguish two jobs a free-floating rationale can do — explain why a trait first appeared and explain why it persists — and I claim these can come apart, so that a rationale may go on floating after the conditions that once made it correct have vanished, in which case it explains the origin of the trait but not its maintenance. Second, I examine cases where the rationale of one system is implemented by the machinery of another: the orchid's deception is carried out by the wasp's own mate-recognition, so the wasp's competence becomes the substrate on which the orchid's rationale runs. Third, I draw what I take to be the general lesson, namely that a free-floating rationale is never truly free, because it must be anchored in some mechanism that tracks the relevant conditions and can therefore fail to track them; on this basis I conclude that talk of the reasons of a market, an ecosystem, or a species, taken in the abstract, is always a disguised borrowing from the mechanisms of the things inside it, and that nothing floats entirely free of every mechanism. I also lean on the further claim that because no reasoner is present, nothing represents anything — which I am now inclined to think is too quick.

What I would like from you is a set of comments. Tell me where the argument is wrong or overstated, with particular attention to that third step and to the claim about representation; tell me whether the origin-versus-maintenance distinction is doing real work or is merely the familiar distinction between selection and maintenance dressed up; and tell me which single paragraph you would cut if the piece had to be shorter. Please give me continuous prose, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables — just an email-length reply I can read straight through.
