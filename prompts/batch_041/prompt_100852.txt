The debate over the definition of death has now run for roughly half a century — whole-brain versus circulatory versus higher-brain criteria — and on my hospital's ethics committee it resurfaces every few years without anyone moving, even though the disputants agree on the physiology: the permanently unconscious patient on a ventilator whose brainstem function has ceased, the patient whose circulation has stopped but whose brain might in principle be restored, the anencephalic infant, the patient in a persistent vegetative state. My own hypothesis, offered as a hypothesis, is that this is not really a disagreement about biology at all but either a verbal dispute about the word death or a moral dispute about which patients we may treat as dead, dressed up in definitional clothing. Diagnose it for me: is the impasse verbal, empirical, metaphysical (a disagreement about what an organism essentially is, or about whether organisms are essentially integrated wholes), or moral — or several of these tangled under a single heading, with each side answering a different question? Argue from what each side actually claims about the cases rather than from the literature. Then tell me what would count as progress given your diagnosis: a distinction, a decision procedure, a change of question, or a reason to think that no progress should be expected and why.
