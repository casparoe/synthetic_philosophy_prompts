Here's the draft's argument in a nutshell. The standard move: if your preferences break the usual axioms — transitivity, completeness, and the independence/sure-thing axiom — then an adversary can offer you a sequence of trades, each of which you'll individually accept, that leaves you worse off or even loops you back to where you started minus some cash. You're a "money pump." Conclusion: incoherent preferences are irrational, so rationality demands expected-utility-maximizing preferences.

My angle is that this argument is doing something sneaky. It slides between two claims that aren't the same: (1) an incoherent agent is exploitable — there exists a sequence of offers that drains them — and (2) an incoherent agent is irrational. The bridge is a picture of rationality as "you must not be able to be made a sucker," and I want to argue that picture is the real hidden premise, and nobody ever defends it head-on. On top of that, the exploitability claim is weaker than people let on: it needs the agent to not see the trap coming, or not be able to commit to a standing policy. A resolute agent who just says "I refuse all trades in this family, full stop" doesn't get pumped, incoherent preferences or not. So my punchline is that the money pump is really an argument for resoluteness and commitment devices, not an argument that your preferences have to maximize expected utility. I also want to throw in that the whole thing quietly assumes we can rank outcomes as "worse off" independently of the agent's preferences, which is the very thing in dispute.
