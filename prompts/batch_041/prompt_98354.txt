Ok so theres this old thought experiment where you either let some astronomically large number of people each get a speck of dust in their eye for a second, or you torture one person horribly for decades, and the "just add it up" crowd says take the dust specks. I keep seeing it presented as a clean test of whether you're willing to aggregate tiny harms, but I think the standard version stacks the deck, because a dust speck is (a) brief and (b) actually felt as an irritation. So the real disagreement there is about numbers, not about whether harm that never registers counts at all.

So let me change the case. N people each get a harm that's genuinely imperceptible: not "tiny but felt," but literally nobody notices anything, no experience is any worse, the only true thing is that if you scanned their lives at a fine enough grain something is marginally worse — their life is a nanosecond shorter, or some future good is delayed by an amount too small to ever matter to anyone. On the other side, one person gets something clearly bad but nowhere near torture, say a rough flu for a week. For large enough N, is it right to impose the imperceptible harms?

What I want is your credence that the answer is yes — that at some finite N the imperceptible harms win — broken into pieces. Roughly: (1) do imperceptible harms count as harms at all, or are they just differences in how we describe things; (2) if they count, do they aggregate linearly or with diminishing returns or with lexical priority given to the flu; (3) is there a threshold below which a harm contributes exactly zero; (4) does the fact that no one could ever know or mind about the nanosecond change anything; plus whatever else you think belongs. Number and one line of reason for each, then combine. Then tell me which sub-question your number is most sensitive to and what argument or example would move it most. And if you'd answer differently for the original torture version, say whether thats just the size of the bad thing or something structural.

Smaller side question: my friend claims that if you say "no" for every finite N you're committed to there being a sharp threshold where a harm is exactly zero, which he says is absurd. Is he right that saying no forces that, or is there a way to deny aggregation without positing a magic cutoff?
