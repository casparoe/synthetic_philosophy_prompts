I read law before I moved into machine learning, and I have just joined a lab that works on scalable oversight, so the debate proposal — two systems argue against each other while a weaker judge decides who won — has gone from a thought experiment I admired from a distance to something I am expected to have views about by Monday. In our reading group the analogy everyone reaches for is the adversarial courtroom: two advocates with opposed briefs, a neutral decision-maker who necessarily knows less about the matter than they do, rules of procedure, a burden of proof, and a verdict at the end of it. The suggestion being made with it is that if that arrangement has served us for centuries as a way of getting at facts under uncertainty, then pitting two models against each other before a weaker judge is not a wild extrapolation but the same trick transplanted. I am both drawn to that and suspicious of it, and what I want is a genuine audit rather than a list of superficial differences. Please give me an explicit table of correspondences — what plays the role of the parties, the advocates, the judge, the jury, the rules of admissibility, the burden of proof, the record, the appeal — and then separate the disanalogies that actually bear on the use the analogy is being put to from the ones that do not. Courts have bailiffs and sitting hours; a debate between models does not, and I do not care about that kind of mismatch. I care about whether adversarial procedure is a truth-finding device or a legitimacy-producing one, because the analogy is being used to license a specific conclusion: that debate is robust to the debater who wins not by lying but by burying a true answer under an argument too intricate for the judge to check. Tell me whether that conclusion survives once the disanalogies are on the table, and where it is the analogy rather than the argument that is doing the work.

Two points in particular are where my own confidence wobbles, and I would like them taken seriously rather than gestured at. The first is that the adversarial trial is a local design choice rather than the form of legal fact-finding as such: much of the world runs an inquisitorial procedure in which the decision-maker investigates instead of refereeing two partisans, and if the debate proposal is really the adversarial model rendered in silicon, the existence of a large, long-functioning alternative ought to tell us something about how much of the courtroom's success is owed to adversarialism and how much to the scaffolding around it. The second is that courts, by and large, are not permitted to decline to decide — they must return a verdict — and their standards of proof are calibrated trades between two kinds of error rather than attempts at accuracy for its own sake. A debate judge who is allowed to answer that it cannot tell, or who is scored on calibration rather than on producing a decision, sits in a very different position from a jury, and I suspect that difference is carrying more of the safety argument than the courtroom analogy lets on. I would also like to know how the analogy handles the asymmetry that a lawyer's obfuscation is constrained by an opposing advocate who has every incentive to expose it, whereas in the debate setting the obfuscator may simply be the more capable of the two systems. Close by telling me what the better analogy would be, if the courtroom is the wrong one.
