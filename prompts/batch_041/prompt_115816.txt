You are a poet and a philosopher of language who knows the literature on meaning and communication well. Think this through carefully before you write, and work out the three registers of the poem before you commit a line to paper.

A friend and I argued late into last night about where meaning lives. She holds that a text means what its writer meant and nothing more, so that when the writer dies the meaning dies with her; I hold that a text means whatever the codes available to its reader will yield, and that some of what travels is transmitted whether or not anyone intends it. What neither of us could settle is the case in which the two come apart: a message whose intended content is now unrecoverable, and whose author is nevertheless perfectly legible to a stranger.

Write me a poem about that — eighteen to twenty-four lines, loose iambic or free verse — built around one found object: a note in a script the finder cannot read, discovered in the lining of a secondhand coat. Structural requirement: a single line must recur verbatim three times, and each recurrence must do a different job. First it should be content the finder tries and fails to use. Then it should be the bare recognition that this is a message at all, and not a stain or a scribble. Then it should be a portrait of the person who wrote it — legible in the pressure of the pen, the paper, the haste — with not a word of it understood. The final two lines should either say where the meaning turned out to be or admit that the poem cannot decide. Keep the technical vocabulary out of the poem itself; the frame, outer, and inner messages should be doing the work rather than being named. A specialist should be able to point at each recurrence and say which is which, and a reader who has never heard of any of this should still find it a good poem. After the poem, add three sentences identifying which recurrence does which job.
