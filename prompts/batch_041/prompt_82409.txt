My objection comes out of my day job. I write production software, and the binding constraint on how fast a large codebase improves has never been the supply of changes; it is knowing which changes are actually improvements. Every candidate has to be evaluated, and the evaluation has to be at least as trustworthy as the change is subtle, or else you merge regressions and call them progress while every dashboard stays green. Now suppose the codebase is the system's own mind. Then the evaluator sits inside the thing being changed. At each round you either keep an evaluator that was certified against the previous version and hope it can still judge the new one, or you certify the change using something no more capable than what you started with. I have started calling the resulting limit the certification ceiling: the rate at which a system can safely improve itself is capped by how fast it can earn warranted confidence in its own changes, not by how fast it can produce them. And the ceiling should tighten rather than loosen as the system gets smarter, since each new capability makes more of its own modifications too subtle for the tests that were adequate one version earlier, so the fraction of proposals it can responsibly accept falls. Grant it unlimited compute and unlimited parallel copies if you like; the limit I am pointing at is warrant, not throughput. Two questions, and please answer the second explicitly: where does this argument go wrong, and if it does not, which weaker thesis are you actually prepared to defend — that takeoff is fast in impact rather than in capability, that the ceiling is real but sits far above human-relevant levels, or something else?
