I have been trying to give a real definition of life, and a friend and I ended up with an argument whose every step looked fine when we wrote it but whose conclusion I am certain is false. I cannot find the bad step.

(1) Something is alive if and only if it is a self-sustaining physical system capable of evolution by natural selection.

(2) A system is capable of evolution by natural selection if and only if it, or the population it belongs to, displays heritable variation in reproductive success.

(3) A sterile worker bee is alive; so is a mule; so is a human past reproductive age. None of them can reproduce. So the capability in (1) must attach to the lineage rather than the individual.

(4) A lineage of viruses displays heritable variation in reproductive success: variants differ in how many new virions they yield, and the differences are passed on. So by (1) and (2), under the lineage reading, viruses are alive. We are content to accept this.

(5) A population of self-replicating computer programs that mutate and compete for scarce memory and processor time also displays heritable variation in reproductive success. So by the same reasoning those programs are alive.

(6) But no one thinks a computer program is a living thing. It has no metabolism and maintains no boundary of its own against a chemical environment.

(7) So (1) is false as stated: evolution by natural selection is not sufficient for life. Add a second requirement — that the system maintain a thermodynamic boundary and harvest energy across it to keep itself going.

(8) Now consider a class of self-assembling mineral structures of the kind one might find near hydrothermal vents: they hold a boundary, catalyze reactions by tapping the ambient proton gradient, grow, and split, passing their layered structure on as a template that varies and is selected. They contain no nucleic acids. By the repaired definition they are alive, and by (1) alone they also evolve, so they are alive there too — which strikes me as too much. Meanwhile a virion has no metabolism of its own and does nothing until it enters a host, and yet many biologists count viruses as alive. So the repair both lets in something that should be out and keeps out something that should be in.

(9) So every candidate set of necessary and sufficient conditions either lets in something that should be out or keeps out something that should be in.

(10) Therefore there is no fact of the matter about what is alive. Life is not a natural kind; whether a virus or a mineral template is alive is a verbal question, settled by stipulation rather than discovery.

I would rather keep some version of (1) than accept (10). Which step should I give up? Tell me why the offending step looked so plausible, and price the repairs: distinguishing reproduction from mere copying, moving to a cluster or family-resemblance account on which life is a bundle of correlated properties none of which is individually necessary, allowing a disjunctive or graded definition, or accepting that the concept is a prototype-based predicate. And say whether any of these repairs lets me deny (10) while still treating is-this-alive as a question with a right answer.
