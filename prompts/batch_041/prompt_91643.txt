Last night I spent three hours arguing with a friend — a historian who has worked for years on postwar Germany — about whether a nation can be guilty, and whether that guilt can outlive the people who incurred it. She put an argument to me that I could not break, and I would like you to break it, or else tell me it cannot be broken and what I have to pay to accept it. She says it shows that the notion of inherited national guilt is either empty or monstrous, and that anyone who wants to keep it has to give up something they would rather not give up.

The background is Jaspers's fourfold distinction from his 1946 lectures on German guilt: criminal guilt, which attaches to people who did specific punishable things and is for a court; political guilt, which is the liability of all citizens for what was done by the state acting in their name, whether or not they personally did anything, and which is the ground of the demand that a whole people make good the damage; moral guilt, which is the guilt of an individual before his own conscience for what he himself did or failed to do; and metaphysical guilt, which is the guilt of one who stayed alive while others were killed, a guilt before God rather than before any human tribunal. I am paraphrasing rather than quoting, but that is the shape of it.

Here is the argument as she gave it.

(1) Moral guilt attaches to a person only for what that person did or failed to do. The kind of guilt that warrants blame, remorse, and apology requires a deed, a will, and at least the possibility of having acted otherwise.

(2) A nation or a people is not a person. It has no will in the relevant sense, cannot deliberate, cannot choose, and cannot be reformed by remorse. So when we speak of a nation's guilt we are speaking either figuratively or about the aggregate guilt of its individual members.

(3) Therefore any non-figurative guilt attaching to a people must attach to the individual members who compose it.

(4) Jaspers's political guilt is meant to be non-figurative. It is the whole point of that category that it falls on people who did nothing and who may have opposed everything, simply because they belonged and the state acted in their name.

(5) Belonging, unlike doing, is not something one chooses. One is born into it, and one cannot resign it by an act of will, or only at a cost — emigration, exile — that most people cannot pay and that no one should be obliged to pay.

(6) So political guilt attaches to a person by birth rather than by deed.

(7) And since membership is transmitted, since children are born into the same people and inherit its position, the liability runs down the generations as well.

(8) Hence a German born in 1990, or the descendant of settlers in a state founded on dispossession, or any of us born into the consequences of what our predecessors did, stands liable for acts finished before she was conceived, whose victims she has never met, and which she had no power to prevent.

(9) She cannot discharge this liability by good conduct, because it was never incurred by conduct. She cannot apologize on her own behalf, because she did nothing. She cannot even be certain she repudiates it, because repudiation would be a further act while the liability is not an act at all.

(10) So her guilt is a status rather than a deed — which is precisely what (1) says moral guilt cannot be.

(11) And if guilt can be a status, then it is a kind of inherited stain, and the same reasoning would make a person guilty for anything done by anyone she descends from or shares a passport with, indefinitely, no matter what she does or thinks.

(12) That cannot be right. So either (1) is false, or (4) is false, or the move from (5) to (7) is bad.

I would most like to keep (1); I think it is the hardest-won insight in the whole area and I do not want to surrender the idea that blame needs a deed. I would also like to keep something like (8) — I do think people today bear responsibilities arising from what their predecessors did — but I would rather that not turn out to be the monstrous status guilt of (10) and (11). My friend says there is no exit that does not cost us something we want, and I suspect she is right but I cannot see which door to take.

Tell me which step or steps to reject, and why that step looked so plausible in the first place. Then price the alternatives: for each of the two or three most defensible repairs, say what it commits us to elsewhere, what it does to Jaspers's four categories (in particular, whether political guilt survives as anything more than a metaphor for collective liability to pay), and whether it leaves us able to say that a people can be guilty at all. If you think the argument is valid and the real question is only which premise is least costly to give up, say so and argue for the cheapest loss. Do not tell me what various authors have written about this; I want the diagnosis, not a reading list. Please write in American English.
