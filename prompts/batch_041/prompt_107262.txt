I am a novelist, and the book I am writing is set in a society that left no writing of its own — no inscriptions, no archives, no voice — so everything about its beliefs and institutions has to be inferred from the things its people made and broke and buried. I have been working with a consulting archaeologist, and twice now she has closed down a question of mine with the same sentence: absence of evidence is not evidence of absence. The first time I had asked whether a burial rite I wanted to depict might have existed, and she had found no trace of it; the second, whether a form of public assembly could have stood in a settlement whose large buildings she reads as purely ceremonial. I cannot tell whether she is granting me permission to invent or merely observing that my inventions cannot be checked, and for a novelist those are opposite permissions: the first invites me to write, the second warns me that I am decorating a silence rather than reconstructing a past.

What I would like from you is a menu rather than a ruling. Set out the distinct precise claims that sentence could be making about the relation between what a record contains and what a past contained — from the nearly tautological, through the substantive and defensible, to the outright false — and for each, say what it licenses me to write and what it forbids. I am especially unsure how the claims shift with the durability of the thing in question: the absence of a woven textile from an acid soil seems to me to say something quite different from the absence of a fired-clay figurine from the same layer, and I suspect the slogan is doing very different work in the two cases. Tell me, as well, which of the readings the sentence actually supports, which reading my archaeologist most plausibly meant when she used it on me, and which reading my draft needs — and where two of the readings come apart in a way that would change what I can honestly put on the page.
