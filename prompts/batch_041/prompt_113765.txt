Take a practice that many institutions share without ever theorizing it: they treat a thing as numerically the same thing across the total replacement of its parts. A museum ship whose last original timber was replaced decades ago is still exhibited, insured, and registered under the same name; a cathedral rebuilt stone by stone after a fire is called the same building; a corporation whose every shareholder, director, and employee has been replaced remains the same legal person. What seems to hold these cases together is neither the material nor even the form, but the continuity of a process and an authoritative record — a maintenance log, a deed, a register — that certifies the succession. Reconstruct the theory of persistence that this practice presupposes. Is it continuity of matter, continuity of form or function, or the continuity of a causal and institutional chain that the record merely documents? Separate what the practice strictly requires from what it merely fits comfortably with, and identify the rival views it quietly rules out. Then press the practice with two cases it cannot indefinitely avoid: a second vessel assembled from the removed original planks, and a faithful replica built to the original plans after the first is destroyed. Which, if either, does the practice treat as the same, and does that verdict follow from a coherent theory or from an unexamined convention about records and title? Close by saying what would have to change in the practice if we adopted a four-dimensionalist account of persistence instead, or the view that an object cannot survive the loss of even one of its parts. Answer in continuous prose, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables — just the way you would set this out in a letter.
