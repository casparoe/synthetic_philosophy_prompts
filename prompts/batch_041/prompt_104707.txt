Until recently I would have said that any talk of history bending toward justice was either naive or disguised theology, and I would have meant it as a dismissal. I have changed my mind, and I want to know how exposed my new position actually is. What I now believe is roughly this: there is a real, non-accidental tendency for human societies, over long stretches, to expand the circle of who is counted morally and to entrench those gains in law and institutions, and I think this tendency can be explained without any providential or teleological force — by appeal to things like the increasing returns to cooperation, the spread of literacy and record-keeping, and the way a moral gain, once legally and culturally encoded, becomes very costly to reverse. I picture it as a ratchet rather than a slope: no guarantee of forward motion and plenty of slips, but a mechanism that makes certain backward moves harder than the forward moves that produced them. I am not claiming progress is inevitable, only that it is biased in one direction, and I take that bias to be a fact about mechanisms rather than a hope — so I would expect gains resting on infrastructure and institutions (the abolition of chattel slavery, the extension of the franchise) to prove more durable than gains resting only on the convictions of a single generation.

What I want from you is an honest map of what would count against this. I do not want only the obvious counterexamples — I know about the twentieth century, and I know that the language of progress has been used to justify horror. I want the specific kinds of evidence and argument that should move someone who holds the view: cases where a moral gain was decisively reversed and stayed reversed; evidence that the expansions I am citing were local or accidental rather than the product of any general mechanism; theoretical worries about whether a ratchet explanation of history does any predictive or explanatory work, or merely redescribes the past after the fact; and the suspicion that any apparent failure of the thesis can be absorbed by saying the timescale is longer or the mechanism subtler than we thought. Sort these defeaters by how much they should worry me, and tell me which ones a committed defender would have to concede rather than explain away.

Finally, I want a straight answer to the question underneath the others: is this position falsifiable at all, and if it is not, is that a fatal defect or an acceptable feature of a view of this kind? If you think the whole thing collapses into either wishful thinking or a tautology, say so plainly and say which of the two, and tell me what would be left of the position if I gave up whichever commitment is doing the illegitimate work.
