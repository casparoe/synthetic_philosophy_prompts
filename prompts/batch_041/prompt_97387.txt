I did a philosophy minor in undergrad and somehow ended up as an ML engineer — these days I spend alot of my time on forecasting models, Bayesian updating, calibration, that kind of thing, and I still read the occasional epistemology paper for fun, so I'm not a total tourist. I keep bumping into this thing people call the dogmatism paradox (I've seen it attributed to like three different people so I won't pretend to know who owns it), and I want you to restate it for me in the vocabulary I actually use — priors and likelihoods, posterior updates, learning rates, trust regions, distribution shift, adversarial examples, calibration curves, conformal prediction, retraining policy — as faithfully as you can manage. As I understand the argument: if you know some proposition, then you're in a position to know that any future evidence against it would be misleading evidence; so someone who knows P is entitled to treat incoming counterevidence as misleading and let it not touch their belief; and that seems to make knowledge a license for closed-mindedness, which is absurd. Fine — now do the translation, and then give me the residue report, without being generous to my vocabulary.

Specifically I want three things. (a) What maps cleanly onto my terms — my own guess is that the closest analogue is a parameter I've locked down: high confidence, low or zero learning rate for it, and any disagreeing batch filed as noise or as a poisoned sample — but tell me if that's a caricature rather than a translation, and where exactly it stops being one. (b) What I had to fudge: does "misleading evidence" have any counterpart when everything in my world is just data and a target? Is "being entitled to ignore it" anything more than a design choice about how aggressively to update? Does my framework even have a notion of a belief being *justified* as opposed to well-calibrated? (c) What has no expression in my vocabulary at all. I have a strong suspicion that the whole paradox is an artifact of treating knowledge as a yes/no state instead of a posterior, and that once you go graded it either evaporates or collapses into a boring claim about robustness — if that's right, say so and say why; if something survives the translation intact, I want to know what it is, because then it's probably a real problem and not a verbal one. And tell me whether the residue, whatever it is, points to a defect in how I think about my models or to the philosophers arguing about something less substantial than it sounds.

One more thing to keep you honest: don't let my framework's assumptions sneak in dressed up as translation. If the paradox needs a factive, categorical notion of knowledge, or a normative ought about inquiry, or a distinction between being right and being justified in believing, then say my vocabulary can't supply it and tell me what that costs me.
