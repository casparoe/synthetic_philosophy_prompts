I am the clerk of a mid-sized city whose charter was amended last year to establish citizens' assemblies: fifty residents, selected by lot, convened to deliberate on a single referred question and to report within a fixed period. The council wrote the mandate in a paragraph and left the selection process to my office. I have to publish an operating procedure before the drawing, because the side that lost the referendum has already announced it will challenge the assembly's composition, and because my staff — career civil servants with no background in democratic theory — will run the drawing themselves from a printed checklist, inside a fixed budget for postage, translation, stipends, and child care, with no authority to improvise on the day. The city attorney signs off on the final text. The pool is drawn from the voter roll, which excludes a large resident population that the question will affect; the charter fixes the size at fifty and bars me from changing it.

Write that procedure. Ground it in the case for deciding by lot rather than by election or by volunteering — the thought that it is the lot, not the vote, that makes a body resemble the population it is meant to speak for — and in the equally old objection that a lottery gives an office to whoever is drawn rather than to whoever is fit. The Athenians answered that objection with a scrutiny of the man the lot produced, and their lot, notably, was drawn from citizens who had put themselves forward rather than from the citizen body as a whole; I want the procedure to keep whatever is defensible in that scrutiny, to confront the self-selection it concealed, and to name what would corrupt the lottery's point.

The procedure must dispose, in order, of at least these questions, with a decision rule for each and an explicit threshold wherever the rule is quantitative: drawing the pool and recording whom the frame leaves out; stratification (which characteristics, how seats are apportioned when the numbers do not divide, and what to do with a group too small to earn a seat at fifty); acceptance (what to do when the acceptance rate is low and uneven, whether to weight, oversample, or redraw, and when to report a skewed composition as a defect rather than correct it); substitution (whether a resignation is filled from the same stratum or from the whole pool); the internal offices (whether the chair and the rapporteur are elected by the members or drawn by lot, and what turns on the difference); and the boundary of my authority, marking the questions that must go to the council, the facilitator, or the members themselves because they are questions of value rather than administration.

Then run the procedure on three cases: a stratum that will receive zero seats; a member who turns out to be an active campaigner on the referred question, whom the procedure does not permit us to remove; and a request from the council that we weight the draw to guarantee a result it considers representative. At least one of the three should come out against what the city, or I, would prefer, and I want that said plainly rather than softened.

Close with what the procedure cannot do — what kind of legitimacy or deliberative quality it cannot manufacture merely by getting the drawing right — and identify each step where a value judgment is being made under the cover of an administrative rule. Give me the checklist first, the worked cases after, and no more theory than each step needs. End with a single final line of the form \answer{<step number and short label>} naming the one step at which the procedure must hand the decision to a person.
