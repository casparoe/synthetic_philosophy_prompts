I'm in a seminar on rational choice, and we've spent the past two weeks on the absent-minded driver: she passes two intersections she cannot tell apart, though the payoffs differ sharply — turning at the first is worth nothing, turning at the second is worth a great deal, and sailing past the second is worth only a little — so whatever she decides to do, she has to do it at both. For our final meeting I'd like a short poem in her voice, somewhere between fourteen and twenty lines, free verse or loosely rhymed, built so that the form carries the argument rather than spelling it out. In particular, I want a refrain that recurs verbatim: the first time we meet it, it should read as the right thing to do at the first intersection; the second time, in the same words, as the act that undoes the very plan it seemed to serve. Make vivid why she cannot simply do the best thing at each node, since she doesn't know which node she is in, and close on the gap between the policy that is best on average and the action that would be best at the second turn, leaving that gap open rather than resolving it into a bare injunction to maximize or a bare injunction to do what is best here. Someone who knows the problem should be able to tell which intersection is which and feel the trap; someone who doesn't should still get a poem. Please don't cite or name any theorists — I want the case itself, not the literature.
