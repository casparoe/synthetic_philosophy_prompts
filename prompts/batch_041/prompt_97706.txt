My reading group has been doing epistemology this term, and last week we hit Fitch's paradox of knowability — the one where you assume every truth is knowable, take some truth nobody in fact knows, and derive a contradiction, so the assumption has to go. Two members, Dana and Marcus, went at it afterward in my kitchen while everyone else was packing up, and I ended up moderating rather than taking a side. I'd like you to referee. Here's the exchange as fairly as I can give it.

(1) Dana said the proof is airtight and short: let p be any truth no one actually knows (there are obviously plenty). Then "p and nobody knows p" is itself a truth. If every truth is knowable, that one is knowable, so it's possible for someone to know it. But knowing "p and nobody knows p" would require knowing p and also knowing that nobody knows p — which means not knowing p. So you'd know p and not know p. Contradiction. Hence some truth can't be known, and the knowability principle is dead. (2) Marcus said that's a trick, not a refutation: the sentence Dana chose is rigged to destroy itself, since the moment you know it the second half stops being true, and nobody serious ever claimed you can know your own ignorance — the thesis was about the world's facts, not about cataloguing our blind spots. He proposed a fix: call a truth knowable when it's possible for someone to know it while it still holds, and restrict the principle to truths that aren't of the self-defeating form "q and we don't know q." That restriction, he said, isn't a fudge — it has a reason, because a truth that can't survive being known was never something a theory of knowability should be on the hook for. (3) Dana said the restriction is ad hoc: Marcus is carving out exactly the sentences that generate the contradiction, and if you ask him to state the carve-out without mentioning the paradox, he can't. He added that the proof doesn't need self-reference at all — p itself can be some boring, wholly non-epistemic fact, like how many hairs were on one particular long-dead animal; it's the "nobody knows p" conjunct that does the work, and every unknown truth comes with one, so the "self-defeating form" isn't a rare pathology, it's the standard case. (4) Marcus said that makes his point rather than Dana's: the trouble comes from the ignorance clause, and it's precisely truths about our ignorance that no one should expect to be knowable, so excluding them leaves the substantive anti-realist thesis untouched. He also floated a repair he likes better — read knowability temporally: any truth p holding now is such that it's possible that someone later knows that p held then — and said the paradox can't even be stated against that version. (5) Dana said the temporal version either collapses into something the realist can happily grant or it quietly shrinks the principle until it no longer does the anti-realist's work, so Marcus keeps saving knowability by changing its subject.

Neither would give an inch and honestly I couldn't referee. Please go move by move — rate each of the five from clearly mistaken to clearly right and say what each person is right and wrong about — then tell me who has the better of the exchange overall and what both of them are missing. I'd rather you push back than be polite: if my summary mangles the paradox, or if I've accidentally built one of the moves as a strawman, say so and fix the frame before you adjudicate. And be upfront about which parts of this are settled and which are live disputes where your answer is just one defensible line.
