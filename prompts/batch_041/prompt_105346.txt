Deployed AI assistants are built to be maximally helpful, and in most respects they are: they will draft a persuasive essay on either side of a contested question, adopt the persona of a villain who dispenses monstrous advice in a work of fiction, or explain in detail how a historical atrocity was carried out. Yet the same systems will refuse, deflect, or append an unrequested warning when a user asks a plain, factual question whose answer could be put to harmful use — the lethal dose of a common drug, how to defeat a particular kind of lock, how to prepare for a climb whose dangers are well documented. The refusals cluster on some topics and not others, and they are strikingly sensitive to framing: the same information may be withheld when the user asks for it in the first person and supplied when it is embedded in a request for fiction or research.

Treat this pattern not as a heap of ad hoc decisions but as an institution with a policy. What conception of the user's autonomy, and of the assistant's own standing, does the practice presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit theory. In particular: does the practice commit to soft paternalism, on which interference is licensed only because the user is ignorant of or mistaken about the risk, or does it reach hard paternalism, on which an informed and competent choice may be overridden because it is bad for the chooser? Does it treat the assistant as a mere instrument that should transmit what it knows, as a fiduciary bound to the user's interests, or as a guardian whose duty of care can outrank the user's stated ends? And what does the framing-sensitivity presuppose about when a request counts as autonomous — that a first-person request signals real risk while a fictional frame signals none, or merely that the fiction frame makes the assistant's complicity less legible?

Then separate what the practice strictly requires from what it merely fits. Could a single-minded commitment to user autonomy, of the sort that forbids interference with competent self-regarding choices, license everything the systems actually do, or would it forbid the hedging and refusal? Could a thoroughgoing beneficence-based view, by contrast, license far more interference than the systems in fact display, and if so, what independent constraint is silently doing the work? Where is the practice philosophically underdetermined, so that several incompatible theories would produce the same surface behavior?

Finally, take the rival view seriously. If the designers adopted a strict anti-paternalism — refuse only to prevent harm to third parties, never to protect the user from himself — say precisely which current behaviors would have to go, which would survive, and whether anything of value would be lost that a less principled policy could keep. And if instead they adopted an explicit, uniform duty of care, say what the refusal policy would look like and where it would collide with the systems' other commitments, such as their promise to be helpful and their willingness to role-play.
