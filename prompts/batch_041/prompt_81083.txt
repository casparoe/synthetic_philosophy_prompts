I am a software engineer with no formal training in philosophy — I work on backend infrastructure — and the only exposure I get to this material is a long-running argument with a friend who took a master's degree in the subject. We have been circling the same disagreement for months without either of us getting any purchase on it. The question is whether a religious believer and an atheist who say the same sentence are really contradicting one another. My friend's position, which he traces to Wittgenstein, is that the believer who says that God is with him is not advancing a hypothesis that could be confirmed or refuted, that his certainty is not the conclusion of an argument and would not be disturbed by one, that the words are held in place by a whole way of living rather than by anything he has observed, and that the atheist who demands evidence is applying a standard the believer never accepted. In the abstract I can see what he is getting at. In practice I cannot find the line: every case we invent either looks like a plain disagreement about how things are, or looks like two people using the same string of characters for entirely different purposes, and I cannot say what makes the difference.

What I want from you is a single example of a conceptual issue of precisely this kind, worked out concretely rather than gestured at: one case in which two people utter the same sentence, one of them treats that sentence as answerable to how things actually are and the other does not, and the verdict is genuinely unclear — where saying that they disagree seems right for one reason and saying that they are not even disagreeing seems right for another. I want the case to make the boundary visible from both sides, so that the move of declaring that this is not a factual claim looks like the correct description of one speaker's use of the words and like an evasion when it is turned on the other. Three constraints, since I know how these conversations usually end. The case should not turn on the doctrines of any particular religion, because I do not know them well enough to check a claim about them. It should not require me to have read any particular text or to know any scholarly dispute — I have no library at hand and no way to look anything up, so please keep to what you can state in your own words. And it should be something I could plausibly meet in ordinary life rather than a philosopher's contrivance: the sort of thing that happens at a kitchen table.

Once you have given the case, tell me what it shows: what makes it a conceptual issue rather than a dispute about facts, what each of the two people would say if asked to describe what is going on between them, and whether anything could settle the matter. If nothing could settle it even in principle, say so, and say what that implies for whether we should call it a disagreement at all. The nearest thing we have managed on our own is a man at his wife's funeral who says that she is not really gone, and his brother-in-law who says, quietly, that she is; it does not help me, partly because I cannot separate the question from the fact that it is a funeral and that the brother-in-law is being unkind, so please do not simply hand that case back to me.
