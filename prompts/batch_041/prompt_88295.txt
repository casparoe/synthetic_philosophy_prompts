ok so I've fallen down a causation-by-omission rabbit hole after rewatching Manchester by the Sea for like the fifth time and now I cant stop thinking about it. the whole movie turns on a non-event: Lee is drunk, he'd thrown a party, he forgot to put the screen back in front of the fireplace, and thats what burns the house down and kills his kids. nobody charges him, the cops call it a tragic accident, he steals a gun off a cop at the station and tries to end it anyway, and some of the town clearly thinks it wasn't an accident at all.

I want a real reading of the film through the philosophy of causation by omission, not a plot summary with a theory stapled on. so: does the movie actually take a side on whether an omission like Lee's can be a cause? theres a line of thought that causation doesn't need any physical connection between cause and effect, so omissions and preventions are causes just as much as positive events are; and theres the opposite gut instinct that only positive events cause anything, and an omission is just a condition, or nothing at all. which side does the film dramatize, if either?

the fireplace screen is the perfect test case because putting it back is a norm -- is that why we (and Lee) single out the missing screen as the cause, and would we still if everyone in that town left the screen off every night? and notice the counterfactual test looks like it nails him -- put the screen back and the kids live -- but that same test makes everyone the cause of everything, since if he'd never married Randi there'd be no kids to burn at all. the law letting him go is basically the doing/allowing line doing its work, and I'm curious whether the film agrees with the law.

meanwhile the movie keeps handing us rival causes: the drinking, the depression, Randi, the town, Joe dying, plain bad luck. I half think its saying causal talk is the wrong frame entirely, that the real subject is guilt and blame, which are normative and dont need a causal story underneath them. Lee blames himself for something the law doesn't, and maybe causation doesn't either. is that a mistake, or can blame outrun causation?

dont just use the movie to illustrate the papers -- tell me where it pushes back. where does the films structure resist the reading (it withholds the fire for like an hour, so we get the omission as an explanation before we ever get it as an event)? what does the movie see about self-blame and omissions that the philosophy misses? use the police-station gun scene, the Randi-and-the-stroller scene, and the one where Patrick asks why Lee can't just stay and Lee says he cant beat it. the guardianship thing is basically Lee choosing to absent himself all over again, so thats in play too.
