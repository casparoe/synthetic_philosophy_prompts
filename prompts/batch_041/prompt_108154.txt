I hold a particular view about what it is for two mechanistic descriptions to pick out the same mechanism, and I want to know what would count against it. On my view, sameness of mechanism is neither sameness of substrate nor sameness of computational description, but sameness of intervention-sensitive dependence: two descriptions describe the same mechanism just when they agree on the system's counterfactual profile under a specified family of interventions, so that 'the same mechanism' means 'the same pattern of what depends on what'. The view is meant to underwrite anomaly detection (an anomaly being a departure from that interventionally defined profile) and to settle the awkward case of two networks trained from different initialisations that compute the same function. Tell me what should move me off it, ranked from fatal to merely awkward: grain-relative individuation (one mechanism at one level of description, two at another), degenerate or multiply realisable parts, and the suspicion that counterfactual equivalence is too cheap, since almost any lookup table can be rigged to match a mechanism's input-output profile. Say which of these a defender would concede and which they would dismiss, and whether the view survives once interventions in learned systems are admitted to be idealised rather than real. End with a single line beginning 'Verdict:', giving one of: fatal, seriously exposed, survivable, insulated.
