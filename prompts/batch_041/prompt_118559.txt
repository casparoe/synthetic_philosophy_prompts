I run the social-science grant panel at a small foundation — a dozen or so proposals a cycle, two reviewers each, and I break ties. We keep having the same fight: someone explains something by appeal to 'the culture of the profession', or 'institutional inertia', or 'the market's expectations', and half the panel calls that an explanation while the other half calls it a placeholder for the part nobody did. I want a decision procedure my reviewers can run in twenty minutes on a proposal, built on the individualism/holism dispute without making them read any of it: questions in order, what counts as good enough at each step, where it stops with a no, and where it comes back to me because it's a values call about what kind of science we're willing to fund. Two things it has to get right. It should separate at least three readings of a collective term — shorthand for a heap of individual facts, a relational structure that constrains what individuals can do, and a thing treated as an agent with its own wants and beliefs — and it shouldn't just ban the third. And it shouldn't demand the applicant derive the macro pattern from micro facts, only that nothing at the collective level pushes individuals around except through them. It also has to work when the applicant has no individual-level data and can't get any.

Then run it on three: prescribing rates explained by a local culture of prescribing; a firm's adoption of a practice explained by its position among similar firms; and a union striking because its members wanted higher wages. One of those verdicts will annoy me — tell me which, and where the procedure misfires.
