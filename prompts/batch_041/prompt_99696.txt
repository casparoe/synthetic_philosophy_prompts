I'm a software engineer with no formal training in philosophy, so I'll set this up in the terms I actually think in and trust you to translate. I'd like a thought experiment built to order about psychiatric diagnosis and what Ian Hacking called looping effects: when a label is applied to people who can know about it and respond to it, the label changes those people, and the changed people then change what the label ought to mean. The version I care about is narrower than the standard illustration: a loop that closes mainly through the paperwork rather than through patients' self-understanding — through intake questionnaires, scoring rubrics, coding rules, training materials, and the documentation habits of clinicians — so that the category converges on a single script even though no one ever decided that it should.

The purpose is to see whether the evidence people actually cite when they defend a diagnosis — good agreement between raters, stable scores over time, accurate prediction of who responds to treatment — can be manufactured by the diagnostic process itself, so that a standard validity argument becomes self-undermining. That fixes what counts as cheating: the scenario has to make the loop happen through ordinary institutional choices that you show, not assert.

The case must satisfy all of the following:

- Contemporary and mundane: an outpatient clinic, a school referral pipeline, or an insurer's prior-authorization workflow — pick one and stay inside it. No invented neurobiology, no brain implants, no science fiction.
- The condition itself plausibly exists independent of the label; do not stipulate that the category conjures its subject matter out of nothing.
- Early on, the criteria catch a heterogeneous group — two or more distinct presentations — and the psychometric report card is mediocre.
- At least three concrete artifacts or decisions must carry the signal back into the next cohort (a revised checklist item, a billing or coding requirement, a supervision habit, a screening tool that gets treated as the gold standard, and so on).
- After the loop has run, the report card improves: agreement between raters, stability over time, and prediction of treatment response all rise, while a patient who would have met the original criteria no longer meets the current ones.
- The case must include a comparison group with the same underlying condition that is never exposed to the label or the instrument.
- No sentence in the scenario may state what any theory concludes.

Answer as a numbered list of exactly five items, no more than three sentences each, with no introduction and no conclusion: (1) the case, with the loop's steps visible; (2) what the natural-kind position — a valid diagnostic category tracks a stable underlying condition, and agreement and predictive success count as evidence for it — concludes about this category, why, and what it predicts for the unexposed comparison group; (3) what Hacking's looping-effect view concludes, why, and what it predicts for that same group; (4) what an instrumentalist view — the category is a tool, warranted by its usefulness rather than by correspondence to anything — concludes, why, and what it predicts for that same group; (5) the single feature of the case that is doing the real work, what becomes of the three verdicts if you delete that feature, and the strongest way a defender of the category would attack the case. Write in American English.
