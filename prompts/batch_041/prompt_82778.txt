I have run into the following argument more than once, and I have never seen it given a name. It begins from a principle that looks almost too weak to deny: every true statement could, in principle, come to be known by someone — if not by us, then by someone, at some time. Now take a truth that in fact nobody knows, for example that Goldbach's conjecture is true, assuming it is. The statement "Goldbach's conjecture is true and nobody knows it" is then also true, so by the principle it must be possible for someone to know that statement. But knowing it requires knowing both of its parts, and the second part — that nobody knows it — would be falsified by the very knowing in question, so it cannot be known. The modest principle is therefore false, and not for a marginal reason: a claim about what could in principle be known turns out to entail that everything true is already known.

I would like to know whether this has an established name and, more importantly, how it is best diagnosed. Which step is bearing the weight — the appeal to what is possible in principle, or the folding of our ignorance into the content of the statement to be known? Which premise has to be surrendered, and what becomes of the original principle if it is narrowed, say to truths knowable by some finite procedure, or to truths that do not themselves concern what anyone knows? The label matters less to me than where the joints are.
