I have spent years arguing about generations and have slowly come to think the interesting question is not which generation has it worse but whether generation names anything at all — whether a label like Millennial picks out a kind of person the way teenager or twin does, or whether it is a bin we draw and then forget we drew. I run a monthly discussion group of about fifteen adults, none with any background in sociology, statistics, or philosophy, and next month I want to give forty minutes to that question, standing at a whiteboard. Do not explain the subject to me; plan how I should explain it.

Tell me the order the pieces have to go in and why — what has to be in place before the next thing can even be asked — and where the two ideas I have been turning over belong. The first is that when two age groups differ on a survey, that one survey cannot tell you whether people changed as they aged, whether the times changed, or whether the groups were formed differently in the first place. The second is that there may be a real difference between a cohort, which is only a set of people sharing a birth window, and a generation, which is a set that has come to see itself as one. If you think that second distinction is a distraction rather than the spine of the session, say so and give me something better to build on.

Then give me the one example that should carry the whole thing, what it must have and what it must lack, and whether the obvious candidate — the smartphone — is the right one to build on. After that, the misconceptions. I expect people to say that generations are obviously real because you can tell them apart, that they are obviously fake because everyone knows people their own age who do not fit, and to talk about a generation as though it wanted things; tell me which of these are actually mistakes, which ones I have missed, and exactly where in the sequence each should be headed off. I also want the session to end with a test they can apply themselves to any generational claim, so that they leave with a tool rather than a verdict: my own attempt is to slide the birth-year cut by three years and see whether the claim survives, and I want to know whether that test is any good or whether it proves too much — if it fails, give me the closing question I should ask instead.

Finish with what I should openly refuse to explain at this level, and say so out loud in the session. Cap the whole thing at 800 words.
