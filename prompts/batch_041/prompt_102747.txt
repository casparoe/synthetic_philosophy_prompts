Right, I've been trying to work out what we actually believe about who gets to decide things, going by what our institutions actually do, and I keep running into something that looks like a straight contradiction. Take monetary policy first. Interest rates — which touch everyone's mortgage, everyone's job — are set by a committee of unelected economists, deliberately walled off from the government we just voted for, and most sensible people think that's obviously correct: you don't want the money supply yanked around by whoever has an election coming. Fine. Now take a criminal trial. Whether someone did the thing gets decided by twelve people pulled off the electoral roll, who may know nothing about forensics or statistics or anything else, and we think that's obviously correct too, whilst also getting visibly uneasy when the experts seem to be deciding it for them — we'd rather have the laypeople than the specialists settle a person's fate. Then take a medicines regulator or a food standards body: experts again, unelected, effectively final, and nobody wants a referendum on whether a drug is safe. Meanwhile the tax code — also hugely technical, also touches everyone — gets written by elected politicians who are practically proud of not understanding it. So sometimes unelected expertise is the legitimate decider, sometimes it's a standing threat to legitimacy and a random layperson is the legitimate decider, and sometimes we insist the amateurs decide even when the stakes are highest.

What I want to know is what view of political legitimacy that whole pattern presupposes. Does it need a seperate competence condition on who may decide, applied selectively by domain — and if so, what's the principled line that puts interest rates on the expert side and guilt on the lay side? Or can you get the same pattern out of a purely democratic premise, something about everyone affected having an equal claim, plus facts about which decisions can be delegated without loss? Is the mix coherent, or does it quietly smuggle in the thing I think is called epistocracy — rule by the knowers — and keep elections around mostly for legitimation? I definately want you to say what the practice strictly requires versus what it merely fits comfortably with, and where it's underdetermined, so that several quite different theories would license the very same institutions.

Then push on the bits I find hardest. The jury: does the defence of it rest on that Condorcet result about crowds getting binary questions right, and does the argument survive the fact that real jurors don't vote independently and the questions aren't binary? The central bank: is independence justified because the experts are better at the means, with the elected government still setting the ends — and is that a real division of labour or a comforting fiction, since means and ends bleed into each other? And where does the sortition fashion fit — citizens' assemblies where a random bunch deliberate for a few weekends and then hand a recommendation to the people who actually decide? That looks like a hybrid trying to have the jury's legitimacy and the agency's competence at once; tell me what it presupposes, and whether it's a stable position or a fudge.

Finally, take the presupposition you think the practice really needs and swap it out. If we replaced it with strict one-person-one-vote competence-blindness all the way down, or with a frank epistocracy where the knowledgeable rule and the rest don't, what concretely would have to change in the institutions — and which of them would turn out to have been riding on the presupposition all along, and which would survive untouched?
