The question (payoffs are (you, them)):

both cooperate: (3, 3)
you defect, they cooperate: (5, 0)
you cooperate, they defect: (0, 5)
both defect: (1, 1)

Q2. (a) [7] In the one-shot Prisoner's Dilemma with these payoffs, D strictly dominates C. Explain what changes in the indefinitely repeated version — same stage game, but after each round play continues with probability δ < 1 — such that mutual cooperation can be an equilibrium.
(b) [7] Is Tit-for-Tat (cooperate on round one, then copy whatever the opponent did last round) an equilibrium of that indefinitely repeated game? Explain.
(c) [6] Now suppose the repeated game is noisy: with probability 0.05 each player misreads the other's move, so an intended C can look like a D and vice versa. What does that do to Tit-for-Tat, and describe one alternative strategy that would do better.
(d) [5] Suppose instead that the population is large and every round everyone is randomly re-paired, with no way to tell who you're playing or to look up what they've done in earlier rounds. What happens to Tit-for-Tat here, and can cooperation be sustained?

The student's answer:

(a) In the one-shot game D strictly dominates: if they cooperate you get 5 instead of 3, and if they defect you get 1 instead of 0. So (D, D) is the only Nash equilibrium, and nothing you do this round has any effect on anything later, so there's no way to reward or punish anyone. What changes in the repeated game is the shadow of the future. Cooperating today can be reciprocated tomorrow, and defecting today can be punished tomorrow, so the immediate gain from defecting (2 extra) can be weighed against the future cooperation you'd lose. If δ is close enough to 1, mutual cooperation every round is an equilibrium. The indefinite horizon is doing the real work: if there were a known last round, that last round would just be a one-shot game, so both would defect there, which makes the second-to-last round effectively last, and the whole thing unravels by backward induction.

(b) Yes, as long as δ is high enough. Against a TFT player, cooperating every round gets you 3 every round. If you defect once instead, you get the one-time gain of 5, but after that TFT punishes you and keeps punishing you, so the gain is a one-off while the punishment goes on forever. Once δ is high enough that 3 forever beats 5 now plus a bad payoff forever, cooperating is a best response, so (TFT, TFT) is a Nash equilibrium. It isn't subgame perfect though: off the path, after a defection has happened, TFT says to defect back, but if the other player is going to cooperate again then defecting isn't a best response, so the retaliation isn't credible. Also worth noting, TFT had the highest total score in Axelrod's round-robins, which isn't the same as being unbeatable head-to-head, and it isn't evolutionarily stable in the strict sense.

(c) Noise wrecks TFT. A single misread C triggers a retaliation, and since both players are only ever copying the last move, the retaliation keeps generating new retaliations and the pair never gets back in phase — they get stuck alternating, one getting 5 while the other gets 0, then swapping. That's better than both-defecting (they average 2.5 against 1) but worse than the 3 they'd have gotten by just cooperating, and neither of them wanted it. A more robust strategy is generous Tit-for-Tat: cooperate first, then after the other player defects, cooperate anyway with some probability (a third is the usual figure) instead of always retaliating. That absorbs most single errors before they turn into a vendetta, while still retaliating often enough to deter deliberate defection.

(d) This is basically the same as the indefinite-horizon case, just with a low continuation probability. You don't know whether you'll run into anyone again, but there's some chance you will, and that's enough for the shadow of the future to do a little work — you can think of it as a repeated game with a small δ. So TFT-type strategies can still support cooperation as long as δ is above the threshold; it's the same logic as in (a) and (b), just harder to satisfy, and it gets harder the bigger the population is.
