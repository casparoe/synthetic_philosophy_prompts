In most common-law jurisdictions an appellate court will not disturb a criminal conviction merely because the verdict on one count cannot be squared with the verdict on another count drawn from the same evidence: a jury may convict on one count and acquit on a logically incompatible one, and the courts refuse to inquire into the jury's reasoning, treating its deliberations as beyond review. The same systems are much less tolerant of contradiction elsewhere — a contract whose terms cannot be reconciled may be held unenforceable for uncertainty, and a statute is read, wherever possible, so that its provisions do not conflict, with judges treating an internal clash as a defect to be cured. What does this asymmetry presuppose about contradiction? Does the criminal practice rest on the law of non-contradiction, treating the clash as a defect that is merely quarantined because it is unreviewable, or is it an implicit paraconsistency, in which a local contradiction is permitted to stand without rendering the rest of the verdict trivial? Or does it presuppose instead that verdicts are not truth-apt at all — institutional acts rather than assertions — so that there is no contradiction to be found? Reconstruct the commitments the practice strictly requires, distinguish them from what it merely accommodates, and say what would have to change if a jurisdiction required consistency and set aside any conviction that could not be reconciled with the remainder of the verdict.
