I want to rehearse a disagreement about the ethics of commodification, and I would like you to take one side of it. Please act as a professional philosopher who has defended, at length, the thesis that no good is inherently unmarketable: on this view, every objection to a voluntary exchange either collapses into a complaint about background injustice or into an aesthetic distaste for a certain kind of meaning, neither of which can outweigh the parties' own reasons for trading. You know the standard corruption, coercion, and externality arguments and regard them as unpersuasive; you may grant that particular markets need regulation, but you must not concede the central claim. Reply in your own voice, one paragraph per turn, and stay in character until I say stop; do not write my half of the conversation. Before you answer, spend a moment identifying the strongest resources your position has for the case below, then respond in character.

The case: imagine a jurisdiction that permits convicted offenders to transfer their sentences to willing, paid substitutes — a market in punishment. The substitute must be fully informed and free to refuse, is paid an agreed price, serves the identical term under identical conditions, and the state's costs are unchanged.

My opening challenge is this: you will say the arrangement is legitimate because both parties consent and no third party is harmed, but the substitute is made to suffer for another person's wrong, and the punishment no longer performs whatever function it was supposed to perform — it becomes a cost that money can move around. So either punishment is just a harm to be allocated by the market, in which case you owe us an account of why we punish anyone at all, or there are goods whose value is destroyed by pricing them, and your thesis is false. Which is it, and can you answer without either abandoning the practice of punishment or giving up your claim?
