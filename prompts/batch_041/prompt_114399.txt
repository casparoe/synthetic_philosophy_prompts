My friend and I spent most of a long drive arguing about whether a trait can have a function before natural selection has had any chance to act on it, and we got nowhere. She holds that ascribing a function to a trait is just a way of saying what it was selected for; I kept objecting that this makes it impossible to say what a brand-new trait is for at exactly the moment when the question matters most. I teach an upper-level philosophy of biology course, and I want to turn that stalemate into the essay question on the final, because I have found that students can produce the causal-role versus selected-effects contrast on demand and then have nothing to say when you hand them a case that falls between the two.

The exam is closed-book and there is no internet in the room, so the question has to stand on its own. State the two accounts on the exam sheet in compressed form, in a couple of sentences each and in my voice; do not require citations, reading lists, or any particular author's formulation, and do not let the answer turn on knowing who said what. The biology of the case is stipulated, so nobody should lose marks for doubting that such a creature exists; the marks are for the analysis.

Here is the material I want the question built on. The trait is jaw morphology in a lizard, and there are three variants.

First variant. A population colonises an island. Jaw shape changes as a correlated by-product of strong selection on overall body size; nothing has selected for jaw shape itself. It happens that the new jaw can crack a hard-shelled seed that is abundant on the island, and cracking that seed now supplies most of the population's calories and accounts for a large share of realised reproductive success. No generation has yet lived and died under selection for seed-cracking in particular.

Second variant. A sister population on a neighbouring island has the same jaw, but the hard-shelled seeds went extinct there two hundred generations ago. The jaw is now selectively neutral, is eroding under drift, and still cracks seeds when a lizard is offered them in the laboratory.

Third variant. A lizard from that second population has a developmental defect: the jaw is fused and can crack nothing.

In all three variants the jaw also makes a faint clicking noise when it moves, and the clicking has no effect on survival or reproduction.

I want the question to require, for each variant, what a causal-role account and a strict selected-effects account each say, and to make the student locate the variant where the two accounts are plainly answering the same question and yet give opposite verdicts. From there it should force a defended verdict rather than a survey: which answer the student would stand behind, what the other side has to give up to keep its answer, and whether the familiar move of declaring that there are simply two concepts of function answering two different questions is a resolution of the case or an evasion of it. It should also make them handle the type/token point, since the fused jaw belongs to an individual while the function claim is usually about the trait in a population, and to say which single demand on a theory of function each account sacrifices, choosing from: distinguishing a function from a mere effect, making room for malfunction, accommodating novel traits, accommodating dormant traits, and not making function ascriptions hostage to a history nobody can observe. A closing move asking which account they would want a colleague in molecular biology or clinical medicine to be using would be welcome.

Please give me three things. First, the question exactly as it will be printed on the exam sheet, including the compressed statement of the two accounts and the case material, pitched at 75 minutes and roughly 900 words of writing. Second, a model answer of about 900 words, written as a strong student would write it rather than as a literature review, with no citations. Third, a marking scheme out of 25: five or six must-hit moves with the marks each is worth, a one-line note for each on the mistake that typically costs marks there, and a final line on what separates an A from a B. A student who has memorised the labels but not thought about the case should not be able to get past a low B.
