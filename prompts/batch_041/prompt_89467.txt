Two archaeologists are arguing over a dig, and I want the argument itself, not a summary of it. The setup: a village occupied for maybe three generations; in one small room they find about forty identical little clay figures, every one deliberately snapped in half at the waist, none of them showing any wear, all buried together under the floor, and then the room is sealed and never entered again. No broken figures anywhere else on the site, no fire, no signs of violence. A says this is clearly evidence of belief -- some ritual, some notion about persons or contracts or about what has to be destroyed to be finished -- and that we can say something about what they thought. B says all you've got is behavior: things were made, broken, buried, a room got sealed, and maybe there was a rule about it; the same pattern fits a dozen incompatible worldviews (grief, debt, initiation, disposal, magic) and you can't rank them without smuggling in the one you already like. Let them go at each other properly. A should press that we infer beliefs from objects all the time, that it's what we do with people who are still alive and it usually works. B should say exactly which inferences he'll allow -- that they had skills, intentions, a concept of something like a person -- and why the belief one is different. Let B handle A's best card, that the sealing means the whole thing cost them something; and let A handle B's best card, that cost shows importance and not content. Neither should be a dope, they should be answering each other's actual points, and at the end I want to know wich disagreement is real and what find would settle it. Don't bother name-dropping scholars, I care about the reasoning.
