I'm a postdoc in philosophy, and next month I have to run a seventy-five-minute session at a methods bootcamp for first-year doctoral students in law, economics and cognitive science. They are a bright but philosophically untrained group — most will have had a single undergraduate course in the subject, if that — and what they have in common is that they lean on hypothetical cases in their own work: the law students on imagined parties, the economists on stylised agents, the cognitive scientists on vignettes they run on undergraduates. The brief I have been handed is 'thought experiments: how they persuade and how they mislead', with twenty people, a whiteboard, and nothing set as reading in advance. I do not want you to write the session for me; I want a plan for teaching it. Specifically: the order in which the components have to be introduced, and for each step why it depends on the one before it, so that the whole thing arrives as the unfolding of a single idea rather than a list of cautions; the one case that carries the weight, chosen against the alternatives; the misconceptions this particular audience will either bring with them or form along the way, and the precise point in the sequence where each should be headed off; a question or short exercise for the end that will show me whether they have grasped the distinction or merely enjoyed the puzzles; and what I should openly refuse to cover at this level.

To be concrete about the case, I have two candidates and I would like you to choose between them. The first is the experimental finding — sometimes called the side-effect effect — in which people are told of a chairman who is entirely indifferent to the environment and who starts a programme for profit alone; when the programme harms the environment, most say he harmed it intentionally, but when the identical programme helps the environment, most deny that he helped it intentionally, though his indifference and his reasoning are the same in both versions. The second is a case I have drafted myself, which I shall call the two roads: a bypass whose construction will, on the best evidence, kill two people over the next decade, with the single difference that in one version the engineer holds a letter from a man living beside the proposed route who will be one of the two, and in the other version nobody is named. Tell me which of these can carry the session, what it has that the other lacks, what the rejected one is still good for, and where the trolley problem belongs — they will all have met it, and it is the case they will want to talk about, but I am fairly sure it must not be the spine of the thing. I should also flag my own instinct, so that you can talk me out of it if it is wrong: I want to build the session on a distinction I have been chewing over, between a case doing argumentative work (it is a counterexample, or it forces you to choose between a theory and a verdict you will not surrender) and a case doing rhetorical work (it makes a possibility vivid, and any argument lives elsewhere). I think that is the right spine, but I would like to be told if it collapses in front of this audience, and what to put in its place. One further thing to keep in view: they will arrive assuming that I am about to tell them thought experiments are worthless, and I want the plan to make plain that this is not the message without my having to say so in as many words.

Please set the plan out in the following sections, in this order, at roughly the lengths indicated, and write in British English throughout: 'Opening' (about 120 words — how I should frame the session in the first five minutes, given what they are expecting); 'The sequence' (about 600 words — the order of the ideas, each with the dependency that forces it to come where it does); 'The carrying case' (about 350 words — which of my two candidates, why, what it must have and what it must lack, what the other is still good for, and where the trolley problem belongs); 'The misconception map' (about 500 words — each misconception, keyed to the step in the sequence where it should be headed off, with the move that heads it off); 'The exit check' (about 150 words — one question or exercise, with a sketch of what a good and a bad answer would look like); and 'What I should leave out' (about 120 words — the topics I should name and then explicitly decline to explain, and why refusing beats a sketch).
