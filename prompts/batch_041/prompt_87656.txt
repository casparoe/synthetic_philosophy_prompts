I'm in my third year and currently taking a course on Hellenistic philosophy; alongside it I've been sitting in on a reading group that works through the modern Stoicism self-help literature, and the two have started to pull in opposite directions. In the course, the notion of what is in our power is treated as a technical claim doing real work inside a determinist physics and a theory of agency; in the reading group it functions as advice, a way of sorting your worries into a pile you can act on and a pile you should stop fretting about. The disagreement keeps surfacing whenever someone says that a Stoic does not care about outcomes, or that the whole doctrine just is the dichotomy of control, or that the three-way split in the popular books — things we control, things we influence, things we do not — is the more honest version of the idea. I want to make a one-page handout that sorts the positions rather than adjudicating them, so the group can see where each of us actually stands.

Please write me a decision tree, built as a classification instrument rather than as a roster of names. Every branch node should be a question a reader can answer without already knowing which position it leads to, and each should be anchored to a concrete case: the doctor who does everything right and loses the patient; the person who resolves to sail tomorrow and adds the proviso that nothing prevent it; someone threatened with death unless they break a promise; whether one's own anger, or one's health, or the memory of a fact is in one's power; whether a promise kept under compulsion was ever in the promiser's power; and the archer who does everything he can to hit a mark that is not his to guarantee. Then locate the actual positions in the space your questions carve out — the strict dichotomy, the three-way split of the popular literature, the reading on which being in our power means attributable-to-one rather than causally guaranteed, the reading on which it covers the whole activity of the rational soul including the correct pursuit of indifferent things, the fatalist conclusion that nothing is in our power in the sense required, the exegetical position that the dichotomy is an artifact of how the modern revival reads a single opening chapter, and the socio-political objection that the revival has converted an ancient claim about the inviolability of the will into a technique for managing emotions. Use the standard label where there is one, and mark a leaf unnamed where there is not.

I would like somewhere between eight and ten leaves, and I would rather the tree grow out of the logical space — first the questions and the combinations of answers they permit, then the positions placed within it — than out of a list of names. Flag explicitly any two leaves the tree cannot separate, and note which combinations of answers no position occupies, with a one-line diagnosis of why: incoherent, unattractive, or merely unoccupied. The audience is a mixed group of undergraduates and interested non-specialists, so no branch should require prior knowledge of the technical vocabulary, and the handout should be usable by someone who has read only the popular books. No references needed — I will add those myself.
