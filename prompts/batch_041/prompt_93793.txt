I have no training in philosophy or psychology — what I know comes from popular books and from arguing with friends — but I have been keeping a list of cases in which people claim that what you expect, want, or already believe changes what you literally see, and I would like the list sorted into tiers according to how strong a case each entry actually makes.

The list:

1. Two lines of equal length, one with arrowheads at its ends pointing outward and one with arrowheads pointing inward, which continue to look unequal long after you have measured them and know that they are equal.
2. A recording of a spoken sentence in which a cough covers one of the speech sounds; a listener who knows the language hears the missing sound anyway.
3. A video of a mouth pronouncing one sound while a different sound is played, after which people report hearing a third sound that was never played at all.
4. A hill that looks steeper to someone wearing a heavy backpack, or to someone who has just finished a long run, than to someone standing there fresh.
5. A golf hole that looks wider to a player who is putting well than to one who is putting badly, and a ball that looks bigger to a batter who is hitting well than to one who is struggling.
6. Two objects of identical weight, one small and one large: the small one feels heavier, and keeps feeling heavier even after both have been weighed and the person knows they match.
7. The same wine rated as more pleasant when the taster has been told it is expensive, and as less pleasant when told it is cheap.
8. Pain reported as less intense when the person has been told a treatment will work, even when the treatment is a sugar pill.
9. A chest X-ray that looks like grey mush to a student and like a definite shape to a radiologist who has read thousands of them.
10. A blotchy picture that looks like random patches until someone says there is a dog in it, after which the dog cannot be unseen.
11. A single flash of light accompanied by two beeps, which people report as two flashes.
12. A cloud, or a patch of damp on a wall, in which you suddenly see a face and thereafter cannot stop seeing it.

I have a scorecard I want the entries graded against. It has three questions, each scored 0, 1, or 2, so six points in total:

A. The no-words question. Imagine a creature with no language and no concepts at all — no idea of equal length, no word for steep, no notion of expensive. Would the effect still occur? Score 0 if it plainly requires the concept, 2 if it plainly does not.
B. The stubbornness question. The person has been shown the truth, accepts it, and still reports the effect. Score 0 if knowing makes the effect go away, 2 if it survives being known.
C. The hands question. The person must respond by reaching, pointing, or adjusting a physical object rather than by saying a number or a word. Score 0 if the effect disappears once the hands take over, 2 if it remains.

What I would like back is a tier list running from top to bottom, with each entry's score, a sentence or two explaining why it landed where it did, and a clear statement of the single case you would defend if you had to convince a stubborn friend that expectation changes seeing itself rather than only changing what people say. Then, separately, tell me where my scorecard is a poor instrument: which entries it grades unfairly, whether any of my three questions is doing work the other two already do, and whether two of my cases are really one phenomenon wearing different clothes, in which case grade them once and say so. I would much rather you argue with the scorecard than apply it mechanically, and if you think an entry is not about seeing at all, move it out of the list rather than force it into a tier.
