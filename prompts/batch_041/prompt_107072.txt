OK so I've been reading rationalist-adjacent stuff about AI x-risk for like a decade now, and there's one fight that keeps resurfacing and never resolves, and I want a real diagnosis of it instead of another round. It's the worst-case vs average-case thing in alignment. One camp says we need worst-case guarantees — that if there's any plausible path to a catastrophic outcome you have to rule it out, and that expected-value reasoning over some guessed distribution of futures is exactly how you talk yourself into obvious doom. The other camp says a worst-case guarantee over an unconstrained model class is basically vacuous, because you can always adversarially construct a failure, so the only meaningful question is average-case under something like the actual distribution of training runs and deployments, and refusing to reason in distributions just means you're smuggling in a bad one without admitting it.

I've watched this loop for years and nobody moves an inch, and I've started to suspect it's not really a disagreement about risk tolerance or about the facts. My hypothesis — and I'm aware it might be wrong — is that it's a disagreement about the scope of the quantifier: worst case over what? Over environments, over policies, over the space of possible models, over the researchers' own mistakes, over possible worlds? And each side silently fixes a different domain, then argues past the other, so that "guarantee" and "average" end up doing completely differnt jobs while sounding like they're contradicting each other.

So: is that the right diagnosis? Is this a verbal dispute, a substantive one about which reference class is legitimate, a hidden empirical question about how tight our actual bounds are, or two camps answering differnt questions under a single heading?
