I'm part of a small reading group on moral responsibility that meets once a month, and next session it's my turn to present. Rather than walk everyone through the assigned reading again, I want to put a short paper of my own on the table and have it taken apart. So I'm sending you the draft in advance. What I want from you is not a summary, a survey of the literature, or a reading list — assume I already know the standard positions and don't need citations — but detailed comments of three kinds. First, on the argument: does it actually go through, or is there a place where I assert the conclusion instead of earning it? Second, on fairness: am I engaging Strawson's account of the reactive attitudes, or have I quietly built a version of it that no one holds in order to knock it down? Third, on the ending: is the principle I land on coherent, and does it survive the two objections I flag at the bottom? Be blunt. I would much rather find the hole now than in front of the group.

Here's the draft. Its working title is "Who Answers for the Deed?"

Strawson's insight, as I read it, is that holding someone responsible is not a metaphysical verdict about causation or free will but the exercise of a family of attitudes — resentment, indignation, gratitude, forgiveness — that we take up toward one another in the ordinary business of living together, and that these attitudes are keyed to the quality of will a person expresses in what they do. To hold someone responsible is to treat them as a participant in a relationship of demands rather than as an object to be managed or treated. I take that to be right and I am not trying to refute it. What I want to press is a question it leaves open: whose quality of will? The natural answer is the will of the agent at the time of the act. But our practice doesn't seem to work that way. Consider two men. Both, at twenty-two, took part in a campaign of political violence, and both personally beat a prisoner. The first, at sixty, has spent four decades in reparation, holds his former self in horror, and calls what he did unforgivable. The second, at sixty, still thinks it was justified. My attitudes toward them differ sharply, and they differ in a way that tracks not what each did but how each now stands toward what he did. This looks like what I've been calling the amnesty problem: the more thoroughly you disown your past, the more we let you off, so that responsibility can be dissolved by a sufficiently sincere act of self-revision.

I distinguish two kinds of change. Renunciation: the agent keeps his evaluative framework and condemns the act within it. Conversion: the framework itself changes, so that the act is no longer condemned at all — the revolutionary who still believes, the person absorbed into a group that recodes the act as sacred. Renunciation I think Strawson's account can absorb, since apology and reparation are themselves participant attitudes and this is just the machinery working. Conversion is where the account strains. If the attitudes track the quality of will expressed, and the will expressed by the sixty-year-old convert is wholehearted endorsement, then by the letter of the account we should either feel no resentment at all, since no ill will is present now, or resent him all over again for the new act of endorsement. Neither matches what I actually feel, which is a steady, unrevised indignation at the original deed that neither the passage of time nor the change of heart touches. So I draw two conclusions. First, the reactive attitudes answer to something more than the agent's current self-relation: they answer to a claim the wronged party has on the wrongdoer, and that claim survives changes in either party's outlook. Second, what fixes whether resentment is warranted is not the agent's present self-understanding but the continuing relation between the parties. The principle I'd put it as: resentment is licensed by the continuing claim of the wronged, and is extinguished only by something that answers that claim — reparation, apology, forgiveness — never merely by the wrongdoer's change of heart.

Two worries I already have. (a) If resentment answers to the victim's claim, what happens when there is no victim left to hold a claim — wrongs against the dead, against people who have forgiven and moved on, or against no one in particular, like desecrating a grave? I want to say the attitudes are still warranted there, but I can't see how my principle delivers that. (b) Self-resentment. I resent things I did at nineteen. On a relational account, self-directed resentment looks like a category error, since there is no second party, yet it seems central to moral life and Strawson's framework, built for the interpersonal case, has no obvious place for it. Maybe the right move is to treat self-resentment as derivative of the interpersonal attitude, but I don't have a story about what it derives from.

Please tell me which of those two worries is fatal and which is just a loose end. I'd also like to know whether the renunciation/conversion distinction is doing real work or is just relabeling, whether the principle collapses into a victim-centered account of blame that can't handle wrongs with no identifiable victim, and what the strongest case against me looks like — ideally a case where I have to choose between saving the principle and keeping an intuition I care about. If you think a different ending is available that preserves more of what I want, sketch it. Please write your comments in American English, and don't worry about being diplomatic.
