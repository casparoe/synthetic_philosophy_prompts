I'm putting together a handout for a reading group on precommitment and I want a tier list to argue with. Here's the thing that keeps bugging me: a Ulysses contract is supposed to bind your future self, but your future self is still *you*, so what's actually stopping them from just... untying the rope? Every device seems to answer that differently, and I want to see the answers ranked.

So: assume the later self is motivated, clever, and would rather not be bound. Rank the following from S tier down to F by how much of a *real* bind each puts on that later self -- not how clever or elegant it is, how much it actually holds. (And no, don't just say they're all the same because it's the same person; the differences are the whole point.)

1. Odysseus-style: physical restraint plus a crew that refuses to untie you no matter what you shout. The binding agent is someone else's refusal, not your own willpower.
2. A penalty contract: you can break the commitment whenever, but breaking it costs you a large pre-agreed sum, paid to someone you can't stand. The bind is a price, not a wall.
3. Central-bank-style independence: you hand the decision to a body insulated from you, with staggered terms so you can't replace them all in one go.
4. Constitutional entrenchment: you write the rule in and require a supermajority, or two successive legislatures, to change it.
5. A public announcement you'd lose face over -- you tell everyone you're doing X, so backing out means social death. Reputational bind.
6. Making it sacred: you recode the rule as an identity ("I'm just not someone who does that"), so violating it feels like ceasing to be yourself rather than like a cost-benefit call.
7. A tripwire: revocation itself triggers the penalty, so *trying* to undo the bind is the very act you bound yourself against.
8. One I made up, tell me if it collapses into #7: a commitment you can revoke, but the revoke button only works if you first perform the exact act you committed not to do -- so the only way out is through.

Put them in tiers, one line each on why it lands where it does, and flag any I've basically double-counted. Then two extra things: first, tell me what you'd have to believe -- about the later self, or about what makes a reason binding in the first place -- for a tier assignment to flip, i.e. what would actually change your mind here. And second, be honest about where this whole exercise is most likely to be rigged or where your reasoning is weakest, because I suspect the rankings are quietly smuggling in a theory of why an earlier preference should beat a later one, and I'd like that theory named rather than assumed.
