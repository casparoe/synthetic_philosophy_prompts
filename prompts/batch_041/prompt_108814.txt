I'm in Year 12 and I've somehow ended up as the student rep on our school's science fair ethics panel, which is probaly a mistake because I've never done philosophy. The panel looks at every project that uses a living thing, and because the school won't allow vertebrates, we get alot of invertebrates: fruit flies, ants, worms, snails, the odd crayfish or crab, and once a project about whether a thermostat or a little robot can be said to be sentient because it prefers a temperature. We meet once a fortnight and usually have about twenty proposals, so I have maybe ten or fifteen minutes per proposal. The stakes are not massive but they're real: if we say yes to something that suffers, that's on us, and if we say no to something harmless, a kid loses their project. Our head of science has to sign off, but she teaches chemistry and says she'll back whatever the panel decides as long as it sounds principled.

A visiting postgrad gave us a handout about sentience being a matter of degree rather than an on/off switch. She called it the two-lane view, with a dial for integration. The bit I actually followed was that you can separate nociception from pain: nociception is just detecting and reacting to damage, like a reflex, whereas pain is there being something it feels like. Then the dial is about whether the response is local or global. A local response is a reflex arc that doesn't get used anywhere else in the animal; a global response is one where the information is available to the rest of the animal, it can trade it off against other goals, learn from it, and so on. She said you can have nociception without pain, and maybe global processing without anything felt, and that moral status should scale with how much of the whole package is present. She also said a thermostat is a responder but not a feeler, though I'm not sure that's not just true by definition. I need you to turn this into something we can actually run.

Please write me a decision procedure for the panel. I want the questions to ask in order, what counts as a good enough answer at each step, where the procedure stops with a no or a come back with a different project, and where it has to hand the decision to a human because it's a values question rather than a fact question. I need it to sort proposals into a few levels of moral status or review, from no welfare concern and approve as normal up to full review and possibly reject, with a middle level for approve but with these conditions. It has to cope with the cases we actually get: insects, worms, snails, crustaceans, cephalopods, and the thermostat or robot limit case. It should be honest about the fact that we're working from a short paragraph, maybe a photo or a video, and whatever we already know, not from lab data.

Then run it on three examples. The first is a project that keeps ants in a maze and removes a leg to see if they limp, with no anaesthetic. The second is a project that gives crayfish a mild electric shock and tests whether they avoid the place it happened, and whether a common painkiller changes that behaviour. I want the worked examples to show exactly where the procedure bites, including one where it gives an answer I won't like, like telling me the crayfish project needs the full review and should probably be turned down, or telling me the thermostat project can't be assessed as ethics at all and has to be reframed as engineering. At the end, say what the procedure can't do and where it leaves me deciding on my own.
