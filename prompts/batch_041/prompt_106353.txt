I run a final-year seminar on practical reason and I'm putting the exam together at the moment. I want one essay question on Buridan's ass that students can't get through just by telling me the donkey starves because it has no reason to prefer one haystack to the other. What I actually want them to grapple with is the difference between the donkey being *indifferent* between the two haystacks (they're equally good) and it having *no preference* because the two options simply can't be ranked against one another — and then whether not being able to rank them makes choosing irrational or merely leaves the choice undetermined.

To stop them reaching for the usual examples, I've been useing a made-up case in class that I'd like the question to hang off. Call it the Tie-Break Protocol. An autonomous warehouse robot has to choose between two routes to a shelf; its sensors report the two routes as identical in every respect it can measure — same length, same energy, same risk, same everything. The engineers, worried the robot will just sit there forever, give it a rule: when two options are exactly equal, take the one whose label sorts first alphabetically. A colleague says the rule is a cheat — it doesn't give the robot a *reason* to take that route, it just papers over the fact that the choice is abitrary. I'm not so sure.

Could you write the essay question (one question, answerable in about an hour), a model answer of around 700 words, and a marking scheme that sets out the must-hit moves and where the marks fall (out of 20), with a line for each on how students usually get it wrong. The question should force them to say something about whether rationality requires a sufficient reason to *act*, or only a reason not to act against what you already prefer. And can it all be in British English, please? Cheers.
