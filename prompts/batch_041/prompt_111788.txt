I'm taking a seminar in decision theory this term, and a claim keeps being made in discussion without anyone pinning it down: that information can never hurt you, or that a rational agent's expected utility cannot be lowered by learning something for free. Our professor cited a short 1967 note by I. J. Good on the principle of total evidence as settling the matter, while answering a question I had raised about a patient who is terminally ill and can no longer act on any result — I asked whether she could be worse off for consenting to a test whose result she cannot use, and the reply was that by the theorem she cannot be. A classmate has since been using the same slogan to argue that anyone who declines a diagnosis, or who leaves the letter unopened, is being straightforwardly irrational, and another classmate objects that she was herself devastated by a result she could do nothing about.

I suspect the slogan is doing several different jobs across these exchanges, and that at least one of them is false. Please set out the distinct precise claims that 'information can never hurt you' could be making, side by side and each with its own verdict, rather than one reading explained at length, and say of each whether it is trivial or definitional, a substantive claim that holds only under stated assumptions, or false. I would especially like the readings separated along these lines: whether the claim concerns the agent's expectation, computed before she learns, or her position once she has learned; whether it assumes that she is a Bayesian who updates and then re-optimizes, and what becomes of it if she is not, or does not; whether the information in question is a free experiment, a single message, or something whose arrival carries non-epistemic costs such as anxiety; what it means for one body of information to be greater than another, and whether the result depends on that; whether her preferences and her available actions must be held fixed, and what happens if learning changes what she wants; what the theorem implies when no action at all depends on what she learns; and whether it survives once the agent is not alone, so that others can observe or respond to what she learns. Finally, tell me which of the readings the professor needed for my question about the patient, and which one my classmate is relying on when he calls the person who declines a diagnosis irrational.
