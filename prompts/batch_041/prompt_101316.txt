I teach a 300-level seminar on philosophy of action, 18 students, 75 minutes, and I want to run a session on weakness of will where they actually feel the problem instead of hearing me lay it out. The itch I want them to scratch is this: everyone in the room already knows the hyperbolic-discounting story, so the interesting bit is the part that story doesn't settle. If your preference for the smaller-sooner over the larger-later just flips as the delay shrinks, then at the moment you cave you don't hold the judgment you're violating -- you've simply become a different chooser. That looks like a change of mind, not weakness of will. But then why does it feel like a betrayal, and why do people buy commitment devices at a real price to stop a future self they're supposed to be identical to? I want them to run into both halves of that, in the room, before I say any of it out loud.

So: design the activity. What I want on paper is rules, materials, a payoff scheme that uses candy or a small extra-credit lottery rather than money, a round-by-round timeline, and a debrief that maps specific moments onto the two things above. Two mechanics I'd like you to build in, or replace with better ones if you have them. First, delays have to be short enough to reverse inside the session -- minutes, not days -- and they have to be real, so the larger-later option actually arrives before we finish. Second, I want them betting on their own future choices: before each round they can stake something on which option they'll take next, so the people who mispredict themselves pay for it visibly. Then somewhere in the middle, let commitments become tradeable -- a TA as house broker, a posted price to lock in a later option or to forbid the earlier one, and players free to buy, sell, or try to wriggle out. I'm curious whether a price for self-distrust does any work, and what it looks like when someone sells their own commitment cheap.

Also give me the honest failures. Who will treat the whole thing as a point-maximizing puzzle and never feel a pull at all, and what do I do about it live? What happens when half the room has read the behavioral econ literature and is already anticipating the reversal? What's the most likely way the market collapses into collusion or nonsense? And be straight with me about what the activity can't reproduce -- my suspicion is that it can produce reversals, regret, and naivety, but not the first-person datum of acting against a judgment you still endorse while you're doing it, which means I have to supply that myself in the debrief and say so. No lecture up front, and they shouldn't hear any of the standard names until afterwards.
