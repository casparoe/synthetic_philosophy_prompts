I host a monthly seminar series on the history of moral change, and next month's guest is a philosopher who defends a strongly honor-centered account of moral revolutions: on her view, the great abolitions — of the Atlantic slave trade, of the duel, of footbinding, among others — were driven less by the discovery of new arguments than by a shift in what counted as honorable, so that arguments available for centuries finally found traction because the practice had become shameful rather than merely wrong. She leans heavily on the Williams-style observation that the case against slavery was made long before it was acted on, and on the thesis that honor is the mechanism by which moral ideas acquire motivating force.

I want eight to ten questions for a forty-minute interview, aimed at the joints of the view rather than at its summary. Please include, at minimum:

1. A question about what exactly the Williams-style observation is supposed to establish — whether it shows that argument is causally idle, or only that argument alone is insufficient — with a follow-up pressing whichever horn she takes.

2. A question about an asymmetry across her cases: British abolitionists were not themselves slaveholders, so the honor at stake seems to be third-party honor, whereas in the duel and in footbinding the practitioners' own honor was at issue. Ask whether these are one mechanism or two, and follow up on what would break the analogy.

3. A question about the ratchet problem: honor-driven change often looks irreversible, but honor codes have also reversed, as with revivals of dueling and the persistence of honor killing. Ask what her account predicts about reversibility, and follow up if she appeals to law or institutions as the real anchor.

4. A question about cases where argument seems to have done the work without an accompanying honor shift — the abolition of judicial torture, or the decline of the blood feud in some societies — and a follow-up asking whether these are counterexamples or just honor revolutions she has redescribed.

5. A question about whether her view makes moral argument epiphenomenal, and whether that is self-undermining given that she is herself arguing for it; the follow-up should ask what she takes herself to be doing when she argues with a defender of the old practice.

6. A question about the difference between honor as a comparative status good and dignity as a universal, and whether modern moral revolutions can be explained by the honor mechanism or whether they mark its decline.

7. A question about the direction of fit: does the practice become dishonorable because a moral argument succeeds, or does the argument succeed because the practice becomes dishonorable, and how could we tell the difference in the historical record?

8. A question about who the relevant honor community is, and how the account handles cases where the practitioners and the condemning public belong to different societies — whether external shaming counts as honor-driven change or as something else.

9. A question about whether the account can distinguish moral revolutions we endorse from ones we regret, such as honor-driven puritanical crackdowns, with a follow-up about what normative work her descriptive thesis is meant to do.

10. A closing question: if she is right, what follows for present-day campaigns, and what would she say to an activist who thinks argument is the point.

Please phrase the questions so they press her where the literature presses, and make the follow-ups responsive to the standard replies rather than generic. Also include one or two questions that turn on the specific mechanics of the honor mechanism she invokes — how honor is transmitted, whether it requires visibility, and whether anonymous or dispersed practices can be reached by it — since I want to test whether the account has enough internal structure to generate predictions rather than just redescriptions.
