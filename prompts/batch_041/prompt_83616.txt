I've been talked into a view about immigration and I'm not sure how much it costs me. Here it is: the right to keep people out isn't a right of the state, or of "the people" as a collective. It's just the summed claims of the people already living there, and it's grounded in reliance -- they've arranged their lives around a certain neighbourhood, a certain labour market, a certain set of neighbours -- so exclusion is justified only where it protects those reliance interests from actually being set back. A border policy is justified roughly in proportion to how much specific existing members would be harmed, and the burden sits on the excluder.

Tell me what counts against this, ranked by how much I should worry. I don't need the standard open-borders-versus-community back and fourth, I know that one. I want the cases that break it, the consequences I'm now stuck with that I won't like, and what a defender has to give up. What I'm most worried about is that it collapses in one of two directions: if reliance is a strong enough interest it seems to justify almost any exclusion, and if it isn't it seems to justify almost none. Is there a version that survives both, and is there any real claim a state has to exclude that holds even when no current member's reliance is at stake?
