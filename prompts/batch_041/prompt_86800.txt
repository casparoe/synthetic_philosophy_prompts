I write about art and ideas for a monthly magazine, and for most of this year I have been reporting a long piece on how museums decide what a work of art is — how attributions are made, defended, and quietly revised. That reporting put me at a dinner with two of my subjects: a curator who has spent three decades on questions of attribution, and a philosopher of art who sits on her museum's acquisitions committee. They argued for the length of the meal, and I have not been able to settle the thing on my own. I would like you to adjudicate it for me, claim by claim.

The case at issue was one the museum had recently been handed. A painter, dead for twenty years, had for most of that time been credited with a celebrated early body of work — canvases that made her name and that critics have described for decades as the work of a young painter discovering her voice. The museum's research now indicates that those canvases were painted by her partner at the time, an unknown painter who never objected to her signing them and who, in the intervening years, has become famous in his own right. No one lied; no signature was forged; the misattribution was simply allowed to stand for forty years. The paintings themselves are untouched, and the market has since bid them up sharply, on the view that they are now early works by a celebrated man.

The argument came down to six load-bearing moves.

(1) The curator: nothing that matters has changed. If you stood in front of the canvas last month and were moved, the same object is hanging there this month. What changed is a fact about biography; if that fact alters your experience, you were never responding to the painting, only to a story about a painter.

(2) The philosopher: many of the properties we actually praise in a painting are relational. An early work, a first attempt at something, the moment she stopped being careful — these are not decorations on top of the picture, they are part of what one sees, and they can be false. A canvas that turns out to be by someone else is not the same achievement, and criticism that was tracking the achievement should change.

(3) The curator: then apply the test. If the museum announced tomorrow that the early canvases had actually been painted after the late ones, would he say their qualities had changed again? If yes, the qualities move with the story and not with the object, which is her point.

(4) The philosopher: yes, some of them would change, and this is no reductio, since late style is a real category and learning that a work is late genuinely alters what one sees. That our categories are historically conditioned does not make them arbitrary.

(5) The curator: if that is so, the museum is in the reputation business, attribution is a service industry, and the value of an original is conferred by us rather than found in the thing. And since in this case no one was deceived — nobody forged anything, nobody was told a falsehood — she wanted to know what exactly had been damaged.

(6) The philosopher: conferred is not the same as arbitrary; money is conferred too, and it still buys bread. He then offered an analogy. A bronze cast pulled from the sculptor's own mold after his death, indistinguishable in every measurable respect from a lifetime cast, is on his view not the same work; and the curator, who would not hang the two as equals, has thereby admitted that she cares about something other than the visible object. She said she would hang them as equals and let the label do the work.

Please go through the exchange move by move, and not merely to declare a winner. For each move, say whether it is right, partly right, or mistaken, and why; identify what each of them is assuming without argument; say whether any part of their disagreement is merely verbal rather than substantive; and tell me what, if anything, both of them are missing. I am not after a survey of the literature — I want the argument itself taken apart.
