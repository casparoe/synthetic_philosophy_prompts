Last night I spent three hours arguing with a friend about deplatforming, and I came away unable to tell whether her argument is airtight or whether it quietly proves far too much. She set it out like this.

(1) To punish someone is to impose a harm on them on the ground that they have done something wrong.
(2) A harm of that kind is legitimate only when it is imposed by an authority that has standing over the person, under rules fixed in advance, after a hearing, and in a degree proportioned to the wrong. She calls this the standing condition and insists it is not a quirk of legal systems but a condition on punishment as such.
(3) Deplatforming -- revoking someone's access, removing their work, or organizing to have them removed -- imposes grave harms (income, audience, reputation) on the ground that they said or did something wrong.
(4) So deplatforming is punishment.
(5) The parties who do the deplatforming -- platforms, employers, loose coalitions of users -- have no standing of that sort, follow no rules fixed in advance, hold no hearings, and calibrate no proportion.
(6) So deplatforming is illegitimate, always.
(7) But the same reasoning reaches refusing to associate with someone, declining to publish or hire them, boycotting their business, and loudly criticizing them in public: each imposes a harm on the ground that they did wrong, and none is imposed by a standing authority after a hearing.
(8) Yet we do not think all of those are illegitimate.
(9) So something in the chain must give.

Her conclusion is that deplatforming is unjust and that the discomfort in (7)-(8) is merely our inconsistency. My worry is that if (7) is right, the argument does not stop at deplatforming -- it condemns the whole practice of holding one another to account, which seems like too much. But when I press her, every premise survives on its own, and when she presses me, every counterexample I offer turns out to be something I would defend anyway.

If I could save two claims they would be (2) and (8): I believe some harms inflicted for wrongdoing are legitimate only under conditions like hers, and I refuse to say that boycotts and public criticism are always wrong. Which step do I reject, and what does rejecting it cost? If the argument is really valid and I must abandon a premise, tell me which is cheapest to give up and what I would then have to say about the cases in (7). And if the repair turns on reading some single word in two different senses at different steps, say exactly which sense is needed where, and whether that is a real distinction or merely a relabeling that lets the argument have it both ways.
