For the last session of a seminar on Sartre, I want to read Kafka's The Metamorphosis against Sartre's early account of freedom and bad faith. Gregor Samsa wakes up one morning as an insect, the story never explains why, and from then on he is a creature whose body, work, and household have all become obstacles, and who stops eating and dies after his family resolves to be rid of him. My students will ask the obvious question -- is Gregor free at all? -- and I want a reading that takes both the story and the philosophy seriously rather than using one to decorate the other. Where does the apparatus of facticity, bad faith, and the look of the Other actually illuminate the text: the family's gradual conversion of Gregor into a thing, his continued clinging to his role as the provider and to the framed picture in his room, the violin scene, the ending in which the parents notice that their daughter has bloomed and the family takes the tram into the country? And whose metamorphosis, in the end, does the title name? Then say where the story resists the reading -- is a creature who can no longer speak, work, or be recognized in a situation at all in Sartre's sense, or a case the theory cannot absorb? Close with what Kafka adds to the debate about freedom that the argument alone does not. I have the text in front of me, so name the scenes you rely on rather than quoting them.
