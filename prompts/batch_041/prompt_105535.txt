A historian I know gave a lecture on the end of dueling and of the Atlantic slave trade, and summarized the philosophical literature on moral revolutions like this: "Nobody was ever argued out of dueling or out of slavery. The moralists had made the arguments for a century or more and the practices went on. What ended them was that they became dishonorable and expensive — dueling stopped being what gentlemen did and became what ruffians did; slavery stopped paying, and the abolitionists made it shameful to own slaves. The Williams thesis is the same point about the slave trade: the arguments we now find so compelling were not what did the work, and they look compelling to us only because we already have the sentiments that make them look that way. So moral revolutions are driven by interest and shame, and reasons are at best the slogans later generations attach to changes that were happening anyway."

I find this bracing, and it fits my own sense that moral argument rarely moves anyone. But I would like to know how much of it I can safely repeat. Which parts would Appiah accept, which parts misrepresent what he and Williams actually argue, and does the general lesson about interest and shame follow from the parts that are accurate? I am also unsure whether the summary slides from a claim about what moves people to a claim about the status of the moral judgments themselves, and whether that slide changes the conclusion I would be taking away. Please be explicit about what a defender of each author would object to, and about what survives once the summary is corrected.
