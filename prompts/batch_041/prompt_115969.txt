A friend who is finishing a history PhD keeps telling me her field is deadlocked over something called overdetermination, and every time she explains it I get the uneasy sense that she is describing, in worse terms, an apparatus I already own. I'm a reliability engineer — a decade of fault-tree analysis, FMEA and incident investigation — so I'm at home with structure functions, minimal cut sets, single points of failure, common-cause failure, barrier analysis, and the distinction between an active failure (the trigger) and the latent conditions that were sitting there waiting for it. Here is the debate as she lays it out. One camp, descended from the old INUS idea (a cause is an insufficient but non-redundant part of an unnecessary but sufficient condition — Mackie, I think), holds that where several conditions each had to be present and none of them was such that its absence would have changed the outcome, they are all causes and the hunt for the cause is a category mistake. A second camp, working from counterfactuals, holds that a cause is something whose absence would have made a difference, and concludes that in a genuinely overdetermined case nothing in particular is a cause; her stock illustration is two bullets reaching the heart in the same instant, though she prefers the July Crisis and, when she wants to annoy me, the evening of 9 November 1989, when an offhand remark at a press conference about when the new travel rules took effect sent crowds to the checkpoints. A third camp says the quarrel is an artifact of forgetting that historical explanation works by locating an event under a larger concept or narrative, so which thing counts as the cause is fixed by the historian's question — and questions come in contrastive form (why war rather than a settlement, why this collapse rather than another). She insists the stakes are moral as much as metaphysical: to call an outcome overdetermined is to say that nobody in particular did it, which is why the thesis is fought over hardest in the historiography of catastrophes. Tort law, I gather, has quietly patched the same hole with a substantial-factor test, which suggests to me that people keep repairing this thing rather than solving it.

What I want is a translation and then an honest audit of what the translation loses. Take those three positions and restate each in my vocabulary as faithfully as you can: write the July Crisis and 1989 out as though they were fault trees, with the top event, the initiating event, the latent conditions, the cut sets and the common causes all made explicit, and be disciplined about not smuggling engineering assumptions in under the guise of translation. My own instinct is that overdetermined just means the top event has several minimal cut sets, at least one of them uncomfortably small, and that the counterfactual camp is running the does-this-component-appear-in-every-minimal-cut-set test and concluding that a redundant cause is no cause at all — tell me where that instinct is right and where it has quietly changed the subject. Then the residue report, which is the part I actually care about: which claims come across cleanly, which survive only with a fudge (and say exactly what the fudge is), and which cannot be said in my framework at all. For each item in that last category, tell me whether the fault is mine — my vocabulary is event-defined, population-level and morally inert, and maybe that is a real limitation — or theirs, the claim having been vaguer than it sounded until the translation exposed it. Three things I would particularly like ruled on: whether the contrast-dependence of the historians' why-questions has any counterpart in a fault tree, where the top event is simply given; whether common-cause analysis is the right home for their worry that the supposedly independent causes are all downstream of one structural condition; and whether anything in my framework can carry the moral weight they attach to overdetermination, or whether I should conclude that the interesting part of the disagreement was never causal at all. No reading list, please — work with what is in front of you and be blunt about the points where the translation runs out.
