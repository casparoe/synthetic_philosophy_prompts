I've been chewing on the Letter Spirit question -- what makes every a an a? -- and I've ended up with a view I want to stress-test. The view is that a letter's identity isn't a shape or a checklist of features. It's a role in a generative system: an a is whatever a competent reader will map to the same letter-category across a family of transformations, where the transformations are constrained by the style's own logic. So the essence of a is more like a schema plus admissible deformations than any particular skeleton. Style is a trajectory through the space of possible letterforms; the letter is the equivalence class. I think this probly handles single vs double-storey a, why some fonts' a looks like an o with a tail, why a can survive huge distortions and still read as a, etc. What would count against it? I want the real defeaters, not just 'it's vague.' Cases where recongizability comes apart from category membership? Cases where the style logic is doing all the work and the letter contributes nothing? The worry that this makes a's essence purely relational, so an a is just whatever contrasts with b and o in a given system -- and then does it collapse into anything goes? Is there a fact of the matter about whether a weird glyph is an a or a failed a, and does my view predict one? Also, if you think the whole frame is wrong -- that letters are more like natural kinds, or more like conventions, or that 'letter' is a made-up category -- say so and why. Rank the objections from fatal to merely awkward, and tell me which ones I should just bite the bullet on.
