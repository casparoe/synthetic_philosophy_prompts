A colleague of mine who writes on the philosophy of family law has been strikingly consistent across the hypotheticals we have put to her in seminar. She holds that the state should recognize as marriages: a same-sex couple raising children; a same-sex couple with no children and no intention of having any; and a heterosexual couple past childbearing age. She holds that it should refuse recognition to: a closed triad of three adults who are mutually committed, cohabit, and are raising children jointly; and two elderly siblings who have shared a household for forty years, are each other's sole caretakers and heirs, and want the legal incidents of marriage. Asked about the siblings, she says only that they already stand in a legal relation that does the work they want marriage to do.

Next month the seminar takes up a new case: two friends, with no romantic or sexual involvement, who have shared a household for thirty years, hold their property jointly, and are each other's designated decision-maker and heir; they petition for a marital status.

I want your prediction for that case, together with the rule or rules that best explain her record, what each surviving rule predicts here, which prediction you would bet on and why, and which further case would most sharply separate the rules that survive. I am ultimately trying to see what theory of marriage's point she is committed to, and whether it is stable.
