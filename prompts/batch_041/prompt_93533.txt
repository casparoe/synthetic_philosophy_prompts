I'm about two-thirds of the way through a novel that hinges on a referendum — a fictional one, a small town on the Norfolk coast, so it isn't Brexit as such — but I've been reading round the real thing for months now and there's a wall I keep hitting. What I want is a proper, disciplined mapping between the 2016 EU referendum and Condorcet's jury theorem, not the bumper-sticker version of it. The result was 51.89 to 48.11 on a turnout of 72.21 per cent, the question on the paper was 'Should the United Kingdom remain a member of the European Union or leave the European Union?', and it was legally non-binding, though the government promised to implement it anyway, which is the bit my novel keeps circling. So: who are the jurors, what exactly is the proposition they're meant to be tracking, and do the theorem's working assumptions survive contact with the thing itself? Each voter more likely than not to get it right — is that even the right question to ask when two official campaigns, Britain Stronger in Europe and Vote Leave, are shouting into the same living rooms? Independence of judgement, when everyone's cues come from the same papers and the same neighbours? And the part people skip over: the theorem is about aggregating beliefs about a matter of fact, and it flips into a machine for amplifying error the moment average competence slips below a half, with bigger electorates making things worse rather than better. Does any of that actually describe what happened, or is it just the wrong instrument for a question where there's no fact of the matter — you can't be correct about whether leaving was the right call, you can only want it or not.

Then the other direction, which is really what I'm after, because I don't want a verdict on the voters, I want to know what the episode does to the theory. If the jury theorem can't get a grip on a referendum, what is a referendum actually measuring? Please take Rousseau seriously as a rival here rather than a slogan, because I half-remember the distinction between the general will and the will of all, and something about what happens to the general will once you've got factions — partial associations, groups each of which ends up with a general will of it's own — and it sounds uncannily like a country split down the middle into two campaigns. Is that a genuine alternative account, or just a dignified way of saying the result came out wrong? If neither holds up, say so plainly and tell me what frame does better. And one design question I keep coming back to: the Swiss rule that a change to the constitution needs a majority of the cantons as well as a majority of the people. Is that a real fix — two juries of different composition instead of one — or does it simply stack one aggregation on top of another and inherit all the same weaknesses? No citations and no reading list, I'm definately not writing a thesis, I just want the argument laid out and the honest places where it goes quiet. British English throughout, please — my copy-editor will have my guts for garters otherwise.
