I have drafted a short essay for a collection on political myth and would like your comments before I circulate it. The draft follows.

**Defeat as epitaph, defeat as installment**

Lost-cause myths are usually sorted by content — by what they claim happened and whom they blame. I want to sort them by temporal logic instead, and I think the sorting does more explanatory work than the content does.

Call a defeat myth *epitaphal* when the loss is narrated as a completion. The cause is dead; the myth's job is to fix its meaning so that the meaning can be tended. The American Lost Cause hardened into something like this: the political programme is abandoned, and what survives is a cult of the dead — of blood, of manners, of a vanished order — precisely because nothing further is to be done. Epitaphal myths are quietist by construction; they convert a live claim into a monument.

Call a defeat myth *installmentary* when the loss is narrated as a down payment. The story is unfinished, and the dead are owed something the living must still deliver. The Serbian material around Kosovo is the clearest case I know: a battle whose outcome was contested gets retold as a covenant of chosen defeat, and the retelling is switched back on whenever a national project needs a warrant. Such a myth does not console. It duns the present.

So far the scheme is tidy. Dunkirk breaks it, and I cannot tell whether that is a fact about Dunkirk or a defect in the scheme. Dunkirk was a defeat: a retreat that cost the army its equipment and ended a campaign. But it was experienced as a deliverance, and the myth that formed around it does neither of my two jobs. It converts relief into obligation, and it makes the later victory feel like a debt being paid rather than a prize won. Nobody is being consoled for a loss and nobody is being summoned to avenge one; a nation is being told that it was spared, and that being spared costs.

I am tempted to add a third category, the reprieve myth, but I suspect it is the installmentary form in disguise, with the debt owed to the bare fact of survival rather than to the dead. I would rather be told that the scheme does no work than keep it by inventing a category for every awkward case.

What I want from you is a critical reader's report, not encouragement. Begin with your overall verdict in the first sentence — whether the two-way distinction earns its explanatory keep or is a redescription in heavier vocabulary — and then give the reasons. After that: (1) tell me whether the reprieve case is a genuinely third kind or a disguised version of the second, and if it is genuinely third, name the feature that distinguishes it without simply restating the difference between owing something to the dead and owing something to the fact of survival; (2) press the hardest objection you can, including the possibility that my categories name phases in a single myth's career rather than kinds of myth — I half suspect the Lost Cause was politically live long after its cause was dead, which would make the epitaphal reading a snapshot rather than a type; and (3) if the scheme can be saved, tell me what to cut or reframe, and if it cannot, tell me what it was groping toward that a better distinction would capture. I have kept the historical claims coarse on purpose and do not need literature or citations; if one of the cases is described in a way that distorts it, say so and correct me in your own words. Prose, no preamble, no summary of what I have written.
