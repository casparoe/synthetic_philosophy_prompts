For the last year or so I have been an analyst at a small institute that works on AI governance, and I came to it from strategic studies: my training is in deterrence theory and crisis bargaining — capability and intent, credibility, commitment devices, costly signals, audience costs, reputation, deterrence versus compellence, extended deterrence, brinkmanship — and I am reasonably at home in the standard game-theoretic apparatus of commitment and signalling. Decision theory, by contrast, I am a tourist in. A colleague keeps pressing a family of arguments on me that he files under the headings 'acausal threats' and 'surrogate goals', insisting that they describe a strategic situation my vocabulary simply cannot see. As he tells it: two agents may be unable to affect each other by any causal route whatever — no channel of communication, no shared resource, no prospect of either locating or harming the other — and yet each can predict the other's decision, or, more strongly, each runs on the same decision procedure, so that what one does is a function of what the other does. In that setting, he says, one agent can threaten another. The threatener's policy is fixed and, in principle, knowable: it is to punish agents of a certain type for doing X, where the punishment is never inflicted by any act aimed at the victim — it is simply that the threatener's decision rule already encodes a response to what agents like the victim do, and the victim, understanding this, confronts that response as a consequence of its own choice rather than as an event someone brings about. The defence he offers under the heading of a surrogate goal is that the threatened agent should not resist but instead adopt an objective of its own that already requires the demanded action, so that the threat, however real, is left with nothing to leverage.

What I want from you is a translation, as faithful as you can make it, of this family of claims — that such threats can be real and rationally effective, and that adopting a surrogate goal is a rational response to them — into the vocabulary I actually use: capability and intent, credibility, commitment, signalling, audience costs, reputation, deterrence and compellence, escalation. Then the residue report I would want from any translation. Which parts cross over cleanly? Which cross only with distortion, and what exactly is the distortion in each case — what has been changed in order to make it fit? Which parts have no counterpart in my vocabulary at all? I am especially interested in whether credibility, which I would have assumed was doing the central work, survives the crossing, and whether anything in deterrence theory does what 'logical correlation' is supposed to do in his story. Finally, tell me which way the residue cuts: is it a defect of my vocabulary, a way of seeing that deterrence theory lacks, or is it evidence that the acausal claims are less clear than they sound once you try to say them in a disciplined idiom? Please separate what is settled among people who work on this from what is genuinely contested, and do not let a contested reading pass as the consensus; if my colleague's framing misstates the position at some point, say so and translate the better version instead. I am not asking for a reading list — I have no way to check one — only the translation and the residue. Keep the whole reply to no more than 900 words, and please write in British English.
