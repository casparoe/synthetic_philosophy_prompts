I've been reading rationalist blogs and forums for the better part of two decades, so I've absorbed the standard paradox material at second hand, and next month I'm hosting a long-form interview with a philosopher whose book defends dialetheism: the truth schema is unrestricted, the liar sentence is both true and false, and some contradictions are therefore true. Two features of their version are why I want to talk to them rather than to a textbook summary. First, they argue that dialetheism forces a non-classical theory of rational credence — the Dutch book argument for probabilism assumes that a contradiction is false in every possibility, so once some contradictions are true, a rational agent may, and on their view must, assign positive probability to some of them. Second, they hold that true contradictions are confined to the semantic vocabulary (truth, reference, satisfaction, and the terms of their own theory) and never arise in the special sciences. I'd like eight to ten interview questions, each with a follow-up keyed to the answer they are most likely to give; I want the questions argued from what is in this prompt rather than backed by citations, so please skip the reading list.

Here is what the sequence has to cover. One question should press the disagreement problem: if discovering that P is false no longer gives them grounds to reject P, how do they register disagreement with someone they take to be wrong? They will say that denial is a distinct act from asserting a negation, and I want the follow-up to that reply. One should press the Curry-style paradox, which uses only the truth schema, modus ponens, and the step from A → (A → B) to A → B to reach an arbitrary conclusion; I want to know which of those they surrender, and then whether the reasoning that got them to dialetheism in the first place survives the loss. One should press a strengthened liar — a paradox built out of whatever vocabulary they use to state their solution — and ask whether their solution can be stated at all without the paradox re-forming one level up. One should press the choice-of-logic objection: since there are many paraconsistent logics, what independent reason is there to think theirs is the right one rather than one engineered to save the phenomena, and why prefer true contradictions to the alternative that the liar is neither true nor false? One should press the probability claim, which is the one I care about most, being a Bayesian by temperament: what a positive credence in a contradiction looks like in practice, how it behaves under conditionalization, and whether their non-classical probability still supports expected-utility reasoning. One should press the boundary: if semantic predicates are where contradictions live, is that a principled line or merely where the paradoxes happen to be, and what would they say about an inconsistency discovered in arithmetic? I'd like the interview to close on what would change their mind, phrased so that I can tell whether they have given me a genuine condition or are just being a good sport. And please leave out the two-plus-two-equals-five exchange; that one is well worn.
