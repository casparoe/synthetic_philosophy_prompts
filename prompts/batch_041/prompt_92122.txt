I belong to a small reading group that has spent the past several weeks working through Augustine's two treatments of lying — the earlier book that he later said he half-regretted writing, and the later one provoked by fellow Christians who wanted to pass themselves off as members of a heretical sect in order to expose it — and we have reached the stage where we understand the position well enough to find it funny, but none of us can actually construct the joke. What strikes us is the internal shape of the view rather than the familiar line that a church father was severe about falsehood. Augustine locates the lie in the intention to deceive rather than in the falsity of the utterance, so that someone who asserts a falsehood in perfect good faith does not lie at all, while someone who asserts a literal truth in order to mislead does; he explicitly sets jokes aside on the ground that the tone of voice and the joker's evident lack of any intent to deceive mean no lie has been told, even though what is uttered is not true; he ranks the various kinds of lies by gravity, from the gravest down to the least grave; and yet in the hard cases he will not permit the benevolent lie even to keep an innocent person from death or violation, allowing only that one may hold a truth back in silence. That combination — a criterion that turns entirely on the inner duplex of the speaker, an exemption for humor, and an absolutism that survives the worst cases — seems to me to be sitting on a genuinely comic seam that nobody has worked.

Please write me one joke. I want a single self-contained joke, the sort of thing a person could deliver aloud at the end of a seminar, with a recognizable setup and a punchline, and I want the humor to turn on the actual content of Augustine's position rather than on the generic observation that theologians are fussy about lying. The punchline should hinge on one or more of the following: the intent-to-deceive criterion and its consequence that a true statement can be a lie while a false one is not; the joke exemption and what it implies about tone as evidence of a single heart; the ranking of lies by gravity; the refusal of the well-meant lie in extremity, paired with the permission to conceal by silence; or the infiltration controversy, in which Augustine argues that one cannot hunt down liars by lying. If you are tempted by the obvious scene of a persecutor at the door asking where someone is hiding, you may use it, but only if the punchline exploits the distinction between falling silent and speaking falsely, rather than just restating that Augustine forbids the lie. The joke should be intelligible to someone who has never read either treatise — it must not depend on the listener recognizing a title, a name, or a numbered list — while still rewarding the listener who has. Do not write a satire of Augustine, a sermon, or a shaggy-dog story; the comedy should come out of the position's own internal pressures rather than from mocking the position. After the joke, add two or three sentences identifying precisely which commitment is doing the comic work and where the tension lies, so that I can tell whether it is funny for the right reason. I do not need any citations, and please do not supply or invent any.
