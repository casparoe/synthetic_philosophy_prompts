I've been chewing on this testimony thing for a while now — you know, the whole argument about whether you're allowed to just believe what someone tells you, or whether you need some independent evidence that they're the sort of person who tells the truth. I ran into it on a podcast and it's been rattling around my head since, and I think both sides are mostly talking past each other because each one is picturing a different kind of conversation. So here's what I want from you: argue for the position that the dispute gets resolved, or at least gets a lot clearer, once you index how much default trust a hearer is entitled to to whether the speaker shares in the cost of being wrong.

The way I'd put it: in some cases the speaker sinks or swims with you. If my brother tells me the trail is fine and it isn't, he's the one carrying me back down the hill, so his word gets weight for free — the fact that he said it, knowing he'd eat the consequences too, is itself a reason to believe him. Call that a shared-cost case. In other cases the speaker is fine either way: the guy at the used car lot, the stranger on the forum, the company that says its product is safe. There, the bare fact that he said it buys you nothing, and you'd be a fool to treat it as a reason until you've got some independent handle on whether he's reliable. Call that a split-cost case. What I want you to argue is that this is the right way to cut it: the anti-reductionists are basically describing shared-cost cases and the reductionists are basically describing split-cost cases, that neither side's blanket rule survives the examples the other side actually cares about, and that once you add the cost-sharing condition you get a view that handles both without giving up anything either side was right about.

And please don't just hand me the tidy version — spend real time on the cases that make it hard. What about a stranger with nothing to gain either way, like a tourist pointing me toward the station? What about someone who shares the cost but is hopeless — my uncle, who's wrong about basically everything and believes every word of it? What about a doctor telling me about a treatment, where he isn't the one at risk but his reputation is? And what about someone who shares my costs only because they're mistaken about that, or because they've got a weird sense of solidarity with me? If the view can't say anything useful about those, say so and tell me how bad the damage is. I'd rather have a real argument with the soft spots marked then a victory lap.
