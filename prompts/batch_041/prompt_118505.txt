Right, so in Nyāya epistemology there are four pramāṇas, four legit ways of coming to know something: perception (pratyakṣa), inference (anumāna), testimony (śabda — stuff you get from reliable people), and upamāna, usually translated as comparison or analogy. The stock example for upamāna: you've never seen a gavaya (a kind of wild ox) and you dont know the word, then someone who does know tells you a gavaya is like a cow; later you're in the forest, you see the animal, clock the resemblance to a cow, remember what you were told, and now you know what gavaya means. Nyāya says that's a knowledge source in its own right, not just the other three mashed together. Rivals (the Vaiśeṣika, for instance) said no, that's just inference.

I want you to compare upamāna with śabda. On the face of it they're siblings: both are non-perceptual and non-inferential, and both get you knowledge you couldn't have generated on your own. So do the reductive job first — make the strongest case that upamāna is not a basic pramāṇa, that it's just testimony plus perception plus memory and nothing more. Then switch sides and defend the Nyāya line that it really is irreducibly its own thing. And once you've done that: does the same reductive move take out śabda too? If upamāna dissolves into "someone told me" plus "I saw it", why doesn't testimony dissolve into an inference from the fact that a reliable person said so? If one of the two survives the reduction and the other doesn't, say exactly where the asymmetry lies.

Last bit, since this is all really about learning what a word means by being told what a thing is like: what happens when there's no perceivable resemblance to lean on? Suppose a physicist tells you a certain particle spins like a top. Is that upamāna, or has it quietly turned into metaphor, or inference, or just plain testimony? Does the Nyāya framework have the resources to sort those cases apart, or would it need a fifth pramāṇa for the structural and metaphorical ones? Give me a verdict, don't just lay out the options.
