I sit on the civilian Use of Force Review Commission in a mid-sized city. We are not a court and we do not decide criminal liability. For each incident, the department's internal investigation is presented to us, and we enter exactly one of two findings on a recorded vote: Authorized, meaning the city stands behind the officer's use of force as a legitimate exercise of the coercive authority we have granted it, or Unauthorized, meaning it does not. We cannot split the difference or attach conditions. Each finding is published with a short rationale; I have paraphrased the five we have issued below in my own words. The commission has been seated for two years.

1. Officers were called to a supermarket parking lot about a man with a machete. On arrival he charged one of them from roughly thirty feet, closing fast. The officer fired once and the man died. Finding: Authorized. The rationale was that at the instant he fired, the officer faced an imminent threat of death or serious bodily injury and had no time to attempt anything else.

2. A man in a mental-health crisis was sitting on the front steps of his mother's house holding a screwdriver, twenty feet from two officers. He did not move toward them and did not answer commands. A Taser and a ballistic shield were on scene. An officer fired four times and the man died. Finding: Unauthorized. The rationale was that no imminent threat existed and that the less-lethal options were on scene and available.

3. An officer stopped a pedestrian for jaywalking. The man ran; the officer caught him after a short pursuit; in the struggle the man got a hand on the officer's holster. The officer fired and the man died. Finding: Authorized. The rationale was that at the moment of the shot the officer reasonably believed the man was about to take his weapon, and that nothing about the stop or the pursuit had been unreasonable.

4. An officer confronted a man holding a kitchen knife ten feet away who had ignored three warnings and was closing the distance. The officer had his Taser already drawn. The man was wearing a heavy leather coat; the officer holstered the Taser and fired. Finding: Authorized, four votes to three. The majority reasoned that the officer's judgment that a Taser would not stop a man in a heavy coat at that distance was reasonable, and that a knife at ten feet and closing is within striking distance. The three dissenters wrote that a less-lethal option had not merely been available but had been in the officer's hand, and that we should require officers to use it.

5. An officer alone on a noise complaint at two in the morning knocked on a door. When it opened, a man holding a baseball bat took a step forward; the officer fired and the man died. Finding: Unauthorized. The rationale was that we were not persuaded the officer had a reasonable basis for thinking the man meant to strike him rather than that he had simply been startled, and not persuaded that the force was necessary; we also noted that the officer had chosen to knock alone at that hour.

Now the incident before us. A man was wanted on a warrant for a stabbing. Officers received a tip that he was staying at his girlfriend's apartment. Department policy requires a supervisor-approved plan and a cover team before serving a felony warrant at a residence; the sergeant was eight minutes out and had approved nothing. The officer who led the entry has six years on the job and no prior force complaints, and says he was afraid the man would slip away. He and his partner knocked, got no answer, found the door unlocked, and went in. In the hallway a man emerged from the kitchen holding a kitchen knife. It was the girlfriend's brother — not the wanted man, no criminal record — who had picked up the knife when he heard strangers at the door. He was about eight feet from the officer, said nothing, and took one step toward him. The officer's partner was in the doorway behind him with a Taser drawn and a clear line. The officer fired twice and the brother died. The girlfriend says her brother was wearing headphones and would not have heard the officers announce themselves; the officers say they announced. The wanted man was not in the apartment.

I am the swing vote and we vote next week. I want four things. First, tell me what rule or rules our five findings are actually consistent with — I suspect more than one rule fits all five, and that our published rationales do not distinguish among them. Second, say what each of those rules predicts about the new incident, identify which of the five is the closest analog and why, and give me the finding I should enter if I want to be consistent with what we have already done. Third, be honest about what principle we would be adopting under each available finding; I want to know what our precedents commit us to, not merely what they permit. Fourth, if the rules genuinely come apart here, name the kind of incident that would settle which rule we have been running all along — not a hypothetical rigged to make one rule look foolish, but a case that could actually reach a commission like ours.

Finally, and briefly: people in the city have begun to argue that a body which only ratifies or refuses to ratify individual killings is a fig leaf, and that the real question is whether the state should be doing this work at all. Set aside the policy proposal. Tell me instead what conception of the state's authority to kill our five findings implicitly commit us to, and whether that conception is defensible.
