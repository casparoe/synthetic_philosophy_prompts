I'm grading a stack of short-answer exams for a second-year philosophy of medicine unit and I keep hitting the same answer, so I want a second opinion on one of them. The question on the paper was this:

"Consider the following proposed criterion for disease, which the hypothetical author calls the *fitness criterion*: an organism has a disease just in case it has a condition that lowers the organism's expected biological fitness (roughly, expected number of surviving offspring) below the level typical for its species, age, sex and environment. (a) Is this a naturalist or a normativist criterion, and does it improve on Boorse's biostatistical theory? (b) Apply it to the hard cases: infertility that the person does not regret, male pattern baldness, and a same-sex orientation. (c) Does the criterion answer the normativist objection to naturalist accounts of disease?"

And here's the student's answer, verbatim:

"(a) Boorse's biostatistical theory says health is the statistically normal functioning of the parts and processes of the body, and disease is a deviation below that statistical norm. It is meant to be a value-free, scientific account: it just reads off what is statistically typical for the species. The fitness criterion is also naturalist, because it defines disease entirely in terms of a biological fact — expected reproductive output — and never mentions any disvalue, suffering, or social judgment. It is an improvement on Boorse because Boorse's statistical normality is really just a convention (what most members of the species happen to do), whereas expected fitness is an objective, biological quantity, so the fitness criterion gives us a firmer naturalist foundation.

(b) Infertility: on this criterion infertility is a disease, because it lowers expected fitness below the species-typical level, and this is the right result — it is a malfunction of the reproductive system whether or not the person happens to mind it. Male pattern baldness: this is not a disease, since it does not lower expected fitness; this also matches intuition, which is why baldness is treated as a cosmetic matter. Same-sex orientation: this lowers expected reproductive output relative to the species norm, so the criterion would classify it as a disease, which shows the criterion is at least consistent and not swayed by fashion.

(c) The normativist objection says that calling something a disease always involves a value judgment that the condition is bad or undesirable, so there can be no purely descriptive account. The fitness criterion answers this because it never makes any value judgment at all — it just measures fitness. This is also why it is better than Wakefield's harmful-dysfunction analysis, since Wakefield's view is really just the normativist position in disguise: the 'harm' component means the whole thing collapses into a social judgment about what we dislike."
