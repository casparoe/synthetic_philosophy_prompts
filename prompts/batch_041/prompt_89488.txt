A friend of mine — philosophically trained, but the sort who reaches for a counterexample before a citation — has spent weeks trying to convince me that the going explanation of Moore's paradox proves far too much, and I have got to the point where I can neither refute him nor quite believe him. He lays it out like this.

(1) To assert that p is to express the belief that p. A sincere assertion is simply the outward face of the belief it carries.

(2) *It is raining, but I do not believe that it is raining* is absurd to assert. It is not absurd the way a contradiction is: nothing stops the world from being arranged so that it is raining and I have simply failed to notice. What is absurd is the asserting, not the content. The absurdity is also oddly local — it evaporates in the past tense and in the second and third person. *It was raining, but I did not believe that it was* is perfectly assertible, and so is *it is raining, but you do not believe that it is*. He confines himself throughout to the omissive form, where the second clause denies belief rather than avowing disbelief, and insists the commissive form be set aside.

(3) The best explanation of the absurdity is that the utterance expresses two beliefs that cannot cohabit a single mind: the belief that p, carried by the first clause, and the belief that one does not believe that p, carried by the second.

(4) That explanation presupposes a principle — he calls it *no-gap* — on which no single outlook can contain both the belief that p and the belief that one lacks the belief that p. Drop *no-gap* and (3) explains nothing.

(5) *no-gap* is a claim about belief, not about assertion. It says the combination is psychologically impossible, not merely that avowing it is defective.

(6) Now take the cases we build whole theories around. The man who reports, sincerely and repeatedly, that he is not afraid of flying, and who has not boarded a plane in eleven years. The woman who says she has made her peace with her brother's death and has arranged her life as though she has not. The visitors who crawl across a glass walkway they will tell you, truthfully, they know to be safe. In each case the behaviour is best explained by a belief the person sincerely denies holding.

(7) By (1), the sincere denial is itself the expression of a belief — the belief that she does not believe the thing. So here is someone who believes that p and believes that she does not believe that p.

(8) By (4), that is impossible.

(9) So either *no-gap* goes, or the whole vocabulary of self-deception, repression and unavowed belief goes with it — along with the cases in (6), which strike me as about as well attested as anything in psychology.

(10) I would rather keep the cases. But if *no-gap* goes, (3) no longer explains the absurdity in (2), and I am reduced to saying that the Moorean sentence is merely bad manners.

One further datum he insists on including, and I cannot tell whether it matters: the absurdity survives insincerity. A man lying through his teeth who says *it is raining, but I do not believe that it is* is still talking nonsense.

Which step do I cut? Tell me why it looked fine, and price the alternatives for me. I am not after a survey of the literature — I want the crux and the bill.
