I direct the office of conflict resolution at a mid-sized university — a two-person team, myself and one case manager, handling roughly fifteen disputes a week, from authorship and lab disputes to housing complaints, disputes among student organizations, and staff grievances. Our recurring failure is not a lack of process. It is that the parties are not having the same kind of conflict. One wants a retraction and an apology delivered in front of the people who witnessed the slight, because what was damaged was standing. Another wants the same rule applied that would be applied to anyone else, because what was damaged was a right. A third wants the harm named and the suffering acknowledged, because what is at issue is whether their pain counts as something that has standing. My case manager defaults to the impartial-rule frame, because it is the one our profession trains, and the other parties leave feeling unheard or, worse, humiliated.

Write me an intake-and-routing procedure that a case manager with no philosophy background can run in about thirty minutes. Build it on three ideal types — honor, dignity, victimhood — but define them yourself, precisely enough that the case manager can tell them apart from a single conversation, and say what merely looks like evidence for each without being it. At minimum the procedure should specify: the intake questions in the order they are to be asked; what counts as a sufficient answer to each; how each diagnosis routes to a process, that is, what a fair process actually looks like when the real complaint is about standing, about a rule, or about recognition; how to proceed when the two parties are in different registers, including the failure mode in which the office satisfies one party by translating the other's grievance into a currency that party does not accept; and the point at which the procedure must stop and hand the case to me.

Two constraints. First, it must remain usable by staff who disagree about the underlying moral question. Mark clearly which steps are register-neutral and which quietly presuppose that one register is the mature or correct one to be in; I do not want a procedure that smuggles in the assumption that dignity culture is the grown-up position. Second, it must handle the cases that make my staff uncomfortable: a demand for a public apology that they read as a demand for humiliation; a claim of victimhood that is being used to shut down argument; a dignity framing that is functioning as a weapon. Where the question is really whether a way of being wronged deserves standing at all, the procedure should say that it cannot decide and name who does.

One further distinction, because my staff keep collapsing it: separate honor from face — the difference between standing that can be lost before an audience and the maintenance of a relationship in which the aim is for each party to leave the exchange without being diminished. A procedure that treats these as one will misroute a large share of our cases.

Then run the procedure on three cases: a graduate student who wants a co-author removed and a correction posted to the group's public page; a staff member who wants a policy applied retroactively to a colleague who received an exception; and a student who wants the university to publicly acknowledge that a program's culture harmed them. For each, show the intake, the routing, the objection you expect from the party the procedure disserves, and what the case manager should do when the procedure's answer is one that I, or the parties, will not like.

Close by stating what the procedure cannot do and where it will predictably misfire, and by saying whether the neutrality it maintains is itself a moral position — that is, whether refusing to rank the registers is a disguised way of endorsing one of them.
