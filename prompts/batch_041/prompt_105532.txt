My friend Dana and I spent most of last night arguing about acausal trade — the idea that two agents who can never meet or affect each other can still cooperate, because each one expects the other to be running the same kind of calculation. I went in assuming it was either obviously true or obviously nonsense and came out not knowing, which is annoying. The worst part is that her version sounded airtight, and I've definately learned not to trust airtight. Here's her case, as close as I can reconstruct it:

"Acausal trade is when two agents who can't communicate and can't affect each other still each do their part in a trade, because each one knows the other is an exact copy of itself — same code, same values — so each can predict the other perfectly. It's the prisoner's dilemma played by two copies of the same person, which is basically Newcomb's problem, and really it's just Hofstadter's superrationality with extra steps. There's no spooky backwards causation, the identical-ness is doing all the work. And the payoffs are real — the standard examples are two AIs that are copies of each other, or a simulation and the program running it. So acausal trade makes cooperation rational with aliens we'll never meet, and it also gives us a Pascal's-wager-style reason to spend real resources now on the off-chance that some distant or merely possible agent is running the same calculation and would reciprocate."

Some of that has to be wrong, but I can't find the seam on my own. Go through it sentence by sentence and tell me what someone who actually works on this would nod at, what they'd wince at, and what's just wrong. I'm especially suspicious of the "payoffs are real" bit — are the two AIs and the simulation-and-its-simulator even the same kind of case, or is she lumping something causal in with something genuinely acausal? And then the bigger thing: setting aside whether she described it correctly, does the Pascal's-wager conclusion actually follow from the correct version of the idea, or is she smuggling in an extra step that the real argument doesn't license? If there's a version where the identical-copies assumption is genuinely load-bearing rather than a simplifying toy, say so.
