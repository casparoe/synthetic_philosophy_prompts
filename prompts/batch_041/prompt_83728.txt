I chair the content review panel at a natural history museum, and I need a written memo before Thursday's meeting. We are about to sign off the labels for a new permanent gallery on evolution, and our education team has built an entire wall around the idea that every structure has a job: the running text tells visitors that each part of an organism is shaped for something, and the individual labels use the word "function" freely. Two of my panellists — a retired palaeontologist and a philosopher of science who has sat on this panel for years — have now gone to war over three of the labels, and I have to bring the committee a recommendation rather than let them relitigate the argument in front of the designers. The three disputed labels are as follows. First, human goosebumps: the label reads "a leftover from our furrier ancestors — it no longer has a function." Our philosopher says that is simply false, because the trait was selected for thermoregulation (and for display) in our ancestors, and that selection history is what makes thermoregulation its function even now; the palaeontologist says a trait that no longer does anything useful has no function, whatever happened in the past. Second, the appendix: the label reads "a vestigial organ, with no function." Our scientific advisers tell us that it does house a reservoir of gut bacteria and that this is a genuine current contribution, but that nobody has shown it was selected for that. The two panellists disagree about whether a real current contribution, with no selection history behind it, is enough to license the word. Third, an interactive about the heart, whose caption says "the sound it makes has a function: it lets a doctor hear your heartbeat" — which one of them calls a category mistake and the other calls obviously true, once you are careful about which system you are asking about. The accounts they are talking past each other with are the two that philosophers of biology usually call the selected-effects (or etiological) account, on which a trait's function is the effect for which it was selected and which explains why the trait is there, and the causal-role (or systemic) account, on which a trait's function is whatever contribution it makes to some capacity of a containing system — the latter being relative to whichever system and capacity you happen to choose, the former being determinate but silent about traits that are novel, drifted, or no longer useful.

Please write the memo I will circulate. I want roughly 800 words, addressed to the content review panel, with a clear recommendation and a stated bottom line, and — if you conclude the wording must change — replacement text for each of the three labels that I can hand straight to the designers. Take a position on which account we should adopt as the museum's house line, or on whether the two are answering different questions rather than competing (I gather that is a live option, and if you take it, say exactly what follows for our labels). Make clear what turns on the choice beyond wording: in particular, whether adopting an account commits us to telling visitors that a part which fails to do its job is malfunctioning, and whether any of the "for" talk the designers love can survive. Before the memo proper, set out in a short opening section — a table is fine — how each account classifies each of the three cases, so that I can see you have actually done the classification rather than asserted a conclusion, and only then draft the memo. Assume your readers are intelligent scientists and educators with no philosophy of biology in their backgrounds, so explain both accounts from scratch in plain language, and assume nobody will look anything up: this is an internal document, and we do not need citations. Do not soften the arguments. Give the strongest case for the account you end up rejecting, and say plainly what we lose by rejecting it. You are a philosopher of biology writing for a lay institutional audience: be precise, be concrete about the wording, and do not hedge.
