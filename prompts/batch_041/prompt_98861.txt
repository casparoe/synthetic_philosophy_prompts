I teach a 90-minute upper-level undergraduate seminar in metaphysics and decision theory -- sixteen students, most of whom have had a single course in probability and none in formal epistemology -- and I want to build one session around an idea that has always struck me as both irresistible and slippery: that the mere fact of where you happen to sit in some larger sequence -- your position in a list, your number in a lottery, your place among all the people who will ever have lived -- could by itself tell you something about how the sequence ends. I want the students to feel that pull in their hands before they have a name for it, and then to feel it weaken when I add a wrinkle. Here is the shape I am imagining, which you should feel free to redesign: each student draws a card from a face-down deck and finds on it a single integer; they are told, truthfully, that they are one resident of a settlement, that their number was drawn uniformly at random from among that settlement's residents, that the settlement is either small or very large but they will not be told which, and that they may stake a small number of tokens -- redeemable for coffee -- on the question of which it is. Then, after they have bet and argued about their bets, I want a second phase in which the ground shifts: the settlement's population is revealed to have been growing over its history, so that a randomly drawn resident is far more likely to have been born late, and the students must work out for themselves whether the inference they just made survives the change. Build me the whole thing: the exact numbers for the two settlement sizes, chosen so that the first phase bites without being trivial; the materials; the betting rule and payoff table; the two phases with a timeline for each; and the precise moment of reversal, staged so that the students and not I supply the realization. The debrief should map what happened at each stage onto the underlying idea -- the assumption that you should reason as though you were a random sample from some reference class, the unresolved question of which reference class that is, and the way a growing population quietly rewrites the inference -- without my having to introduce any terminology the students cannot derive from the game itself. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it (I expect at least one student to refuse to bet at all, and I do not yet know whether that refusal is a defect in the design or the most interesting thing that could happen in the room), and be frank about which part of the idea the activity cannot reproduce and that I will have to supply in words afterwards.

Finally, I would like you to flag your own design choices the way you would flag a risky empirical claim: tell me where in the design you are least confident that the intended experience will actually occur, which specific thing I could observe in the room that would count as evidence the activity is failing to do its work, and what you would change about the design if that happened.
