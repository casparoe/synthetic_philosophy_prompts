I have been keeping a list of my reactions to cases in which there appears to be a reason to hold an attitude — a belief, a desire, a feeling, an admiration — and I would like to know what principle, if any, my reactions add up to. I gather this is what philosophers call the wrong kind of reasons problem, but I want the verdicts to do the work, not the label.

My cases, and what I think about each:

1. The offer. A surgeon offers me ten thousand dollars if I can sincerely believe that the lump is benign. I think the offer is a reason to want that belief, and to try to induce it, and no reason whatever to think the lump is benign. A threat works the same way in reverse: believe it or be fired is a reason to be rid of the belief, not to hold it.

2. The plane. I am frightened on a flight, and I know that if I sincerely believed the plane were safe I would be calm and would enjoy the journey. I do not think that is a reason to think the plane is safe. But I do not think it is nothing either: it is a reason to want the belief, and I would think better of someone who managed to acquire it than of someone who took the surgeon's money.

3. The painting. A close friend shows me a painting she has made. It is bad. I know she will be crushed if I do not admire it. I think the fact that it is hers, and that she will be hurt, is a reason to want to admire it and a reason to be kind about it, and no reason to think it is good. Yet the fact that it is hers does change what I actually feel when I look at it, and I am not prepared to call the feeling a mistake.

4. The demon. A demon announces that he will destroy the city unless I sincerely admire him. I think that is a reason to want to admire him, not a reason to admire him. But the very same fact — his power and his willingness to use it — strikes me as an excellent reason to fear him, and I cannot tell whether those who say it is also some reason to admire him are confused or correct.

5. The mud. A demon offers to make me rich if I sincerely desire a saucer of mud. Reason to want the desire, not to have it. If instead a chemist tells me the mud is nutritious and I am starving, that is a reason to desire it.

6. The promise. I promise a dying friend that I will be angry with the person who wronged her. I take the promise to be a reason to act angry and to want to be angry, and not a reason to feel angry — though I notice it feels far more like a reason than the demon's threat does, and I want to know why.

7. The self-fulfilling belief. My friend tells me her business will succeed. I know that if I believed in her she would work harder and would probably succeed. That the belief would help her succeed is a reason to want the belief; but it is also, in a way, evidence that she will succeed, and I cannot decide whether that makes it an ordinary reason after all.

8. The expert. A stranger in a white coat tells me a vaccine is safe, and I take his word for it, with nothing I could check for myself. I think that is a good reason to believe him. Had he told me instead that believing him would be good for me, that would not have been. So being uncheckable is not what makes a reason the wrong kind.

9. The joke. A cruel joke makes me laugh harder because it is cruel, and I take that to be a fault in me rather than evidence that it is funny; but when my oldest friend tells it, with thirty years behind us, I think it genuinely is funnier, and I would defend that.

My tentative principle is that a reason is of the right kind when it bears on whether the attitude gets its object right — whether the thing really is true, or good, or dangerous — and of the wrong kind when it bears only on the costs and benefits of having the attitude. Cases 4, 6 and 7 make me doubt that this is sharp enough, and cases 3 and 9 make me doubt whether the object is always a mind-independent thing.

What I want from you: a precise statement of the best principle, or a small set of rival principles, that captures this pattern; for each clause, the case that forces it; the verdicts that principle returns on cases I have not listed — in particular one in which the right-kind and wrong-kind considerations are the very same fact, and one in which the attitude is directed at a person rather than a proposition; and an honest account of which of my verdicts the principle cannot accommodate and would have to overturn. If the best available answer is a pair of principles that each save some verdicts and lose others, say so, and tell me which verdict is cheapest to give up.
