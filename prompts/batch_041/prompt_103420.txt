For the past several months I have been reading about algorithmic fairness on my own — I have no academic training in it, I work in operations at a mid-sized company, and I got pulled in by the news coverage of risk scores used in sentencing and lending. What I keep circling back to is the result that everyone seems to reach eventually: there is apparently a proof that you cannot satisfy all the fairness properties you would want from a decision rule at the same time. You can have the scores mean the same thing across groups, or you can have the same error rates across groups, but not both — and the obstacle is not sloppy engineering but the plain fact that the groups differ in how common the outcome is. What unsettles me is how the industry answers this: vendors advertise de-biasing and fairness as a box you can tick, when the proof seems to say the box cannot be ticked, that somebody has to decide whose errors to absorb, and that this is a moral choice dressed up as a slider.

I would like you to write a parody of a software product landing page and its FAQ, in the deadpan voice of a confident business-to-business startup, for a fictional service that claims to solve precisely this problem. Around 600 words, formatted as a page with a headline, a few feature blurbs, a pricing table, and a short FAQ. The humour has to come from getting the impossibility right: the fine print should quietly concede that the single fairness dial trades one definition of fairness against another, that the tradeoff is forced by unequal base rates rather than by bad code, and that the setting the company recommends is the one most likely to survive a lawsuit rather than the one that is most just. Let the pricing tiers map onto which definition of fairness the buyer is purchasing, and include a customer testimonial in which two clients want opposite things and both are told they are getting fairness. Do not let the narrator wink — the whole piece should read as sincere corporate copy, so that a reader who knows the literature laughs while a reader who does not still finishes it understanding what was given up and who got to choose. Please invent the company and product names rather than using real ones.
