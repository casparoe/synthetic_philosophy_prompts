I'm a software engineer, no philosophy background, and I've somehow ended up with a 20-minute slot at a perception reading group a friend runs in the psych department (I got in the door by building a little demo where a vision model flips its answer when you prime it with a word). Here's the argument I want to make, roughly: whether perception is 'cognitively penetrated' isn't an empirical question about brains. Same data, different architecture, opposite answer — wire it strictly feedforward and penetration is false by construction, nothing up top can talk back down; wire it as predictive coding and it's true by construction, top-down is the whole point. So each side can keep its position just by choosing an architecture, which means the fight is really about where we draw the line around 'perception', and that line is something we choose rather than something we discover. My positive proposal is to stop asking whether an effect is 'really perceptual' and ask instead which computations the influence can alter — I think that question is actually answerable.

The room is about a dozen people, mostly psychophysicists. Two are hardliners who think basically every famous top-down effect is memory, judgment, or task strategy leaking in. Two are predictive-processing people who think the hardliners' demands can't be met even in principle. One vision scientist only cares whether the effects replicate and is bored by the philosophy. And there's a computational modeler who will definately bring up feedback connections.

Prep me for the questions: what this specific room will actually throw at me, ordered by how much damage it does, in each person's own words, with a two-minute reply each, a note on where each reply is soft, what I should just concede up front, and the one question I'm praying nobody asks. Also tell me which of my framing moves to drop, cause I have a feeling one of them is gonna get me killed.
