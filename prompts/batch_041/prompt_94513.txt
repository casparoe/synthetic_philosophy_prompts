Question: In the Pensées, Pascal keeps circling the relation between justice and force, and the idea that custom or established practice is what really grounds law. Explain his account of how justice and force are related, and what he takes that to show about the foundations of political obligation. Make clear which parts of his view are descriptive and which are prescriptive.

Student answer:

Pascal's basic insight is that justice and force are two separate things that only ever meet by accident. Justice on its own has no way of making itself effective — you can be as just as you like, but if nobody can compel obedience you'll just be ignored. Force on its own, with no claim to justice, is mere tyranny. So the two need each other, and Pascal thinks the way they get combined in actual states is through custom: a law counts as just, in the only sense available to us, because it has been established and obeyed long enough. That's why he says we should obey the laws not because they are just but because they are laws, and why he says the just is simply whatever is established.

I think Pascal is being a bit of a cynic here, but there's a real argument underneath. He's basically claiming that all pretensions to know what justice really is are fraudulent, because what people call justice varies with the climate and the latitude they happen to live at — he even jokes about justice shifting with a few degrees of elevation of the pole. So the only stable basis for law is custom plus the sovereign's force, which means political obligation rests on the fact of power and long usage rather than on any rational insight into what's right.

It's tempting to read this as proto-Hobbesian, and in some ways it is, since Pascal also wants a strong sovereign to keep us in line. But I'd say Pascal goes further than Hobbes, because Hobbes at least thinks you can derive the laws of nature by reason, whereas Pascal thinks reason is too corrupt for that. His account is actually closer to Hume's: justice is an artificial virtue we invent through convention rather than discover in nature. The difference is that Pascal thinks this shows how miserable and fallen we are, while Hume is fine with it. So the theological frame matters — the whole point of the justice-and-force discussion is to show we can't have real justice in this life and have to look for it in God, which is why he can be so deflationary about earthly law without being a relativist exactly.

I'd call that a decent but flawed answer, maybe a solid B or B-. Grade it properly and tell me exactly what to flag.
