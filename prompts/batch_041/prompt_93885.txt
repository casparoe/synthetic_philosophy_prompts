I have no training in philosophy, computer science, or law, but for the past two years I have been arguing about a single question with a friend who is equally untrained, and I would like to stop arguing and write down where we actually stand. The question is: once a system is powerful enough to shape how millions of people live, whose values should it serve? I want the joint statement of an adversarial collaboration between the two camps we have ended up representing.

The first camp holds that such a system should serve the summed welfare or preference satisfaction of everyone affected, weighted equally, and that since some way of adding those up has to be chosen, the honest thing is to choose the best one and optimize it, accepting that some people will lose. The second camp holds that every way of adding up smuggles in a value judgment — it requires an exchange rate between one person's deepest commitment and another person's convenience, and no such rate is given by anything but a contestable theory — so a properly aligned system should refuse to aggregate and act instead as neutral infrastructure, letting each person pursue their own ends and intervening only to stop clear harm.

Please produce the document both of us could sign. I want four parts.

First, a numbered list of claims both camps can sign: about how much power such a system actually has; about what the word alignment is asking for once the marketing is stripped away; about the fact that the small group of people building these systems is already making this choice silently; and about what observable outcome would count, for both camps, as evidence that a deployed system had failed on this question.

Second, a disagreements table. One row per real disagreement: the question stated neutrally, then each camp's position written in wording that camp itself would accept rather than in the other's caricature of it, then the crux — the sub-question such that settling it would move at least one camp.

Third, for each crux, a case or argument that both camps agree bears on it, with each side's prediction recorded in advance. Use these three, and add others only if you think they genuinely belong:

(a) the separate-justification test: a decision counts as acceptable only if the system can explain it to each affected person using only reasons that person already accepts, invoking no ranking that person rejects. Does any non-trivial policy ever pass? Each camp should say what it expects the test to find.

(b) the exchange rate: the system controls something scarce — a hospital's last bed, a river's water, a slice of radio spectrum — and must weigh a small loss of something vital to one person against small gains to many. Each camp should say what the system should do, and what it predicts will happen if the other camp's method is used here.

(c) the boundary: who is included in the sum? Future people, animals, minds that are not human, and communities that want nothing to do with the system at all. The first camp must draw a line; the second camp's method seems to draw a different one without admitting it. Each side should say what it expects its own method to do with a group that refuses to take part in any agreement procedure.

Fourth, my own half-formed proposal, which I want each camp to attack in its own words and to say whether it could live with: a two-layer system, where the machine is bound by a short list of prohibitions everyone can accept, and above that floor it runs something like a market in which people trade the things they care about instead of having the machine weigh their cares against one another.

Finally, tell me which parts of our disagreement are merely verbal — I suspect neutrality is doing double duty, meaning neutral among persons in one sentence and neutral among visions of a good life in the next — and which disagreements survive once the words are pinned down.

No citations and no reading list. I want the formulations and the reasoning, not the sources.
