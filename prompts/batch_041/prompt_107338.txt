I recently joined the historical memory working group in my department — we're the ones who end up drafting the language for plaques and for the short notes that hang beside portraits, and I've been struck by how often the whole discussion collapses into a single sentence. Whenever the question of culpability surfaces — whether Jefferson's ownership of enslaved people should lead us to retire his portrait, whether Churchill's views on empire or his handling of the Bengal famine deserve a note, whether a Columbus monument still says something about us now — someone eventually remarks that the figure in question "was a man of his time," and the room treats the remark as though it had settled the matter. I'm not confident it has, and I'm not confident it hasn't; I genuinely can't tell whether the people who say it are stating an obvious truth, a deep and contestable one, or a false one dressed up as a truism.

What I'd like from you is a careful disambiguation of that one sentence. Treat it as a piece of language whose meaning is up for grabs, and lay out the distinct, precise claims it could be making, as a menu of readings rather than a single favoured one. I'm interested in sorting those readings into the ones that are mere historical platitudes (of course everyone is shaped by his time), the ones that are substantive and defensible (for instance, a claim about what was epistemically available to the agent, or about which moral standards were actually live in his community), and the ones that are actually false or incoherent. The distinction I keep groping toward, and would like you to sharpen if it can be sharpened, is between excusing someone — the standards apply to him, but his situation gives him partial or total cover — and exempting him, treating him as simply not the kind of thing that is answerable to those standards at all; I suspect the slogan trades on sliding between the two. I'd also like you to say, for each reading, which one the person in the room probably meant, and which reading would actually have to be true for the slogan to do the work it was being asked to do in that argument. Where a reading only supports a conclusion about what we should do with the plaque or the portrait rather than about the person's culpability, say so explicitly, since those strike me as different questions that are being run together.

Please write this as continuous prose — no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, just the way you'd write it in an email. And please be explicit about how confident you are in each claim you make, singling out the one point in your answer you're least sure about.
