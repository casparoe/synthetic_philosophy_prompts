I've somehow let myself get volunteered into running the Thursday-night slot at our local crime-writers' circle — it's billed as a "law and fiction" evening and I'm down to do the Hart–Fuller thing, legal positivism versus natural law, because about six months ago I said in the pub, loudly, that it was the single most useful bit of philosophy for anyone writing a courtroom scene, and apparently people remember that sort of thing. The crowd is maybe fifteen people, a few published novelists, two retired solicitors, nobody who's read any jurisprudence, and I've got fifty minutes and no projector. What I want from you is not an explanation of the debate — I roughly know it, Hart saying law and morality are separate questions and Fuller saying morality is somehow built into what law is — I want you to plan the teaching of it, because I keep starting in the middle and losing them. So: what has to land first before the question "is a monstrous law still a law?" even makes sense to a room of storytellers; which single case should carry the whole session, and what does that case need to have and to lack — I'd assumed the post-war German informer case, the woman who turned her husband in over some remarks about Hitler and then argued the statute made it lawful, but tell me if something leaner would do the job better, and if you keep it, what about it will quietly derail them. Then walk me through the sequence of ideas and tell me exactly where each of the predictable misconceptions surfaces so I can head it off at the right moment — my guesses are (a) "positivism means you should obey any law," which is obviously not it but they'll say it anyway, (b) "natural law means unjust laws aren't really laws" delivered as a factual claim about the statute book rather than a moral one, (c) everyone collapsing the question of a law's validity into the quite different question of whether you're obliged to obey it, and (d) someone insisting the whole thing is just the old "activist judges" fight in a frock coat.

Two more things and then I'll stop. I want a fresh hypothetical I can chalk on the board in the last ten minutes to test whether they've got the distinction rather than the history — something that isn't Nazi Germany and isn't the segregation cases, because those are the only two examples they'll have half-absorbed from somewhere, so build me a small invented statute-and-defendant scenario and say what the positivist answer to it is and what the natural-law answer is and where they come apart. And tell me honestly what to refuse to cover at this level — I was going to skip the rule of recognition and Fuller's eight principles of legality entirely, and to keep the whole thing tied to decisions a novelist actually makes in a scene (does my judge say "this is law, however vile" or "this was never law at all"?), because otherwise it floats away from them. Finish with the one closing question I should ask to see whether it landed, and flag anything in my plan above that's just wrong, I won't be offended.
