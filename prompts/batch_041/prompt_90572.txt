I recently saw an Escher print of two hands drawing each other and have not been able to stop thinking about it; it has turned out to be uncomfortably relevant to a paper I am writing on self-representation. In our reading group, a colleague offered the following argument, and I cannot decide whether it is a genuine proof that no computational system can understand its own language or a reductio:

(a) A representation is always at a higher level than what it represents; otherwise it could not represent it.
(b) In a tangled hierarchy, a system represents itself, so it must be at a higher level than itself.
(c) That is impossible unless there is an inviolate level outside the system that does the representing.
(d) A computational system has no outside level; everything it does is within its own operations.
(e) Therefore no computational system can represent its own semantics.
(f) Human language users routinely represent the meanings of their own words, so human minds are not computational.
(g) But this proves too much: it would also show that a formal system cannot represent its own syntax, that a programming language cannot contain an interpreter for itself, and that a person cannot think about their own thoughts. I would rather keep (a) and (f) than accept the counterexamples in (g), but I do not know what that commits me to. Where does the argument go wrong, and what does rejecting the faulty step cost me if I want to preserve the distinction between a system and its representation?
