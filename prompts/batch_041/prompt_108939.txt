I'm a fourth-year PhD student in early modern philosophy and I've somehow ended up TAing an upper-level seminar this term on "mind and money in the long eighteenth century" — the last unit is Berkeley's economic writings, which is definately the only reason I agreed to it. The thing that's been eating at me: everyone can recite the idealism, and the Querist gets waved through as a curiosity, but when you actually sit with the money theory it's strange. He asks "Whether the four elements, and man's labour therein, be not the true source of wealth?" and then a bit further on "whether the true idea of money, as such, be not altogether that of a ticket or counter?" — and he doesn't stop there, he says gold, silver and paper are all just tickets or counters for "reckoning, recording, and transferring," and that the denominations could be kept even if the bullion were gone. So you've got a labour theory of what wealth really is, a flatly conventionalist (basically fiat-friendly) theory of what money is, and on top of that he clearly wants to moralize — industrious poor, idle rich, drones, luxury and idleness. What I can't shake is whether these hang together. If "ticket" is the whole story about money, what is the ticket a claim on, and what stops "wealth" from collapsing into whatever a nation's prevailing will and fashion happen to make it? He says fashion creates appetites and the prevailing will determines the current of industry, which sounds like it could eat his own distinction between real industry and parasitic churn. And then there's the sign thing: this is the guy who says words don't always have to stand for ideas, and that nature itself is a language of signs, so "money is a ticket" is presumably a special case of some general sign-theoretic picture — but I can't tell whether it's the same sense of "sign" or a different one, or whether the ticket has to be redeemable in something that isn't itself a ticket.

So — I'm writing the take-home final and I want exactly one essay question that forces them to deal with this, rather than summarizing the idealism for the tenth time. Level is upper-level undergrad / MA; they've read the Principles Introduction, the Alciphron dialogue on language, and excerpts of the Querist, and they'll have the queries in front of them, so don't build the question on them recalling query numbers or hunting for citations. Please give me (1) the essay question itself, phrased so it can't be answered by reciting the standard material; (2) a model answer of about 700-800 words that a strong student would produce; and (3) a marking scheme out of 20, with the specific moves a top answer has to make and the specific confusions that should cost marks — I especially want the line that separates someone who just says "there's a tension between his conventionalism and his realism" from someone who can say where exactly it bites, and what resources Berkeley has, or lacks, to relieve it.
