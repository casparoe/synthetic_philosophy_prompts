I am a high school student working on a long paper about the principle of Reflection, which I have mostly seen associated with Bas van Fraassen, and I have ended up believing it in what I suspect is a stronger form than most people who mention it intend. As I understand it, the principle says that my present degree of confidence in any claim ought to equal my best guess about how confident I will be in that claim later, once I have thought about it more, provided I expect my later self to be at least as rational and at least as well informed as I am now. I have been treating this not merely as a consistency requirement but as the source of whatever authority my present opinions have at all: on my version, an opinion that I have good reason to think I would abandon after further honest inquiry is not something I am entitled to hold now, and the only opinions worth having are the ones that would survive that process. Please take it that I have the principle roughly right and tell me what would count against it.

I am not looking for a bibliography or a reading list, and I would rather not be handed a set of names to go look up, since I am trying to work out what I think rather than assemble other people's views. What I want is the actual material: the cases, arguments, and formal results that should move me, ranked by how decisive they would be, with the ones that would be fatal separated from the ones that are merely awkward, and with an honest account of which of them a defender of the principle would themselves concede count against it rather than dismiss as a misunderstanding. I am especially interested in whether the restriction I have built in, that the principle binds me only when I expect my later self to be better placed, makes the principle circular or leaves it with almost no content. I also want to know what the principle asks of me in a case where I know that deliberating will change my mind but not which way, and whether it can ever be rational to defer now to an opinion I know will be formed under conditions I currently regard as distorting, or simply after I have forgotten something I now know. Relatedly, if the principle can be derived from the idea that my confidence should not be expected to drift on its own, I would like to know whether that derivation quietly assumes something I should not accept, and whether the same reasoning pushes me toward deferring to other people in ways I would resist. Finally, I would like an honest verdict on whether the principle has been qualified so heavily that nothing could count against it, and if so, whether that is a defect or just what a norm of this kind looks like.

To be clear about what would actually persuade me: I would be most moved by a case in which the principle demands something I am confident is wrong, or by a demonstration that it quietly collapses into a rival view, and least moved by the observation that it is hard to apply.

Please write your answer as continuous prose, without headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, in the manner of a long and careful email. And please do not hedge by telling me that this is a large literature; I know that it is, and I am asking you to take a position on what actually matters.
