Before I commit to any position in the conciliationism–steadfastness dispute, I want the standard of adequacy itself laid out. Please write the desiderata that any adequate theory of how an agent should respond to disagreement with an epistemic peer must satisfy: the cases it must get right, the distinctions it must preserve, the background results it must not contradict, and, for each requirement, the consideration that motivates it. Treat the following as a working inventory and organize your answer around it, but tell me where I have smuggled in a requirement that is really just a prejudice of one camp.

On the cases the theory must handle, include at least these, and for each say what the theory owes us and why:

1. Two agents of equal competence and equal evidence perform the same calculation (a bill split, say), work carefully, and reach different answers; one of them is in fact right.
2. Two equally informed and equally careful parties disagree persistently on a contested question, with no prospect of new evidence — a standing disagreement rather than a one-off.
3. The agent faces not one dissenting peer but a majority of them.
4. The agent has reason to think the dissenting peer's judgment is not independent of her own — the peer reached his view by deferring to the same sources, or the two of them have been talking to each other and reinforcing a shared error.
5. The disagreement is about whether the other party is a peer at all: the agent suspects he lacks a competence or a body of evidence she has.
6. The disagreement concerns the correct theory of disagreement itself, so that any general prescription must also apply to the dispute it is meant to settle.

On the distinctions the theory must not collapse, I am thinking of: first-order evidence about the subject matter versus higher-order evidence about the reliability of one's own reasoning, and whether the peer's opinion enters as evidence at all or only as a reason to distrust one's own process; disagreement over a credence versus disagreement over a qualitative belief; the ideal peer who shares one's evidence and competence versus the ordinary peer one actually meets; what it is rational to believe versus what it is rational to assert or act on; and the difference between a theory of the rational response and a theory of the epistemically justified response. Say which of these are load-bearing and which can be dropped without loss.

On background constraints, I would like the theory kept consistent with the probability calculus and with conditionalization if it is credal, with whatever one says about whether evidence determines a unique rational credence or merely permits a range, and with the demand that it not be self-undermining — a prescription that, applied to the fact that philosophers disagree about the prescription, would undercut itself. Add any further constraints you think belong on the list.

Then do the two things I most want. First, mark each requirement as non-negotiable or as a preference of a particular camp, and identify any subset of the requirements you believe cannot be jointly satisfied — I am especially interested in whether the requirement that a peer's dissent always supplies genuine higher-order evidence can be held together with the requirement that confident belief about contested matters remain rational, and in the tension between treating the peer's opinion as independent evidence and requiring the agent to condition on her total evidence. Second, and briefly, score conciliationism, steadfastness, and one intermediate view of your choosing against the finished list. Keep the weight on the criteria and their motivating cases; the scoring is secondary.
