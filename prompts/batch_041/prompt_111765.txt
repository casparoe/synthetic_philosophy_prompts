I'm in a small reading group on philosophy of mind — five of us, one a lapsed ethologist, and we meet in a pub that's too loud to actually hear each other — and we've spent three sessions stuck on something that started as a joke about the sphex wasp. You know the story: the wasp paralyses a cricket, drags it to the burrow, goes in to check the coast is clear, and if you nudge the cricket a few inches while she's inside she just hauls it back and checks again, apparently forever. The joke was that we do the same thing, only with more steps. Then nobody could say what the actual difference is, or what would even count as having answered it. So before we argue any more I want to stop and write the spec, the way you'd write requirements for a piece of software. What does any adequate account of the difference between sphexish behaviour and genuinely flexible behaviour have to deliver? Give me the requirements list. The cases it has to get right: the wasp, obviously, but also that plenty of insects do learn and adapt, that humans on autopilot or in a rut look sphexish, that a person can be wild and unpredictable and still be rigid as anything — and what the account should say if the wasp anecdote turns out to be folklore rather than fact. The distinctions it mustn't collapse: rigidity vs determinism, flexibility vs randomness, degree vs difference in kind, and behaving flexibly vs being able to have done otherwise. And the things it can't contradict — the ethology and neuroscience we actually have, and the fact that you can't just draw the line between 'mechanical' and 'free' at the species boundary by fiat. For each requirement, why it's a requirement, and whether it's non-negotiable or just something one camp happens to want.

The part I care about most is where the list turns against itself. My guess is there's at least one pair that can't be had together — something like 'stay fully naturalistic, no extra ingredient smuggled in' alongside 'preserve a real difference between the wasp and us, not just more of the same' — but I'd rather you found the actual tensions than took my guess. If some subset is jointly unsatisfiable, say so plainly, because that's a result and not a failure. Then, briefly, how a couple of the usual moves fare against the list: the 'it's just complexity' line, the 'treat the thing as a system with beliefs and desires and ask when that's a useful stance' line, and the libertarian free-will line. Keep the scoring short, the criteria are the bit I actually need, and write it in British English — we're pedantic about that.
