I teach a final-year seminar on logical uncertainty -- the problem of assigning probabilities to claims you havent proven and can't check by brute force. I'm writing the in-class final and I want one essay question, 45 minutes, 20 marks. The trouble is my students can all recite "logical omniscience is unrealistic" and then they're stuck, so I want the question to actually make them work. Here's the setup I'd like it built around. One of my students proposes two principles for a reasoner: (P1) if you can actually derive A from your axioms, you may treat A as certain; (P2) your derivations are sound, so you may reason with the rule 'if I would derive A, then A' -- that is, treat your own provability as a guarantee of truth and not merely as evidence. Assume the reasoner's axioms are ordinary arithmetic and that it can reason about its own proofs (its language contains a predicate for 'I can derive...'). I want the question to get students to show that P1 and P2 together let the reasoner derive everything, contradiction included, and then to say which of the two is the culprit, what should replace it, and what the replacement costs. Please put a nudge toward a self-referential sentence in the question so it's doable in 45 minutes, but don't hand them the derivation.

Give me the question, a model answer of about 700 words, and a marking scheme with five must-hit points, the marks each is worth (totalling 20), and a one-line note on how students typically blow each one. On the culprit point especially, make the scheme reward the students who name the right principle over the ones who name the plausible-but-wrong one, and give marks for explaining why patching it by adding a consistency axiom just moves the problem along. They have no internet in the exam, so don't ask for names, papers or citations.
