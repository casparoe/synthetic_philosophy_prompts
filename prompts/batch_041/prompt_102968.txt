I am assembling a short verse piece for a reading group on the problem of choice without preference, and I want the form to carry the argument rather than merely illustrate it. Please write a pantoum — five quatrains, twenty lines — spoken by the will as Buridan conceived of it: the faculty that, finding two alternatives exactly equal, cannot break the deadlock and can do nothing but suspend judgement until the circumstances change. It has been standing between two identical bales for some time now, and has had the leisure to think about its position.

The form should do the real work. In a pantoum the second and fourth lines of each quatrain reappear as the first and third lines of the next, so every statement is re-heard in a changed context and the poem cannot advance by accumulation; arrange the closing quatrain so that the poem's opening line returns as its final line, closing the loop. The deadlock should register as a property of the form rather than a topic the poem mentions.

Inside that loop I want the counter-arguments to get a genuine hearing, because they are strong ones, and I would rather you set them out properly than gesture at them. First, that acting without a preference is not irrational: one can simply pick, and picking is not the same as choosing, so no failure of reason has occurred. Second, that a reason need not precede the act, since the act of committing to one option can itself generate the preference — this is the oldest reply to the puzzle, that a free creature has within it some quality capable of differentiating between two similar things. Third, that the whole two-bale set-up never actually arises, because the balance is always already broken by leanings too faint to be noticed, which is why nobody in real life is ever truly stuck. Give this last thought a structural tether: somewhere in the pantoum let two lines that the form requires to be identical differ by a single word, so that the smallest perceptible tilt is also the smallest textual one. But none of these should release the speaker. The poem must end inside the loop, unresolved, because that is honestly where the problem sits: the escape is available in theory and the form still will not take it.

Accuracy matters more than atmosphere. Someone who has thought about these replies should recognise the positions in the poem, and someone who has not should still find it a good poem. One further reply you may use if it helps, but without letting the piece become a history lesson: that the creature in this position would indeed die, and that this shows only that it should be called an ass rather than a man.

At the end, add a short prose note — a few sentences — stating where the poem takes liberties with the ideas, and naming the single claim it leans on that you are least confident about.

Write in British English.
