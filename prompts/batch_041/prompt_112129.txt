I am assembling a session for a jurisprudence seminar whose visiting speaker defends what she calls the Ulysses model of constitutionalism. On her view a constitution is a precommitment device: a people, foreseeing its own future weakness of will, binds itself in advance through entrenchment, judicial review, and supermajority amendment rules, much as Odysseus had himself lashed to the mast so as not to steer for the Sirens. She has two standing deflections. When pressed with the fact that later majorities will disagree with the entrenched settlement, she says that disagreement is not an objection to precommitment but precisely what precommitment is for. When pressed on one generation's authority over the next, she says that the people is a continuing agent rather than a succession of generations. I want a sequence of questions that makes those two moves progressively harder to use, and that otherwise presses her where the analogy is weakest.

Please draft nine to twelve questions in the order I should ask them, together with a short follow-up for each, keyed to the answer she is most likely to give, so that the follow-up is ready whichever way she goes. The questions should turn on the content of the position rather than on her influences or career. Please avoid anything that merely invites her to restate the view. The sequence should include, at a minimum, questions on the following:

- The crew. In the Odyssey Odysseus does not bind himself; the sailors bind him, and they are under instruction to ignore his later pleas and to tighten the ropes if he begs to be released. The binding is thus administered by others who are told to treat his earlier will as authoritative over his later one. Ask whether the Ulysses model is really a model of other-binding, and what that does to the claim that a democratic people binds only itself.
- What the precommitment is against. Odysseus was warned by Circe in advance exactly what he would meet, and the Sirens' offer is not merely sensual: they promise to tell of the toils of the Achaeans and Trojans at Troy and of all that comes to pass on the earth, so the temptation he guards against is in part a temptation to know. Constitutions, by contrast, are framed in ignorance of the circumstances they will govern. Ask whether the story models weakness of will or the correction of ignorance, whether the two can be run together under one heading, and what becomes of the model if the later majority is not weak-willed but simply better informed.
- Reversibility. The ropes on the mast cannot be untied by the man they hold, whereas a constitutional commitment can be undone by the very procedure it constrains, if enough people agree. Press her on whether a supermajority requirement is a genuine binding or only a tax on change, and be ready for the answer that raising the cost of change is exactly the point.
- Elster's own later concession, in his second book on the subject, that constitutions are frequently devices for binding others — future majorities, successor governments — rather than their authors. Ask whether that concession demotes the Ulysses story from a description of constitutionalism to a decoration on it.
- Who the binding self is. The demos, the framers, the ratifying generation, and the present majority are not the same agent, and the countermajoritarian difficulty is this problem in its legal dress. Ask her to name the self. If she answers that it is the people as a continuing agent, press on what makes that agent's earlier act authoritative for its later ones.
- Bootstrapping. A precommitment binds the later self only if the later self already grants the earlier self the authority to bind it, which is the very point in dispute. Ask what performs the granting, and whether the answer is not circular.
- Which device is doing the explanatory work. A constitution may be less a self-lashing than a focal point that lets many agents settle on one of several equilibria, in which case entrenchment does a coordinating job that has nothing to do with weakness of will. Ask her to distinguish the precommitment reading from the coordination reading, and to say what evidence would show that precommitment is the better diagnosis.
- The conditions under which the original commitment was made. A precommitment is only as good as the self that makes it; a constitution adopted by an unrepresentative or coerced assembly entrenches the advantage of a faction rather than the sober judgment of a people. Ask whether the model can distinguish an Odysseus who binds himself against a known temptation from an Odysseus who is bound while asleep.

End the sequence with a question about what would count as refutation rather than mere imperfection, and give the follow-up for that one too.

Finish your reply with a single line of exactly the form \answer{...}, replacing the ellipsis with a three-to-six-word label naming the one question in your list most likely to force her to abandon a stated commitment rather than deflect.
