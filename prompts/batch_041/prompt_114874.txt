Two philosophers have argued for a year about whether agent-centered deontological restrictions — constraints that forbid an agent to do certain things even when doing them would prevent more of the same — are rationally defensible, and their argument keeps circling one charge: that such restrictions are either self-defeating or arbitrary. Draft the joint statement their adversarial collaboration would produce: the document both could sign.

The parties. (A) A deontologist who accepts agent-centered restrictions, denies that they reduce to rules of thumb, and holds that they can be given a rationale that survives the charge. (B) A critic who treats the charge as a reductio: on A's own account of why the forbidden act matters, the restriction cannot be a requirement of practical reason.

The charge to be assessed. Put it in its strongest form; use the following reconstruction as a starting point and revise it if you can improve it. (1) A restriction on acting in a certain way must have its rationale in the badness of the act or of the states of affairs it produces. (2) If the restriction's rationale lies there, then in a case where one such act would prevent several other agents from performing acts of the same kind, performing it must be at least permissible — the very disvalue that justifies the restriction must be able to defeat it. (3) The deontologist denies this. (4) Hence the deontologist is caught: either the restriction has no rationale in the badness of the act, or it forbids minimizing that badness. The statement must say whether this is one argument or several, which premise the deontologist should reject, and whether any premise is question-begging.

Two stipulated cases the statement must handle, both fixed by stipulation rather than by appeal to any real event:
(i) The multiplier. It is a stipulated causal fact that if you act in the forbidden way once, no one else ever will; if you refrain, five other agents will each do it once, and their doing so neither depends on your example nor is deterred by anything you do. State what each position implies here, and say whether the charge's force depends on the case being realistically possible.
(ii) The never-executed threat. A justified policy requires threatening the forbidden act; the policy works precisely because the threat is never carried out and no one ever performs the act. Say whether this is the same puzzle as (i) or only adjacent to it, and if adjacent, what exactly the difference is.

Two further questions must be settled in the document. First, thresholds: does A's restriction admit a threshold beyond which the act becomes permissible? If it does, does the charge return at the threshold, so that the restriction is an expensive rule of thumb rather than a restriction? If it does not, what is A's answer to the charge of absurdity, and does that answer require denying that agent-neutral considerations carry any weight? Second, rationales: sort the candidates — the act's badness, respect for persons or the separateness of agents, and the effects of holding the practice — and say which of them the charge actually targets and whether it retains any force against the others. The parties should also assess, without either having to endorse it, a division-of-standpoints proposal: that the agent-relative question of what I may do and the agent-neutral question of which outcome is better have different answers, and that this is not a contradiction but a division of practical reason. If they can agree the proposal is worth stating, they must say whether it resolves the charge, relocates it, or restates it.

Deliverable, in this order:
1. Agreed claims, numbered: the statement of the charge both accept as the target; what the charge does and does not establish; the cases both accept as relevant; and what both would count as a satisfactory resolution.
2. A shared lexicon: definitions both accept of restriction, agent-relative reason, agent-neutral reason, prerogative, threshold, and residue — each written so that the party who does not rely on the term still accepts the definition.
3. Disagreements, in a table: each disputed claim stated in the wording its own side would accept, the other side's response in the same form, and whether the disagreement survives translation into the other side's vocabulary.
4. Cruxes, numbered: the sub-question; why settling it would move at least one side; the argument or case both agree bears on it; each side's prediction or verdict, recorded in advance; and whether the crux is conceptual or empirical.
5. Verbal disputes: which parts of the disagreement, if any, are about words, and what translation dissolves them.
6. Concession conditions: for each side, what would make it abandon or substantially revise its position.

Do not paper over residual disagreement: where the parties cannot agree on a formulation, record the best formulation each would sign and mark the item unresolved. No citations, no bibliography, no names of authors; the statement must stand on the arguments as given. Keep it to roughly 1,500–2,000 words.
