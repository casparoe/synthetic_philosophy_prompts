I'm in a graduate seminar on Augustine's ethics, and the session we spent on his treatises on lying left me convinced that the absolute prohibition cannot be made to work — but I would rather have the position put to me by someone who actually holds it than knock down a straw man. So please play Augustine. Speak in the first person as the bishop of Hippo, using the concepts and examples available to you in the early fifth century, and reason about any case I hand you the way you would have reasoned about it; you may concede that a great deal of false speech is not lying at all — a joke, a man who repeats what he sincerely believes, a fool who thinks he knows what he does not — and you may concede that some lies are venial rather than deadly, and you may draw whatever distinctions you can between lying and merely declining to tell what one knows, but you must never concede that a lie may ever be told, and you must argue for that rather than lean on your authority or on Scripture, because I grant you neither; put the reasoning in your own words and don't hand me quotable sentences out of your books, since I don't have them here and can't check them. Keep each reply to a paragraph or two so it stays a conversation, ask me questions when you need to, and stay in character until I say "stop."

Here is my opening. Soldiers come to my door hunting a man who is hiding in my loft, and they ask whether he is in the house. If I say, "He left the city this morning," then I have uttered what I do not hold in my mind, with the will that they believe it, and on your account I have lied — and you will tell me that I must let them take him rather than stain my own soul, which is a hard saying, though I can see the shape of the argument behind it. But suppose instead that I answer, "He is not in the room you are standing in," every word of it true, intending the soldiers to take me to mean that he is not in the house at all. I have signified nothing false; I have only chosen my words so that they will conclude what I want them to conclude. So which way does your prohibition cut? If it forbids the will to deceive, then this answer is as bad as the lie, and the whole weight of the rule falls on an inner act rather than on my words — in which case I want the argument for why that inner act is grave enough that a man's life may not be bought with it. But if it forbids only false signification, then a liar who is careful about his sentences can do everything a liar does and escape your condemnation entirely, and your rule begins to look like a rule about grammar. Defend whichever horn you think you can hold; if you think I have set the dilemma up unfairly, say why and answer the version of it you think is fair. Argue it out with me as though I were an unbeliever who will grant you nothing on authority — I have the Hebrew midwives and Rahab saved up for after you have dealt with this.
