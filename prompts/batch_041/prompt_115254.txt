I am a doctoral candidate in philosophy of psychiatry, and I sit as the trainee member of a national psychiatric association's working group preparing the next revision of our diagnostic manual. Before Thursday's meeting I have to circulate a short written recommendation, and I would like you to draft it. The single agenda item is a proposal from the adolescent-mood subgroup to introduce a new diagnosis -- call it chronic adolescent affective dysregulation (CAAD) -- for young people with two years or more of fluctuating irritability, reactive low mood, and unstable self-image, which the subgroup insists is distinct from our existing mood and personality categories. Their four-site pilot found that once the provisional label entered routine use, recorded prevalence roughly tripled in eighteen months, the median age at first diagnosis fell by about two years, and clinicians began reporting that adolescents arrived already fluent in the label's vocabulary and contested diagnoses they did not receive. The subgroup reads this as overdue recognition of a real condition. Our adult-personality lead reads it as a fashionable label colonizing the clinic, and has circulated a note arguing that Hacking's work on looping effects shows the category to be manufactured rather than discovered. I think that note misreads Hacking in a way that matters for what we decide.

Please write the memo. Give a bottom line -- adopt, adopt with conditions, or defer -- and if you attach conditions, make them specific enough to vote on, including whether to require a scheduled re-review with drift data and on what interval. Engage the looping-effect issue on its merits rather than treating it as either decisive or irrelevant: say what looping effects do and do not show about a psychiatric category's validity, whether the drift in the pilot counts against CAAD or is instead what any successfully communicated diagnostic vocabulary would look like, what follows for a category whose presentation is still moving, and how our own decision functions as part of the loop rather than standing outside it. Correct the misreading if you think it is one, and state the strongest case for the position you do not adopt. No references are needed; the group knows the literature. Finally, tell me how confident you are in each main claim, and single out the point you are least sure of.
