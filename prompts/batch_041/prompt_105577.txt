For a reading group I'm running on Maxwell's demon and the thermodynamics of information, I've been drafted to write the specification we'll grade every proposed resolution against, and I want to get the specification right before anyone starts defending a favorite. Please write the list of desiderata that any adequate account of the demon — of what it can and cannot do, and why — has to satisfy. Fix the purpose this way: the account should be strong enough to tell us whether the second law is a statistical regularity that a sufficiently clever agent could in principle beat or an absolute constraint no agent can beat, and precise enough that an experimentalist could read off what to measure in order to test it. For each requirement, give the case or consideration that forces it, and mark which requirements are non-negotiable and which are demands of a particular camp rather than of the subject matter itself.

I want the list to do justice to at least the following, which came out of our first meeting and which I suspect pull in different directions: the original box of gas, where a being sorts fast molecules from slow ones; the one-molecule engine, where a single particle's side is detected and a piston is lowered; a "lazy demon" one of us proposed, which measures the gas but never does anything with the reading and claims the work for free; a relay of demons, each handing its record to the next so that the erasure seems to happen somewhere else; a demon whose memory is a closed quantum system that never needs resetting; and the ordinary case of a real computer that measures, stores, and later overwrites its bits. The list should also say which distinctions a good account cannot collapse — measurement versus erasure, the information the demon has versus the information it stores, logical reversibility versus thermodynamic reversibility, the entropy of a probability distribution versus the entropy of a physical system, the demon's knowledge versus the demon's memory — and which established results it cannot contradict: the second law in its statistical form, the erasure cost of overwriting a bit, and the fluctuation theorems that let small systems transiently buck the average. One of us keeps insisting that no amount of logical bookkeeping can yield a law of physics and that the whole memory-cost story is a cheat; the list has to make clear what would be lost if he were right.

Please give it to me in three labeled parts. Part 1, "The Requirements," should be the bulk of the answer — a numbered list in which each item states the requirement in a sentence or two and then justifies it in a short paragraph. Part 2, "Tensions," should name the conflicts among the requirements and take a position on which requirement gives way, roughly a paragraph per conflict. Part 3, "Scorecard," should be brief — no more than a few lines per approach — on how the main existing lines (the memory-erasure line, the reversible-computing line, and the statistical-mechanics line) fare against the list. Write it in American English, and keep it usable by students who have no references in front of them.
