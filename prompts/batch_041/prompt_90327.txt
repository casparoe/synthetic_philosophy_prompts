I'm in a second-year micro seminar and we spent the better part of three weeks on iterated elimination of dominated strategies, and I still can't tell whether the argument we kept having is a real disagreement or people talking past each other. Here's the shape of it. One camp treats the whole thing as a mathematical fact about the game: if a strategy is dominated -- and it can be dominated only by a mixture of your other strategies rather than by any single one of them, my favorite toy case is a 3x3 where the bottom row is strictly worse than an equal coin-flip between the top two rows even though neither top row beats it on its own -- then no rational player with beliefs about the opponent would pick it, so cross it out, repeat, and whatever survives is the answer. The other camp insists the crossing-out is modeling reasoning: each round is a layer of knowledge, I know you're rational, you know I'm rational, and the fixed point of that iteration is what the solution concept really encodes. The trouble is that the two stories come apart exactly where it gets interesting. If we only ever delete strictly dominated pure strategies, the order of deletion doesn't matter and the stories at least seem to line up. Once mixtures enter the picture, and especially once anyone reaches for weak dominance, order starts to matter, and the knowledge story seems to need a notion of rationality stronger than just "maximize expected utility" at every step. So one side ends up defending a theorem about sets of strategies, the other a claim about what players can be assumed to know, and both think they're discussing the same object.

What I want is a diagnosis, not a ruling on who's right. Is this substantive, verbal, or two groups answering different questions under one name -- one about what a player may choose given her beliefs, one about what an outsider can prove about her from the structure of the game? Is there a hidden formal question doing the work, some characterization result that would tell us when the algorithmic and epistemic stories coincide and when they can't? And is the impasse a sign that "solution concept" is carrying more weight than it can, or just that this stuff is genuinely hard? My own suspicion, which I'd like you to attack rather than adopt, is that the standoff is kept alive by an assumption both camps share: that the opponent is an expected-utility maximizer with a definite prior. If you weaken that -- assume only that she won't play something that's bad for her no matter what -- does iterated elimination survive as a procedure at all, or does it collapse into something much thinner? If it survives, the epistemic camp looks right that elimination is about knowledge; if it doesn't, the mathematical camp looks right that we were never doing epistemology, just filtering.

So: give me the diagnosis with reasons, grounded in what each side actually says, and tell me what would count as progress given it -- a distinction, a theorem, a change of question -- or say honestly that no progress should be expected here and why. And please separate the parts of this that are settled from the parts that are genuinely contested among people who work on it; I don't want a contested reading of what's going on handed to me as if it were the consensus.
