And here's what the student wrote:

Both accounts agree that modal claims are evaluated with respect to possible worlds; the disagreement is about what happens to the individual. On the Kripkean view, Humphrey is a transworld individual: the very same man who lost in 1968 exists in other possible worlds, and the claim that he might have won is true because there is a world at which that individual has the property of winning. Since identity is strict and necessary, whatever is true of Humphrey at another world is true of him simpliciter, so the view needs an account of which of his properties he could not have lacked — his essence. Kripke holds that the essence of a person includes things like their origin and their being human.

Lewis denies that anything exists in more than one world. If Humphrey himself were wholly present both in this world and in a world where he won, he would have incompatible intrinsic properties, which is the problem of accidental intrinsics. So on Lewis's view, when we say Humphrey might have won, what we are saying is that some other world contains a counterpart of Humphrey — an individual who resembles him closely enough — and that counterpart won. The counterpart relation is a relation of similarity rather than identity; it is not transitive, and an individual can have more than one counterpart. Because Lewis is a modal realist about possible worlds, holding that they are concrete entities of the same kind as this one, counterpart theory is the piece of the package that handles de re modality; you cannot really have the counterpart theory without the modal realism.

The Humphrey objection, which comes from Kripke, is that this changes the subject. We wanted to say something about Humphrey — that he himself could have won — but the counterpart analysis makes the winning be done by somebody else. The counterpart is not Humphrey, so the analysans is about a different individual than the analysandum. Kripke's own response to the problem is to insist that we need a principled criterion for matching individuals across worlds, and he locates it in the individual's essence, which he thinks we grasp a priori; this is why he can say that Humphrey could not have failed to be human but could have failed to win.

Lewis replies that the objection begs the question. It assumes that a modal claim is only about Humphrey if Humphrey himself is part of the other world, which is precisely what is at issue. He maintains that the claim that Humphrey might have won is about Humphrey: the counterpart relation is a relation that Humphrey bears to the winner in the other world, and whether the claim is true depends on how similar Humphrey is to that winner. Since the objection only has force if you already accept transworld identity, it is not an independent reason to reject counterpart theory. I think this reply is reasonably strong, though it does leave the counterpart relation looking a bit mysterious, since Lewis says it is a matter of overall similarity without being able to say much about how the relevant respects and weightings are fixed.
