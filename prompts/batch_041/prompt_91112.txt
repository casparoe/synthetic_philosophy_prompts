I have a set of verdicts about weakness of will, and I want the principle behind them, if there is one. A woman who judges at six that she should go to the gym, still judges so at six, and stays on the couch: weak-willed, plainly. A man who planned to spend the afternoon on his thesis, sincerely reconsiders, decides the walk is the better use of the time, and walks: not weak-willed, though he gave up on the plan. A woman who judges at nine that she should sleep, still judges so at midnight, and keeps scrolling: weak-willed, and the repetition makes it worse. A man who had resolved to quit smoking, who calmly and sincerely judges at noon that one cigarette now would be fine, who smokes it and is disappointed in himself afterwards: not weak-willed, and I am confident of that, but I cannot say what the disappointment is tracking. A man who disables the site blocker he installed last week so that he can keep scrolling: weak-willed, and worse than the third case. Someone in a depressive episode who judges that he ought to get out of bed and does not: not weak-willed, because that judgment is not fully his. Someone whose judgment that he ought not to eat the cake is inherited from a childhood of being told that pleasure is sin, and who eats it anyway: weak-willed, though I am uneasy about it. And someone offered $100 tomorrow or $110 in two days who takes the $100, though yesterday he would have chosen the $110: not weak-willed at all, since at no point did he act against the judgment he then held. My tentative proposal is that weakness of will is nothing but present bias, and that a quasi-hyperbolic discounting model will handle the whole list. Test that against the cases, tell me which of my verdicts it forces me to give up, and give me the best principle that survives. If the list cannot be unified, or if my talk of a judgment's being "fully his" is doing work that no principle can underwrite, say so plainly rather than patching it over.
