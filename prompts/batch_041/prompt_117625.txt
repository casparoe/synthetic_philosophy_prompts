I'm a retired soil chemist — forty years of measuring nitrate leaching out of farm fields — and since I hung up the lab coat I've been working my way into moral philosophy, which is a lot harder than it looks from the outside. Something's been nagging at me. My old climbing club has split in half over whether an individual has any business feeling guilty about flying to the Alps, eating beef, or (in the case of one very political member) voting, given that none of those acts changes any outcome anyone can point to: the flight takes off whether or not I'm in seat 23B, the steer is already dead by the time I order, my biking to the polls doesn't move the count in my state. Half of them say this is just an excuse, half say that "one person can't make a difference" is a perfectly good reason not to have the obligation in the first place, and nobody can say why. I've noticed the same fight breaks out over all three cases and that the disputants seem to be answering different questions without noticing.

So what I'd like, if you don't mind, is a decision tree I can fit on one page and hand round at the next meeting — a "which position do you actually hold?" flowchart, not an essay. Root it at the thing everyone in the room already agrees to: my individual act, by itself, makes no detectable difference to the outcome. Each node should be a question the reader can answer without already knowing where it leads — I don't want a tree whose second question is "are you a consequentialist?", I want questions like whether the relevant unit is the particular act or the act-type, whether the difference has to be detectable in order to count, whether it matters that my seat gets filled by someone else if I stay home (and whether that's equally true of the vote and the steak — my hunch is the three cases come apart right there, and I'd like the tree to show me where), whether the obligation lands on me as an individual or on us as a group and then gets distributed down to me, whether the objection is really about a threshold that somebody's going to cross regardless of what I do, and whether the reason to act is about outcomes at all rather than about what the act expresses or what kind of person one wants to be. Keep it to twelve nodes at most — no, twelve total, it has to survive a photocopier and a windbreak — and make the leaves the positions themselves, named where there's a standard name for one and marked "unnamed" where there isn't, each with a single sentence on its most awkward commitment.

Two more things. Flag any two leaves your tree can't genuinely separate; I suspect a couple of them return identical verdicts on all three cases and differ only in their story about blame, and I'd rather you say so than paper over it. And tell me which combinations of answers nobody seems to occupy, with your best guess as to whether that's because the combination is incoherent, unattractive, or just unvisited. Please don't hand me the usual menu of "complicity, collective action, thresholds, virtue" as headers with nothing underneath — the whole value here is in the diagnostic questions themselfs, so make them concrete enough that my climbing partner, a hydrologist who thinks supererogation is a kind of rock formation, can tell which branch he's on without me translating.
