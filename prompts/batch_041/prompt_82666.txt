I am a novelist, and I am in the last stages of a book whose central character, Miriam, is a philosopher of mind. The final third of the novel is organised around a graduate seminar she teaches — specifically, around the examination she sets and then marks. The paper itself appears in the text, and her expectations for it surface as interior monologue while she grades. I want the philosophy in these pages to be real rather than decorative, and I would sooner it be genuinely difficult than plausibly generic. What I need from you is the examination question itself, the model answer Miriam would have written had she sat her own paper, and the marking scheme she carries in her head.

The setting: a graduate seminar, twelve students, one three-hour written paper, closed book, one essay question with sub-parts. No citations are expected or permitted; the students are to reason, not to report. (The novel is set in a period when the seminar room has no network access, and in any case I do not want a page to turn on anyone's bibliography.) The question should be self-contained: a student who attended none of the lectures but read the scenario carefully should be able to reason her way to a defensible answer.

Here is the material I want the question built on.

The familiar case: a subject undergoes a procedure in which her neurons are replaced one at a time, each with a silicon device occupying the same functional role in the same network. Throughout she reports nothing unusual; conversation, memory and behaviour are unimpaired. It is often argued that this is a case of survival — that a fading of experience would have to be noticeable from the inside, and that a system with the same fine-grained functional organisation will have the same experiences.

The twist I have invented for the novel: the excised neurons are not destroyed. They are kept alive and, in parallel, reassembled into the same network configuration, so that when the last neuron has been swapped out there exist two systems — the original body, running on silicon and continuously active from the start, and a vat containing the original biological tissue, wired into the same functional organisation but switched on only at the end. Two further variants occur to me, and I want the paper to force students to distinguish them: one in which the vat is kept continuously active throughout, so that there are two streams of experience rather than one; and one in which the vat is switched on for a single second in every hour, so that its activity is intermittent.

What the question must do. It should oblige a student to say which of the end-state systems, if either, is the person, and — this is the part I care about — to make the criterion do the work rather than recite it. It should compel a distinction between continuity of matter, continuity of occurrent mental life, and continuity of organisation, and should let the student discover that the vat case puts pressure on any criterion generous enough to license both candidates. It should reach the level-of-grain problem: what it would mean for the silicon devices to reproduce the organisation exactly, and whether the criterion can be stated at a grain fine enough to be plausible without collapsing into a restatement of physical identity. And it should force the student to confront the fact that gradualness seems to be doing the work in the original case — the all-at-once version of the procedure is widely taken to be death — so she must say what the one-at-a-time version preserves that the one-step version does not, and whether that difference can bear the weight placed on it.

Deliverables. First, the examination question with its sub-parts, written as it would appear on the paper. Second, a model answer of about 900 words in continuous prose, of the standard Miriam would expect from the best student in the room, and requiring no citations. Third, a marking scheme worth 30 marks, itemising the specific moves a first-class answer must make, the moves that earn partial credit, and a short list of errors that should cost marks — including at least one error that a strong student is likely to commit, not only the confusions of a weak one. Fourth, two or three sentences on what separates an A answer from a B answer on this particular question.
