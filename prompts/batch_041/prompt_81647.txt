I cover science and ideas for a general-audience monthly, and for the past year I have also chaired the seven-member editorial board of our sister quarterly. Our job is to sign off on the magazine's unsigned position pieces — the ones that speak in the first person plural — so every position we take is formally the view of all seven of us, even when it plainly isn't.

At the start of the year we adopted a rule to keep those positions honest: we would call on a regulator to withdraw an approval only if we judged both that the underlying evidence was too weak to support it and that the approval process itself had been defective. Call the three claims (a) the evidence is too weak, (b) the process was defective, and (c) the approval should be withdrawn. Our rule makes (c) follow from (a) and (b) together.

Last month's slate concerned a supplement that a national regulator cleared two years ago and that a group of researchers now say was oversold. We voted on (a), (b) and (c) separately, by simple majority, with individual votes recorded and published in the minutes. The tallies were:

(a) 5–2 in favour
(b) 4–3 in favour
(c) 2–5 against

All seven of us voted consistently. The two who voted for (c) voted for both (a) and (b); the five who voted against (c) each rejected at least one of the two premises. And yet the magazine has now published the claim that the evidence is too weak, that the process was defective, and that the approval should nonetheless stand. A reader wrote in to say this is nonsense, and she is right. Worse, we cannot say which of the seven holds the position the magazine now holds, because none of them does.

Two fixes are on the table, and they do not agree with each other. Marcus wants us to vote on the bottom line first: put (c) to a straight vote and let (a) and (b) be whatever they have to be for (c) to come out the way the majority wants. On last month's numbers that yields a clean, coherent position — no call for withdrawal, and no claims at all about the evidence or the process. Priya wants the reverse: vote on (a) and (b), which are the things we actually have views about, and let (c) follow mechanically from the rule we already adopted. That also yields a clean, coherent position, and it is the opposite one: call for withdrawal, on the strength of the majority's view of the evidence and the process, even though a majority of us voted against withdrawal when asked directly.

So the two procedures give opposite answers from identical ballots. I keep looking for a tiebreaker and finding that each procedure is defensible in terms of something I also care about: Marcus's respects what the group actually wants to say, Priya's respects what the group actually thinks.

Three other proposals are floating. Kenji wants individual votes off the record, on the grounds that we currently vote for the reader rather than our own judgement, and that the minutes have turned us into performers; I dislike this, partly because the published minutes are our selling point and readers use them to hold us to account. Sofia wants the chair — me — to decide what goes on the slate in the first place, so that we never vote on a combination that can come apart like this; I notice that this hands me the outcome. And there is a fourth wrinkle: two members have outside ties, one having advised the regulator and one having done paid work for the supplement's maker. Excluding them would change the tallies and might, by luck, make the package coherent again, which strikes me as the wrong reason to do it.

One further complication: one of us leaves at the end of the month, and her replacement is known to think the evidence is fine and the process was fine. She will vote that way. On those numbers (a) and (b) would both fail, (c) would fail, and the incoherence would vanish without our having fixed anything.

What I need from you is a decision, not a reading list. Please don't cite anything — I can't check it, and I don't want to lean on authorities I can't see. I want to understand the shape of the problem and then be told what to do at Thursday's meeting.

Please structure your answer as follows.

1. What the tally actually shows (about 150 words). Explain in plain terms what went wrong and why every individual vote was nonetheless reasonable.

2. Premises first, or bottom line first (about 350 words). Lay out the two fixes, what each commits the magazine to asserting, which you would choose, and why. If the choice depends on something I haven't told you — what the magazine is for, or what our readers are entitled to — say so explicitly and tell me how the answer changes.

3. The other three proposals (about 250 words). One short paragraph each on the secret ballot, on chair-controlled agendas, and on recusing members with outside ties. Give me a verdict on each, and be honest where the verdict is that the proposal fixes the optics rather than the problem.

4. My recommendation, and the wording to propose (about 250 words). The standing rule I should put to the board, written out as the single sentence I would read aloud, plus how I should explain last month's contradiction to readers.

5. What would change my mind (about 100 words). The failure mode of your recommended rule, and the evidence that would tell me it is failing.
