I have no academic training in philosophy or psychology; I came to this by watching a film and then chasing down what it reminded me of, and the problem has been with me since. Before I ask for the reading I want, let me set out the problem, because I suspect it is not common knowledge and I do not want to presume you already have it.

There is a perceptual illusion involving two dots. A blue dot is flashed near the top of a screen, and a fraction of a second later a red dot is flashed near the bottom. Most viewers report seeing a single dot travel between the two positions and turn from blue to red about halfway along the path. What is strange is that the reported color change seems to occur before the second dot has appeared, as if the experience had somehow been assembled using knowledge of an event that had not yet happened. There are two broad ways to make sense of this, and Daniel Dennett named them after two styles of political revision. On the Orwellian account, the experience happens when it happens, but the memory of it is doctored afterwards, so that what you later recall is a tidied version that never actually took place — the record is rewritten after the fact, as in Orwell's Nineteen Eighty-Four. On the Stalinesque account, nothing is doctored after the fact because nothing is ever experienced in a form that would need doctoring: the first event is simply held back, and the brain presents one already-consistent show, the way a show trial has its evidence arranged in advance. Dennett's disquieting suggestion is that the two accounts predict the very same reports, so there may be no fact of the matter as to which is right, and asking when an experience happened may be asking the wrong kind of question.

Now the film. Eternal Sunshine of the Spotless Mind (2004) is about two people who hire a firm to erase each other from their memories, and a great deal of it takes place inside one of them, Joel, while the erasure is being carried out. I would like you to read the film through the problem above, in both directions — the film should be allowed to illuminate the problem as much as the problem illuminates the film.

Specifically: does the film commit to either explanation, or does it deliberately keep them indistinguishable? Which scenes should I be looking at — the sequence where Joel tries to hide his memories of Clementine inside memories that have nothing to do with her, the collapsing beach house at Montauk, the morning after, or the ending, where the two of them listen to their own erased tapes and decide to try again anyway? I am particularly struck by the fact that the film gives us the one thing the theory says we can never have: a first-person view of a memory being revised in the moment. Is that a genuine insight the film is dramatizing, or does it trade on a picture of a self that persists underneath the record — a witness to its own erasure — which is precisely what the two explanations agree cannot exist?

And where does the film resist the reading? I have a suspicion that its emotional force depends on a continuous self whose record can be damaged but not destroyed, and that this is a commitment the philosophy does not share. If that is right, say so plainly, and tell me what the film adds to the debate that the argument alone cannot — if anything.
