Write the desiderata that any adequate theory of why pain is bad must satisfy. The theory has to say what makes bodily pain bad for the one who feels it — and whether that badness is intrinsic to the sensation or borrowed from something else — so organize the list around the phenomena it must accommodate rather than the labels of the camps. Those phenomena should include at least: ordinary pain that hurts and is avoided; pain asymbolia, in which a patient reports pain yet denies that it bothers them and shows little avoidance; pain that is informative or merely unwanted without being bad; pain that is sought or welcomed; the way pain's motivational pull can come apart from its felt awfulness; and the demand that the account not make the badness of pain depend on capacities a newborn or a nonhuman animal lacks. Add to these a constructed case of your own devising in which a subject reports pain, denies disliking it, and yet still flinches and withdraws when undistracted, and a mirror case in which a subject says the pain is unbearable while showing no avoidance — say what each is meant to probe. For every desideratum, give the case or consideration that forces it and mark whether it is non-negotiable or an optional commitment of one camp. Then flag any pair you believe cannot be jointly satisfied, and identify which single requirement does the most to rule out the popular contenders (hedonic, desire-based, evaluative or imperative, and perceptual accounts). Keep the weight on the criteria and their motivating cases; a brief scorecard at the end is sufficient.
