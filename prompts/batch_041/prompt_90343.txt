I'm moderating a public conversation next month with a political philosopher whose forthcoming book draws a sharp line between guilt and liability in debates about historical injustice. Her thesis, roughly, is that nations can and do inherit liabilities across generations — standing, forward-looking burdens of repair that attach to whoever occupies a given role or institution — but that they can never inherit guilt, because guilt is a backward-looking status presupposing a first-person relation to a deed, whereas liability is a forward-looking burden that need not track desert. On her reading, Jaspers' fourfold scheme of criminal, political, moral, and metaphysical guilt goes wrong precisely by running these two things together: what Jaspers calls political guilt is really inherited liability dressed up as guilt, while moral and metaphysical guilt are the only genuine kinds of guilt and are strictly non-transmissible. She is a careful interlocutor and quite good at conceding the easy objections early so that they can't be used against her later, which is exactly why I don't want the conversation to become an advertisement for the view.

Please draft eight to ten interview questions that interrogate the position rather than let her restate it. I want at least one question each on: the asymmetry argument, namely that nations routinely take pride in their ancestors' achievements, so if desert-adjacent attitudes transmit across generations in the credit direction, what principled reason blocks their transmission in the blame direction; the bearer problem, i.e. whether a liability attaching to a role attaches to the collective as such or distributes over the individuals who currently staff it, and what follows for those who dissent or emigrate; the non-identity point that later generations owe their very existence to the wrong in question, and whether that changes what they owe; the successor-corporation analogy she leans on, together with its limits, especially the case of a genuinely discontinuous polity that has broken with its predecessor by revolution or a new constitution, where I'd like to know what grounds the surviving liability if not continuity of guilt; whether Jaspersian metaphysical guilt can be collective at all, given how tightly he ties it to individual conscience and solidarity; and the apology problem, since official apologies seem to presuppose precisely the inherited guilt she denies, so I want to know whether she is committed to their being necessarily insincere or merely expressive. For each of those, add a one-line follow-up keyed to the answer she is most likely to give — including, in the pride case, the reply that pride is also a category error and so no asymmetry arises, and, in the apology case, the reply that apologies can function as undertakings rather than admissions. Close with one question about what evidence or argument could count against her separation of guilt from liability, and what she would have to concede if the political-guilt category turned out to be doing work her liability frame cannot capture. No questions about her intellectual biography or what drew her to the topic.
