I'm a third-year PhD student in philosophy of biology, and I keep running into a distinction I can state but not really defend. Daniel Dennett distinguishes cranes from skyhooks in the context of evolutionary explanation, and separately warns against greedy reductionism. I can recite the slogans, but I want the actual conceptual machinery rather than the slogans.

Please explain, at a level pitched to someone who already accepts the basic Darwinian picture, precisely what work the crane/skyhook distinction is doing. What makes something a crane rather than a skyhook? Why can a crane count as a legitimate explanation even when the mechanism it invokes is itself the product of further cranes? And does the regress of cranes terminate in anything that would qualify as a skyhook, or is the whole point that it cannot? Then explain how greedy reductionism differs from the kind of reduction Dennett would accept, and locate the line between explaining a higher-level pattern in terms of lower-level mechanisms and explaining it away.

I'd like the two halves connected rather than treated as separate topics. My suspicion is that the same error is at work in a skyhook and in a greedy reduction — that both are ways of declining to do the constructive work of showing how the thing gets built — and I want to know whether that suspicion holds up, or whether the two failures are actually quite different in kind.

Finally, I have a case I cannot classify, and it is the real reason I am asking. Suppose someone explains a biological trait by appeal to natural selection, but treats the selective environment — the predators, the climate, the available niches — as an uncaused given, never asking how that environment came to have the shape it does. Is the unexplained environment functioning as a skyhook, or is it merely an unanalyzed background condition, which every explanation requires? Dennett seems to want to insist that the environment is itself the product of earlier cranes, but I cannot tell whether that is an empirical claim, a regulative ideal, or a definitional move dressed up as one. I would like you to say which reading is right and what turns on it.
