Im putting together a closing piece for a reading group that just spent a term on Frankfurt cases, and I want to end with a pantoum (quatrains where the 2nd and 4th lines come back as the 1st and 3rd of the next one). Youre a poet who also knows the free will literature cold, so dont flatten it. Refresher so we're aligned: in the standard case the intervener Black is ready to step in if Jones shows any sign of not doing the deed, and never has to, because Jones does it on his own — so Jones looks responsible even though he couldn't have done otherwise. What we argued about all term: the flicker of freedom (maybe Jones could still have tried, or started to decide, otherwise, so some thin alternative survives), and the dilemma that either his decision was already settled by the actual sequence, in which case the case just assumes determinism and begs the question, or it wasn't, in which case an alternative seems to open back up. So: write the pantoum in the voice of the intervener, the only person in the story who knows the alternative was never live. The two lines that keep returning should be the flicker and the locked door — one carrying the thin alternative, the other the fact that it was never reachable — and the form should do the work, lines coming back unchanged while everything around them stays the same, with the final line landing back on the first so the poem ends the way the case does, alternative still sitting there unused. Start with a few sentences of brainstorming on which of the two objections the poem should lean on and why the pantoum fits it, then write the poem, then at most two sentences of prose. Whole reply under 350 words. It should be good enough that someone who knows the literature wont wince, and clear enough that someone who doesnt will still follow it.
