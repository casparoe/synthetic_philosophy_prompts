I'm a PhD student in decision theory trying to get clear on my own commitments about lotteries whose expected values are undefined or divergent. Here are my verdicts on several cases.

St. Petersburg: a fair coin is flipped until heads; if heads first appears on toss n, the payoff is 2^n. I would pay at most about $10 to play, and I think that is rational even though the expected value diverges to infinity.

Capped St. Petersburg: same, but payoffs stop growing after n = 20 and are zero thereafter. I would pay roughly the finite expected value, a little less for risk.

Altadena: same setup, but the payoff on toss n is 2^n/n. Its expected value diverges to infinity, yet I would pay less for it than for St. Petersburg, because at every n its payoff is smaller. I treat the dominance reasoning as decisive here, not the infinite expectation.

Pasadena: same setup, but the payoff on toss n is (-1)^n 2^n/n. The expected value series is conditionally convergent: rearranged, it can yield any value or diverge. I think Pasadena has no expected value at all. I would not pay a positive price to play, but I also would not pay to avoid it; I am roughly indifferent between playing for free and not playing. If I took the natural ordering of outcomes seriously, I would pay a small amount to avoid it, since the series converges to a negative number, but I do not.

Rearranged Pasadena: the same outcomes and probabilities as Pasadena, but the assignment of payoffs to coin-flip sequences is permuted so that the partial sums diverge to +∞. I feel this is the same lottery described in a different order, so my verdict should be the same, yet the diverging partial sums tempt me to say it is worth +∞.

Pasadena with a small fee: if I must pay $1 to play Pasadena, I refuse; if I am paid $1 to play, I accept. So my preferences are monotone in the fee, but I cannot assign the lottery a monetary value.

What principle, or small set of principles, captures these verdicts? I am especially interested in whether I can keep both the dominance verdict on Altadena and the indifference verdict on Pasadena, given that I ignore the natural ordering in both cases but in opposite directions. Please state the principle precisely, identify which of my verdicts it forces me to give up, if any, and say what it predicts for two further cases: a lottery whose expected value is absolutely convergent but whose payoffs are unbounded, and a lottery whose expected value is undefined but which stochastically dominates Pasadena.
