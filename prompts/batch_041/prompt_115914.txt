I'm in a small metaphysics reading group that meets fortnightly, and last month we did laws of nature. One of the members — a graduate student who works on dispositions and does not much hide where his sympathies lie — sent round the following summary the night before, and most of the group took it as more or less the received view: "Humeanism about laws is the thesis that there are no necessary connections in nature, so a law of nature is nothing more than a regularity, the sort of thing you write as a universally quantified conditional — all metals expand when heated. On this picture the laws are just the patterns we happen to notice and find convenient to state, and the Humean adds that the patterns are all there is: no powers, no dispositions, no modal glue holding the world together. The best-developed version is the best-system account, on which the laws are the axioms and theorems of whichever deductive system of truths best balances simplicity and strength, where simplicity and strength measure how easy the system is for us to use and how much it lets us predict. The obvious trouble is that this can't separate laws from accidents — all gold spheres are less than a mile across would qualify if it happened to fit the system — and it can't explain why laws support counterfactuals, since a bare regularity tells us nothing about what would have happened had things gone otherwise. Anti-Humeans, by contrast, hold that laws govern: the law makes the metals expand. The two live options are Armstrong's, on which laws are relations of necessitation between universals, and the dispositionalist view that properties are essentially powers. Because the Humean must treat a law as a summary of what happens rather than a reason why it happens, Humeanism can't account for explanation, and arguably can't make induction rational either, which is why almost nobody who works on laws of nature defends it anymore." I've read enough of the Humean literature to think this isn't quite right, but I can't put my finger on where it goes wrong, and I'd rather not be the person in the session who says it feels off and then stalls.

Please go through it claim by claim — sentence by sentence is fine, and where a single sentence does two jobs, split it — and for each one tell me whether a Humean, ideally a best-system Humean rather than a strawman regularity theorist, would accept it as stated, wince at it as an oversimplification, or reject it outright. I want the audit to keep three categories apart: claims that are accurate as far as they go, standard simplifications that a competent popularizer might defend but that mislead if pressed, and outright misrepresentations of the position. I'd especially like to know which of the objections in the summary are live problems for the view and which are objections to a cruder position that no careful Humean holds, and whether the anti-Humean half is fair to the anti-Humeans, since it seems to me to lump together views that disagree with one another about whether laws govern at all — though I can't tell whether that lumping does any real damage. Finally, does the closing claim survive the corrections — that Humeanism can't account for explanation and induction, and that almost nobody defends it now?

I'd rather have a verdict that tells me where I am likely to be wrong than one that flatters my suspicion that my friend is being unfair to the Humeans, so please be as self-critical as the material allows. Tell me what would change your mind: which argument or consideration, if it turned out to be correct, would move you to a different assessment of how fair the summary is — and where in your reasoning you'd put the least confidence. No reading list, please; I want the audit, not citations.
