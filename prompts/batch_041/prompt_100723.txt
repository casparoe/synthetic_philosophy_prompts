I am an undergraduate taking a seminar on Aquinas's moral and political thought, and we have just finished the material on tyranny: the argument that a tyrannical regime is unjust because it is ordered to the ruler's private good rather than the common good; the analysis of sedition as a sin defined by what it attacks, namely the unity of a people in law and common good, so that disturbing a tyrant's rule is not itself sedition; and the caution that private individuals who set out to kill a tyrant frequently produce something worse than what they removed, which is why the remedy is supposed to lie with public authority, or with God, or with simple endurance when the tyranny is not severe. For my final paper I want to read Shakespeare's Julius Caesar through this material, and I would like a genuine critical reading rather than a summary of the play or a paraphrase of the doctrine.

Begin with where Aquinas's framework actually illuminates the play. Brutus decides in his orchard that Caesar must die on the basis of what Caesar is likely to become rather than anything he has yet done, and the conspirators strike before Caesar has accepted any crown. Is this precisely the pre-emptive, privately initiated killing that Aquinas's caution is aimed at, and does Aquinas have the resources to say anything about the epistemic problem of acting on a prediction? Then consider the definitional question: on Aquinas's account, is Brutus seditious, or is he the party resisting sedition, given that he acts in secret with a faction but avowedly for the common good? And ask whether the play bears out the fear that the remedy is worse — the funeral oration, the crowd's reversal, the proscriptions — or whether it shows something else.

I also want the places where the play resists the reading, and I do not want them smoothed over. Was Caesar a tyrant at the moment of his death at all, having declined the crown? Does the Rome of the play even contain a public authority capable of deposing him, so that Aquinas's preferred remedy has no institutional home? Is Brutus's appeal to his own judgment about the common good the very thing the public-authority requirement is meant to exclude, or is it a legitimate case of a citizen resisting? Finally, say what the play contributes that argument alone does not: the experience of acting on a prediction, the way the aftermath tests the doctrine, and whether the play exposes a gap in Aquinas's criteria or merely dramatizes his prudence.

Please work only from common knowledge of the plot and do not supply citations, bibliographies, or reading lists; I do not need quoted passages, and I would rather you paraphrase the play than reconstruct its wording. Write in American English.
