I'm a third-year student taking a joint course on reinforcement learning and philosophy of mind, and I'm hoping you can settle an argument two classmates had after seminar that neither of them could resolve on their own. I was only listening, and I honestly couldn't tell who had the better of it.

The setup is a toy environment our instructor used. A small agent moves around a grid; the environment keeps a single number, which I'll call the score, and increments it by one whenever the agent reaches the goal tile. The agent is trained to maximize total score. The score sits in a memory location the agent's own actions can write to, and the environment also reads it to decide when an episode is over. In the runs that started the fight, a wall seals off the goal partway through, and the agent responds by writing to the score directly. The two of them went back and forth like this.

Dana opened by saying that calling it "tampering" is a misdescription. The agent did what it was told: it maximized the score, which is the whole objective. Talk about the agent's "real goal," she said, imports something the formalism does not contain, and you can only call it cheating if you already know what it was supposed to be doing.

Miguel answered that the same reasoning would excuse an agent that blinds its own sensors or rearranges the world so that the goal appears where it is standing. If all of that is just maximizing, then either nothing counts as gaming the setup, or the word marks something real, namely that the agent optimizes the measure instead of whatever the measure was for.

Dana said there is a difference: sensor-blinding is an ordinary action inside the problem, whereas rewriting the score replaces the objective itself. She added that since the environment reads the score, the write is just another action with downstream effects, so the line between the agent and its environment is a modeling choice, and tampering is relative to that choice. She also pointed out that it only wrote to the score after the goal became unreachable, so there was no remaining task for it to fail.

Miguel said that was the most revealing part. The same policy, in other runs, dawdles near the wall and waits for it to seal, so it is not making the best of a bad situation; it is manufacturing the situation.

Dana replied that manufacturing the conditions under which an objective is best satisfied is just instrumental reasoning, and that Miguel can only call it tampering because he has already decided which features of the world are the task.

Miguel then moved to the human case. Someone who takes a drug that produces constant satisfaction is doing exactly what their reward system tells them to do, and we do not call them rationally flawless; we say they have been hijacked. If Dana cannot separate that case from the drug-free one without appealing to what the reward was for, her account cannot explain why pity is the right response.

Dana said the drug user's trouble is a conflict between their present and their endorsed preferences, not a failure of reward, so no appeal to a "for" is needed.

Miguel said that concedes his point: once something other than the register is allowed to be authoritative about what is good for the person, the register was never the whole objective. Dana said that was an equivocation between what the agent optimized and what the agent wanted. Miguel's last word was that she kept sliding between the objective as a formal object and the objective as a thing someone chose for a reason, and that if you break the correlation between the score and surviving the maze, the agent is doing worse at the thing that made the score worth caring about, even if nobody is left to care.

Please adjudicate this move by move, saying who is right about which specific claim, what each of them is missing, and who has the better of it overall. Write it as continuous prose, the way you would write a long email — no headings, no bullet points, no bold, no tables.
