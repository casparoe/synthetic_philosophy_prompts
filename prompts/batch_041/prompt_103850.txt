I'm presenting at a cognitive science reading group my account of the self as a strange loop in Hofstadter's sense — the "I" as a symbol that ends up denoting the very representational process that produced it, so that the self is less a thing the brain finds than a fixed point its self-model settles into. I want to stake out one move that is more contentious than the standard story: that a loop counts as strange only when the levels being crossed are representational rather than merely causal, which is supposed to rule out thermostats, autopoiesis, and ordinary feedback as selves. I am not confident that line holds, and I would rather find out in advance.

The room is mostly predictive-processing people who are comfortable treating the self as a useful fiction the brain generates, plus a couple of illusionists in the Dennett–Frankish vein who care less about whether the self exists than about why the illusion is so inescapable, and one phenomenologist who will insist that no third-person loop story can touch the first-person givenness of experience.

Write my Q&A prep sheet: the seven or so objections I will actually get from this room, ordered by how likely and how damaging they are, each phrased in the vocabulary of whoever would raise it, with a ninety-second reply and a blunt note on where that reply is soft. Tell me what to concede up front so I don't look like I'm dodging, and flag the one question I'm hoping nobody asks — the one that, if it comes, I have no answer for.
