After a career in the earth sciences, I have spent my retirement reading history and philosophy, and I keep encountering an argumentative move that I would like to see laid out properly. At a discussion of whether the Trojan War, King Arthur, or the Exodus really happened, the exchange was brought to a close by the proverb that there is no smoke without fire. The suggestion, as I understood it, was that the mere existence of a long-told story gives us reason to believe that something happened, and that the longer and the more widely the story is told, the more we are entitled to conclude about the event behind it.

I have a scientist's impatience with an argument whose conclusion I cannot state precisely enough to test, and I suspect the proverb is doing several different jobs at once. Please set out, as a menu rather than a single preferred reading, the distinct precise claims that no smoke without fire could be making in this setting, ordered from the trivial to the radical. I would like the disambiguation to turn at least on the following: what plays the role of the smoke — the bare existence of the story, its wide currency, its stability across retellings, the incidental and unflattering detail it preserves, or the survival of particular names; what is inferred — that some event or person occasioned the story, that its central action occurred, that its central action occurred at roughly the scale and in roughly the manner related, or that the story is a garbled but broadly reliable report; and what kind of claim is on offer — a logical truth, a causal regularity about how stories arise, a defeasible inference to the best explanation, or a methodological presumption.

For each reading, tell me whether it is a platitude, a substantive and defensible hypothesis, or false, and what evidence would count for or against it. Then say which reading the proverb is usually meant to carry when it is used to close a conversation of this sort.

What I want most, however, is the application. Say which of the readings, if any, actually licenses the conclusion that was drawn in each of the three cases. My suspicion is that the proverb equivocates between a reading that is nearly vacuous — some cause or other must have set the storytelling going — and a reading that is strong and dubious, namely that the story's central claims are approximately true, and that only the vacuous reading is secure. Tell me whether that suspicion is correct, and where the three cases come apart, since a legend about a place, a legend about a single named figure, and a legend about the migration of an entire people seem to me to strain the proverb in different ways. Finally, I would be grateful if you would distinguish it from its near relations — that every legend contains a kernel of truth, and that there must be something to it — and say whether any of them does better.
