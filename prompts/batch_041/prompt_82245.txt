I run teaching and learning across a small group of secondary schools, and part of my job is sitting in on curriculum review. Every term each department brings us the units they want to change -- new ones, rewrites, the ones that have gone stale -- and we get about half an hour per unit with the head of department and whoever wrote it. Right now my only real test is whether the assessment at the end lines up with what was taught, which is fine as far as it goes and completely useless for the thing that actually worries me, which is that we keep producing kids who can do the thing in the exam and can't tell you why anyone would want to.

I've been reading Whitehead on education and I think the two bits worth stealing are these. First, his complaint about what he called inert ideas: knowledge that gets received, written down, tested, and never once thrown into a fresh combination or used to do anything. His test for whether something belongs in a curriculum isn't whether it's true or important, it's whether it can be put to work, connected to other things, and made to matter to the person learning it. Second, the rhythm: romance, then precision, then generalization, and then the whole cycle starts again at a smaller scale. Romance is where you meet the material as a live thing, where there's an itch of interest and a sense that something is there to be found out. Precision is the disciplined, systematic, often boring stage where you actually get competent and learn the technique properly. Generalization is where you can use it, see it elsewhere, do something with it. His point, I'm fairly sure about this, is that precision without a prior romance is dead labour, romance without precision is just entertainment, and the cycle repeats -- a whole schooling, a term, a single lesson can each have their own arc, and they nest and overlap rather than happening one after another in a tidy line. He also ties it to freedom and discipline: freedom, then discipline, then freedom again at a higher level.

So what I want from you is a procedure I can actually run in that half hour meeting, on a unit proposal, in front of the person who wrote it, who is not a philosopher and will not be impressed by me waving a book around. A set of questions in order, with what counts as a good enough answer to each, and I need it to be clear where the procedure stops and says no, and where it stops and hands the thing to me or the head of department as a values call rather than a design call. Include the inert-ideas filter and the rhythm filter, but I don't want them as two seperate checklists stapled together -- I want to know how they interact, because I suspect a unit can pass the inertness test while failing the rhythm, and I want to know what to do about that. And it has to handle the nesting: I need to be able to say whether we're judging the arc of the whole key stage, the unit, or the individual lesson, and what changes when you move between those levels. If a unit is precision-heavy but the romance happened two years earlier in a different unit, is that ok? If so, say so, and give me a way to check that it actually happened rather than assuming it.

Then run it on three proposals and show your working, including one where the procedure tells me to kill something I like:

- A year 9 unit on the periodic table, currently taught as: here's the table, here are the groups, here are the trends, practise the trends, test. The head of department is proud of how well the kids do on the trends questions.
- A unit built entirely around a big open project -- students choose a question about their local area, investigate it, present it. The teacher's proposal has no direct instruction in it at all, deliberately, because she thinks direct instruction kills the interest. She's a good teacher and the presentations are usually great.
- A unit on poetic metre for a lower set, where the teacher has planned a really beautiful opening lesson: a recording, a bit of drumming, students clapping out rhythms, everybody leaves buzzing. Then it's back to worksheets for six weeks. He loves this lesson and it's the reason he volunteers to teach the unit.

The third one is the one I expect to be told to cut or rebuild, so be honest about it if so, and tell me what to tell him. Also be honest about what this procedure can't do. I don't want it pretending to be more objective than it is: some of these calls -- whether this cohort has enough prior precision for the romance to take, whether a project is genuinely generalisation or just unstructured -- are judgment calls, and I'd rather the procedure say so and tell me who should be making them than dress them up as a test. And I want a short section at the end on how it misfires, especially in the exam-driven subjects where the generalization stage might legitimately be deferred for a year or two, and on the temptation to turn everything into a three-part lesson with a fun hook at the start, which I'm worried is what happens when people get hold of this idea without the nesting part.
