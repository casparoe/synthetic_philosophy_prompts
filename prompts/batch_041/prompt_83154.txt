Alright, so I've somehow ended up running a session at our local rationality meetup, and everyones read the same five posts about the sunk cost fallacy and thinks the question is closed ("it's a fallacy, obviously, next slide"), which is exactly the smugness I want to poke at. So I want to build a one-page decision-tree handout — the flowchart sort where you answer questions about cases and end up at a position — that takes someone from "yes, fine, people really do honour sunk costs, and in the snowstorm-ticket sort of case that's a mistake" to a named position on whether honouring them is ever rational.

Build it out of the logical space rather than a roll-call of famous names. Every node should be a question about a case or a claim that someone could answer without already knowing which position it leads to — nothing like "are you a Humean about reasons?". I'd like the root to split the senses of "honouring a sunk cost" (letting the past spend enter the expected-value sum vs. feeling bad about quitting vs. refusing to quit for non-consequentialist reasons vs. treating the past as a source of entitlement), and then the nodes that actually do some work: whether the cost enters as a reason or only as a symptom or evidence about the future; whether we're assessing the act, the policy, or the character; whether it might be rational for the agent but not for the type (or the other way round); whether it can be rational to engineer sunk costs for yourself (the non-refundable ticket you buy so you'll actually go); whether the investment having changed your preferences means the cost isn't really sunk at all; and whether it matters that people claim to find the effect in other animals too, i.e. whether we're dealing with a reasoning error or a rule of thumb that's right on average.

Leaves, 10 to 14 of them: straight expected-utility eliminationism, the self-commitment-device line, the credible-threat/burning-boats line, the "past facts can be reasons — promises are past facts too" line, the entitlement/social-norm line, the narrative or unity-of-a-life line, the ecological/type-level rationality line, the deflationary "this is really about loss aversion or waste avoidance, so the question's misframed" line, and whatever else belongs there that I've not thought of. Each leaf gets the name of the position (or "unnamed" if nobody's actually defending it — say so rather than inventing a defender), one line on its most awkward committment, and a note on which answer pattern lands you there. Flag any two leaves the tree can't actually separate, and mark which combinations of answers are unoccupied and why (incoherent, just unattractive, or simply unexplored).

Cases I want it to handle: the government finishing a dam nobody wants; someone staying in a PhD they hate; a couple staying together because of ten years; the deliberately non-refundable ticket; and at least one where the sunk cost is someone else's and I'm the one deciding whether to tell them to quit. No citations needed, I don't care who said what — I care that the branches are the real cruxes.
