I recently attended a lecture on Plato's ethics in which the professor summarized Plato's attitude toward drunkenness as follows:

"Plato is usually remembered as a killjoy. In the Republic he bans the flute-girls, throws out the Lydian and Ionian modes, and insists that the guardians never touch wine, because drunkenness clouds the reason that should rule the soul. But in the Laws, his last and longest dialogue, he reverses himself. There the Athenian Stranger proposes that citizens should drink together at symposia, that wine is a medicine for old age, and that getting drunk is a form of education and a test of character. He lays down an age scheme: no wine at all before eighteen, moderate drinking until thirty, and after forty the freedom to drink deeply. The drinking party is a gymnasium of the soul, where the intoxicated man reveals his true nature; since the lawgiver is supposed to use wine to test and train the citizens, Plato ends up endorsing something like a state-run drinking culture. So the common picture of Plato as a puritan is wrong: the Laws treats drunkenness as a legitimate philosophical tool."

I know that the Laws is a late dialogue and that the Athenian Stranger is its main speaker, and I have a vague sense that the age thresholds are real, but I also suspect the professor has smoothed over some distinctions and perhaps overstated the reversal. I would like a careful audit. Please go through the summary claim by claim and tell me: (1) what a Plato scholar would accept as accurate; (2) what is a defensible simplification that a popularizer might legitimately make; (3) what is a genuine misrepresentation, conflation, or overstatement that Plato would deny or that distorts his argument; and (4) whether the professor's concluding claim -- that Plato ends up endorsing a state-run drinking culture and that the Laws treats drunkenness as a legitimate philosophical tool -- follows from what is actually in the dialogue once the errors are corrected. In particular, I would like to know how the Laws' treatment of drinking parties relates to the Republic's prohibitions, whether the Athenian Stranger's argument is best understood as a test of character, a training in self-control, or something else, and what role the sober president and the educational aim of singing play in the proposal. Please answer in American English, and do not give me a bibliography or reading list; I want the audit itself, with reasons.
