I'm convening a small reading group on self-deception, and we keep getting stuck in the same place. We've been through the usual material on the paradox and the main families of response, and every session degenerates into a fight about whether there is any coherent phenomenon here at all or whether "self-deception" is just a dramatic name for ordinary motivated irrationality plus a misleading metaphor. Before we start on this term's papers I want the specification rather than another candidate theory. Acting as a philosopher of mind and philosophical psychologist who has worked seriously on this problem, and taking care to separate the requirements anyone actually needs from the ones people merely import from their preferred solution, write me the desiderata that any adequate theory of self-deception must satisfy. Fix the purpose as follows: the theory must be good enough that we can take an arbitrary alleged case — a constructed one, or a real one from the clinic or the news — and say whether it is a genuine instance of self-deception and what makes it so, and good enough that we can tell when two theories in the literature are answering the same question rather than talking past each other.

For every requirement, give the case or consideration that motivates it and say what it rules out. I expect the list to cover at least four kinds of demand. First, the cases it must get right: the man who sincerely announces he is not dying while quietly arranging his affairs; the jealous man who convinces himself of his partner's fidelity and happens to be right — must the theory count that, and on what grounds either way; the woman who reports no fear while her hands shake; and the case where the comforting belief is itself the mechanism that makes it true. Second, the distinctions it may not collapse: self-deception from lying to oneself, from weakness of will, from wishful thinking, from plain motivated reasoning, from deceiving someone else, and from simply holding a false belief — plus the question of whether self-deception is a state, a process, or a disposition. Third, the phenomena it must accommodate without contradiction: that the self-deceived are typically anxious and effortful and seem to be "on the verge"; that self-deception comes in degrees and often coexists with the very awareness it denies; that it can be adaptive as well as ruinous; and that we can sometimes catch ourselves in it. Fourth, the constraints it must respect: whatever it posits has to be psychologically possible for a single subject with a normal cognitive architecture; it must not require the agent to believe and disbelieve the same thing in the same respect at the same time; and it must leave self-deception as a genuine epistemic failure rather than quietly reclassifying it as sound reasoning or as a merely arational reflex. Mark each requirement as negotiable or as the prejudice of one camp, and be explicit about which entries are really demands that the theory agree with a particular solution rather than demands that it explain something.

Then the part I care most about: tell me which subsets of these requirements cannot be jointly satisfied, and rank the tensions by how much damage they do. My suspicion, which I want you either to vindicate or to show is confused, is that the demand that self-deception be intentional in something like the ordinary sense — the deceiver intends to produce a belief in himself and succeeds — and the demand that the resulting state be a genuine belief are not jointly satisfiable, since if you set out to acquire a belief on a basis you know to be bad, you thereby know the belief would be unwarranted and so cannot acquire it; and that this is precisely what forces either a divided mind, which threatens the unity of the subject that the phrase "self-deception" seems to require, or a deflationary account on which nothing is really being deceived. Say whether that is a real impossibility result, a family of them, or a pseudo-problem generated by one reading of "intention". Finish with a short and honest assessment — a paragraph, or a table if that is cleaner — of how the intentionalist line, the deflationary or anti-realist line, and the divided-mind or compartmentalization line fare against the list, and name the single requirement that does the most to exclude candidates. Start by setting out the requirements before you evaluate anything, and don't hedge every entry into a both-ways formulation.
