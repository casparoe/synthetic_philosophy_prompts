I am putting together a research proposal for a one-semester graduate seminar that straddles moral philosophy and history, and I need the proposal written out properly. The setting: a 6,000-word paper, one semester, no archival work, and I will add all citations myself, so the proposal should refer to positions by their content rather than pointing at a literature. Please write it as a polished prospectus, not an outline.

My half-formed idea is this. Debates about moral revolutions tend to treat three candidate mechanisms as rivals: changing material interests, the pressures of honor and shame, and moral argument. I suspect they are not rivals over the same explanandum but explain different phases, and that we can tell which one actually did the work by looking at the settlement the revolution leaves behind rather than at the rhetoric that surrounded it. Argument explains why a practice became contestable at all. Honor explains why the class that practiced it stopped defending it publicly. Interest explains the timing and, crucially, the terms of the exit. My candidate diagnostic is the distribution of burdens in the settlement: emancipation with compensation paid to the owners, and with a transitional regime binding the newly freed into unfree labor, looks like the signature of an interest-driven exit wearing moral language, whereas the ending of dueling and of footbinding left no compensation because the people who bore the cost of ending the practice were the same people who had borne its honor. If that is right, the honor mechanism does not scale from self-regarding practices, where the practitioner and the person constrained are the same, to other-regarding practices built on extraction, where they are not. That would make the honor-based account of moral revolution a local explanation rather than a general one, and it would give a reason to think that the interesting variable is not which motive is strongest but which party is positioned to demand a price.

The proposal should do the following. State the question so that it has a determinate answer, not a survey shape: I want a claim that could be false. Explain why the standard answers leave the question open, in terms of the arguments rather than the authors. Lay out the method concretely: what I would compare, what features of each case I would look at, how I would code or characterize the settlement, and how I would avoid reading the outcome back into the motives. Say what would count as the project failing, and be specific: if the three mechanisms turn out to be observationally equivalent given the historical facts I can cite without new research, or if the settlement diagnostic cannot discriminate because every successful revolution pays off the losers somehow, then the thesis is in trouble and I want the proposal to say so. Say what a successful result looks like and who it would matter to.

Two constraints. First, do not build the proposal on contested or obscure historical detail, and do not invent dates, figures, or titles. You may use facts I have supplied here, and well-known background about British colonial slavery, dueling, and footbinding; if a step needs a fact you are not confident of, restructure the step so it does not. Second, be honest about the division of labor between philosophy and history: tell me where I am doing conceptual work and where I am leaning on empirical claims I am not in a position to establish, and say whether the project is too ambitious for one semester if it needs the comparison to carry that much weight.
