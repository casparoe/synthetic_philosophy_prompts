I teach a graduate seminar in formal epistemology, and I have to write the take-home essay for the final this week. This morning a colleague and I got into a long, fairly heated argument that I would like to turn into the exam. He is a committed defender of imprecise probabilities: he says that when the evidence is genuinely ambiguous — a coin of unknown bias, a novel risk, a one-off policy choice — an honest agent should not pretend to have a single sharp credence, and that representing her state as a set or interval of probabilities is the more faithful model of both her evidence and her rationality. I pushed back that this seems to buy honesty at the price of action. A set of credences does not by itself tell you what to do, so you have to bolt on a decision rule: maximize the minimum expected value, maximize the maximum, take the acts that are optimal for at least one permissible prior, discard only the acts that are strictly dominated, and so on. Those rules give different verdicts in the same case, and choosing among them looks like yet another decision under uncertainty, so I do not see how the view escapes arbitrariness; it seems only to relocate it one level up. He came back with two points I could not immediately answer. First, he said the apparent arbitrariness is a feature of the agent's evidence rather than of her preferences, and that this is precisely what the framework is meant to capture. Second, he cited what he called dilation: if you begin with a set of priors and then learn evidence that is, conditional on the relevant parameter, independent of the event you care about, your posterior set for that event can come out wider than your prior set — so gathering seemingly irrelevant information leaves you less confident, not more. I want the exam to force students to engage this exact dispute rather than recite a textbook summary of ambiguity aversion, and I want it to be answerable without any reading beyond the course.

Acting as an expert in formal epistemology and decision theory, please write me the exam. Start by brainstorming, in a few lines, the two or three genuine cruxes buried in this dispute — the places where the disagreement is substantive rather than verbal — and then build the question so that a student cannot pass by summarizing the standard taxonomy. Draft one essay question suitable for a ninety-minute take-home, a model answer of roughly 700 words, and a marking scheme worth 20 points that is organized around five must-hit moves and, for each, a one-line note on how a mediocre answer typically gets it wrong. The model answer should take a position and defend it rather than survey both sides neutrally; it should say what an imprecise credence is supposed to represent, whether the choice of decision rule is a real regress or a pseudo-problem, and what dilation does or does not show about the epistemic credentials of imprecision. Keep everything self-contained: no bibliography, no citations, no names or dates — any theory or phenomenon you invoke should be explained well enough that a student who has never seen the label can still reason about it. Aim the difficulty at the reasoning, not at recall of who said what.
