I'm in a graduate seminar on animal minds and the ethics of uncertainty, and one case has been nagging at me: the line the United Kingdom drew in its Animal Welfare (Sentience) Act 2022, which recognises all vertebrates as sentient and, on the strength of a commissioned scientific review, extends recognition to a couple of invertebrate groups -- octopuses and their cephalopod relatives, and crabs, lobsters and other decapods -- while leaving insects outside the fold entirely. Two years later a large group of philosophers and neuroscientists signed a declaration at New York University (April 2024) that goes the other way: it puts insects, at minimum, among the animals for which the evidence indicates at least a "realistic possibility" of conscious experience, and it says that when such a possibility exists it is irresponsible to ignore it. I understand the biology and the philosophy of insect sentience well enough, and I don't need the standard arguments rehearsed. What I want is a disciplined case analysis of these two episodes through the lens of the scale argument -- the reasoning that because there are something on the order of 10^18 or more insects alive at any moment, and vastly more living and dying over a year, even a very small probability that each one is sentient yields an expected amount of morally significant experience that swamps every vertebrate welfare concern we have, including the ones the statute did recognise.

Run the analysis in both directions. In one direction: map the episode onto the argument with real care about what the argument needs -- who the relevant decision-maker is and what the actual options were; what work the phrase "probability of sentience" is doing, and whether it is a determinate credence about a natural fact or an artefact of our competing theories of consciousness (and what happens to the argument if it is the latter); why the review could conclude what it did about a lobster and still find nothing it could say about a fruit fly, and whether any non-arbitrary prior over such a reference class exists; and how the cost asymmetry should enter, given that what the statute actually imposes on government is a procedural duty to have regard to recognised animals' welfare rather than any prohibition, so that inclusion looks cheap on one side and the stakes look enormous on the other. In the other direction: what does the episode tell us about the argument? Consider whether the legislative line is best read as an implicit probability threshold (and whether such a threshold is defensible or just institutional inertia, given that the whole machinery of animal law was built around vertebrates and the review's remit was carved to fit that); whether the fanaticism objection bites here, in the form of the worry that if the numbers can swamp the probability then the same reasoning forces conclusions about nematodes, mites and soil arthropods that the argument's defenders would not accept; and whether the case shows the argument to be oddly insensitive to the very quantity it makes central -- if a probability of 10^-5 and a probability of 10^-15 both yield the same practical verdict, the long debate about how low the probability is may be doing no work at all. Tell me plainly where the theory is silent rather than making it speak, and be honest about whether it has anything to say about the moral weight of an animal whose sentience is uncertain as opposed to the expected number of such animals.

I'm not asking for a literature review and I don't need citations -- I'll add those myself. Nor do I need the history of the statute or the signatories' biographies; the facts above are the ones I'd want the analysis to use, and if a step in your reasoning depends on some further empirical or legal detail, flag the dependency rather than asserting the detail. Finish by saying which of the two episodes is the better evidence about the scale argument's merits -- the statute that declined to extend recognition to insects, or the declaration that named them -- and how much of the difference between them is a difference in the kind of act (binding procedure versus public statement) rather than a difference in what the evidence supports.
