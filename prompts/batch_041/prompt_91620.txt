I'm a reporter on the science-and-ideas beat, which in practice means I write alot about religion, and I keep running into the same move: someone defends a practice or a law in scripture by saying it was never the point, it was a concession to the world people actually lived in, a way of meeting them where they were. Animal sacrifice is what started this for me. Maimonides makes exactly this argument about the sacrifices in part III of the Guide of the Perplexed, chapter 32, and it's a genuinely good argument: God doesn't just abolish the sacrificial cult, because you can't yank a people straight from one extreme to the other, so the worship they already knew — altars, incense, priests — gets redirected toward the one God instead of the stars. The Law takes the medicine in doses, as he puts it, more or less. My problem is I can't tell when that's a real explanation and when it's an alibi. It's everywhere now — the slavery laws were a concession, the dietary rules were a concession, some rule about women in church was a concession, the thing my local congregation does on Saturday mornings is a concession — and I end up either printing it as though it were settled or hedging so hard the paragraph says nothing at all.

So I'd like a decision procedure I can actually run before filing. A checklist, in order: the questions to ask about any claim that a practice was an accommodation to its time, what counts as a sufficient answer at each step, where the procedure stops and just says no, and where it hands the thing back to me because it's a values question rather than a factual one — does it even matter for a practice's authority if it turns out to be a workaround? I don't think a procedure can settle that, and I'd rather it say so than fake an answer. Keep what Maimonides actually argues; don't water it down into "context matters." The parts that do the work are the impossibility of abrupt change, the idea that a real concession is a weaning and therefore should leave some trace of a trajectory or an expiry date in the law itself, and his own worry that if you push this reading too hard you've turned the commandments into means to an end, which is a cost someone has to pay. Also build in the asymmetry I keep tripping over: the same claim made from inside a tradition and made by an outsider with an axe to grind are doing different work, and I don't want a procedure that pretends they're the same. Then run it on three cases. One, the sacrifices themselves, since that's the original. Two, the claim that the biblical laws permitting slavery were a concession to the ancient economy — I'm pretty sympathetic to that reading and I suspect the procedure will tell me something I don't want to hear, which is fine, but be honest about it. Three, a modern one: a claim that some current custom, or the dietary laws, is "just" a holdover from its era.

Constraints, such as they are: I've got maybe an hour, I can't call a rabbi every time, my editor signs off on framing but not on the argument, and I need to know when the honest thing to write is "this is contested" instead of picking a side. And please tell me what the procedure can't do — I'd much rather know where it will misfire than get a checklist that looks confident everywhere. I also can't seperate the question of whether a reading is true from the question of who's selling it to me, so if the procedure needs me to keep those apart, say so out loud.
