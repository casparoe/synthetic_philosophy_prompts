You are a philosopher specializing in decision theory, social epistemology, and the ethics of collective action. I am preparing to interview a guest for a seminar Q&A. His position is a novel extension of the unilateralist curse: he argues that the curse is not fundamentally a coordination failure but a selection-by-confidence effect. When many actors each have some chance of being right and any one can act unilaterally, the act that gets done is selected by the most confident actor rather than the most competent one; even if each actor's evidence is on balance favorable, the expected value of the resulting unilateral action can be negative. He also claims that the standard remedies, such as requiring consensus or a supermajority, can be worse when competence is heavy-tailed, because they suppress the one actor who is actually right; his alternative is epistemic licensing, where unilateral action is permitted only by actors who can demonstrate structural competence or a track record, not merely high confidence.

Think carefully before drafting. First, brainstorm the dialectical structure: what the guest's central claim implies, where the standard objections will come from, and what replies he is most likely to give. Then draft eight to ten interview questions that go beyond having him restate the view. Include at least one question each on (a) the distinction between selection by confidence and correlated error, (b) the heavy-tailed competence objection and whether it reintroduces a unilateral license for the one expert, and (c) how epistemic licensing would work institutionally without relying on the very confidence signal the view distrusts. For each question, add a follow-up keyed to the answer he is most likely to give. The questions should press on the actual content of his position, not on his biography or influences.
