I've been reading the rationalist blogs since the old LessWrong days and I've thought about this alot, and I run a small monthly discussion group at a coworking space — twelve or so people, mostly programmers, one med student, one actuary, nobody past a college intro stats course. Next month it's my slot and I've claimed the reference class problem.

Don't explain it to me, plan how I explain it. Ive got 45 minutes and a whiteboard. What I want is the order the pieces have to go in and why each one has to land before the next one makes sense — which piece is load bearing, what I can safely put on the board first without it being a lie I have to walk back later. One case has to carry the whole thing, and I want to know what that case needs. My candidate: my sister asks me her odds of getting into one specific PhD program, and I can put her in the class of everyone who applied to that program, or everyone with her GPA and GRE, or everyone who applied that year, or everyone who has ever asked me for a life prediction, and finally in the class with exactly one member, her — which yields 0 or 1 and is therefore useless. Does that case do the job, or does it smuggle in something (all the classes being natural, the answers being far apart) that I should be engineering on purpose? Tell me what to swap or add.

Then the misconceptions. My guesses: that this is just the small-sample problem; that a more specific class is always better; that Bayes makes it go away if you're careful; that it only bites frequentists; and that if no class is privileged then anything goes and all probabilities are made up. Which of those will actually show up, which am I imagining, what's the one I'm missing, and at what point in the sequence do I have to head each one off — before the case or after it? Also give me one question to ask at the end that shows whether they got the problem or just learned the vocabulary, and tell me what to leave out at this level and say out loud that I'm leaving it out.

Last thing: if the shape I've got in mind is wrong — like if the thing I'm treating as the punchline is really a different problem wearing the same coat, or if the lesson I want them to take away is the wrong lesson — just say so. Don't humor me and build the plan anyway. I'd rather find out now than in front of the group.
