I teach a 75-minute seminar on consequentialism to about 18 undergrads and I want them to actually feel the overdemandingness problem in their bones rather than hear it described. The specific thing I want them to run into: the "keep giving until the cost to you outweighs the benefit to others" rule has no natural stopping point — every extra bit of giving looks justified by exactly the same logic that justified the first bit — and I want that pressure to show up in their own choices before anyone names it. So design me a staged role-play where each student holds a small budget of something they actually care about (money, time, attention, sleep, whatever) and then faces a sequence of real decisions about whether to spend it on a needy case, with the rule that any refusal has to be justified out loud, and that earlier justifications stay written on the board and can be reused or challenged in later rounds. I want them to notice that their own reasons start to wear thin. Somewhere around the middle, inject an exit for them: a card or a rule stating that if everyone in the group gives their fair share then the need is met, so you only ever owe your share — and make them decide whether to take that exit and what it costs to take it. No lecture up front, and don't let them hear words like "demandingness" or "fair share" until the debrief. Give me the roles, materials, a timeline, a way to keep score without real money, the debrief that maps specific moments onto the argument, what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle it, and be honest about which feature of the idea the activity just can't reproduce and that I'll have to supply myself.
