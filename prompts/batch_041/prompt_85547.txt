I'm taking a historiography course, and a classmate has produced an argument that either demolishes the standard complaint about Whig history or shows that the complaint was never saying anything. I can't find the flaw, and I want to know which step to give up and what giving it up costs.

(1) Every history must select and arrange its material; a chronicle that records everything is not a history.
(2) Selection requires a criterion of significance, and the criteria available to a historian are the questions that matter from where the historian stands — that is, now.
(3) So every history is organised by reference to the present.
(4) A history organised by reference to the present treats the present as the point of the story, and earlier events as steps toward it or as dead ends.
(5) That is precisely what the complaint about Whig history objects to.
(6) So every history is Whig history, and the complaint distinguishes nothing: it is at best a tautology, at worst an insult that could be aimed at any book.
(7) But the complaint is not empty. A textbook that files a failed rebellion as a mere prelude, or that treats a settlement nobody at the time thought final as a milestone on the road to democracy, distorts something, and the distortion can be corrected.
(8) So one of (1)–(6) is false.

I am confident of (1) and (7) and would rather not surrender either. So which step goes, and what does giving it up cost? If the answer is that (3) and (4) are reading the same phrase in two different senses, say what the two senses are, which one (2) actually licenses, and whether the distinction survives the obvious counter-move — that any criterion sharp enough to convict the textbook in (7) is itself a present-day criterion, so the slide simply reappears one level up. If instead the step to drop is (6), tell me what a complaint that applies to every history can still be doing, and whether it can mark degrees without a baseline that is itself present-minded.
