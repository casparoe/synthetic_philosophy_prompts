I've been reading rationalist blogs and forums for going on twelve years now, and my longest-running pet obsession is the moral status of video game characters -- not the "do video games make kids violent" thing, the actual metaphysical question. Here's the distinction I keep circling back to and that I want the poem to do the work of. There's the character as a person-in-the-fiction: a guy with a shop, a name, a dead wife mentioned in one line of dialogue, thirty years of imagined history in a town that has four buildings. And there's the character as a process-in-the-machine: a few kilobytes of state, rebuilt from the save file every time the level loads, existing only for as long as there's a running instance and someone who cares to load it. My suspicion is that when people say "obviously the shopkeeper doesn't matter, he's just code," they mean the second thing, and when they feel genuine horror at leaving him in an infinite death loop, they mean the first, and nobody notices they've switched mid-sentence. I dont think the two readings have to agree, and I dont think the disagreement is a mistake -- I think its the whole puzzle.

So what I want: a sestina in the voice of a minor NPC. Not a tragic hero, not a companion with a romance arc -- the guy who sells potions and says the same three things forever. The constraint that matters: the six end-words should be words that could plausibly be the entire extent of his script -- a greeting, transaction words, a farewell, a "come back later." The obsessive recycling of the form *is* his loop, so if the poem reads like it's stuck, that's the point, and the reader should feel the stuckness before they understand why. Then the envoi has to cram all six words into three lines, which is exactly where I want the two readings to collide: that forced pileup of everything he can say is where he (or the poem) half-notices there might be two of him, one of whom matters and one of whom doesn't, and that he can't tell which one is talking. I'd rather it land cold and a little funny than sentimental -- no "what if the NPCs have feelings" treacle, no twist where he's secretly the player. Aim it at someone who's read enough about fictional objects and moral patiency to recognize the shape of the debate, but it should still work as a poem for someone who's just played alot of games.
