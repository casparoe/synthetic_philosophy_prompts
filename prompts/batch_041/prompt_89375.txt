I'm a novelist, ~80 pages into a book about a guy who builds a very clever machine and slowly works out that he's the pet, not the owner. For texture I've been reading around in 19th-century stuff about machines and evolution, and I'm stuck on the preface to Butler's Erewhon — the 1872 one he wrote for the second edition, I think. Here's the bit I keep circling:

"I regret that reviewers have in some cases been inclined to treat the chapters on Machines as an attempt to reduce Mr. Darwin’s theory to an absurdity. Nothing could be further from my intention, and few things would be more distasteful to me than any attempt to laugh at Mr. Darwin; but I must own that I have myself to thank for the misconception, for I felt sure that my intention would be missed, but preferred not to weaken the chapters by explanation, and knew very well that Mr. Darwin’s theory would take no harm. The only question in my mind was how far I could afford to be misrepresented as laughing at that for which I have the most profound admiration. I am surprised, however, that the book at which such an example of the specious misuse of analogy would seem most naturally levelled should have occurred to no reviewer; neither shall I mention the name of the book here, though I should fancy that the hint given will suffice."

And then a few lines later:

"I have been held by some whose opinions I respect to have denied men’s responsibility for their actions. He who does this is an enemy who deserves no quarter."

I can hear that the tone is defensive-ironic and that he's being slippery on purpose, but I dont actually know what he's claiming. Who's the unnamed book he won't name? Is "specious misuse of analogy" a charge he's ducking or one he's turning around on someone else? And the line about preferring not to weaken the chapters by explanation — I'd have assumed the opposite, that satire gets stronger when you show your work. Also, what's the link between denying moral responsibility and the machine chapters at all? Is that a separate worry about the malcontents stuff, or is it the same argument wearing a hat?

What I'm really after is whether the machine chapters are an argument or a reductio that isn't quite a reductio. My character is a cognitive-science dropout who thinks in that Darwinian / Skinnerian / Popperian / Gregorian ladder of minds, and he'd want to know where Butler's machines land on it — are they Darwinian creatures, are they Gregorian, or does Butler's whole move make the ladder look like a parochial way of sorting things? I need to know what the defensible reading is before I can have him half-get it and botch it in an interesting way, so don't be polite about it if my instincts here are off.
