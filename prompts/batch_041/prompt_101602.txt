I keep a private list of verdicts on supertasks and want to know what principle, if any, unifies them. Zeno's runner arriving at the wall: fine, he arrives, nothing paradoxical there. Thomson's lamp, toggled at 1/2, 1/4, 1/8, ... of a minute: I have no verdict at all — the description simply fails to determine whether it is on or off, and I don't think that is a gap in my imagination. Now alter the lamp so that instead of toggling, the filament is heated each step to a temperature halfway between its current value and a fixed maximum: here I say the final temperature is perfectly determinate, despite the infinitely many heatings. The ball-and-urn: before step one put in balls 1–10 and remove ball 1, before step two put in 11–20 and remove ball 2, and so on; at the limit I say no balls remain, and I will defend that even though it feels like a cheat. Remove ball 10n at step n instead, and I say infinitely many remain — same urn, same times, different labeling, opposite answers, and I accept both. My tentative principle is that a supertask has a determinate outcome exactly when the quantity at issue converges as the steps accumulate, convergence standing in for the existence of a limit that the physical story can be said to realize. Does that principle really cover all five cases, or does one of them force a revision? What does it imply about the reverse supertask, in which the runner is to complete an infinite sequence of ever-shorter steps but there is no first step to begin? And please tell me where the principle is most likely to be wrong — which single consideration would lead me to abandon either the convergent-lamp verdict or the urn verdict — rather than defending it at all costs.
