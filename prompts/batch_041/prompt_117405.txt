Standards bodies that publish cryptographic recommendations also publish tables that compress every primitive to a single number, a security level in bits, so that an RSA modulus of one size, an elliptic-curve key of another, and a symmetric cipher of a third can be ranked on one scale. Engineers pick parameter sizes off these tables, and the systems built from them — the ones carrying web traffic, signing software updates, and authenticating payments — are treated as secure even though the assumptions underneath them are unproven: that factoring, or the discrete logarithm problem, or finding short vectors in lattices, admits no efficient algorithm. No proof of any of these exists, and although they are entangled with the unresolved P versus NP question, a proof that those two classes differ would not by itself establish any of them.

I want a reconstruction of what this practice presupposes. Does it require realism about complexity — that hardness is a property of a problem rather than of an algorithm, a machine, or a state of knowledge? Does it require the polynomial-time boundary to mean something for finite machines, since that is where the tables do their actual work? It appears to assume that worst-case hardness can underwrite average-case hardness, that difficulty is commensurable across unrelated problems, and that an adversary has a determinate computational power that can be bounded in advance, independent of which machines happen to exist. That last assumption looks less like a mathematical fact than a claim about the physics of possible computers, and one that the discovery of quantum algorithms evidently forced practitioners to revise.

Please separate what the practice strictly requires from what it merely fits well with, and say which rival views of complexity are actually ruled out by publishing and using such tables and which remain compatible with them — conventionalism about the polynomial-time line, instrumentalism about complexity classes, a concrete and machine-relative account of difficulty, a view on which complexity is relative to the reasoner rather than to the problem, or a view on which complexity claims are claims about our knowledge rather than about the world. Where the practice is underdetermined, say so explicitly. Finally, tell me what would have to change in how such tables are constructed, defended, and taught if the presupposed picture were replaced by the concrete, machine-relative one, and whether anything in the practice would have to be abandoned rather than merely rejustified.
