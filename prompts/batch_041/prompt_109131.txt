I've been going to a small reading group that's slowly working through Leibniz, and the bit that's stuck with me is the mill: he says if you took a machine that could think and sense and blew it up until you could walk around inside it, you'd find nothing but bits of it shoving each other around, and never anything that could explain a perception. Thats basically the hard problem of consciousness a couple hundred years early, or at least that's how it reads to me, and since then I can't stop wondering what Leibniz himself would have made of the way we argue about it now. I'm not a philosopher, I did a couple of units years ago and forgot most of it, so what I really want is a prediction. Imagine he gets dropped into a modern seminar. On the table: Chalmers's split between the easy problems (explain the functions, report, attention, discrimination, and you're supposedly done) and the hard problem (explain why any of it is accompanied by experience at all); the meta-problem move, where instead of answering the hard problem you try to explain why we're so sure there is one; the illusionists, who say phenomenal consciousness as we usually imagine it doesn't exist and the hard problem is an artefact of how we talk to ourselves about our own minds; and the panpsychists, who say experience goes all the way down and then get stuck on the combination problem, i.e. how a lot of tiny experiences add up to one big one instead of just sitting there side by side.

Would Leibniz think the hard problem is a real problem, or a confusion he can dissolve, and if he dissolves it, does he land near the illusionists or somewhere else entirely? I know he separated perception from apperception and thought we have loads of little perceptions we never notice, and I've been thinking about this alot -- how much work does that distinction do for him here? Does it make him a debunker of phenomenal consciousness, or does it let him keep it and just relocate the mystery somewhere else? Would he sign on with the panpsychists about experience being fundamental, and if so what does he do with the combination problem, given that his monads are supposed to be windowless and can't merge into each other? (There's also the pre-established harmony thing, which I have never really understood, so tell me if it matters here or if it's a red herring.) And would he think a computer or a big enough neural net could ever be conscious -- is the obstacle in principle or just engineering, and what exactly does he think we're getting wrong when we try to build a mind out of parts?

I'm not interested in a list of his doctrines read off a shelf. I want your best guess at how he'd actually come down, with the reasoning, plus which one or two of his commitments are doing the most work, which modern camp he'd find least crazy and which he'd call a category mistake, and where you're least confident about the whole prediction. If two readings of him pull in different directions, tell me which argument or which case would settle which one he'd go for.
