I am in the middle of a seminar on the philosophy of probability, and an unresolved disagreement with a classmate has left me unsure how the standard interpretations are supposed to handle probabilities that attach to singular, unrepeatable events. To pin the issue down, consider a case I have been turning over. A single unstable atom sits in a sealed chamber; it will either decay during the next hour or it will not, and then it is destroyed. There is no ensemble of identical atoms anywhere in the world, no reference population, and no long-run sequence of trials to appeal to. We nonetheless seem able to say that the probability of decay within the hour takes some definite value, and we can in principle justify that value by appeal to nuclear physics. My classmate argues that any such probability talk must be either an expression of our ignorance about a determinate fact or a disposition of the physical setup, whereas I keep reaching for a frequency-based story and find that there is simply nothing there to count.

I would like you to sort the three broad families of interpretation — frequentist, subjectivist (Bayesian), and propensity — into tiers according to how well each accounts for probabilities of this single-case kind. For each family, give a tier, a brief sentence explaining the placement, and name the single feature of the atom case that is doing the most work in your judgment. Then add a sentence identifying which of the three, if any, you regard as the most defensible interpretation of probability overall, and say whether that overall verdict agrees with the ranking you produced for the single-case problem. I am less interested in a survey of who said what than in seeing which parts of each interpretation are strained by an event that happens exactly once.
