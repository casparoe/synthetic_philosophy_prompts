Bit of an odd one. I'm a novelist — third book out next spring, nothing to do with any of this — and an old friend who runs a small foundation that does environmental mediation has asked me to give the opening remarks at a two-day sit-down she's convening between the families who work boats out of a small harbor and a conservation trust that wants to buy up the licenses and close the harbor to let a reef recover. She thinks a novelist can say things she can't, because she has to stay neutral and I don't.

Here's what I want the remarks to do. Reframe what they're about to do as a trade rather than a surrender. My own view — build the speech on it, don't just assert it — is that two sides can both come out ahead not by splitting the difference but by trading what each ranks lower for what each ranks higher. The boat families may care about continuity, dignity, and a skill passed down more than they care about a particular quota number; the trust may care about the reefs recovery and about being able to say honestly that they didn't kill a town to get it. If their rankings really differ, there's a deal that leaves each side holding more of what it actually cares most about — same reason countries trade, except the currency is the stuff people hold sacred-ish. So if it helps, treat each side as a set of dimensions they'd rank (livelihood, autonomy, honesty, the reef as something owed to people not here yet), and treat the deal as a reallocation, not a midpoint.

But I don't want to sound like I'm saying the reef or the harbor is just a commodity with a price, and I especially don't want to be the guy who tells a room that their deepest commitments are preferences on a curve. There's a real objection here — that some things can't be traded at any rate without being dishonored — and I want the speech to take it seriously on its feet rather than dodge it, without giving up the trade framing either. If you think the framing can't survive that, say so in your last couple of sentences instead of pretending.

Practical stuff: 500 words max. Don't use the words compromise, win-win, or common ground. Nobody in the room is a philosopher — thirty people: the boat families, a couple of trust scientists, a lawyer for each side who is paid to be suspicious of exactly this kind of speech, and my friend. It has to be sayable by me out loud in one go, and it has to leave neither side able to quote a line back later as the other side caving. Give me one concrete image, something with a boat or a reef in it, not a metaphor about bridges. Lead with the bottom line — the single sentence I'm really asking them to accept — then the speech itself.
