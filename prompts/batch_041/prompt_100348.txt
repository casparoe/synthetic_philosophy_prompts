I have no formal philosophy background and I'm not a mathematician by training, but I read fairly widely, and I keep running into what is usually called the strategy-stealing argument, illustrated with games like Hex and Chomp.

As I understand it, the claim is that you can prove the first player has a winning strategy in such games without ever exhibiting one: suppose the second player had a winning strategy; then the first player makes an arbitrary opening move and thereafter simply plays the second player's strategy, ignoring the extra piece, and if that strategy ever tells them to play on a square they already occupy, they make another arbitrary move instead — so the first player wins, contradicting the assumption, and hence the second player cannot have a winning strategy. Combined with the fact that the game cannot end in a draw, this is supposed to yield that the first player wins, as a matter of existence, with no strategy on the table.

Please restate this argument as faithfully as you can in the vocabulary I actually use, and be precise about which assumptions it needs and where each one does its work.
