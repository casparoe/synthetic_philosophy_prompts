I am a novelist, and the question that keeps surfacing in my work — and in my own life, since I live on a block that is arguing about exactly this — is who gets to decide what a neighborhood becomes. I distrust abstractions, so I have tried to discipline my instincts by recording verdicts on particular cases, the way I would write down how a character actually behaves rather than how I would like them to. I would like you to tell me what principle, or what small set of principles, those verdicts add up to.

The cases, and my gut verdicts:

1. Two owners on my block want to add a rear extension and carve the ground floor into a separate apartment. The neighbors object on parking and "character" grounds. My verdict: the extension should be allowed; the neighbors' objection deserves no weight.

2. A homeowners' association in a leafy, transit-rich district with large lots lobbies to keep minimum lot sizes high so that no parcel can be split. My verdict: they should lose. They are using the law to hoard a public good they did not create.

3. In a dense, low-income district under speculative buying pressure, a tenants' association wins a downzoning that also blocks a nonprofit's plan to build a hundred mixed-income units on a vacant lot. My verdict: I side with the downzoning. The tenants' claim to stay outweighs the new units.

4. Neighbors try to block a group home for people in recovery from opening on their street, citing safety. My verdict: the neighbors should lose. This is exclusion wearing a different coat.

5. Long-time residents of a rent-stabilized building try to block a luxury tower that would replace it. My verdict: they should win, even though the tower would add more units than it removes.

6. A neighborhood moves to ban short-term rentals so that apartments stay available to residents. My verdict: fine.

7. A wealthy enclave designates itself a historic district to freeze its appearance. My verdict: they should lose.

I notice that in 1, 2, 4, and 7 I side against the incumbents, and in 3, 5, and 6 I side with them. The distinction I feel is that the incumbents in the first group are protecting a preference, while those in the second are protecting a necessity. So my tentative principle is this: control over a place belongs to those for whom the place is a necessity rather than a preference, and only against changes that would destroy the necessity. But I can already see that this principle cannot, by itself, deliver my verdict in case 3, because the nonprofit's future residents also need housing, and arguably need it more than the tenants who are already there. Something else is doing the work, and I want to know what.

There is one case I keep going back and forth on. A working-class neighborhood votes to incorporate as its own small municipality, chiefly in order to control its own zoning and fend off annexation by the growing city beside it. Some mornings I think this is exactly the move I condemned in case 2 — a group fencing off access to land it happens to sit on. Other mornings I think it is the opposite: the one way a poor neighborhood has to keep from being swallowed by someone else's growth.

Please give me a committed answer. Do not lay out the options and leave the choice to me: take a definite position, name the principle you would defend, state what it says about the case I cannot settle and about at least one further case I have not raised, and tell me plainly which of my seven verdicts it forces me to give up and why I should accept that loss.
