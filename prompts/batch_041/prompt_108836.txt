I retired a few years back after 35 years in atmospheric chemistry — alot of it on advisory panels, plus one long stretch helping draft those big UN climate assessment reports, where the summary has to be approved word by word by government delegates in a plenary that runs till 2 a.m. I always figured that was just how science gets translated into policy. But I've joined a reading group with a couple of grad students and we've been working through stuff on moral compromise, and one of them floated the idea that compromise is basically trade: two people with different values can both come out ahead of the disagreement point the same way two countries do when they swap goods, and it's precisely because they rank things differently that there's anything to gain. Elegant, and I don't trust it. So I want to test it against my gut on a bunch of cases, and I'd like you to tell me whether one principle gets them all or whether I have to give some up. Dont be polite about it.

Here's what I think. (1) Two departments each want a new hire; the dean gives each one line, so neither gets its top pick — fine. (2) My state rep votes for a colleague's pointless highway, the colleague votes for my rep's pointless dam, both projects are pure waste — fine, or at least that's politics and not corruption. (3) A colleague's paper has an error I'm sure about; I agree to stop pushing for a correction, he agrees to back my grant — not fine, and still not fine even if the error turns out to be harmless. (4) In the assessment report we let a sentence about our confidence get softened, from high down to medium, because two delegations said they'd block the whole summary otherwise. I did that, I'd do it again, and I still think getting the report out mattered more than the sentence. (5) A town votes to take a waste facility in exchange for a pile of money, but the risks land mostly on the poorer half of town, which voted no — not fine, even though the town as a whole comes out ahead and a majority voted for it. (6) A ceasefire with a government that's doing horrible things, which leaves it in place and hands it a bit of legitimacy, in exchange for the killing stopping now — not fine, I'd rather have no deal at all. (7) A family and an oncologist agree to stop treatment even though the doctor thinks one more round has a real chance, because pushing would wreck the trust she needs with the next family — fine. (8) A company pays a huge settlement and admits no wrongdoing — fine, everybody knows what that is.

So: is there a principle — or two or three rival ones — that captures these, stated precisely enough that I can tell what it forbids? For each clause, tell me which of my cases motivates it. Tell me which of my verdicts it forces me to drop, and which one you think I'm most likely just wrong about. Then run it on a case I didn't list — your choice, the stranger the better — since that's the only way I'll know whether the thing is doing any work or just redescribing my gut.
