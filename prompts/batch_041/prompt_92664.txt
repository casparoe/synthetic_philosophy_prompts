I'm putting together an opening session for a first-year seminar I'm calling "Minds, Machines, Moral Standing" and I want to run Kafka's Metamorphosis through the question of whether sentience comes in degrees. I'd like a reading, not a summary. Tell me if the angle holds or if I'm forcing the story onto a theory it doesn't care about.

The reason it seems promising: the story hands us a case that makes the gradiations question bite. Gregor wakes up as a vermin but keeps his memories, follows what the family says, still worries about the office and the debts, so his inner life looks continuous with a person's. Then it slides. The food he used to like stops appealing and he goes for the stale and rotting stuff. He loses interest in the furniture and in the family's business, crawls the walls, and by the end the narrative barely has him caring about anything. So inside one character, something seems to descend a scale, and the family's treatment descends with it, from Grete feeding him and cleaning his room to the apple his father puts in his back, to the lodgers, to Grete saying they have to get rid of it.

What I can't decide, and want you to sort out: is the thing that slides sentience itself, or is it the shape of his welfare -- what he's capable of caring about at all? Those seem like different claims, and I don't know if the ethics comes out differently depending on which one it is. Also, the reader gets his mind and the family only gets a body, so they're reacting to form, smell, and usefulness the whole time. Does the story end up as a reductio of the idea that our attributions of moral status track sentience, when really they track shape and disgust? And push it to the bottom of the scale for me: if you take the integrated-information / panpsychist line where even a thermostat or a logic gate has some sliver of experience, then zero isn't the floor, and I want to know whether the whole business of a cutoff is an artifact. The ending, where the parents watch Grete and start thinking about a husband for her, seems to make their relief about their own future rather than about what he was.

Please keep it honest in both directions: don't reduce the story to an illustration of a theory, and don't bend the theory to fit the story, and finish by saying what the story adds to the debate that the arguments by themselves don't. And seperate what's actually settled in this literature from what's genuinely contested -- I don't want to be told that sentience being scalar is consensus if it isn't, or that scalar moral status is obviously coherent -- and flag where you're giving me a reading of a scene that other critics would read the other way.
