I'm a backend engineer by trade — distributed systems mostly, though lately I've been working with learned components and trained policies — and I've been sitting in on a graduate seminar on free will and agency this term, largely because a friend in the department kept telling me the questions there are the ones my day job keeps bumping into. There's a notion I keep running into that I understand in a hand-wavy way but not in the way the seminar seems to want. Some behaviour is supposed to be "sphexish": rigidly mechanical, running its sequence regardless of what the situation actually calls for, and this is supposed to be precisely what a genuinely free, or at least genuinely flexible, agent is not. The picture case everyone tells involves a solitary hunting wasp that paralyses a cricket, drags it to the mouth of her burrow, leaves it on the threshold and goes inside to check that the tunnel is clear; if you nudge the cricket a few inches away while she is inside, she comes back out, drags it to the threshold again, and inspects again — and you can do that as many times as you like. The moral is always that she isn't responding to the situation, she's executing a subroutine. (The term, I gather, is a coinage of Douglas Hofstadter's, from a column in the early 1980s.) What bothers me is that I work with systems that look exactly like this from one angle and nothing like it from another. A chess engine is a fixed search procedure and a trained policy is a fixed function of its inputs, so in one sense both are maximally sphexish; yet the trained policy generalizes to situations nobody designed it for, and we would ordinarily call that flexible rather than mechanical. It seems to me the sphexish/non-sphexish contrast is doing two jobs at once — one about the grain at which you describe the behaviour, one about how much of the environment the agent is actually tracking — and that the free will discussion quietly needs only the second, while the examples always deliver the first. I also suspect the distinction is close to unfalsifiable as usually stated: any finite system is sphexish under some description, so the question has to be made comparative and perturbation-relative — roughly, how large a class of changes to the task can the system absorb before its behaviour collapses into the same fixed routine, and measured against what baseline.

State the question precisely enough that it has a determinate answer — I would much rather have a narrow question I can actually settle than a grand one I can only gesture at — then say why the existing treatments leave it open, and why the obvious move (flexibility just is responsiveness to a larger set of features of the environment) does not settle it. Describe the method: I'm happy for the project to be conceptual, but if a formal apparatus or a worked comparison of cases would do real work here, say what it would be and why that apparatus rather than another. Say what a result would look like and what would count as the project failing. Be honest about scope — tell me what part of this I would have to leave out for a semester, and what the smallest defensible version of the project is — and be willing to conclude that the distinction I'm worried about cannot be drawn in the way the free will literature wants, if that is where the argument goes. Please don't give me a reading list, citations, or references to specific papers; I'll get those from the convenor, and I'd rather the proposal stand on the shape of the problem. Don't lean on the wasp as if the anecdote were doing the philosophical work. Keep the whole thing under 900 words and organise it under four or five headings.
