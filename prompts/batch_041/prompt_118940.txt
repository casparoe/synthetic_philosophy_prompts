For thirty-five years I read spectra for a living — NMR mostly, and infrared before that — and the part of the job I never tired of was the smallest part: deciding whether two peaks were the same peak. Two spectra can agree in every measurable respect and still invite different groupings, and the whole interpretation turns on which grouping one accepts. Since retiring I have been sitting in on a graduate seminar in cognitive science, and there I came across a program from the late 1980s called Copycat, built by Douglas Hofstadter, Melanie Mitchell and their colleagues, whose entire universe is problems of the form abc is to abd as ijk is to what. What delights me is how quickly the toy stops being a toy. With ijk the answer is obvious; put x, y and z in that place and the problem becomes genuinely open, because one must now decide what a letter is — a position in a sequence that ends, a position in a sequence that circles back on itself, a token with a successor, or a mark that merely happens to sit at the end of the alphabet — and each of those readings licenses a different answer. The larger claim I take from the project is that this is no peculiarity of letters: a concept has no sharp boundary but a spreading halo of neighboring uses, and which part of the halo comes forward is settled by the situation at hand. If that is right, then conceptual slippage is not a malfunction of thought but its ordinary mode of working, and the very event that lets us catch a new analogy may be the one that lets us commit a howler.

I would like you to write a short story that makes that idea visible in a microdomain of your own invention. Please do not use the letter strings, and do not write about a computer, a programmer or an artificial intelligence; I want a human world, small enough that a reader could work an example by hand. Invent a compact domain of transformations — three cards and a rule for swapping them, a system of knotted cords used to keep accounts, the tile pattern of a floor, a bell-ringing sequence, the moves of a game played in a village square — with rules few enough to be stated in a paragraph, and give me a community entirely at home in it: a workshop, a scriptorium, a guild, a hospital ward, a court. Then let the slippage happen in a single act. One practitioner treats an element of the domain under a concept the others do not use for it, and the story must show, concretely and at the level of the objects themselves, what each reading predicts, where the two come apart, and what it costs the community to settle the question — a rule amended, a teacher embarrassed, an apprentice vindicated or dismissed. State the rules early and plainly enough that I can check the examples myself, and stage at least one worked case in which the two readings yield visibly different results, so that I can see the divergence rather than merely be told of it.

The slippage should be neither carelessness nor madness nor a trick: I want an act of analogy that is half insight and half error, so that a reader cannot easily say which it was, and I want the characters to disagree about that rather than about the facts. Keep the scale intimate — one place, few people, a short stretch of time — and let the story run to no more than a few pages. If any character articulates the underlying idea, put the words in the mouth of someone who has only half of it. End without a verdict, and without a closing sentence that tells me what the story was about.
