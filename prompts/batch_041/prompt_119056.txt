I've been roped into giving the appeal at our local refugee legal aid clinic's fundraising dinner next month — six minutes at the end, right before they pass round the pledge cards — and I'm stuck, so I'd like you to write it for me.

Here's my problem. I'm a Dummett man on immigration, and the view I hold, roughly (paraphrasing him badly, and please don't give me citations, I don't want any), is this: the duty to take in people fleeing persecution isn't charity and it doesn't wait on our having caused the trouble in the first place; and immigration control is intrinsically unjust, not just badly designed, because any system of control has to sort people by criteria that have nothing to do with what they're owed. Which means I can't give the speech the clinic wants. The standard appeal is all generosity — we're the lucky ones, imagine if it were you, give what you can — and every one of those framings quietly turns a claim of right into a plea, and turns the people in the room into benefactors. So: write the appeal without the language of charity, and without pretending that the fix is a kinder visa regime. The money, for what it's worth, goes to solicitors — asylum appeals and family reunion cases — not to blankets, so the ask is for lawyers' fees, which is its own kind of minefeild.

The room: about eighty people. Half are volunteers, students, and two immigration solicitors who will notice every move you make. The other half are local business people who came because a friend bought a table, and a good few of them think the borders should be tighter, not looser. The chair has asked me to keep it "non-politcal", which as far as I can tell means don't say anything about the government, so you'll have to decide what can be said under that constraint and what's worth breaking it for. It has to end with the actual ask for money, and it has to be warm — these are decent people and I'm not going to stand there and lecture them.

Please commit to a line and don't hedge it into "some philosophers argue". If you think the right move is to rest the ask on our complicity rather than on the bare claim of what's owed, take that position and say why you took it. Then, underneath the speech, give me a short note on where you've made the hardest call and what you'd say if someone at the next table pushed back on it.
