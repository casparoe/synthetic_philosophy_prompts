I am assembling a handout for a graduate seminar on the challenge Glaucon presses on Socrates in the second book of the Republic — the shepherd who finds a ring that makes him invisible, uses it to seduce the queen and take the throne, and the suggestion that anyone, just or unjust, would do the same if only he could escape detection — and I would like the handout to take the form of a decision tree rather than a list of famous answers to the question of why one should be just at all.

The tree should be organized around what an answer is trying to supply, not around the customary sequence of names. Its first split should ask what the challenge is asking for: a justification that gives the just agent a reason to be just, a motive or mechanism explaining why people in fact comply, or a denial that the challenge is well posed on the ground that it presupposes morality to be an optional constraint an agent might step outside of. Later splits should separate, among other things, answers that concede the amoralist's premise that reasons are ultimately self-regarding from answers that reject it; answers resting on detection and enforcement from answers resting on internalized disposition, self-respect, or the good of the agent's own character; answers treating the just life as instrumentally best for the agent from answers treating it as constitutive of practical reason; and answers that explain the practice of morality from answers that vindicate it.

Every internal node must be phrased as a question a reader can answer without already knowing which position it leads to, and the tree should carry between eight and twelve leaves and no more than nine internal nodes. Each leaf should be labeled with the name of the position where a standard one exists and marked unnamed where none does, and should carry one sentence on its most awkward commitment and one sentence on which of the following cases it handles worst: the ring-bearer who never uses the ring because he has no desire to; the ring-bearer whose invisibility is common knowledge, so that no one can be deceived; an agent who is just and thoroughly miserable; an agent whose moral commitments are settled and to whom the challenge never occurs; and an amoralist who understands every argument on offer and remains unmoved.

Three further requirements. First, build the tree from the space of possible answers before locating named positions in it, and note which combinations of branch answers remain unoccupied and whether that is because the view is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. Second, flag explicitly any pairs of leaves your questions cannot separate, rather than papering over the gap. Third, close with a short note on which leaves survive when the ring is replaced by the anonymity available in ordinary life — unobserved transactions, large cities, online conduct — since that is what I want the seminar to argue about.

No citations and no reading list: the handout should be usable by a reader with no library at hand.
