I have been reading LessWrong and the surrounding rationalist blogosphere for the better part of a decade, and I keep returning to the cluster of questions around moral testimony: when, if ever, may one person let another person's moral judgment stand in for their own? A friend of mine—call her M—has thought about this longer than I have, and I have a decent track record of predicting her verdicts on new cases. But her verdicts so far are confounded in a way that makes the prediction non-trivial.

Here is what she has said about earlier cases.

- A first-year student who knows almost nothing about factory farming reads a well-supported expert consensus that the practice is morally indefensible and believes it on that basis. M says the student can know the moral claim, and relying on testimony is fine.
- A tenured moral philosopher is deciding whether to publicly accuse a former mentor of intellectual dishonesty. The philosopher has all the non-moral facts but cannot decide whether the accusation is morally warranted, and adopts a colleague's verdict without working through the arguments. M says this is not fine; the philosopher may end up with the right answer, but not with the kind of moral understanding that matters.
- A person who has never thought about whether gratuitous cruelty is wrong is told by a reliable teacher that it is wrong, and accepts it immediately. M says this is fine; some moral truths are basic enough that testimony can put you in a position to see them for yourself.
- A person with no experience in charitable giving follows a trusted advice columnist's recommendation about which of two effective charities to support. M says this is fine.
- A person raised in a community whose moral code is systematically shaped by its members' economic interests accepts that code's verdict on a disputed question, sincerely and without coercion. M says this is not fine, even if the code is internally coherent and the person is epistemically reliable by local standards.
- A moral philosopher who has been betrayed asks a colleague whether to forgive the betrayer. M says this is not fine; the decision has to be the philosopher's own, even if the colleague is right.
- A person with no independent grasp of the ethics of algorithmic hiring reads a well-reasoned consensus statement by a panel of moral philosophers and adopts its verdict on a novel case. M says this is fine, or at least permissible.

Notice the pattern: every case where she permits reliance is one where the person is a novice and the decision is not constitutive of their identity; every case where she forbids it is one where the person is an expert and the decision is identity-constitutive. I cannot tell which feature is doing the work.

Now the new case. A person who is not a novice—someone who has read a good deal of moral philosophy and can follow arguments, though not an expert on this particular issue—is trying to decide whether to support a complex new policy. They cannot work out the moral verdict themselves; the considerations are too tangled. They ask a highly reliable large language model, which gives a clear moral verdict and a concise rationale. After reading the rationale, the person sees why the verdict follows and endorses it, but they would not have reached it on their own. The decision is not constitutive of their identity; it is a policy question. M is asked: was it permissible for the person to rely on the model's moral testimony?

What will M say? I care less about the bare yes or no than about the rule she seems to be running. Then name the further case that would best tell those rules apart, and say what each rule would say about it.
