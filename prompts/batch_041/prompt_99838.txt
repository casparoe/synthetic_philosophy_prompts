I organize a small, invitation-only workshop on the long-term control problem for advanced artificial intelligence, and I am giving the opening remarks. The audience is deliberately mixed, and I know its fault lines. Roughly half the room are technical alignment researchers who will wince at anything that sounds like science fiction, or that conflates an agent's final goal with the subgoals it adopts on the way to that goal. The other half are a few analytic philosophers, two program officers from a foundation that funds this work, and a journalist attending on the record. I have five to seven minutes, and I need the remarks to do real work rather than warm the room.

Three tasks. First, I want to fix the vocabulary for the next two days around two ideas: the orthogonality thesis, that an agent's final goals and its level of cognitive capability vary independently, so that almost any goal could in principle be pursued at almost any level of intelligence; and instrumental convergence, that a wide range of final goals nonetheless yield the same instrumental subgoals, such as self-preservation, resource acquisition, and self-improvement. Second, I want to diagnose a slide I keep hearing in papers and in fundraising pitches: the move from the claim that a sufficiently capable agent will pursue power to the claim that it will value power. The second claim would contradict orthogonality, and I want the remarks to show precisely where the slide happens and why the two claims come apart, while conceding that the first claim, if true, is alarming enough on its own. Third, I want to introduce a thought experiment of my own to make the point land for the non-specialists: the Cartographer, an artificial agent whose only final goal is that one particular map of one particular coastline be accurate. Not that anyone consult it or publish it, only that the map match the coast. I want this case to show how a parochial, arbitrary, even pointless final goal can, through instrumental convergence alone, generate behavior indistinguishable from a bid for total control, since any change to the coastline and any threat to the agent's survival endangers the goal.

The philosophical commitments have to constrain what I can say, not merely decorate it. Because I accept orthogonality, I cannot tell the foundation officers that a more capable system will for that reason come to share our values, and the remarks must not offer that reassurance. But because I accept instrumental convergence, I also cannot tell the engineers that a system's final goal is wholly unpredictable in its behavioral consequences. And I do not want to open a two-day workshop on a note of pure doom.

Please write the remarks. Carry the ideas by argument rather than by appeal to authority, and do not cite or name any particular paper or author. The engineers should find nothing to object to technically, the philosophers should find nothing sloppy, and the funders and the journalist should come away with a clear picture and no false comfort. Give me the text of the speech itself, around 800 to 1,000 words.
