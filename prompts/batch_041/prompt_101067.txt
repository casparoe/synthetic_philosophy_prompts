I'm an undergraduate in a moral philosophy course, and something from our unit on supererogation has been bothering me. I keep noticing that whether an action counts as going beyond the call of duty seems to depend less on what is done than on who does it. A lifeguard who dives in to save a swimmer is just doing her job; a passerby who does the same thing is a hero. Someone who gives blood easily is thanked less than someone who is terrified of needles and gives anyway. And when a person keeps doing more than is required, the extra seems to get absorbed into what is now expected of them, so the very same act stops being supererogatory the second time around. Is there a single established name for this family of cases, where the "beyond-ness" of an act is fixed by facts about the agent -- their role, what the act costs them, what they have done before -- rather than by the act itself? If there is no such name, I'd like you to propose a term and define it, and either way I want the edges: how does this differ from ordinary role obligations, from the view that supererogation is relative to what an agent is able to do, and from the plain fact that praise is sometimes undeserved? I would much rather be told that I am lumping together things that do not belong together than be handed a tidy label that hides the seams, so please push back if the pattern I'm describing is confused or dissolves on inspection. Please answer in American English.
