So a friend of mine in our phil of science reading group — shes writing on the reflection principle, van Fraassens thing where your future credences are supposed to be your guide — has got this arguement that I think proves way too much, and I can't find the seam in it. Here it is, more or less in her words:

(1) If you're certain that at some later time your credence in A will be x, your credence in A right now should be x. Your future self is you plus more evidence, so its opinion is a better guide than yours and you should be able to adopt it in advance. That's the whole point of the principle.
(2) Suppose I'm certain that tomorrow my credence in A will be 0.9. Not probably — I've taken this pill before, I know exactly what it does to me, and I know it'll do that.
(3) The pill isn't evidence about A. It doesn't track A at all, it just makes me feel confident. So my evidence about A tomorrow will be exactly what it is today.
(4) By (1) and (2), my credence in A right now should be 0.9.
(5) By (3), my credence in A right now should be whatever my present evidence supports — say 0.2.
(6) So (1) is false.
(7) And it isn't about pills. Any principle that makes my credences now anticipate my credences later, whatever causes the later ones, makes me hostage to whatever nonsense I'll believe. So the idea that you can read your future selfs opinion off in advance and adopt it is just wrong.

I can't accept (6)/(7), because (1) has to be right in the good cases: if I know that tomorrow, after I've read the lab report, I'll be 0.9 confident that the drug works, then I should be 0.9 confident now — that's just learning something early, and telling me I shouldn't would be telling me not to trust evidence I already know is on its way. So I want to keep (1) where it matters and kill it where it's absurd, and I want to know which step in her argument is actually doing the damage.

Two things. First, name the step that goes wrong — or, if nothing in the argument is invalid as she's stated it, say so and tell me which bit of the principle itself is the culprit — and explain why it looked fine. Second, price the fixes: is there a version of the principle that keeps the lab report and kills the pill, or is every such version gerrymandered to fit the cases I already like? And here's the case that actually worries me — suppose the pill is 60% reliable, hypnotism that installs true beliefs about A most of the time. Then neither 0.2 nor 0.9 looks right to me, and I suspect the principle has nothing to say here. If that's right, tell me what I should actually do with a known future credence, and whether the reflection principle was ever doing any work or was just a special case of ordinary conditionalization all along. Also, if I keep (1) restricted and (5) unrestricted, versus the other way round, what does each choice cost me?
