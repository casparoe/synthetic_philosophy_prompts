For thirty years I was a field ecologist, and I used the theory of games the way one uses a borrowed tool — competently, and without ever asking what the tool was. Since retiring I have been reading philosophy in earnest, which has been by turns a pleasure and a humiliation, and I have lately gone back over the elementary ideas of choice and strategy with an attention I never gave them the first time. One small worked example has taken hold of me, and I would like to commission a poem that enacts it. I already have the explanation; what I lack is something I can hold in the mind all at once, and I have come to believe that verse is the instrument for that. So, the example, and then the poem.

Two parties choose at the same time. The first — call it the chooser — has three options, A, B and C. The second — call it the responder — has three, X, Y and Z. The chooser's payoffs, in the usual arrangement:

|   | X | Y | Z |
|---|---|---|---|
| A | 5 | 5 | 0 |
| B | 2 | 3 | 4 |
| C | 3 | 3 | 1 |

and the responder's, in the same cases:

|   | X | Y | Z |
|---|---|---|---|
| A | 3 | 1 | 1 |
| B | 2 | 3 | 1 |
| C | 1 | 1 | 3 |

Here is what I find when I work through it; I have checked it twice, and the poem should follow it.

No single option of the chooser's disposes of C. Against Z, A does worse than C — 0 against 1 — and against X, B does worse than C — 2 against 3. But A and B taken together, half of each, say, do better than C in every case: 3.5 against 3, 4 against 3, 2 against 1. So C can be struck out, but only by a blend, and only because blending is permitted at all. That permission is the hinge of the whole thing.

Once C is gone the board does not settle; it unravels, in this order. The responder's Z was only ever the best answer to C — it pays 3 against C and 1 against either A or B — and with C gone, X does better than Z against both of the chooser's remaining options, so Z goes. The chooser's B was only ever the best answer to Z — it pays 4 against Z, and 2 and 3 against X and Y — and with Z gone, A does better than B in both remaining cases, so B goes. The responder's Y was only ever the best answer to B, and with B gone, X beats it, so Y goes. What is left is A and X, each the best answer to the other. And — the part I most want the poem to carry — had the blending of options been forbidden, none of this would have happened: no option is beaten by another in every case, nothing is ever struck out, and the board stands exactly as it began. The entire cascade hangs on the permission to take half of one option and half of another.

What I would like:

1. Five stanzas, of six, five, four, three and two lines. One line for each option still standing, so that the stanzas thin out exactly as the board thins out, and the last two lines belong to A and X alone.
2. Let the six options be voices — each with an image, a trade or a verbal habit that recurs — so that a reader can tell, stanza by stanza, who has fallen silent. Keep the letters if you need them for clarity, but the poem should not read like a table.
3. The fall of C must be visibly the work of two voices and not one. Whether the fall is announced at the turn of a stanza or afterwards I leave to you; but the condemnation should be carried across the A-line and the B-line of the following stanza in such a way that a reader who covers either line and reads the other alone finds the case against C collapse. It must collapse: each of those two is beaten by C in one of the three cases.
4. The later falls should feel like consequences rather than decisions — Z falls because C fell, B because Z fell, Y because B fell. If a reader can feel the chain, the poem has done its work.
5. Do not explain the theory; be it. The vocabulary of the subject should not appear — no option in the poem need be called dominant, eliminated or an equilibrium — and I want no references of any kind; the poem should stand on the example alone.
6. If it can be managed without strain, let the closing couplet also hold the thought that all of this depended on the blend being allowed, and one related thing I have only just learned and may well be reporting badly: that so long as the test for striking an option out is strict, the order of striking cannot change where you end up, and that it is only when the test is loosened, when an option may be struck out for doing merely as well as another, that the order begins to matter.

I will count it a success if someone who knows this material can reconstruct the board from the poem, and someone who has never seen a payoff table finds in it a good poem about a structure coming apart under a single permission.

Finally, after the poem, on a line of its own, give me a one-sentence summary of what the poem's shape enacts about the elimination, introduced by the word Summary and a colon, with nothing following it but that sentence.
