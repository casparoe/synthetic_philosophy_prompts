Last night my friend Priya and I got into it for like two and a half hours at the all-night diner about whether any belief can be justified by something that isn't itself a belief. She's a definate foundationalist — there's got to be a floor, some stuff you just get to know — and I kept pushing that every floor she names is just another claim that needs a floor under it, and by the time they kicked us out we were both just repeating ourselves and she said "fine, then the chain goes on forever and that's fine," which I don't think she actually believes. Anyway, I want to try writing a short story that makes this whole foundationalism/coherentism/infinitism mess concrete instead of just three names on a slide, and I'd like you to write it for me.

Here's the seed, change whatever you need: a city where you're only permitted to act on a claim if you hold a stamped warrant for it, and a warrant only counts if it's endorsed by an earlier warrant, which needs one too, and so on. The protagonist is a junior clerk in the archive. Someone files a request that can only be granted by citing the very first warrant in the city's history, and the archive's rules are (a) no warrant may endorse itself, (b) no warrant may endorse a warrant that isn't already on file, and (c) warrants have been filed continuously for three hundred years and nobody, including the supervisor, has ever seen the founding one. The supervisor's advice is to just cite the oldest one on the shelf, since everyone does and nobody checks. I want the story to take seriously that the chain really might run forever and that this isn't a defect — so don't make the infinite option the punchline or the joke, and don't let her solve it by unearthing a bedrock document. But I also don't want her to just shrug: give her someplace to land, even if it's an uneasy place. One thing I do want in there: a moment where she realizes that any rule you could cite to say "stop here, this one doesn't need a warrant" would itself be the kind of thing that needs a warrant, so the stopping rule is doing exactly what the rules forbid. That should be a real snag in the story, not a throwaway line.

No lectures, please: no character at a whiteboard explaining the three options by name, no "and so the moral is." Let the archive's rules do the philosophy. Maybe 1500-2000 words. And if you can swing it, make the circular option — a small set of warrants that endorse each other and nothing else, so the pile holds itself up — genuinely tempting, because that's the one I find hardest to argue against and I'd like to see what it looks like when someone actually tries to live inside it.
