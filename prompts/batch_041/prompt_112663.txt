I run a small bargaining/decision-theory reading group — about a dozen people, mix of econ grad students and philosophy people who've each had one game theory course — 90 minutes, one room, and next month its my turn to lead. I want to do the Kalai–Smorodinsky solution and I want an activity that makes them feel why anyone would want it, rather than a slide they nod at. In case it's not at your fingertips: you take the disagreement point d and the ideal/utopia point a (each player's best feasible payoff), draw the straight line from d to a, and where it meets the Pareto frontier is the solution — so each side ends up with the same fraction of the gain it could have made over disagreement. It's what you get if you keep Pareto, symmetry and scale-invariance but swap independence of irrelevant alternatives for a monotonicity condition (roughly: if one player's options improve at every level of what the other can get, that player can't end up worse off). Nash keeps IIA and can therefore hand you less when your options improve. The two solutions coincide on symmetric problems, so the asymmetric cases have to carry the whole session.

What I want them to do is actually negotiate, in pairs, over a real payoff table, for real tiny stakes — candy, or points that buy nothing but that they'll somehow still care about. Then we pool every pair's outcome on the board and compare the distribution to where Nash says they should land and where K-S says they should land. The tables need to be built so the naive split-the-difference answer, the Nash point and the K-S point are three different places; otherwise the session falls flat. Then a second round where I change the feasible set by adding an option that raises one player's ideal but is so lopsided nobody would ever pick it, and we watch whether the outcomes move. If they move, we've got IIA by the throat and I never had to say the words.

Give it to me as something I can actually run: the payoff tables (a few, so I can pick), what the pairs see versus what only I see, how long each part takes, how pairs report their outcome, how to tally and display it on a whiteboard, and a debrief that maps specific moments in the activity onto Pareto, symmetry, scale-invariance, and monotonicity-vs-IIA, plus the equal-proportional-gains idea — what's genuinely appealing about it and what's arguably crazy. The dependence on the ideal point is the part I find hardest to defend, so make the debrief land on that.

My guess is at least one pair will refuse to engage and just split 50/50 out of politeness, and someone will insist the results are only telling us about risk aversion — give me fixes for both.
