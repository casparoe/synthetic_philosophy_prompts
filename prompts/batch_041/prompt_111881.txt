I'm a third-year PhD student in economics, working on welfare measurement, and I keep getting dragged into the same unresolved argument in my department's reading group. It concerns what "utility" is actually a name for, and the two sides have been circling each other for months without either giving ground. On one side are the people who take the ordinalist settlement seriously: utility is a representation of choice and nothing more — a numerical index assigned so that observed choices come out as maximization, carrying no commitment to any inner state or felt quality. On this view, preference just is the pattern of choice, welfare comparisons are licensed only up to what the choice data can bear, and asking what someone "really" wants or feels over and above what they do is either a confusion or a different subject matter entirely. On the other side are the people who think this emptied-out object cannot do the work we ask of it: they point out that when choices are reference-dependent, menu-dependent, or unstable across elicitation procedures, the revealed-preference object either fails to exist or becomes circular, and they want to reintroduce something like experienced well-being — a substantive, in-principle measurable quantity of how a life is going — as the thing welfare economics is ultimately about, with choice demoted to evidence for it rather than a definition of it. Both sides are careful, both know the other's literature, and yet the exchange never gets past the same trade of intuitions.

My own suspicion, which I'd like you to test rather than flatter, is that this is a hybrid case: part of it is a verbal dispute, since the two sides have quietly fixed on different referents for the single word "utility" and are then surprised to disagree about its properties; part of it is a genuine disagreement about whether there is a fact of the matter about how well a life is going that is independent of what the person chooses; and part of it is a methodological disagreement about what may legitimately be measured and aggregated, in which operationalist and realist instincts are doing most of the work. What I want from you is a diagnosis rather than a verdict: tell me what kind of disagreement this actually is, using what each side says about the specific cases that divide them — the coherence requirements imposed on preferences, the status of choice data when it is inconsistent, and the legitimacy of interpersonal welfare comparisons — and separate the strands that are merely terminological from the ones that are not. Then tell me what, if anything, would count as progress here: a distinction, a formal result about what choice data can identify, a change of question, or an argument that the impasse is exactly what one should expect and that no resolution is owed. I'd rather have a diagnosis I can argue with than a survey.
