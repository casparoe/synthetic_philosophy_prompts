I'm putting together the question set for a small reading group I run — we do one long-form essay prompt a month, everyone writes something, then we spend the session arguing about it — and this month I want the "do the numbers count?" one, i.e. Taurek's line that if you can save either one person or five, you're not required to save the five. But I dont want the vanilla version of the question, since everyone's written that one before and can more or less do it on autopilot. So here are three things I want the essay forced to chew on. First: the standard anti-aggregation move is to say you should toss a coin between the one and the five, so that nobody's life gets weighed against anybody else's. But look at what the coin actually does — it puts the five on one side and the one on the other, so the five get fused into a single unit, which is exactly the fusing we were told not to do, whereas a genuine lottery over all six people, one ticket each, is itself an aggregation, just of the other kind. So it looks like there may be no way to be fair here that doesn't aggregate somewhere, and I want that dilemma taken seriously instead of waved off. Second: run it repeatedly. Five separate incidents, each one person against five, each time I'm permitted to toss the coin. Across the five incidents the coin toss is expected to kill fifteen people, always saving the five kills five, always saving the one kills twenty-five. If it's really a permission, why does exercising it five times look so much worse than exercising it once, and does the anti-aggregation view have to be a view about one-off choices only? Third: in one of the five incidents the lone person is my brother. My hunch is that partiality is doing a lot of hidden work in how people feel about all of this, and I want to see whether the anti-aggregation view needs partiality as a premise or whether it can stand without it.

So: write the essay. Give me the strongest version of the case that the numbers don't count — the separateness of persons, the thought that there's no common currency in which lives can be added up, the idea that what each person is owed is an equal chance rather than a comparison — then the strongest replies, and then land somewhere. Does the coin toss survive the fairness point and the repetition point, or do the numbers count after all, and if they do, in what sense and above what threshold, if any. I'd much rather have a sharp verdict I can disagree with than a survey that lists everything and commits to nothing.

One thing though: be careful to seperate what's actually settled among people who've worked on this from what's genuinely contested, and don't hand me a contested position dressed up as the consensus — say plainly where you're unsure or where the answer just depends on a premise we could reasonably argue about. And no headings, no bullet points, no bold, no tables — continuous prose only, the way you'd write a long email to someone you actually like arguing with.
