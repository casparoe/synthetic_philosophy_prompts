My mate Dave and I got into it for like three hours last night about whether directly electing a president is a catastrophy waiting to happen, and I'm still annoyed about it, mostly because I have to give a speech on Sunday and now all his points are lodged in my head.

Here's the setup. I got randomly picked for a citizens' assembly that's been meeting for about a month to recommend a new constitution for our country. Sunday is the vote on which proposal goes forward, and each of us gets five minutes to make the case for ours. Mine is the one that splits the executive, and there's a decent chance it loses to the "just copy the Westminster model, it obviously works" option, so I actually need to be persuasive and not just correct.

The room is about 120 ordinary citizens who've been sitting in these meetings for a month, so they know the basics but not the jargon, plus maybe six constitutional-law academics who've been advising us and will absolutely clock it if I say something sloppy. My mum is also coming, which shouldn't matter but somehow does.

What I'm arguing for, and what Dave thinks is a fudge: a president elected directly for a single six-year term who appoints the prime minister; parliament can only remove the PM if it names a replacement in the same vote (so you don't get an endless death spiral of snap elections); and if it does remove them, the president can't dissolve parliament for a year. The president gets a suspensive veto over ordinary legislation only, which parliament can override with a second vote. Dave says this is the worst of both worlds. His line is that you get two competing claims to democratic legitimacy with no clean way to settle them, you keep all the horse-trading and backroom party-elite stuff of a parliamentary system, and you've fixed the president's term so the public can't even kick them out. I think his whole case for the pure parliamentary option ignores that voters never actually get to choose the head of government in those systems — the party picks the PM after the election — so the "short chain of accountability" he keeps going on about is a chain to the party, not to the voters.

What I need from the speech: it has to land for the 120 people who don't know the phrase "constructive vote of no confidence" from a hole in the ground, without being condescending, and it has to survive the six academics. It has to be honest that Dave's objection is a real cost and not pretend the hybrid is free. And it can't just be a pros-and-cons list — I have to actually make the case and stick my neck out. Please don't invent statistics or cite studies, since I can't check any of them and I'll get caught; argue from the logic of the design and from what we can reason about. No reading list.

Write me the five-minute speech.

And then on the very last line by itself, put the one sentence I'd want them to walk away with, formatted exactly as "Takeaway: ..."
