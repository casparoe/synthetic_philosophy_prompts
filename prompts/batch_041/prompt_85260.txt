I'm a second-year PhD student in political theory working on policing and legitimacy, and their's an analogy that keeps surfacing in workshops that I can't decide weather to use or to kill on sight. Someone says: the police are the immune system of the body politic. Crime and disorder are the pathogens, officers are the cells that identify and neutralise them, and a society without police is immunocompromised, so defunding is immunosuppression. Then someone flips it: in that case the over-policing of poor and Black neighbourhoods is autoimmune disease, the system attacking the body it's meant to defend, and that's the argument for abolition. Both camps seem to think the analogy is carrying them, and my suspicion is that it's carrying whatever conclusion they walked in with rather than one they earned.

Can you audit it? I want the mapping spelled out (what plays the role of the pathogen, the self/nonself boundary, the immune response, the fever, the memory cells), then the places it breaks, and then some sorting of which breaks matter for the two conclusions and which are just noise. Two things I especially want you to press on. First the immunology: my understanding is that the field has largely moved off self/nonself towards a danger-signal picture, where the system responds to distress cues rather than to foreignness as such. Does swapping in the newer picture rescue the analogy -- policing as harm-response rather than us-and-them -- or does it just relocate the problem somewhere worse? Second, discretion and accountability: immune cells can't be sued, don't have a chain of command, and don't have a legitimacy problem, and I think that's precisely where the interesting part of the policing case lives. Also, does the analogy quietly assume that crime is exogenous, a pathogen arriving from outside, when alot of it looks like something the body itself produces? And tell me which of reform / defund / abolish it can actually support -- if it's compatible with all three then it isn't an argument, and I'd rather know that now. If there's a better analogy lurking, I'll take that too.
