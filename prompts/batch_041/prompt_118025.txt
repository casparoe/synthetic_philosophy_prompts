I'm a second-year grad student in a philosophy department and I've somehow ended up running our interdisciplinary reading group this term — nine people, mostly not philosophers: two linguists, a CS person, someone from the law school, and a couple of MA students who between them have done one logic course. Next session is mine and the topic is Bradley's regress and the unity of the proposition. I've read enough to know what I want to say but every time I rehearse it out loud I get tangled, so please don't explain the regress to me — plan how I should explain it to them, in about seventy minutes with a whiteboard. What I want is the order, with the dependencies made explicit: what has to be in place before "unity" is even a live question for them (my instinct is that it's the distinction between a bunch of things and one thing, but I can't tell whether that comes first or whether I need something about what a proposition is supposed to be doing before that lands), where the regress actually gets generated and how to stage it so it feels like a trap rather than a magic trick, and where the fork opens — that you either treat the relation as one more item, in which case you need something to attach it to the others, or you refuse to, in which case you owe an account of what the relation does instead. Then the carrying example: I want one case to do the work for the whole hour. My instinct is a chess position — the pieces plus the fact that they're arranged thus — or maybe just "a is left of b," but I suspect the chess thing smuggles in rules and intentions and that the bare two-place relation is too thin to hang the last third of the session on. Tell me which one and why, and what the example has to have and has to lack.

The misconceptions are the part I most want, because I've watched this go wrong three different ways. One person will hear "regress" and assume regresses are automatically fatal — I want them to see the real question is whether this one is vicious, and that a serious option is denying the demand for a further unifier altogether. Someone else will decide the argument proves relations aren't real and leap straight to Bradley's monism, which I definitely don't want to teach as the punchline. And the CS person will say sets solve it, which is exactly the move I want to provoke rather than squash — I want them to say it out loud and then have to answer what unifies the set. Also, having come into philosophy from computer science myself, I spent a good year thinking Frege's unsaturated concepts were a refutation of the regress rather than a relocation of it, and I don't want to plant that. For each misconception, tell me the precise moment in the sequence where I should deal with it — before it's voiced, the instant it's voiced, or by letting it stand for ten minutes and then turning it — and why that timing and not another.

Finally: a couple of things I should openly leave out and announce that I'm leaving out, and one question to ask at the end that would show me they've got the shape of the problem rather than memorised the word "vicious." And if the honest answer is that this can't be done in seventy minutes with that crowd, say so and tell me what to cut — I'd much rather cut than bluff. No reading list, we don't do those.
