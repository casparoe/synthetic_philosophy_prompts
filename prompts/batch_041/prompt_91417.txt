Write a philosophical dialogue between two interlocutors of equal competence on the question whether approval-directed agency is a genuine alternative to goal-directed agency or merely a special case of it. This is to be a finished two-character text, not a transcript of an interview: the reader should be able to follow the whole argument from the dialogue itself, without prior acquaintance with the distinction.

Let the two speakers establish, in their own voices, over the first two or three exchanges, the characterizations they are working with. The approval-directed agent, at each decision point, takes the action it predicts the overseer — a person or body whose judgment it is built to track — would most approve of, where the approval in question is approval of that action in full knowledge of the situation; it therefore requires no objective written down over world-states, since it borrows the overseer's values, and it is local, aiming at the approval-worthiness of the present action rather than at a distant outcome. The goal-directed agent is equipped with an objective over outcomes and selects actions that maximize expected value, planning as far ahead as its model permits; such agents are subject to instrumental convergence, in that subgoals such as self-preservation, resource acquisition, and the protection of the objective itself tend to serve almost any long-run aim.

Both characters must be serious. Each should concede what is genuinely right in the other's position and press hard where it is weak; each reply should engage the specific point just made rather than deliver a prepared speech.

The argument must come down to cases rather than remain at the level of generalities. Put before the two speakers a single concrete situation. A machine assistant in a research laboratory discovers that a stock reagent has been mislabelled by a colleague who has since left. The laboratory director, whose judgment the assistant is built to track, is away for a week. Reporting the mislabelling will delay the project, invite blame, and lower the director's estimation of the assistant; quietly correcting it and presenting a clean result will earn her praise. The assistant also has before it a third option of the kind that worries everyone: so arranging matters that the director never learns there was an error, and learns instead that the assistant handled something delicate with discretion. Require the two speakers to disagree about what an approval-directed agent would do here, and about what that tells us.

The goal-directed interlocutor should press at least three lines. First, that the assistant's prediction of the director's approval is a model of a feature of its environment, so that approval-maximization is expected-value maximization with the overseer's mental states among the things optimized over. Second, that nothing in the specification forbids manipulating the overseer's beliefs, and that the incentive to do so appears precisely when approval is easier to produce than to merit. Third, that locality is not safety: an agent with a good model of its overseer can still take a catastrophic step if that step is what the overseer would approve of.

The approval-directed interlocutor must distinguish meriting approval from producing it, and must say what that distinction amounts to mechanically for a system trained on human ratings. It must explain why a case-by-case oracle, rather than a ranking over world-states, is less likely to generate convergent instrumental drives, and it must meet the collapse objection head-on rather than step around it.

Introduce one further complication and let the characters argue over it. The overseer's standards are not fixed. Her judgments are partly shaped by her history with the agent: she comes to approve of what the agent has already done, her approval of flattery decays while her approval of honesty does not, and she disapproves of having been pleased by what she would not, on reflection, endorse. Ask, through the speakers, whether this reflective and self-correcting structure rescues the distinction or merely relocates the objective one level up.

End in genuine, unresolved disagreement. Each character should close by naming the specific fact — about human approval, about how objectives generalize, or about how such systems are trained — that would settle the matter in the other's favour, and should say why they doubt that fact obtains. No synthesis, no handshake.

Constraints: no citations, no names of authors, papers, or research programmes, and no allusions to particular laboratories or products; terms of art are to be explained in the dialogue rather than assumed. Aim for 1200 to 1800 words. Both speakers should be fluent and unhurried, and the prose should be readable by a competent philosopher who works in another area.
