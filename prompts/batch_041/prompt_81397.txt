Since last autumn I have been working part-time in the conservation studio of a small regional museum, mostly ceramics and metalwork — cleaning, consolidating, reassembling — and the work has turned the old statue-and-clay puzzle from a seminar curiosity into something I think about with a scalpel in my hand. No one in the studio believes we have made a new pot when we glue a shattered one back together, and no one believes we have made a different pot when we decide to leave a crack visible; yet the accession records, the insurance schedules, and the loan agreements all treat the object as one thing with one continuous biography, and I have come to see how much of that identity rests on facts that are not physical at all — the maker's intention, the curators' recognition, the institution's willingness to keep counting the thing as a work. I am also taking a graduate seminar on material constitution, and every case we read turns on rearranging or replacing matter: breaking, melting, rebuilding, swapping planks one at a time. I would like the case that runs the other way — where the matter never moves and the metaphysics is nevertheless supposed to change.

Please construct a thought experiment to the following specifications. The scenario must make the constitution view and the identity view disagree about whether anything comes into or goes out of existence at a particular moment, and the disagreement must be generated entirely by a change in a relational, historical, or normative fact, with every intrinsic, physical, and causal fact held fixed. By the constitution view I mean the position that the clay and the statue are two numerically distinct objects occupying the same place at the same time; by the identity view, the position that there is only ever one object there, now described as clay and now as a statue. Further constraints: (i) exactly one portion of matter is involved, and it is never added to, subtracted from, or rearranged; (ii) the only thing that changes is something of the sort that can be altered by fiat, by a change of mind, or by an act of recognition — an intention, a designation, a status, an entry in a record — and it can be changed back again; (iii) no science fiction, no teleportation or duplication, no supernatural intervention, and no appeal to vagueness about which atoms compose the object; (iv) the scenario must not assume its own conclusion, so that neither view can complain that the case has already stipulated that a new object did or did not appear; (v) keep the scenario itself to a single paragraph. If you can manage it, make the case turn on the object's ceasing to be a statue rather than on its becoming one, since the coming-into-existence direction is the one that has been worked to death.

Then do the demonstration. For each view, state what it says exists before and after the change, and show that the verdict follows from that view's own principles rather than from the wording of the case. Add a one-line verdict from the position that there are no statues at all, only lumps of matter, and say whether it lands with the identity view or with the constitution view, and why. Then tell me which single feature of the case is doing the work; what becomes of the disagreement if that feature is deleted, and whether the case then collapses back into the ordinary lump-and-statue case; and how the defender of whichever view comes out worse would attack the case — I want the strongest objection available, not a straw man. Finally, answer the question the case is for: does it show that the identity view must allow the existence of a material object to depend on facts that are not physical facts, or can it escape that conclusion — and if it escapes, at what cost, given that the two coincident objects of the constitution view appear to share every intrinsic property and so would need something non-intrinsic to tell them apart?
