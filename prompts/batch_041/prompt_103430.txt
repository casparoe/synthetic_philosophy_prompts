My closest friend and I have been arguing for weeks about whether the First World War was overdetermined, and last night it came to a head without either of us giving ground. His position is that the war was going to happen regardless — the alliance system, the Balkan rivalry, the Anglo-German naval race, the Schlieffen Plan, the whole apparatus — so that Sarajevo was merely the spark and any of a dozen other incidents would have served. Mine is that "overdetermined" is doing no real work in that sentence; it sounds like a compliment paid to a conclusion he already held. I would like you to adjudicate, but not by retelling the July Crisis, which I know. What I want is a disciplined analysis that runs in both directions, and I am happy for you to tell me I am the one who is wrong.

Begin with the concept itself. Set out what overdetermination genuinely requires — two or more causes, each individually sufficient for the same effect, so that the effect would have occurred even had any one of them been absent — and separate it cleanly from two neighbors it is usually confused with: mere multi-causal or conjunctural explanation, in which each cause is necessary and only the set is sufficient, and a trigger or occasion, which is neither. I am also aware that the word reached history by at least two other routes, one psychoanalytic (dreams and symptoms have multiple determinants) and one Marxist (a social formation is determined by several contradictions at once); say whether the historian's usage owes more to either of those than to the metaphysicians', and whether that matters for the analysis.

Then map the case onto the concept. Which of the candidate causes are plausibly sufficient on their own, and which are only jointly sufficient; is the assassination a cause, a trigger, or a pretext; and does the fact that the earlier crises — Morocco, the Bosnian annexation, the Balkan Wars — did not produce a general war count against the sufficiency of any single factor? Finish with the reverse direction: what the case shows about overdetermination as a historiographical tool. Is it an explanatory model, an expression of inevitability, or an admission that no single cause can be isolated? And if it fails here, is there a better account — causal preemption, a probabilistic counterfactual, something else — or is the honest verdict that the theory is simply silent about the question my friend and I were really asking?
