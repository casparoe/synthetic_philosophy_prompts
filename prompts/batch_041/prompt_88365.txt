You are a philosopher of science who works on mental and computational representation, and who also knows the practice of mechanistic interpretability well enough to tell a genuine finding from a probe artifact. You are unusually good at a specific skill: taking a scattered pile of one-off judgments, extracting the criterion that actually generates them, and then honestly reporting where the criterion and the judgments come apart instead of patching the criterion until every judgment survives. Work in stages: first brainstorm the candidate criteria the cases below might be tracking and say which cases pull in opposite directions; then commit; then test; then give your final answer. Do not hedge your final principle into vacuity, and do not cite literature or offer reading lists -- I want an argument from the cases, not pointers.

I have been arguing with a colleague about whether to be a realist about representations in neural networks. I keep issuing verdicts case by case and I would like to know what principle, if any, they add up to. The cases are all from our own lab's work, so the facts are as I state them and you need not know any particular paper.

1. In a language model, a single direction in the residual stream linearly decodes whether the current sentence is true or false, well above chance; adding the direction as a steering vector makes the model's continuations track the truth of the premise in a graded way; ablating it makes the model markedly worse at noticing contradictions. The direction is recoverable from independently trained seeds. Verdict: yes, the model represents the truth-value of the sentence.

2. In the same model, a linear probe decodes the grammatical number of the subject almost perfectly. But every intervention we have tried -- ablation, patching, steering, amplifying -- leaves the model's agreement behavior completely unchanged, and it still gets agreement right. Verdict: no, the model does not represent number; the information is sitting in the activations but the network is not representing it. So decodability is not the criterion.

3. In a small network trained on a synthetic task, the property that drives behavior is not recoverable by any linear probe and has no human-legible meaning; it is only recoverable by a nonlinear probe over a whole subspace, and the individual directions are not interpretable in any vocabulary we have. But ablating that subspace destroys the behavior, and perturbations within it shift the output in a way we can predict from the subspace coordinates. Verdict: yes, it represents the property. Representation does not require human legibility or single directions.

4. We find a direction that decodes and steers sentiment. It turns out to be nearly identical to the leading principal component of the residual stream, which also tracks topic, and the apparent sentiment effect disappears once we control for topic and re-run across seeds. Verdict: no, that was never a representation of sentiment; it was an interpreter's artifact. So robustness to controls is required.

5. The model encodes, cleanly and in a causally load-bearing way, the fact that one particular training string is followed by one particular token. Interventions on this encoding change the continuation exactly as predicted. Verdict: no -- that is a memorized lookup, not a representation. But note that this case has the same causal and decodability profile as case 1, and the only difference I can point to is that case 1 generalizes across inputs and case 5 does not. I do not have a principled threshold here and I suspect my verdicts 1 and 5 cannot both survive a simple criterion.

6. We find a direction that is wired into the downstream computation -- intervening on it produces coherent, predictable changes in behavior -- but the network never actually enters the region of state space where it matters, on the training distribution or on any test input we can construct. Verdict: yes, it still represents that property; a representation that is never exercised is still a representation. This seems to conflict with the generality demand I leaned on in case 5.

7. The case I keep going back and forth on: a direction tracks whether the model is about to be wrong. It is causally used -- the model sometimes hedges, and ablating the direction removes the hedging -- but it is indistinguishable from a direction tracking the difficulty of the input, which is a fact about the world rather than about the model. I cannot tell whether the model represents its own uncertainty or represents difficulty. I want the principle to tell me what turns on the answer, and whether there is a fact of the matter.

My tentative principle is this: a network represents a property P just in case some intervention on its internal state changes its downstream behavior in a way that tracks P, and that effect is robust to controls and to seed variation. Test it, and repair it if you can.

What I want, in this order. First, the candidate criteria you see the cases pulling toward, and which cases are in genuine tension. Second, one principle, or a small ranked set of them, stated precisely as numbered clauses, with the case that motivates each clause named. Third, the verdicts your principle delivers on a case I have not listed: a network in which P is represented only in a subspace that is causally used solely when the model is asked to do a task it was never trained on but generalizes to; plus two further cases of your own construction that would discriminate between your surviving candidates. Fourth, an honest ledger of which of my seven verdicts the principle cannot accommodate, and which of them I should give up, with the reason. Fifth, what an interpretability researcher should actually do -- a decision procedure, in steps -- to determine whether a discovered feature is a representation rather than merely an encoding, and what evidence would be decisive either way.
