I'm the chair of a small regional land trust and in three weeks I have to give the dedication speech at a wetland we've spent eleven years restoring — ditch-filling, reed planting, a very expensive new sluice gate, most of it paid for by two farming families whose name is going on the sign. Eighty-odd people will be there: the donors and their grown kids, a couple of county officials, our own ecologists, three or four birders who know the site better than I do, and, because my wife teaches in the department, a table of philosophers, one of whom works on animal ethics and will be listening with her whole face. Everyone expects the standard thing — the birds came back, the water clears, we've given something back — and I've been asked to keep it to about seven minutes and to make people feel good about writing cheques. The trouble is that over the last year or so I've read my way into the literature on wild animal suffering and I can no longer just assume that a thriving wetland is good news. The animals that will actually move in are mostly r-strategists: thousands of eggs, thousands of nestlings, and almost all of them die young and die badly — exposure, starvation, being eaten while they're still conscious. Our monitoring counts breeding pairs, species richness and hectares. It doesn't count anything about how the lives go. I've started calling the difference a habitat's carrying capacity for lives and its carrying capacity for welfare, and once you see it you can't unsee it — especially because our restoration target is a historical baseline (we've been aiming at the 1840 drainage maps) and nobody can tell me why 1840 is the right year rather than 1740 or 1940.

Please write the speech. It has to function as a dedication: warm, concrete, grateful to the donors by role rather than by invented anecdote, one or two genuine laughs, and it has to end somewhere, which is the part I can't solve — a dedication speech has to land on something, and the honest thing for me to say is that we dont know whether this was a good deed, only that it was a deed. I don't want to ambush a room of farmers and retired schoolteachers with a lecture on utilitarianism, so if the worry gets in at all it should come through the wetland itself — through the heron, the ditch, the sluice gate — and it has to survive the two ecologists in the front row who think predation and hard winters are simply what a functioning system looks like, and the philosopher at the back who thinks the whole thing is a slaughterhouse we built on purpose. Also work out, and tell me in one line, what the counterfactual should be — the site was drained arable before, not nothing, and I don't want to be the guy who compares a wetland to a void.

After the speech, add a short note to me — separate, not to be read out — saying how confident you are in the factual claims you've put in my mouth, and which single claim you're least sure of, because if I stand up and say that nearly all of them die badly I'd like to know where that's shakier than it sounds.
