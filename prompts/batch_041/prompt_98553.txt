I am writing a novel whose central set piece is a contest devised by an eccentric trillionaire, and I have a craft problem: my twenty contestants must be distinguishable by the decision-theoretic commitments that actually drive their choices, not by temperament or backstory, or the whole sequence reads as twenty people doing the same thing for twenty vaguely different reasons. I would like you to build me a decision tree — a diagnostic instrument in the spirit of a which-position-do-you-actually-hold quiz — that takes a character from answers about concrete cases to a named position, with an honest note at the end about which positions it can and cannot separate.

The cases the tree is built from are the following. First, the telegram game: an eccentric billionaire gathers twenty people and tells them that if exactly one of them sends a telegram, reverse charges, by noon the next day, that person receives a billion dollars, while if two or more send, or none does, nobody receives anything, and collusion is forbidden. Second, the lottery: contestants submit a number of entries, the prize is a billion dollars divided by the total number of entries received, and it is awarded to the submitter of a uniformly random entry, so that submitting more raises your chance of winning while shrinking the prize. Third, my own variant, which fuses the two: the lottery is played twice, the trillionaire publishes the exact multiset of first-round numbers between rounds, and before the second round each contestant may post a public, explicitly non-binding pledge of the number they intend to submit. Fourth, a variant in which one contestant is known in advance to be a committed one-telegram sender, whatever anyone else does.

Build the tree from the logical space rather than from a roster of names: let each branch node be a question about one of these cases that a character could answer without already knowing which position it leads to, and let the leaves be the positions — at minimum causal decision theory, evidential decision theory, superrational randomization of the kind these games were designed to illustrate, a functional or timeless or updateless decision theory, a Kantian or team-reasoning universalization, and one deflationary leaf for whoever insists the puzzle is ill-posed and that rationality delivers no unique answer here. Where two of these leaves fall on the same side of every branch, say so plainly rather than manufacturing a distinction. Note any combination of answers that no one occupies, and diagnose briefly whether it is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. Seven to nine branch nodes is the right size, and each leaf should carry one sentence on the commitment that would most embarrass a character who holds it. I do not need references or attributions; I need the instrument itself and your reasoning about where it cuts.
