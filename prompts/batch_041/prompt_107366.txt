ok so I do a tiny podcast with a friend of mine, basement setup, like twelve listeners, and next month our guest is a working alignment researcher — not a famous one, just somebody who actually does the work — whose whole deal is that the field is wrong to be chasing average-case performance and should be chasing worst-case guarantees instead. Assume the standard version of that view; I don't want you guessing at what some specific real person said, just build the questions around the position itself. The position being: optimize for the worst thing the system could do rather than the typical thing, treat the environment like it's out to get you, don't trust the test distribution, safety should hold no matter what.

I've been chewing on this on my own for like two years and I have a pile of half-formed objections I'd love to actually land on air, but I am terrible at coming up with follow-ups live, so I want them written out in advance. Can you draft me 8-10 questions, and for each one a follow-up keyed to the answer he's most likely to give, so I'm ready when he dodges.

Here's the stuff I keep circling. Use whichever bits are any good:

- The thing that bugs me most is what I've started calling the composition tax. Each component of a pipeline gets a worst-case bound, great, but when you chain them the end-to-end bound goes slack so fast it stops meaning anything, and that's exactly the point where people quietly go back to average-case reasoning because otherwise they'd have nothing to say. So does worst-case-first survive being composed, or does it only ever hold for a single isolated piece?

- Related: a worst-case claim needs a set of worlds to be worst over. If it's all logically possible worlds then it's vacuous, since there's some world where the code does anything at all. So he must be restricting to some set. But the set is picked by the same lab that wants the guarantee. Isn't worst-case-over-a-set-we-chose just average-case wearing a hat? I want to press him on what makes the restriction principled and not just whatever they happened to think of.

- I have a distinction I can't tell if it's real or if I made it up: worst-case over environments versus worst-case over objectives. A system can be bulletproof against the worst environment and still be pointed at a terrible goal. When he says worst-case guarantee, which of those is he actually guaranteeing, and does he think the second one is even the same kind of problem?

- The robustness people in regular ML — the adversarial example crowd — their worst case is worst case over a little ball around each input, and that ball is doing basically all the work. So what's his ball? If he can't say, is worst-case just a mood?

- A worst-case-optimal policy can be arbitrarily bad on the distribution you'll actually face. Is he fine with that trade? If yes, does he think safety is different from performance in a way that makes it fine, and if no, where's the line?

- Is the guarantee about the training process or about the finished artifact? Worst case across training runs and worst case across inputs feel like different claims and I don't think he can have both.

- Does worst-case reasoning even mean anything when the threat is a system that could be lying to you about its own internals? The adversary isn't just the environment then.

Also throw in, near the end: one question that makes him say concretely what evidence or outcome would get him to drop worst-case-first, and one where he has to admit a case where average-case thinking is clearly the right call, so I can watch him squirm a little.

Keep them pointed at the actual content of the view, no fluff like how did you get into this. And please write the follow-ups so they land whether he gives the polished answer or the honest one.
