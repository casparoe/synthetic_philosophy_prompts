Write a sestina in the voice of a formal system answering the Gödelian anti-mechanist who claims to see past it — the argument, pressed by J. R. Lucas and later by Roger Penrose, that for any consistent system rich enough for arithmetic there is a Gödel sentence it cannot prove but that we can see to be true, so the mind is no such system. Treat the sestina's form as the system itself: fix six end-words for its vocabulary (proof, true, consistent, machine, see, I), let the rigid permutation stand for its rules of inference, and let the envoi, compressing all six words into three lines, be the diagonalization that turns the system upon itself. Let the system's reply carry the standard objections: that whoever sees its Gödel sentence as true must first trust its consistency, which the system cannot certify and neither, by the same theorem, can she; and that refuting this particular system merely yields a stronger one with a fresh Gödel sentence one level up. Close so that the poem's own submission to its form — or its breaking of it, at the price of ceasing to be a sestina — enacts that last turn. A philosopher of mathematics should find the argument and its rebuttals right, not merely the names.
