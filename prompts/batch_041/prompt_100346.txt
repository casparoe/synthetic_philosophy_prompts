I have no philosophical training and have never studied the subject formally, but I have spent years arguing about capital punishment with relatives and friends, and the argument always collapses into the same few moves: deterrence, the risk of killing an innocent person, the sanctity of life, and revenge dressed up as justice. Last year I read Albert Camus's essay against the guillotine, written in the 1950s, and was struck by how much of what he actually argues lies off those tracks. He opens not with an argument but with his father's reaction to witnessing an execution, which left the man ill for days. He then insists that he is not arguing from sympathy for the condemned but from logic and statistics. He claims that the state's killing is worse than a murderer's because it is premeditated in a way no private murder is, since the condemned man is kept alive in suspense and effectively told the date of his death. He points out that executions used to be public and are now hidden inside prison walls, which quietly removes the reason usually offered for them, since an example nobody sees is not an example, and he notes that crime did not rise in countries that abolished the practice. He grants that the penalty made a kind of sense in a society that still believed in God, where the executioner could take the body and leave the soul to a higher judgment, and argues that in a society that believes in nothing, the state takes everything at once and leaves no room for atonement; and since no man is innocent enough to judge another absolutely, the harshest thing we may do is keep a man alive at labor, leaving him the option of choosing death himself.

Here is the thing I cannot get past. The parts of his case that seem to me strongest are the ones that depend on what death means in a world without God, and those are exactly the parts that will be rejected by the people I argue with, for whom death is not the end and the penalty is therefore less absolute than Camus says it is. The parts that travel best, the hidden execution and the statistics, are the ones that look like empirical matters that could in principle be settled the other way. I suspect his case is strongest where it is least theological and weakest where it is most distinctive, but I may have it backwards.

I have roughly a year of spare evenings and I want to put them into one long letter to a relative who firmly supports the death penalty, and possibly a short talk at my church. Given that, which threads in Camus's case deserve the bulk of my attention, and which are overrated or would backfire with that audience? I also want to know which of the questions he leaves hanging, rather than the ones he answers, are worth my evenings at all. Give me an order and the reasoning behind it: which arguments are upstream of which, which survive if the empirical claims go against us, which do any work on people who do not share his premises about death, and what would move an item up or down that order. I am less interested in a summary of the essay than in your judgment about where the weight belongs, and I would like you to say plainly if you think I have misread the tension I described above.

Please write it as continuous prose. No headings, no bullet points, no bold text, no tables, just paragraphs, the way you would write a long letter.
