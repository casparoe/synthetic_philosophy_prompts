I'm a third-year student in a course on the ethics and politics of work, and I need to submit a one-page research proposal — a prospectus for a term paper of roughly five thousand words — by the end of the month. I have a half-formed idea and would like you to turn it into that proposal.

The idea is this. Discussions of the 35-hour week and discussions of karoshi (death from overwork) are almost always run separately: the first as a question about how to distribute a fixed quantity of work, the second as a pathology of a particular corporate culture. But both seem to rest on the same assumption — that leisure is what you are owed only after work has been discharged, so that rest is a reward for working rather than a precondition of being able to work at all. I want to know whether that assumption is doing the real work in the debate, and whether the so-called right to work and right to leisure are genuinely two rights or one right seen from two sides. My suspicion is that a regime which guarantees employment but no enforceable limit on hours grants a right whose exercise corrodes the very thing — the capacity to rest — that makes the right worth having, and that the work ethic we inherit from the Protestant tradition is what conceals the inconsistency. A test case I keep circling: a firm that advertises both unlimited overtime and unlimited unpaid leave, so that both "rights" are present on paper and everyone is nonetheless exhausted. I suspect that is not a coincidence but a symptom.

What I need from you is a proposal that does the following. State the question precisely enough that it has a determinate answer — including disambiguating the several things "a right to work" is used to mean (a right to be employed, a right to work at all rather than be idle, a right against compelled idleness), since the argument turns on which one is in play. Explain why the standard framings — labour-rights, liberal-neutralist, and productivity/efficiency — leave the question open rather than settling it. Describe the method: what I would actually do, week by week, given that this is conceptual rather than empirical work, and why that method rather than the obvious alternative of a policy case study. Say what a successful result would look like, and what would count as the project failing. And give me an honest assessment of whether the project is too large for a single term and, if so, how to cut it down without losing the point. Do not give me a bibliography or a reading list; I will add the citations myself, so refer to positions and arguments by description rather than by name or title.

At the very end, after the proposal, state the research question in the following fixed form as the last line: \question{...} — with the question contained in a single sentence.
