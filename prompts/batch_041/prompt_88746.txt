In a committee meeting on our university's hardship fund, a colleague argued for attaching a work requirement to what are currently unconditional grants — attendance at a job-readiness course, with forfeiture of that term's payment as the sanction — on the ground that the fund should offer 'a hand up, not a handout.' I mean to reply in writing and want a full inventory of what that phrase could be claiming before I do. Set out the distinct theses it could express: the definitional one (that a conditional transfer simply is not a handout), the empirical ones about dependency, incentives, and labour-market behaviour, the reciprocity-based ones about what a recipient owes in return for support, and the paternalist ones about the value of work to the recipient. For each, say whether it is trivial, substantive, or false; identify the further premises it would need in order to license a sanction-backed condition rather than a mere expectation; and say which reading my colleague is most likely relying on. I am especially interested in what the metaphor itself carries beyond the claims — the assumed baseline of self-sufficiency, the one-way trajectory from dependence to independence — and in whether any reading survives once one asks why a university's obligation to a student in financial difficulty should be modelled on charity rather than on an entitlement the student has, in part, already earned.
