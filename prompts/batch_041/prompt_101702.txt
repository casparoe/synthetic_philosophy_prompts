I'm a PhD student working on Buddhist ethics, and before I commit the position I've been developing to a chapter I'd like it stress-tested. Here is the package as it currently stands:

(1) I am a consequentialist about rightness: whether an act is right depends on how it affects the welfare of sentient beings, where that is understood as the reduction of suffering and the promotion of well-being; I do not treat deontic constraints or agent-relative prerogatives as fundamental.

(2) I hold that karma is determined by intention (cetana), and that it operates across a beginningless series of lives: the moral quality of an act is fixed by the mental state behind it rather than by what it happens to bring about.

(3) I accept the doctrine of no-self: persons are not enduring substances but causally continuous bundles, usefully designated but not ultimately real.

(4) I hold that the ultimate aim is the cessation of suffering — nibbana — and that this is not itself a state of pleasure or positive well-being, but the ending of the process that produces experience at all.

(5) I take non-attachment to be central to the ideal: the accomplished agent does not cling to outcomes.

I would like an audit rather than a summary. Which of these commitments pull against one another, which only appear to, and how severe is each genuine tension? For every real conflict, say what would have to give and what giving it up would cost me; then tell me which single revision would resolve the most at once. Please discriminate rather than flag everything — I would rather be told that some of the pairings I might worry about are answering different questions than have all five treated as equally fraught.
