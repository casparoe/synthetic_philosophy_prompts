I'm putting together a film night for a reading group that's been working through philosophy of AI, and next up is Alex Garland's Ex Machina. I want to come at it sideways, through the odd bit in Turing's 1950 paper where he takes extra-sensory perception seriously. The gist of his worry (as I remember it, so don't quote him back at me) is that the imitation game only proves anything if the interrogator has no route to the answer except the conversation itself. If telepathy is real, the interrogator might just know which one's the machine, and the whole thing falls apart. His fix was to run the test in a room screened against telepathy. I'd like a reading of the film through that.

So: is Caleb's week at the house a Turing test at all, or has Nathan already broken it before it starts? Caleb knows Ava is a machine going in, he's being watched the whole time, and the power cuts are Ava reaching into the room from outside it. The interrogator's channel isn't sealed — but notice that in the film the leakage mostly runs the other way: it's Ava reading Caleb, or really shaping him, not Caleb psychically sniffing out a machine. Does that count as Turing's objection, or does the film show his worry pointing the wrong way? And does the screened-room fix even make sense for a test where the tester is a guest inside the system he's testing?

Which scenes should I point the group to? I'm thinking the early sessions, the power cuts, the monitors Nathan keeps glancing at, the Kyoko reveal, and the last shot through the glass. I also wonder whether the original version of the game — the one about guessing which player is the man and which is the woman — matters here, given Ava's body and Nathan's voyeurism. But be honest with me about where the reading breaks down: maybe the film is really about desire and power and who gets to count as a subject, and the ESP frame is doing less work than it looks.
