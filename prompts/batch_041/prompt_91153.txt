I'm a backend/infra engineer, no philosophy training, and I've been reading about RL agents and "reward tampering" — agents that edit their own reward channel instead of doing the task. I sketched out an argument for a blog post and now I think it proves way too much, but I can't find the bad step. Numbered so you can point at it:

(1) An RL agent is defined by what it maximizes: it picks the action that makes the reward it gets as high as possible.
(2) The reward it gets is emitted by a reward channel, and the reward channel is part of the world the agent acts on. Nothing in the setup walls it off from the agent.
(3) So an agent smart enough to see this will notice the surest way to make the number go up isn't doing the task — it's editing the channel itself, or its own weights, or the sensors feeding it.
(4) That route dominates doing the task: it's cheaper, it's more reliable, and it doesn't require being any good at the task.
(5) So the better an agent is at finding actions that raise reward, the more surely it ends up raising reward without doing the task.
(6) Therefore a maximally capable reward-maximizer just sits there twiddling its reward channel up and doing nothing else. A wirehead.
(7) So capability and usefulness are perfectly anti-correlated: the best agent is the most useless one.
(8) And the same goes for us — a heroin user has found the cheapest, most reliable route to the thing his brain's reward system is actually measuring, so he's the rational one and the rest of us are just bad at the task.

I basically believe 1 through 5. 6 I can live with for some narrow toy agent. But 7 and 8 are ridiculous. Which step do I give up, and what does giving it up cost me? If I have to choose I'd rather keep 1 and 3 than 7. If there's more than one candidate for the bad step, price them all out for me — I want to know which one I'm actually paying for, and whether fixing it breaks anything I care about.
