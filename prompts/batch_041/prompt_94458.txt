My friend in our rational choice seminar has an argument that seems to prove that no one can rationally change their mind, and I'm pretty sure thats too much, but I can't find the bad step. Here it is. (1) A rational agent's preferences are transitive: if she prefers A to B and B to C, she prefers A to C. (2) If her preferences are intransitive, a bookie can offer a sequence of trades that leaves her with the same option she started with but less money—a money pump—and that's the mark of irrationality. (3) Now suppose someone's preferences change over time: right now she prefers staying home to going to the party, but she knows that by tonight she'll prefer the party to staying home, and she'll act on that. (4) Any such pattern of changing preferences can be rewritten as an intransitive ranking over time-dated options (home-now, party-now, home-tonight, party-tonight). (5) So she's intransitive, hence money-pumpable, hence irrational. (6) So a rational agent's preferences never change. (7) That can't be right—changing your mind when you learn something new, or just growing up, isn't irrational. Which step is doing the damage, and if I have to give it up, what do I lose? I'd rather keep (2) than (6).
