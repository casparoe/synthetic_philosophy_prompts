I have just naturalized in a country where voting is compulsory and abstaining carries a fine, and the question of what exactly I am being obliged to do has been on my mind more than I expected. A friend who teaches political theory gave me an argument over dinner that he offered as a reductio of the idea that there is a duty to vote. I have written it out below as he put it. I suspect it proves too much, but I cannot say where the excess comes in.

(1) The reasons bearing on how I vote are of two kinds: outcome-based reasons, which concern my vote helping to bring about one result rather than another, and expressive reasons, which concern my vote declaring what I stand for.
(2) My vote almost never decides anything. The chance that mine is the one that tips a national election is on the order of one in ten million.
(3) It follows that the outcome-based reasons are vanishingly weak — weak enough that any expressive reason will outweigh them.
(4) Expressive reasons require the statement to be true to what I stand for, so I should vote for the candidate I most endorse, whether or not that candidate can win.
(5) Hence I should always vote for my most-endorsed candidate, and the standard plea for voting for a lesser evil — that it blocks a worse one — has no force, since my vote is not what does the blocking.
(6) And since the only weight-bearing reasons left are expressive, and expression is an optional act, nothing requires anyone to vote at all. A fine for abstaining therefore punishes people for declining an optional act of self-expression, which is unjust.

I want to reject (6): I do not believe I am being fined for declining an optional act, and if that is the only reading left to me, I would rather find the mistake elsewhere. I also want to reject (5). If I lived in a constituency where a competent moderate and a demagogue were neck and neck, I do not think the voter who chooses a fringe candidate in order to express herself has simply done nothing wrong, and I do not think it is only the outcome that makes her choice criticizable. Yet (2) seems to me plainly true, and (4) has real appeal. Step (3) does a great deal of work for one sentence, but I cannot say what is wrong with it.

Tell me which step to give up, why it looked plausible, and what giving it up costs — and say whether there is a way to keep the sincerity requirement while denying that the strategic voter in a dead heat is beyond criticism.
