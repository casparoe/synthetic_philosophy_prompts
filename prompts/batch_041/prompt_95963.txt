ok so i've been going down a rabbit hole on the surprise exam paradox. quick setup in case it matters: teacher tells the class there'll be an exam some day next week (mon-fri) and that when it comes they won't be able to predict in advance wich day it'll be. the students reason it can't be friday, because if it hasn't happened by thursday night they'd know it's friday and it wouldn't be a surprise; then the same argument rules out thursday, wednesday, and so on down to monday, so it can't happen at all. teacher then gives it on wednesday and everyone is in fact surprised.

i've collected a bunch of proposed diagnoses of where the students' reasoning goes wrong. i want a tier list. tier them by how much of the paradox each one actually dissolves — does it pin down a genuine flaw, or does it just relocate the weirdness somewhere else? use S/A/B/C/D, where S means basically settles it and D means doesn't touch the puzzle. one or two sentences each on why it lands where it does. and at the end, tell me which entries are secretly the same move as each other, if any.

1. the students' reasoning is airtight, which means the announcement is simply inconsistent with itself — no teacher can truthfully announce a surprise exam. the exam itself can still surprise them; the paradox is about the announcement, not the exam.

2. the students rely on knowledge (by thursday night we'll know it's friday) that their own reasoning destroys. you can't use a piece of knowledge as a premise and undermine it in the same breath, so the induction is self-undermining rather than sound.

3. surprise can't be a fixed target the students reason about, because whether they're surprised depends on what they end up believing, and that depends on the announcement and on this very reasoning. the puzzle is a self-referential prediction, closer to a self-defeating prophecy than to an induction.

4. quantifier scope. the students establish that for any day, if the exam lands on that day they won't know it in advance. the paradox needs something stronger, roughly: there is a particular day such that they know the exam is on it and also don't know it's on it. conflating those two is the whole trick.

5. the announcement can't be common knowledge in the iterative sense — after the teacher speaks, the students' information state changes, and what breaks is the step that needs them to keep knowing that they know that they know. formalize it with dynamic/epistemic logic and the surprise goes through fine.

6. it's moore-paradoxical: the exam will happen and you won't know in advance when is a proposition that can be true but can't be known in advance by the people it's about, since knowing it would make it false. that's all that's going on, no flaw in the induction, just a truth that can't be known ahead of time.

7. it proves too much. run the same backward induction on a teacher who announces nothing, or on any future event whatsoever, and you'd conclude it can't happen. so the bug is in the students' use of we would know it by then, not in the announcement.

8. drop the certainty. if the announcement is only probable, the last step of the induction (so it definitely won't be friday) fails and the whole thing evaporates. the paradox only exists because the students treat a likely prediction as a certainty.
