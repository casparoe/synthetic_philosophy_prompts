I sit on the science advisory panel of our national environment agency and there's a decision coming up that I want to get ahead of. Our record on this kind of thing so far:

- we approved a self-limiting soil bacterium for open-field trials even though some lab work hinted at mild, temporary harm to soil invertebrates, because the strain dies out after a few generations and we can stop the trial any time;
- we turned down a fungicide on the strength of one medeocre rat study, because it would build up in a lake with no outflow and no realistic way to clean it;
- we approved a preservative with a known, small, reversible allergy risk in a subgroup, because it cuts spoilage a lot where refrigeration is bad and the alternatives are worse;
- we turned down a gene-edited barley where nobody had found any harm at all, because our own model put gene flow into a wild relative at better than even, and it would be permanent;
- we approved a spray with no long-term soil data, since any effect would show up within a season and registration can be pulled.

Now: a company wants to seed a modified algae into a big reservoir to strip out a fertilizer pollutant. It would fix a serious health problem for about two million people downstream at a fraction of the cost of the alternative, and nothing bad has turned up in lab or mesocosm work. But the reservoir feeds a chain of lakes, and the company's own model puts a 1-in-50 chance on the algae permanently outcompeting the native phytoplankton there. No further study will narrow that number before the season starts.

Predict what we do, assuming we're being consistent with ourselves. I want the rules our past calls are actually consistent with -- I suspect more than one fits -- what each one says about the algae, which of our past calls is the closest analog, which rule you'd bet on and why, and the one next case that would seperate them cleanly. And if our record genuinely doesn't settle it, tell me what principle we'd be adopting with a yes and what we'd be adopting with a no.
