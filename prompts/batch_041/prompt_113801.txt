I'm a second-year PhD student working on early modern philosophy of religion, and I've got a side-obsession with the Catholic grace controversies that keeps eating my weekends. Lately I've been reading around the Congregatio de Auxiliis — the commission Clement VIII set up to settle the fight between the Dominicans and the Jesuits over efficacious grace, which ground on for something like twenty years and eighty-five sessions held in front of the popes before Paul V finally shut it down in September 1607 by telling both sides they could go on teaching their own view, that neither was to censure the other, and that they should wait for a final decision from the Apostolic See which, as it turned out, never came (and then in 1611 the Inquisition banned publishing anything further on efficacious grace at all). What I want is a proper two-way analysis: read that episode through the lens of Molinism, and then turn round and use the episode to test Molinism.

So start with what the theory actually claims. Molina's Concordia came out in 1588, and the whole point of middle knowledge, scientia media, is to slot a third moment of divine cognition between God's natural knowledge of necessary truths and his free knowledge of what he has decreed: God knows, prior to any decree, what each possible free creature would do in each set of circumstances, so that he can choose which circumstances — which graces — to actualise, knowing who will freely respond to grace A and who freely won't to grace B, and so secure his ends without determining the will. Lay that out, then say what work it was doing in the controversy: why it was supposed to hold together both the infallible efficacy of grace and the freedom of the creature, how it lines up with the Jesuit side of the dispute, and why the Dominicans thought it was Pelagianism in disguise while the Jesuits thought physical premotion was Calvinism in disguise. Then the harder half, which is what I'm really after. Run it the other way. The Dominicans' objection — the grounding objection, as it's usually called now — is that there's simply no fact of the matter about what a merely possible free creature would do, because freedom is indeterministic and nothing makes the counterfactual true before God decrees the creature into existence and moves it. Is that objection doing real work in the episode, or is the 1607 stalemate better explained by something else — institutional politics, the impossibility of condemning either order, both sides claiming Augustine and Aquinas, the pope's reluctance to pick a winner? And what does the stalemate itself show about the theory: is a non-decision the natural resting place for a dispute that turns on an ungrounded counterfactual, or does the fact that neither side could be made to recant tell in favour of one of them?

I want you to be honest about the limits — say where middle knowledge is simply silent (I suspect it has almost nothing to say about the institutional and political dimension of the episode, but tell me if I'm wrong) rather than bending it to cover everything. Where the modern analytic debate is relevent — the truthmaker framing of the grounding objection, the idea that a counterfactual of creaturely freedom might be true without any truthmaker, the distinction between what God knows and what makes it true — use it, but don't just report it: say which move you think actually answers the Dominican, and whether Molina himself already had it. Don't bother with a bibliography or citations, I know the literature well enough; I just want the analysis. British English, please.
