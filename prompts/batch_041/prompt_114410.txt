Take the collapse of the eastern Mediterranean palace societies around 1200 BCE — the fall of the Hittite empire, the destruction of the Mycenaean palaces and of Ugarit, the disappearance of Linear B, the breakdown of long-distance trade, with Egypt surviving in reduced form — and analyze it through two rival accounts of collapse: one that places collapse where further investment in social complexity stops paying for itself, and one that treats complex societies as trading resilience for efficiency, so that collapse is a release-and-reorganization phase rather than a failure. Do the mapping carefully — what counted as complexity, what the returns were, who paid for them — then say where each account breaks, and whether the case instead fits a third story in which the success of the networks produced the fragility that destroyed them. Finally, how much of the collapse is a real discontinuity in these societies and how much an artifact of a record that preserves palaces and the paperwork of palaces — and does collapse name a process, or a category we impose on the past?
