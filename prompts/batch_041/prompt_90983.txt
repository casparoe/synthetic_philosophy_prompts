I'm putting together a teaching packet on the timing of conscious experience, and I'd like a poem to open the session on the colour phi phenomenon. The demonstration I'll be using is the standard one: a blue dot is flashed near the top of a screen, a red dot near the bottom a fraction of a second later, and at the right spacing and timing subjects report a single dot that begins to move and then changes colour partway along its path, a change they locate before the second flash has even been presented, which cannot be right if experience is assembled in real time. Dennett and Kinsbourne's two readings of this are the Stalinesque one, on which the experience is delayed and built up after the fact, so that a coherent story is staged before it ever reaches the subject, and the Orwellian one, on which the experience is real-time but the memory of the first flash is rewritten once the second arrives. I want a poem of about twenty lines carrying a refrain, where the refrain's second occurrence differs from the first by exactly one word. The poem has to be built so that a reader cannot settle, from the text alone, whether the first occurrence already anticipated the change, i.e. the change was staged in advance, or whether the second occurrence retroactively rewrites what the first one meant. Make the altered word one whose earlier occurrence tolerates both construals, through a pun, a scope ambiguity, or a reading that survives in its first context, so that the reader cannot tell whether the text changed or only their construal of it: the experience of reading the poem should reproduce the puzzle rather than describe it. Please don't name either mechanism, or the phenomenon, inside the poem, and leave both readings live at the close rather than adjudicating between them. It should be accurate enough that a philosopher of mind recognises the two hypotheses and finds neither of them a straw man.
