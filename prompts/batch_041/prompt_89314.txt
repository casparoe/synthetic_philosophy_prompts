I am a software engineer with no formal background in philosophy or cognitive science, and I would like to approach a claim in that literature the way I would approach writing a specification. Douglas Hofstadter and Emmanuel Sander argue that analogy-making is not a specialized faculty or a figure of speech but the basic engine of thought -- that perceiving, categorizing, remembering, and reasoning are all, at bottom, the making of analogies between situations. Suppose I take that seriously as a research programme. Before I look into any particular account, I want the specification: the list of what any adequate theory of analogy-making must deliver in order to be a theory of the core of cognition rather than of a peripheral trick.

Write that list of requirements. For each requirement, give the consideration or case that motivates it, and indicate how binding it is -- which requirements are non-negotiable and which are preferences of a particular camp. I especially want to know whether some subset of the requirements is jointly unsatisfiable, because if it is, the list itself is the result. And tell me which single requirement does the most to rule out the popular ways of theorizing about analogy. The purpose is narrow: I want to be able to judge, for any account that is put in front of me, whether it is even in the running.

Here are cases and observations I want the specification to be answerable to. I would rather have these handled properly than have more added.

- Novices and experts asked to sort a set of physics problems group them differently: novices by surface features (pulleys, inclined planes, springs), experts by the underlying principle. The same stimulus is carved differently by different people, and the theory has to accommodate that.
- Two analysts look at the same epidemic curve. One reads it as a contagion, the other as a speculative bubble. Both readings fit the data points, and they license different interventions. The theory must say whether there is a fact about which analogy is better, and on what grounds, or whether aptness is exhausted by fit-to-data plus usefulness.
- Someone learning a second programming language keeps seeing the new language through the first. Sometimes this produces fast competence; sometimes it produces subtle bugs from assuming that the old language's conventions carry over. One mechanism has to account for both the productive and the misleading case.
- Recognizing a letter in an unfamiliar typeface, or a friend in a crowd, feels immediate and not at all like a comparison -- yet on the claim it is one.
- An analogy can be apt and still mislead: it fits the known cases, licenses a prediction, and the prediction turns out wrong. Aptness and truth have to come apart in the theory, or the theory has to explain why they do not.
- Ordinary speech is saturated with analogy that no one experiences as reasoning -- a deadline creeps up, a market digests news, a rumour spreads. If analogy is the core of cognition, the theory cannot treat these as dead metaphors or decoration.
- Some analogies organize an entire field for decades without anyone experiencing them as a deliberate comparison -- an economy treated as an ecosystem, a brain treated as a computer -- and they quietly constrain which questions get asked. Say whether a theory needs one mechanism for the momentary analogy and another for the long-lived one, or whether the same mechanism covers both.

Additional constraints on the answer:

- The theory must not presuppose the very thing it is meant to explain. In particular, accounts that begin with two situations already parsed into the right features and then specify a matching stage appear to assume that the representational work has been done in advance. Say whether that is a genuine defect, and what an account would have to do instead.
- The theory must either respect the distinctions between analogy, metaphor, similarity judgment, and the retrieval of a past case, or argue that they are one thing.
- The theory must say whether it is descriptive (how minds actually work) or also normative (when an analogy is good), and whether the two can be separated. The compelling but fallacious analogy in an advertisement is the case behind that.
- The theory must explain why analogies are graded -- why some mappings feel forced and others click -- without positing a pre-given similarity metric, since a similarity metric would do the work that analogy is supposed to do.

Do not give me a reading list, citations, or the names of specific works; I cannot look anything up, and I do not want to take your word for attributions. Reason from the requirements outward. The list of requirements is what I want, in as much detail as the cases will bear. If you want to close with a short pass showing which families of approach each requirement rules out, keep it brief and secondary to the criteria.
