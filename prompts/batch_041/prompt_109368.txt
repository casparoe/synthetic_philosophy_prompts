I teach a third-year module on the evolution of communication — mostly biologists, plus a few philosophers who've wandered in and stayed — and I've got a two-hour workshop slot coming up for about 24 of them, in a seminar room with movable chairs and a projector I'd rather not have to depend on. What I want is for them to feel the force of the handicap principle properly: not the peacock version they've all heard in passing, but the actual mechanism, which I'm fairly sure most of them have got backwards. Last term someone said in a seminar that Zahavi's whole idea was just showing off dressed up in maths, and that it was unfalsifiable anyway because you can look at any signal after the fact and call it a handicap, and I didn't have a good answer ready. So design me an activity — a game with rules, hidden information, and a payoff scheme that doesn't need real money (chocolate, a leaderboard, bragging rights, whatever works) — where they run into the thing itself. The counterintuitive bit I want them to hit is this: honesty in signalling doesn't come from a signal being hard to produce, it comes from it being differentially hard, cheap enough for the good types and dear enough for the bad ones, and the upshot is that the equilibrium is expensive on purpose, so the waste is the mechanism rather than a side-effect of it. If they end up slightly indignant that everyone would be better off if nobody did any of this, and then see why nobody can unilaterally stop, that's the session working.

Structure it so the discovery happens in the rounds and not in my introduction — I'd much rather they spend a first round under a rule that makes their signals worthless, and only then be handed the variation that makes them informative. Give me the materials list, the hidden-information setup (I'm imagining cards for quality types plus a seperate cost schedule, but tell me what actually works in a room this size), the payoff table, a round-by-round timeline for the two hours including how long to leave for the debrief, and a note on how much of this survives if only twelve turn up. I'd also like a variant round where they get to invent their own signalling convention once they've watched the first one collapse, because I want them to reach for something unfakeable and then notice that this is a genuinely different solution to the same problem rather than a version of the costly one. In the debrief, map the specific moments in the game onto the parts of the theory: the differential-cost condition, why the equilibrium display is pinned by the marginal incentives of the worst type that still bothers to pay it, the difference between a signal that really does cost its bearer and one that only looks costly from the outside, and where the line falls between costly signalling and plain cheap talk. Be straight with me about what the activity can't reproduce — I assume it can't do the population dynamics or anything frequency-dependent, and I suspect it can't show why the marginal version of the condition is the right way to state it rather than some total-cost version, but tell me what else is missing and what I'll have to say out loud myself. And tell me what predictably goes wrong the first time: I'm already worried that half of them will refuse to spend a single token out of pure loss aversion, and that one person will find the exploit in round two inside ninety seconds and spoil the reveal for everyone else.
