For a graduate seminar I run on idealization in the empirical sciences, I would like a short story. Its setting is a fictional river-forecasting bureau whose founding maxim is that one never idealizes the quantity one is trying to predict, and whose standing rests on that maxim; across the valley sits a rival bureau that is openly indifferent to the truth of its assumptions and whose flood forecasts are, infuriatingly, better. Set the story at the moment when the junior modeler of the first bureau produces a forecast that is right, that is right only because she has idealized the very quantity she was predicting, and whose success she can trace to two distortions that happen to cancel inside this valley and nowhere else — so that nothing she has learned carries over to the next flood, the neighboring valley, or the question of what would have happened had the dam been sited differently. I want the reader to feel the pull of Friedman's point that the falsity of a theory's assumptions is no objection to it while its predictions are good, and the countering pull of the thought that the assumptions are precisely what determines which of a model's successes travel. Let the choice she must make about whether to report the result carry that tension, rather than any speech from a character. Close with a brief note in your own voice: how confident you are that the story is faithful to the dispute as it actually stands, which reading of the F-twist it leans on, and the single point in it you are least sure of.
