Next month I'm giving a talk to a policy-school seminar and I'd like you to prep my Q&A. My thesis is that historical analogies mislead us not because they get the facts wrong, but because apt and inept analogies are equally unfalsifiable and we've been telling ourselves the difference is accuracy. My case study is the Thucydides trap: rising power meets ruling power, war follows, and nothing can count against it, because if the war comes the trap's sprung and if it doesn't, statesmanship averted it, so the framework takes credit both ways. The diagnostic I run is what I've been calling the blind-spot test — an analogy is dangerous in proportion to how hard it is to state what would count against it. I put Munich and Vietnam through the same test and argue their lessons are one-sided conditionals: they only ever fire against acting, never against not acting, so a failure of restraint gets booked as evidence for restraint applied somewhere else. I also claim the reason we can't retire Munich isn't epistemic at all, it's that the analogy does real work in an argument, which is a different job than the one we pretend it's doing. The room is mostly diplomatic historians, two political scientists who work with dyadic conflict datasets and have actually estimated power-transition models, and one retired foreign service officer. Give me the prep sheet: the objections this particular room will raise, in their vocabulary, roughly in the order they'll come, ranked by how much damage they do, what I should concede up front and what I should hold, a short reply to each with an honest note on where the reply is soft, and the one question I'm quietly hoping nobody asks. Continuous prose, please — no headings, bullets, bold or tables, just write it like an email to a colleague. And dont bother with citations or reading lists, I know the literature.
