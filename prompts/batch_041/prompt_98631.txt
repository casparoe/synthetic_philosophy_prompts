I am preparing a seminar on Ian Hacking's account of human kinds, in particular his claim that many classifications of people are interactive kinds: a category can be taken up, resisted, or inhabited by the people it sorts, and their responses can bend the category in turn — the looping effect by which the human sciences take part in making up the people they study. I want to teach the idea through Charlotte Perkins Gilman's The Yellow Wallpaper, and I need a reading that is faithful both to the story and to the philosophy.

The familiar reading of the story is the feminist one: the rest cure, the physician-husband, the ban on writing, the confinement. I want the Hacking reading set beside it, not in place of it. The narrator is handed a diagnosis and a regimen built on it, and the story can be read as the diagnosis producing the very derangement it purports merely to describe. Push that reading as far as it will go. Is what happens to her a looping effect in Hacking's sense, or something weaker — iatrogenesis, the ordinary harm of a cruel treatment, a self-fulfilling prophecy — and what exactly would have to be true for the stronger claim to hold? Where does the story show her taking up the category, and where does it show her resisting, deflecting, or quietly rewriting it? And what should we make of the story's own form — it is a case history written by the patient, in secret, against medical orders — does the text thereby perform a loop of its own, returning the clinical gaze to the clinician?

Then I want the harder question pressed. Hacking's paradigm cases — multiple personality and the diagnoses that arrive and depart — are categories a person can avow or disavow, and that can therefore talk back to the classification. The nineteenth-century hysterical or neurasthenic woman may be a category one cannot step outside, only inhabit. Does that make it a different kind of case, and does the story dramatize a limit of the looping-effect picture — a classification that shapes the person while leaving no room for the person to reshape the classification? If so, is that a defect in Hacking's account, or a distinction he already allows and that the story merely makes vivid? Finally, say whether the story is better read as an illustration of dynamic nominalism — the kind coming into being together with the classification — or only of the weaker claim that a label alters behavior.

Close by saying what the story shows about the power of classification that the philosophical account does not, and flag honestly any point at which the Hacking reading has to distort the story to make it fit. There is no need to quote the text; work from its plot and structure. Please write in American English.
