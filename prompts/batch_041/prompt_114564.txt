I am a novelist at work on a book whose central character, a cognitive scientist, argues that the mind is to the brain as software is to hardware — that a person is a program, and that a mind might therefore be copied from one machine to another, so that dying is merely a hardware failure and a saved mind is a surviving one. The comparison is, of course, a commonplace, and I do not want to lean on it more heavily than it can bear. Please set out the correspondence in full: what answers to the hardware, the program, the data, the input and output, the operating system, and the programmer. Then show me where the mapping fails. I am most interested in whether the brain offers anything like the stored program and the clean separation of software from hardware that my character's conclusion requires, and in what development and learning do to that separation; a friend of mine in computer science insists that under some description any lump of matter is computing something, and I would like to know whether that objection is fatal or merely clever. Finally, separate the disanalogies that matter for the conclusion from those that do not, and tell me whether the conclusion — that a mind is the kind of thing that can be copied from one machine to another and survive the transfer — holds once the breaks are in view, or whether the metaphor was carrying it all along.
