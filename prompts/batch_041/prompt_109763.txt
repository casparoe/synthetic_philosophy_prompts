Next month I am leading a ninety-minute session of an interdisciplinary graduate reading group — economists, psychologists, a linguist or two, none of them philosophers by training — on a single question: is the relation 'better than' transitive? The session will work through the family of spectrum arguments in which a long chain of small improvements, or of small worsenings, is used to produce a failure of transitivity. Do not explain the arguments to me; plan how I should explain them.

Set out the order in which the components must be introduced and say what depends on what. My own guess is that a bare definition of transitivity, kept apart from completeness, has to come first, and that nothing in the argument can be assessed until the group has the distinction between the strict relation and the weak one — in particular the possibility that 'better than' stays transitive while the indifference relation does not, which is what threshold cases of the sugar-in-coffee kind seem to show; if I have the name of that structure right (a semiorder?), say so, but do not let the session hang on the label. Tell me where that ordering is wrong and where I should depart from it.

Then tell me what should carry the session: the one spectrum I build on the board, what it must have — adjacent steps that are each a real and not merely apparent difference in value, a stark gap at the two ends, enough steps that any cutoff looks arbitrary — and what it must avoid, so that the group does not spend the hour litigating population ethics or whether a life can be worth living. Say whether the stock sorites cases, the heap and the bald man, would serve as the carrying example or whether the difference between vagueness and a difference in value is exactly what they would obscure, and whether the example should run along one dimension or be assembled from several goods at once.

Finally: the misconceptions to expect and the point in the sequence at which to head off each. I anticipate that the economists will reach for the axioms of expected utility and the money pump, that the psychologists will redescribe the case as framing or as preference reversal, and that the linguist will insist it is a sorites about a vague predicate that dissolves once we stop pretending the predicate has sharp boundaries. Give me one question to put at the end that would distinguish understanding from assent, with what a good and a poor answer would look like; tell me what to refuse to resolve at this level and to say so; and tell me whether the honest ending is a verdict or a trilemma, and how to leave the group standing inside it. No reading list — I want the plan.
