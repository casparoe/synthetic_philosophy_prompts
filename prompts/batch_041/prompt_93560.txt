I'm the retiring chief justice of the Supreme Court of the Republic of Marisol—yes, the fictional one—and I have to give a 12-minute farewell speech at my successor's swearing-in next month. The room is a mess: half politicians who just pushed through the new Constitutional Reform Act, half law students who can recite the countermajoritarian difficulty in their sleep, and my family, who still think I spent 30 years telling people what to do. The reform added a legislative re-enactment clause: if we strike down a statute 4-3, the legislature can re-enact it by simple majority after the next election, and then we can only strike it again if we find they didn't seriously engage with our reasons. If the original decision was unanimous, they need two-thirds. The first test of the clause is happening right now, over a law I voted to strike down. I need a speech that doesn't pretend the clause is either the death of judicial review or its salvation. I want it to be honest that I'm not sure unelected judges should have the last word, and I'm also not sure majorities can be trusted to protect the things that make majority rule worth having. Make it spoken, not written. Let it turn on the difference between having the last word and having a say, and use the clause—especially the 4-3 vs unanimous split—as the concrete thing that makes that distinction matter. Dont cite cases or scholars; I don't want it to sound like a law review. Keep it under 1200 words. After the speech, add a short note, not part of the speech, saying how confident you are in the claims it makes about the real debate over judicial review, and single out the one point you're least sure of.
