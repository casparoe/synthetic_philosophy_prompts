I keep circling a question about Robert Nozick's Wilt Chamberlain argument, and I want a number rather than a verdict. The argument is usually read as showing that any patterned principle of distributive justice — any principle that fixes how holdings ought to be distributed along some dimension other than the history by which they arose — will be disrupted by the free choices of consenting adults, so that holding the pattern in place requires continuous interference with those choices. I want your credence in a sharper and deliberately stronger claim:

(S) The argument refutes every patterned conception of distributive justice, including a variant I will construct and call Pattern-as-Rules, on which the pattern is implemented not by recurring redistribution of outcomes but by a fixed ex ante scheme of property, contract, inheritance, and liability rules, engineered so that voluntary exchanges tend to leave the target distribution roughly intact.

Treat (S) as the proposition under evaluation; its truth condition is that the argument's central mechanism defeats Pattern-as-Rules as well as the outcome-redistributing versions. If you judge (S) not to be a single proposition, say how you would split it, then still give me a number for the most natural reading.

Give me your credence in (S). Then decompose it: list the sub-questions the number depends on, assign each a rough probability with a single line of justification, and show how they combine into your final figure. The sub-questions I have in mind — add, drop, or replace as you see fit — are:

1. Whether a principle can count as patterned in Nozick's sense while being defined over procedures rather than over time-slices of holdings, or whether that is a category mistake that makes Pattern-as-Rules a non-starter.
2. Whether free exchange necessarily drifts any static pattern, or whether there exist patterns that are stable, or even self-restoring, under voluntary transfers — and if such patterns exist, whether they are interesting or merely gerrymandered.
3. Whether the argument's force depends on the starting distribution being independently just, and if so, how much of that force survives once the initial holdings are themselves the product of past injustice.
4. Whether ex ante rule-setting that forestalls drift is fairly described as continuous interference, or whether the interference charge presupposes a baseline of outcome-redistribution that Pattern-as-Rules denies.
5. Whether the argument's real work is done by a deontic side-constraint — that no one may be used as a means to another's pattern — rather than by the consequentialist observation that liberty upsets patterns, and whether (S) holds on either reading.

After the decomposition, tell me which single sub-question your final number is most sensitive to, and what argument or piece of evidence would move it most. Finally, if two people both accept the Chamberlain transfers in the original case yet disagree about (S), where would you expect their disagreement to live?
