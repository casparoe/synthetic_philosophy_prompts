I run a small theory group inside a lab that has bet heavily on debate-style oversight: two AI systems argue a question before a weaker judge, and the judge's verdict is supposed to be more trustworthy than the judge's own unaided judgment. I have three researchers, two years, and no capacity to run training experiments. Our funders want to know whether this line is worth continuing at all, and what would have to be true for it to succeed. Please give me a defended research agenda: the four to six open questions about the limits of debate-based oversight that most deserve a theorist's next two years, ranked, with the ranking justified by explicit criteria — which questions are upstream of which, which are answerable with conceptual tools rather than experiments, which are neglected, and what result would move each up or down the list.

Two diagnoses of the difficulty have been competing inside my group, and I want your verdict on them folded into the ranking. The first is obfuscation: a dishonest debater can construct a case whose flaw exists and is in principle findable, but whose discovery costs more than the judge's budget allows, so the honest side loses by exhaustion rather than by being wrong. The second is underdetermination: for a large share of the questions we actually care about — what a user really wanted, whether an action is harmful, whether a model's stated reason is its real reason — there may be no fact of the matter that any judge could use to settle the dispute, however strong it is or however long it deliberates; interaction can amplify verification only where verification is possible in principle. My suspicion is that the field has been treating the second as a hard case of the first, and that the standard remedies — a stronger judge, a longer exchange, cross-examination, tool use by the debaters — address only the first. Tell me whether that diagnosis holds, and whether it should change what gets worked on first. If you think a third diagnosis is the real crux — say, that the guarantee is only ever relative to a judge that is itself a fallible model, so the hard question is whether any fixed point escapes that regress — place it in the ranking rather than substituting it for an argument.

Assume I know the standard positions; I want an agenda, not a survey, and not a reading list. Include, at the end, the work that looks important but that a group with our constraints should deliberately not touch, and say which of your top questions you expect to be dissolved by a single good argument rather than settled by accumulated work.

Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence — the one question you would put first — then give the reasoning. No preamble, and don't restate my question.
