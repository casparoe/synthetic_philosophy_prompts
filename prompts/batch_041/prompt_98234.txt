Something that's been bugging me while writing up my methods section, and I keep putting off thinking it through properly. In practice we just slap a flat prior on a parameter and call it "non-informative," on the grounds that we've got no reason to favour one value over another -- and somehow that's supposed to make the analysis objective. But Bertrand's chord thing is the classic embarrassment (same set-up, three different "uniform" answers depending on which variable you make uniform), and even Jeffreys priors only stay flat under certain parameterizations, so the choice of variable is quietly doing all the work. What does the everyday habit of grabbing the "obviously" symmetric prior actually presuppose about ignorance and about what probability is? Which rival pictures does it rule out -- straight subjective Bayesianism, fiducial-style reasoning, reference priors -- and is the habit even internally consistent once you notice that reparameterization flips the answer? It feels definately fine in simple finite cases, so I'm really asking what theory it commits you to, and what would have to change in day-to-day Bayesian practice if we gave up the principle of indifference and just owned that the prior is a modelling choice. Try to separate what the practice strictly needs from what it merely tends to travel with.
