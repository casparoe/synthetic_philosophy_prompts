In my graduate seminar on the outbreak of the First World War, two sentences are used interchangeably as though they settled the question: 'there was no single cause' and 'the war was overdetermined'. Both get treated as the conclusion that the war was bound to happen, and a classmate has pressed the second into service to argue that the counterfactual question — what would have happened had the assassination failed? — is not merely unanswerable but idle.

I meet overdetermination in a different register in a philosophy of causation reading group, where it names something narrower: the case in which two independent causes each suffice on their own for the same effect. I suspect my seminar is running together several quite different claims, and I want them separated. What precise theses could each of those two sentences be expressing — from the weak claim that many factors contributed, through the claim that several were each individually sufficient, to claims about inevitability, about robustness across a range of counterfactual variations, and about the impossibility of identifying any necessary condition? For each, say whether it is a platitude, a substantive and defensible claim, or false, what it presupposes, and whether it actually licenses the dismissal of counterfactual history. Tell me as well whether the two sentences are consistent with one another, whether the strict reading can be true of a singular, once-only event such as a war, and which reading the people who repeat these lines most likely intend.
