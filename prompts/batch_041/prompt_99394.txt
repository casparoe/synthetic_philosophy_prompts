I have no formal training in philosophy, but I have been thinking about advance directives since watching Still Alice, and I would like a reading of the film through the problem of the authority of the earlier self. In the film, Alice Howland, a linguistics professor with early-onset Alzheimer's, records a video message to her future self instructing that self to take an overdose of sleeping pills once she can no longer answer a set of personal questions. Later, she finds the video, gathers the pills, and is interrupted before she can act; by the end she can barely speak, and the film closes on her daughter reading to her and on Alice's one-word response about love. The standard debate asks whether a competent person's earlier wishes should bind the later, incompetent person, and whether the later person is the same self at all. I am interested in a version of the question that the film seems to dramatize but not settle: is an advance directive better understood as a command issued by one self to another, or as a letter from an earlier self to a later self who is under no obligation to obey it? If it is a command, what gives the earlier self authority over someone who may no longer share her values? If it is a letter, does the later self's present experience have any authority at all, or does the earlier self's voice simply become one more piece of evidence about what the later self would have wanted? I would like a reading that takes both the film and the philosophical problem seriously, points to the scenes that matter, and says where the film resists the reading. Does Still Alice come down on the side of precedent autonomy, or does it quietly side with the later self? And what does the film add to the debate that a purely philosophical argument might miss?
