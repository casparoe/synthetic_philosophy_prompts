My two closest friends are getting married in three weeks and I've been asked to give the toast, and I've rewritten it four times now because of a problem I can't get around on my own. Both of them are philosophers — one's finishing a dissertation on Hume on promising, the other is a public defender — and they wrote their own vows, and the vows are strange. There's nothing in them about loving each other forever, or about always being happy, or about any of the other things you can't actually control; they said they weren't going to promise each other things they can't be sure of delivering, because a promise you might not be able to keep isn't a promise, its a wish. What they did promise is the small stuff: to tell each other the truth even when it costs something, to keep showing up for the boring parts, and then a third thing it took me a while to understand — each of them promises that if the other ever breaks one of the other promises, the one who was let down gets to say so, out loud, and neither of them is allowed to pretend it didn't happen or act like the other one is being crazy for bringing it up. They call it the repair clause, which is very them. Half the people at this wedding are philosophers from their department, and half are the two families — loud, sentimental, one grandmother who will definately be waiting for the word "forever" to turn up somewhere, plus a set of divorced parents who are not going to enjoy a toast that sounds like it's grading the marriage for logical validity. I'm not a philosopher myself, which is sort of the point of me doing this.

Here's my problem. I keep noticing that when people talk warmly about promises at weddings, they're usually running one of two pictures, and the pictures come apart in a way that matters. On the first one, promising somebody is basically handing them a reliable expectation — I promise X, so now you can count on X, and the bad thing about breaking it is the disappointment, the dashed expectation, the plans you made on a false basis. On the second one, promising is more like handing somebody something: you don't just get to expect X, you get to require it, and if I don't come through then you've been wronged rather than merely let down — you have standing to be angry with me, to bring it up, to hold it against me, and to let me off the hook if you'd rather. I've been arguing about this with their friends for weeks and I've convinced myself that the two of them are on the second picture, and that this is exactly why the repair clause is in there and why there's no "forever" in the vows. On the second picture a promise isn't a prediction, it's a transfer of authority, and what they're doing up there is giving each other authority over the things you can actually hand over. So the toast can't be about how "you can always count on each other" — that's the first picture, and it turns the whole wedding into a well-managed logistics operation, and it's also just not what they think they're doing.

So: write it. Five minutes, warm and funny and a little sentimental, for a room that's mostly not philosophers — concrete stories, no jargon, nothing that sounds like a seminar — but under the surface a genuinely correct piece of philosophy, so that the twenty philosophers in the room hear the second picture and not the first, and hear it as the thing being celebrated rather than as a technicality. Put the repair clause at the emotional centre; I want the room to feel that the third promise is the romantic one, not the cold one, and that a promise you can be held to is a better gift than a promise that merely comes true. Invent whatever small details you need — how they met, a running joke, the grandmother, the year one of them spent abroad — just keep them mundane and plausible and don't let them carry any weight they can't hold. Don't quote anybody and don't cite anything, and please don't use "forever" in a way that implies they've promised it.
