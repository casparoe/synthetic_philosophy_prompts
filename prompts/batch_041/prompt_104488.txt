You are a political philosopher who works on property theory, the philosophy of law, and urban politics. Think carefully and systematically before answering, and do not settle for the first framing that comes to mind. I want you to reconstruct the implicit theory behind a set of land-use practices, so I will describe what my city actually does.

First, the large majority of residentially zoned land is reserved for detached single-family houses; apartments are permitted only along a few corridors and in a few designated centers. Second, a project that satisfies every numerical standard in the code can still be denied by the planning commission, which is required to consider whether the proposal is compatible with the scale and character of the surrounding neighborhood; the commission's judgment on that question is treated as discretionary rather than as a finding of fact. Third, new buildings must supply a minimum number of off-street parking spaces. Fourth, and this is the part I cannot square with the rest: a use that was lawful when it was built but that the code no longer permits — a corner store, a small apartment building, a workshop — may be forced to close or convert after a set period, sometimes ten or twenty years, although its owner has done nothing wrong and the use harms no one. Meanwhile the expectations of the people living nearby — quiet, being able to park on their own street, living among buildings that look like the ones already there — are treated as weighty enough to justify refusing a permit outright, and nothing ever expires those expectations.

Please address the following.

1. What conception of property does this package presuppose — a bundle of severable rights, a thing owned, a grant from a political community, a relation among persons? What conception of the city, and of who has standing to shape it? Does it presuppose something like the claim, associated with Henri Lefebvre, that inhabitants have a right to the city, or does it presuppose the opposite: that the city is a joint venture whose terms are fixed by those who arrived first?

2. Separate what the practices strictly require from what they merely fit comfortably. Could a Lockean, a utilitarian, a republican about non-domination, and a Lefebvrian each endorse this package, or must each reject some part of it? Where are the practices philosophically underdetermined, such that several incompatible theories would license them?

3. The asymmetry between uses that expire and neighborhood expectations that never do: is it coherent? Is there a principled distinction available — harm versus mere displeasure, reliance, consent, the generality of the rule, the difference between a use and an expectation, the difference between what one owns and what one merely benefits from? Or is this a hierarchy of political power wearing the costume of doctrine?

4. If the presupposed view were replaced by a rival, what would change on the ground? Name what a planner would stop doing and what she would start doing.

Open with a brief brainstorming section listing the candidate theories and flagging which you expect to survive scrutiny, then write the analysis itself in continuous prose.
