I'm in a graduate seminar on the philosophy of prehistory, and we've spent the last few weeks moving between Sahlins' case that foragers were the original affluent society — the argument that affluence is a relation between wants and their satisfaction, achievable either by producing much or by desiring little, and that hunter-gatherers took the second road — Diamond's 1987 Discover essay "The Worst Mistake in the History of the Human Race," and Scott's Against the Grain. Diamond records a Kalahari forager's answer when asked why he hadn't emulated his farming neighbours: "Why should we, when there are so many mongongo nuts in the world?" I've been asked to write a short position paper on whether the agricultural revolution was a mistake, and I'd like substantive criticism rather than line edits. My draft is below.

The claim I want to defend is that "was the agricultural revolution a mistake?" is not one question but four, and that the ones people care about are the ones the evidence cannot settle. First, did the transition lower average individual welfare — nutrition, disease load, work effort, security? Second, did it lower aggregate welfare, given that it also multiplied the number of people whose welfare there was to sum? Third, was it a mistake in the sense of a choice the choosers had reason to regret, rather than a trap no one chose? Fourth, was it the origin of social inequality? The usual staging of the debate — Sahlins' leisured and well-fed foragers against the progressivist story of farming as escape from misery, with Diamond as prosecutor — blurs all four. The interesting position, I think, is that agriculture can be a catastrophe on the first question, a success on the second, not a mistake at all on the third, and irrelevant on the fourth.

On inequality I think the equation of farming with hierarchy is false as stated. Sedentary, ranked, slaveholding societies existed without agriculture — the Northwest Coast peoples, the Calusa of Florida, the Chumash — and there is status differentiation in the forager record: the Upper Palaeolithic graves at Sungir, with their thousands of ivory beads, and Mesolithic cemeteries such as Vedbæk and Skateholm, are hard to read as egalitarian, whatever else one says about them. The distinction that seems to do the work is the one between immediate-return and delayed-return foraging systems: hierarchy tracks the possibility of storing, defending, and inheriting wealth, and some foragers have that possibility while others don't. On this reading agriculture doesn't invent inequality; it makes a particular kind of inequality — durable, inheritable, and legible to a state — the default, which is a weaker and more interesting claim. Something like the insistence in The Dawn of Everything that the Neolithic is neither a revolution nor a single event belongs here too.

On welfare I am less confident than the standard citations suggest. The famous fifteen-to-twenty-hour working week comes from Lee's Ju/'hoansi material and counts only the acquisition of food; add processing and cooking and it moves toward forty hours, and the Hadza work harder still. Diamond concedes, in the same essay, that the foragers we can observe were largely those whom farmers had pushed onto the worst land, which makes them a biased sample for inferences about the Palaeolithic. But even if every one of those numbers is wrong, I don't think the affluence thesis is refuted, because Sahlins defines affluence as a ratio rather than a quantity, and a ratio can be closed from either end. Work-hour data measure only the producing-much end. If affluence is the relation between wants and their satisfaction, no amount of archaeology settles it, and that seems to me the real reason the debate is stalled.

That gives me an uneasy conclusion. On aggregate welfare the arithmetic is the familiar one: more people, each perhaps worse off, and a sum that comes out ahead only if one accepts the structure of the mere-addition argument. On choice, the trap story — Boserupian population pressure, the ratchet of sedentism, Scott's point that cereal agriculture is what makes a population taxable and therefore what makes a state worth building — looks like a reason to say there was no mistake, because there was no choice. So "agriculture was a mistake" is, I think, either a compressed way of asserting the average-welfare claim, in which case it is an empirical claim the evidence does not clearly support, or an expression of regret that is not a claim at all.

What I want from you, roughly in order of how much it would help: first, whether this four-way decomposition is the right one or whether it collapses — in particular whether the question about mistakes survives being separated from the welfare questions, or is just a confused way of asking them, and whether my closing dilemma is a real dilemma or a false one. Second, which of my empirical claims are actually secure and which I should hedge or drop; I wrote this quickly and my grasp of the archaeological literature is patchy, so I would rather be told that Sungir is doing work it cannot bear than be told my prose is clumsy. Third, whether the trap/Boserupian move makes the word "mistake" coherent or destroys it, and whether that matters. And fourth, honestly, whether the paper is interesting — or whether it merely restates what everyone in the seminar already believes in a more elaborate vocabulary.
