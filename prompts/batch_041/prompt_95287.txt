I am a professor of philosophy, and my route into the field ran through economics: I completed a master's degree in economics before my doctorate, I still teach the rational-choice segment of our department's methods course, and I am comfortable with the formal apparatus at roughly the level of a good graduate micro sequence — extensive-form games, Bayesian equilibrium, correlated equilibrium and its epistemic characterization, common knowledge as against mutual knowledge of finite order, focal points, equilibrium selection, cheap talk, and the folk theorem for repeated games with punishment. My research is in social ontology, and for the past two terms I have run a graduate seminar on nations and nationalism, where I have taught the modernist line with real sympathy: that nations are modern artifacts rather than ancient continuities, that much of what passes for immemorial custom was deliberately fashioned within the last two centuries, and that the nation is a community imagined rather than encountered — imagined as limited, as sovereign, and as a deep horizontal fellowship among strangers — with print capitalism doing much of the work of making that imagination possible.

What I want from you is a translation. Restate that position as faithfully as you can in the vocabulary I have just described: coordination games and their multiple equilibria, payoff structures, focal points, correlated equilibrium and the public randomization device that sustains it, common knowledge and the higher-order beliefs it requires, self-enforcing agreements, equilibrium selection by an actor able to shift payoffs, and the technology of public events that manufacture common knowledge. Michael Chwe has worked in this neighbourhood, treating rituals and public ceremonies as machines for producing common knowledge in coordination problems; I would like to know whether his translation is the right one and where it distorts. Keep the translation honest: where a step requires me to accept a game-theoretic assumption the modernists never made — that the relevant payoffs are material, that participants are expected-utility maximizers with correct beliefs, that a self-enforcing arrangement is what they were aiming at — say so, flag it as an addition rather than a translation, and tell me what it buys. And please do not hand me a two-by-two matrix and call it a nation.

Then the residue report, which is the part I actually care about. First, does anything in the formalism correspond to the temporal content of the modernist claim — the nation experienced as moving through what Anderson, following Benjamin, names homogeneous empty time, the tradition experienced by its bearers as ancient? Second, what becomes of the critical bite of the word invented? In equilibrium terms an invented tradition is simply an equilibrium that holds, and the framework seems indifferent to whether the beliefs sustaining it are true; if the accusation loses its force in translation, is that a defect of the framework or evidence that the accusation was doing rhetorical rather than explanatory work? Third, coordination gains are a thin motive for dying; can the framework represent the willingness to die for the nation without positing a taste for it as an exogenous payoff, and if not, what does that show? Fourth, what becomes of the class-analytic strand in Hobsbawm — the claim that invented traditions serve particular class positions — once everything is expressed as payoffs and beliefs? Fifth, Rubinstein's email game shows that arbitrarily high finite orders of mutual knowledge can behave discontinuously from common knowledge; if the modernist story depends on something like common knowledge being produced by print, does that result make the story more fragile than its proponents acknowledge? Finally, tell me whether the residue points to something the game-theoretic vocabulary cannot express at all — normativity, or collective intentionality in Searle's sense, or the first-person plural as such — or whether it points instead to the modernist thesis being less determinate than it sounds.
