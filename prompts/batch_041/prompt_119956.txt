Two camps in the philosophy of mind have been talking past each other for decades. Illusionists hold that the properties we take ourselves to be acquainted with when we attend to our own experience — the raw feel of a colour, the ache of a tooth, the taste of mint — are not properties of our mental lives at all, but artefacts of introspection and of the way we talk about ourselves; the task for a science of consciousness is to explain why we are so thoroughly convinced that such properties exist, not to locate them. Realists about phenomenal consciousness hold that those properties are perfectly real, whatever their ultimate nature, and that a theory which leaves them out has left out the very thing it was meant to explain. The two sides agree on almost nothing except that the other side is wrong. Your task is to write the joint statement they could both sign after a genuinely good-faith adversarial collaboration. Write in British English.

The document should have four sections.

1. Agreed claims. A numbered list of propositions both sides can put their names to. Push the agreement as far as it will honestly go: what the explanandum is; which first-person reports count as data and which are already theory-laden; what any theory of consciousness must be answerable to; and what neither side is committed to, so that a reader does not mistake either position for something cruder (behaviourism, the denial that anything happens when one sees red, the denial that introspection ever tells us anything). Each claim must be one that a naive observer might reasonably have expected the two sides to disagree about. No platitudes, and nothing so weak that nobody would deny it.

2. Disagreements. A table with one row per remaining disagreement and four columns: the issue; the illusionist's formulation of its own position, worded so that an illusionist would accept it; the realist's formulation of its own position, worded so that a realist would accept it; and the residual point of substance once the two formulations sit side by side. Where you suspect a row is largely a verbal dispute, say so, and identify precisely which word or contrast is doing the work.

3. Cruxes. The sub-questions such that settling one would move at least one side. For each: why it is a crux rather than a restatement of the disagreement, and what each side would count as an answer telling against its own view. The likeliest territory is whether a seeming can justify a belief about what it seems to be, independently of any theory; whether introspective reports are about properties or about vehicles and attitudes; and whether an illusion can be an illusion of the very thing whose existence is denied. Treat these as pointers rather than a checklist, and add cruxes of your own. Prefer cruxes that are tractable — distinctions that can be drawn, findings that could in principle be had — over ones that merely re-assert a metaphysics.

4. Two cases, with each side's predictions recorded in advance.

(a) The dial case. A subject is fitted with a device allowing an experimenter to shift, independently of the stimulus, her tendency to report a particular qualitative character, so that she reports the cool taste of mint while drinking warm salt water, and reports it with her usual confidence. What does each side say her experience is like in the moment before she speaks? What does each say her report is tracking? And what further measurement, if it were available, would separate the two predictions?

(b) The borrowed-vocabulary case. A subject is brought up with no qualitative vocabulary at all and is trained to describe her experiences exhaustively in functional, attentional and dispositional terms — what she is attending to, what she is disposed to do, what she can discriminate. She becomes fluent. Does she still report her experiences, in a different idiom? Has she lost the ability to report them? Or was there never anything there to report? Each side states its answer, and states what would follow for its own view if the other's answer turned out to be right.

Close the statement with a short section giving, for each side, the strongest consideration the collaboration left unresolved, and saying plainly whether the two positions can be expected to converge on further evidence or whether the remaining difference is a choice between two ways of describing the same facts.

Do not attribute any of this to named authors or works, and do not introduce schools or labels beyond illusionism and realism; write both positions in plain terms, so that the substance is intelligible to a reader who has never encountered the debate.
