I'm a novelist — three books, the most recent one set in a made-up coastal town — and I've somehow ended up on the editorial-standards panel of my publisher, which mostly means reading angry letters and voting on whether we slap disclaimers into the front matter. This is the case I can't stop chewing on, and I have to bring a written opinion to next Tuesday's meeting, so I need your help.

The setup: one of our authors, call her D., published a novel two years ago set in a town she invented and named Marisol Bay. She liked the sound of it, had never been anywhere near that coast, and says the whole place came straight out of her head. Turns out there *is* a real Marisol Bay, a couple thousand people, and it also turns out the name is a phrase in a local Indigenous language she doesn't speak (something like 'bitter water,' the townspeople say) and that it predates her book by about a century. The town council has now written to us demanding we add a note to future printings saying the Marisol Bay in the novel is not their Marisol Bay and that the events are invented. D. is furious. Her line is that she meant *her* town, the one in her head, and it's not her fault some real place happens to share the name; she says if we cave, every novelist who ever picked a pretty-sounding place name is definately on the hook for what some real town thinks of it. The council's line is that the book is *about* their town now whether she likes it or not — that's what the name picks out, and they're the ones who have to live there. I half-remember a philosophy thing from college where someone imagines a planet exactly like ours except the water isn't water, and the punchline is supposed to be that what our words mean isn't entirely up to what's going on in our heads. I have a feeling it cuts into this case but I genuinely can't tell which way.

What I need from you is the opinion I'll circulate: a clear recommendation (add the note, don't, or add something else) plus the reasoning, in plain terms that a room of editors and one lawyer can follow, and be honest about the strongest consideration on the side you come out against — D. will be sitting right there and she's not wrong about everything. Give me the bottom line first, then the reasoning, then a one-paragraph version of the losing side. Keep it under 700 words, no footnotes, and please don't hand me a reading list — I'm not going to read a philosophy paper before Tuesday, I just need to know what to say.
