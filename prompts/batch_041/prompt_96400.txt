I run the quarterly forecasting review for a mid-size specialty insurer. Four times a year my team has to hand the risk committee a single probability for each of a short list of events the firm is exposed to — whether a regulator clears a filing before a stated date, whether a named counterparty is downgraded within a year, whether a jurisdiction restructures — and the committee uses those numbers directly to size hedges and set reserves. There is no budget or time inside a cycle to commission new research: the number has to come out of a ninety-minute meeting. I am not a statistician; two of my five analysts are; and the chief risk officer, who signs the paper, will not read anything containing a formula.

The recurring problem is that two analysts look at the same event and come back far apart — 30% and 60% on what is nominally the same question — and the meeting ends either in an average nobody believes or in whatever the most senior person in the room asserts. I know there is a formal result somewhere behind this, Aumann's agreement theorem, and an assumption sitting underneath it, the common prior assumption, and I suspect it tells me something I could actually use. What I cannot do is get from the result to anything my analysts could run on a Tuesday.

Please write me that procedure. I want the questions to ask, in order, of two people who disagree, together with what counts as a sufficient answer to each and who is responsible for giving it; the tests that separate a disagreement about what the evidence is from a disagreement about where you start from before any evidence arrives from a disagreement about how the same evidence should be read; the point at which the procedure concludes that further discussion will not help and says so; and the point at which it stops and escalates, on the grounds that the remaining difference is not a factual one and somebody has to choose. The procedure has to be runnable by a chair who does not know the theory, so translate rather than cite — I am not going to read the literature, and anything I am meant to use needs to be inside the procedure itself. It should also tell me what to record at the end of a review so the next cycle is not starting from nothing.

Then run it on three cases and tell me, for each, what the procedure does, where it stops, and what it hands back:

1. Two analysts at 30% and 60% on whether the regulator clears the filing. The one at 60% has had a private conversation with a contact at the regulator and is not permitted to say what was said. I want to know what the procedure does when the condition it needs cannot be met because the information cannot be pooled, and whether the person who cannot disclose can still be made to answer anything useful.

2. Two analysts who, after a full exchange, agree on every fact, give the same answer when asked what they would have said knowing nothing but what is public, and still disagree — because they read the same leaked draft differently, one treating it as diagnostic of the regulator's intentions and the other as noise. I expect to be told this is outside the theorem and that I have to pick a reading by fiat. If that is the answer, say it plainly, and tell me who should be doing the picking and on what authority.

3. The case I will not enjoy. Two analysts converge quickly and comfortably on a number, and I need the procedure to make me distrust that agreement rather than reward it: last year the team converged on a number that was badly wrong, because both analysts had leaned on the same vendor's scenario set, and nothing in our process noticed.

Finally, tell me which conditions of the result are doing real work in meetings like mine and which are idealizations I can drop without losing the point; whether a procedure of this kind can ever establish that a common starting point exists rather than merely detecting when it fails; and what the procedure cannot do. If the honest conclusion is that the result has very little to say about the meetings I actually hold, say that instead of dressing it up as a method.
