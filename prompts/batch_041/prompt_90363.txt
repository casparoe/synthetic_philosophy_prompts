Some friends and I have been going back and forth for weeks about the necessity-of-origin thesis — the claim, usually traced back to Kripke, that you could not have come from a different sperm and egg, so that your actual biological origin is essential to you — and we keep getting stuck not on whether the thesis is true but on what it would commit us to if it were. I have the possible-worlds machinery well enough in hand; what I want is the downstream picture. Please describe the most important implications of the thesis, assumed for the sake of argument, for how we should think about the practical apparatus of assisted reproduction and the moral and legal categories built on top of it: genetic parenthood, gamete donation, embryo selection, cloning, mitochondrial replacement, and the identity-based harms people invoke when they argue that a child with a given condition should have been conceived differently, or not at all. I would rather have three or four implications traced carefully to the end than a dozen gestured at, and I would like the ordering justified — if you tell me something is the most important implication, say what makes it more important than the runners-up.

Three cases keep defeating our intuitions, and I would like the implications traced through them rather than bracketed off as complications. First, twinning: a single fertilized egg splits and two people result. If the origin is the fertilized egg, then the twins share an origin and the thesis cannot distinguish them at all; if the origin is the genome, then the twins are distinguished but a clone whose nuclear genome is drawn from an adult's body cell would seem to inherit the donor's origin, which is not what the thesis is supposed to say. Second, manufactured gametes: suppose sperm and eggs could be produced from ordinary somatic cells, so that neither parent ever produced a gamete in the ordinary sense. The idea of a different sperm and egg then seems to lose its grip, since the gamete is not an individual that predates the child and might have been paired with a different egg; it is a manufactured artifact derived from cells that were already part of someone. Third, freezing and delay: if a sperm and an egg are frozen and combined twenty years later, the thesis apparently permits the resulting person to have been conceived much later, since the very same gametes are involved — which drives a wedge between sameness of origin and sameness of time, and I suspect that wedge matters for the non-identity problem, where the thought that a child conceived later would be a different child is often doing unacknowledged work.

I do not need a bibliography; I will chase down the literature myself, and I would rather you spend the effort on the argument than on hedging about who said what. What I want is for you to assume the thesis and then follow it honestly, including into the places where it turns out to have little or no practical bite, and to be explicit about where the metaphysical claim and the ethical claim come apart — in particular, whether the thesis actually constrains any decision that anyone faces, or whether it mostly supplies the modal backdrop that other arguments quietly presuppose. And if you think the three cases above are not the right pressure points, say which cases are, and why.
