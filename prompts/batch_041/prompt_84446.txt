I'm presenting a seminar paper in two weeks to a political theory group and I need to be ready for the Q&A. The paper defends something I've been calling the counterfactual baseline test for political legitimacy. Roughly: a regime's rule is legitimate to the extent that the people living under it would not, on reflection, prefer the realistic alternative actually available to them — which for most of the world is not liberal democracy but chaos, a worse strongman, a foreign-backed kleptocracy, or a slightly less bad version of the same regime. I use China and Singapore as the live cases. Two sub-claims I lean on hard. First, the test applies to the state, not to the government, so a regime can be legitimate even where a particular leader or party isn't — that decoupling is what I think lets Singapore count without me having to defend everything the PAP has ever done. Second, "realistic alternative" has to mean something like a nearby possible world, not a thought experiment where everyone suddenly becomes a Swiss liberal; I try to constrain it with what I call the reachability constraint, basically that the alternative has to be one that some actual coalition at the time could plausibly have brought about.

The audience is the problem. It's mostly liberal political philosophers — consent people and public reason people — who think legitimacy has to be earned through some kind of justification to the governed and who will hear "performance legitimacy" as a dressed-up version of "he built roads so he can do what he likes." There's one Singaporean public lawyer in the group who is quietly sympathetic to my view but hates the way Westerners talk about his country, so he'll object from the other direction, that I'm still measuring Singapore against a Western yardstick even when I'm defending it. And there are two China scholars who will say the whole framing smuggles in proceduralism and that my empirical claims about what the "realistic alternative" was are hand-waving dressed up as counterfactual analysis.

What I want from you is a prep sheet, not a defense. Give me the eight or so objections I'll actually get from this room — not the generic ones any political philosopher would produce, the ones this specific crowd will reach for — ordered by how likely each is times how much damage it does to me. Phrase each one the way the person who'll raise it would phrase it. For each, a reply I can give in about two minutes, and a note on where that reply is soft or question-begging. Tell me what I should concede up front to buy credibility, and which single reply has to land for the paper to survive the hour.

A few things I already know are coming, so don't spend much space on them unless you think I'm wrong to treat them as easy: the adaptive-preferences objection (preferences formed under a regime can't validate it), the measurability objection (who decides what counts as prosperity and order), and the collapse-into-consequentialism objection. I'm more worried about the ones I haven't anticipated.

Also: I've noticed my test seems to license a competent colonial administration, a well-run hereditary monarchy, and — if I'm honest — probably Pinochet's Chile for the people who weren't disappeared. I don't know if that's a reductio or just the cost of the view, so include that as an objection and tell me how bad it really is. And end with the question I'm hoping nobody asks.
