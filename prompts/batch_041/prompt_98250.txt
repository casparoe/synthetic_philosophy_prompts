My working life is spent deciding what may be a type, what may be a row, and how the two are allowed to relate: I design schemas and type systems, and my metaphysics amounts to a single undergraduate course. I keep half-suspecting that the problem of universals is a dispute I have already had with colleagues in a different vocabulary, and I want to know whether that suspicion survives contact with the details. So please make the translation systematic.

First, restate the central positions in the debate over universals — realism about universals, resemblance nominalism, and trope theory — in the vocabulary I actually use: schemas and instances, types and values, abstract versus concrete types, interfaces and their implementations, structural versus nominal typing, foreign keys and referential integrity, and the machinery by which properties are represented (columns, attributes, and the constraints that restrict them). Take the positions one at a time, and for each say precisely what maps onto what.

Then do the harder part. The debate turns on a few load-bearing puzzles, and I want each of them re-run in my terms. There is the argument that many particulars can share a single property because there is one thing they all are, rather than merely resembling each other; there is the regress that seems to threaten any account which treats a shared property as one more thing of the same kind as its instances; there is the question whether there can be universals that nothing instantiates; and there is the question what we are doing when we appear to refer to a property itself rather than to any of its instances. For each, show me how it looks once restated — for instance, what becomes of uninstantiated universals in a system that already tolerates empty types, unused schemas, or constraints that no row satisfies, and what becomes of the regress in a framework that must give an account of the type of its own types. Where the translation works, show the working. Where it only appears to work, say what you had to fudge to get it.

Finish with the residue report, which is what I actually want. Which parts of the metaphysical debate cannot be expressed in terms of schemas, types, and instances at all? Be specific about the candidates: the question whether properties exist independently of any mind or any schema; the difference between a thing's satisfying a constraint and a thing's having a property; the sense in which an instance is one of a universal rather than merely conforming to a declaration. Then give me a verdict, and defend it: is the residue a defect of my framework — something a richer representational scheme could capture — or is it evidence that the philosophers' dispute is less substantive than its vocabulary suggests, so that my framework falls silent only because there is less there to say? I would rather have a definite answer with its costs spelled out than a survey of both options. Take the space you need; thoroughness matters more here than brevity.
