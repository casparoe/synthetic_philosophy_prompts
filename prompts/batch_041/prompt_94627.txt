Me and a friend got into it over whether calling Jefferson a hypocrite or Columbus a monster is fair, or whether the "he was a man of his time" move just settles it. He says his version settles it for everyone, and I can't find the exit. It goes:

(1) Blaming someone means saying they ought to have done otherwise.
(2) Ought implies can, so you're only on the hook for what was actually available to you.
(3) What was available to you depends on what you could have known.
(4) Nobody can be expected to see past the moral vocabulary their whole society has on hand — the concepts you'd need to criticize the thing are collective achievements, not something one person just up and invents.
(5) So if a society hadn't articulated the wrongness of something, the individual couldn't have known better.
(6) Virginia in the 1770s hadn't articulated it, so Jefferson gets a pass; same for Churchill and the empire, same for Columbus.
(7) And the same reasoning runs forward: whatever we're doing right now that our great-grandkids will find monstrous also gets a pass, since nobody's written the criticism yet.
(8) So moral criticism of anyone, past or present, is never justified.

Which is obviously nuts, and yet I want to keep (1) through (5). Which step is doing the damage, and what do I pay if I drop it? What I care most about keeping is that blame needs the agent to have had some real shot at knowing better — I don't want to just say "everyone's always blameable." But I also don't want the pass to be automatic.
