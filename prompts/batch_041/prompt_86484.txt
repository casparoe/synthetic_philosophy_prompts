I'm co-writing a methodological piece on non-human causes in history — pathogens, volcanic aerosol veils, multi-year droughts — and my co-author and I have reached an impasse that I'd like you to adjudicate numerically rather than rhetorically. She proposes what she calls a constraint/actor test: a non-human factor counts as a historical actor in an explanation just when (i) it makes a difference to a human decision or institution that would otherwise have gone differently, (ii) it is individuated as a dated event rather than as a standing feature of the environment, and (iii) its contribution survives both a corrected chronology and a differently chosen but equally defensible evidence base. On her reading, environmental-determinist claims are rarely conceptually confused about agency; they fail (iii). They are fragile to chronology, and the familiar charge that such accounts treat the weather as a decision-maker is a redescription of that fragility rather than an independent error. I suspect the two diagnoses come apart — that some of these claims are wrong in a way no amount of better dating would fix — and I want to know how much of the terrain is hers.

What I want is your credence that she is right: that, across the well-documented cases in which a non-human factor is alleged to have reshaped a human institution (a pandemic that removes a third of a labor force, a stratospheric veil that shortens two consecutive growing seasons, a drought that outlasts a polity's reserves), the characteristic failure of environmental-determinist claims is evidential fragility — chronology, magnitude, confounding, attribution — rather than a conceptual error about agency, or a naturalizing of arrangements that were in fact contingent and political. Decompose it, with a number and one line of justification for each term: the share of the failure that is genuinely evidential; the share that is genuinely conceptual, where "conceptual" has to mean something not already entailed by the evidential story; the probability that the two diagnoses are not distinct at all, so that the question has no determinate answer — I would rather you say that outright than bury it in a middling number; and the probability that the case class is too heterogeneous for a single verdict.

Then combine them, tell me which term you are least confident in, and tell me what would move the number most: a re-dated eruption that rescues a case, a case that fails even on the best possible chronology, or a demonstration that the two diagnoses make divergent predictions about which claims to discard. If you think the dispute dissolves rather than resolves, say what it dissolves into.
