I have no training in philosophy — I read it on my own — but I keep returning to the opening of Tom Stoppard's Rosencrantz and Guildenstern Are Dead (the play he first staged at the Edinburgh Fringe in 1966), and I cannot decide what that scene is really about. Rosencrantz calls heads on a coin toss, and the coin comes up heads ninety-two times in a row; Guildenstern grows steadily more agitated, tries to account for the streak by appealing to the law of probability, and finally wonders aloud whether the two of them have fallen "within un-, sub- or supernatural forces." What strikes me is that he never settles on what he takes probability to be. At moments he talks as if the coin itself owed him a tails; at others he recites the fact that each toss is independent and so no single result should surprise anyone; and at still others he treats the streak as a fact about his own state of mind, or wonders whether he is somehow willing the outcome. I would like a reading of the play — that opening scene, and the way everything afterward unfolds behind it, with the Player and his troupe, the swapped letter, the pirates, and the two of them finally dying offstage exactly as they do in Hamlet — through the philosophical debate over what probability actually is. In particular, read it against the three standard positions: the frequentist idea that a probability is nothing but a limiting relative frequency in a long run of trials; the subjectivist idea that it is a degree of belief held by some agent and answerable only to coherence; and the propensity idea that it is a tendency or disposition lodged in the physical setup itself, in the coin or the tossing arrangement.

I realize the play may not line up neatly with any of the three, and I don't want it forced. So be as careful about the philosophy as about the play. Say which position, if any, Guildenstern is groping toward in each of his moods, and which position the play as a whole seems to presuppose — or whether it quietly slides between them, or trades on a confusion between chance and mere unpredictability, or between a probability that reflects our ignorance and one that belongs to the world itself. I would also like the standard difficulties brought to bear: what a probability can even mean for a single toss, and which reference class a run like this is supposed to belong to. And where the play resists the reading — where it is doing something the philosophy of probability has no room for, perhaps about fate, or about the difference between a coincidence and a script — I would rather you say so plainly than smooth it over. End by telling me what the play adds to the debate that the arguments alone do not, if anything. No citations or reading lists, please; I want the argument and the reading themselves.
