Next month I'm interviewing a philosopher for my podcast who's defending a view I haven't quite seen put this way: she says the norm of assertion isn't knowledge or truth or belief, but epistemic trustworthiness -- you may assert p just in case your audience would be entitled to take your word for it, and she thinks knowledge is what makes that entitlement apt. I want questions that actually press this instead of letting her restate the usual knowledge-account talking points. Give me 8-10 questions, each with a follow-up keyed to the answer she's most likely to give, and make sure I hit: the lottery case (the flat-out assertion that you'll lose); Moore's paradox in both the 'I don't know that' and 'I don't believe that' forms, and whether her account gets the second one for free or has to bolt on a sincerity condition; whether making the norm audience-relative lets in too much (asserting to no one, or to someone who'd trust anything you say); whether this really just collapses into the idea that assertion aims to update the common ground; and whether the account can explain why lying is a distinct wrong from merely asserting without knowledge. End with one that asks what would actually count against her view. No softball 'how did you get into philosophy' stuff.
