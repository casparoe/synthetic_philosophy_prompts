I have been thinking about the puzzle in Plato's Meno about why knowledge should be worth more than true belief. As I understand it, Socrates' answer is that a true belief is like one of the statues of Daedalus, which looks fine while it stays put but runs away unless it is tied down, and that knowledge is simply a true belief that has been fastened in place. If that is the whole answer, then what turns a true belief into knowledge is nothing more than the believer's grip on it: a belief I am certain I will never abandon — even one that a hypnotist has made me incapable of doubting — would count as knowledge, provided only that what I believe is true, and the talk of justification that fills modern epistemology is an extra requirement invented by later philosophers. It would follow that knowledge is more valuable than true belief merely because it is more durable, and that the Meno problem is not a deep problem after all. I would be grateful if you could tell me whether this reading is correct, and if it is not, what the tying down is supposed to be if it is not simply my own confidence.
