For a graduate seminar in the philosophy of science, draft a deadpan product warranty and terms-of-service agreement, about 700 words, issued by a fictional firm called Structure and Co. that licenses scientific theories to users. The warranty promises to preserve a theory's mathematical structure across theoretical replacement while expressly disclaiming liability for the unobservable entities the theory names. Let the numbered clauses quietly take positions on the central issues: what exactly is retained under theory change, whether the retention is epistemic or ontic, how a Ramsey-sentence formulation earns its keep by existentially quantifying over theoretical terms, and what happens when a customer invokes Newman's objection, the complaint that any structure can be satisfied trivially if the domain is made large enough. Use the no-miracles argument as the sales pitch and pessimistic meta-induction as the returns policy, and let the distinction between structure and nature do the work of the coverage exclusions. The humor should come from getting the position right rather than from inventing problems; a philosopher of science should recognize the objections and find the parody fair. Do not name living theorists. Close with a three-question customer-service FAQ.
