I've been reading a lot of the "lessons of history" literature — Munich, Vietnam, 1914, the Thucydides trap stuff — and I want to put a number on a hypothesis of mine, with the reasoning split apart so I can see where it lives and where it dies.

Here's the claim. Call an analogy "canonical" when it keeps showing up in strategic argument, the way Munich does. My claim is that the canon gets selected for usability rather than accuracy: an episode becomes a "lesson" when it has a vivid outcome, a simple moral, and a visible decision attached to it that someone can point at. But those are also exactly the features that make an analogy travel badly, because they're what strips out the structural detail that made the source case turn out the way it did. So the analogies invoked most often should be, on average, among the least reliable ones — the whole lessons-of-history apparatus is a machine for manufacturing confidence, not accuracy.

Three things I want kept seperate. (a) Whether the canon-selection story is right. (b) Whether it matters — if the analogy gets invoked after the decision is already made and does no real work, then my claim is true in a boring way and false in the way that matters. (c) Even granting it's invoked and does move things, whether the net effect is negative: does the false confidence it supplies outweigh whatever genuine structural transfer it carries? And (d) what the counterfactual even is — nobody decides in a vacuum, so the alternative isn't "no analogy," it's some other analogy or the bureaucratic default.

Give me your credence in the claim as stated, decomposed into those terms (or better ones), with a number and a line of justification for each, how they combine, and which one the final number is most sensitive to. I'm at about 0.6, and I suspect the disagreement with someone who'd say 0.2 is mostly about (b) — though I cant be sure wether it's (b) or (d). And if you think the claim doesn't have a truth value as stated, since "reliable" is doing a lot of work there, tell me which part breaks it and what you'd swap in rather than giving me a number for a broken claim.

Format: exactly five numbered items, one or two sentences each, no intro and no conclusion. Item 1 is your number plus the term it rests on most; items 2 through 4 are the terms with their probabilities and how they combine; item 5 is the one you're least sure of and what evidence or argument would move it.
