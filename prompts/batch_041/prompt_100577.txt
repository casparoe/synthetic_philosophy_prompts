I chair the finance and investment committee of a grant-making foundation with an endowment of about $80 million, and I need a written opinion to circulate before Thursday's meeting. The committee is split, and I would rather put a reasoned recommendation in front of members than referee the argument in the room.

Two items are on the agenda, and our consultant claims they are really one item. The first concerns the general fund. A donor consortium has offered us a guaranteed $1.2 million, which is exactly what we need to keep our flagship programme running for the next three years; the programme director says she cannot plan on anything else. The consortium has also offered, as an alternative, a structure that pays $1.2 million with probability 0.89, $6 million with probability 0.10, and nothing with probability 0.01. The second item concerns a restricted capital fund left to us by a donor. The same consortium has offered two structures against that fund: $1.2 million with probability 0.11 and nothing otherwise, or $6 million with probability 0.10 and nothing otherwise. The contingencies are shared — the 0.89 and the 0.11 are two faces of the same event, whether the consortium's lead funder clears its own board — and the consultant says the two items are therefore the same choice with an irrelevant common branch bolted on.

His note says that the combination most of us are drawn to, the guaranteed sum in the first item and the $6 million chance in the second, cannot be made coherent: no way of valuing money, however cautious, reconciles those two choices with each other, and the pair violates the independence condition on which expected-utility reasoning rests. He adds that a counterparty who knew our pattern of choices could arrange a sequence of transactions that would leave us worse off for certain. One trustee answers that we are a charity and not a casino, that the flagship programme is the foundation's public identity and cannot be exposed to a one-in-a-hundred chance of collapse, and that the second item is restricted money which morally belongs to the donor's purposes and has nothing to do with the first. Our standing policy, adopted two years ago at my predecessor's insistence, says that each opportunity is to be assessed independently, on its own merits, without regard to the source of funds or to whatever else is on the agenda. Whatever we decide goes into the minutes and the annual report, and two peer foundations have told me they follow our published reasoning, so I am unwilling to leave a record that contradicts itself.

Please draft the opinion I will circulate. I want a clear recommendation on each of the two items, a view on whether that policy clause should stand, be amended, or be read narrowly, and the reasoning that gets you there, including the strongest version of the case for the option you reject, put in terms its holders would recognise as fair. If you think the consultant has framed the choice in a way that distorts it, say so plainly. I am not asking for a literature review or references; I want your judgment applied to our numbers. Tell me what would change your mind — which consideration, if it turned out differently, would lead you to recommend otherwise — and where your reasoning is most likely to be mistaken. Write it as continuous prose in the form of a letter to the committee, with no headings, bullet points, bold text or tables.
