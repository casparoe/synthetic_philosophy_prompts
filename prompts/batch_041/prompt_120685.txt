I sit on the content advisory committee of the medical history museum attached to our university teaching hospital, and we are in the last weeks before we sign off on the scripts for a permanent gallery about the Nazi killing of institutional patients: the programme administered from the office at Tiergartenstraße 4, the decentralised killings that went on after the central operation was halted in 1941, and the uncomfortable fact that the gas technology and much of the personnel later reappeared in the extermination camps. Our disability advisory panel has reviewed the draft and has formally asked us to use the word genocide on the wall labels, in place of the current wording, which uses the regime's own euphemism in quotation marks and otherwise speaks of mass murder. Our legal adviser has pushed back: she points out that the 1948 Convention's definition reaches only national, ethnical, racial and religious groups, that the drafting history is full of deliberate exclusions, and that a museum which uses a term of art loosely is borrowing trouble it cannot repay. Two committee members have said the whole quarrel over the label is a distraction from getting the content right, and the chair wants a decision on Thursday.

Please draft the written opinion I will circulate before that meeting. I need a clear recommendation with wording I can actually put in front of the committee — the label text itself, plus a view on whether we should adopt a standing policy for how the museum uses the word across its collections rather than deciding it case by case, and a realistic timetable — followed by the reasoning, not preceded by it. The reasoning is where I need help, and I would like it to be genuinely philosophical rather than a recitation of the legal definition. Specifically: Lemkin's own conception of the crime was considerably broader than what the Convention ended up saying — it took in the destruction of a group's language, religion, culture and economic existence, and a cultural limb was in the early drafts and was deliberately dropped; if the moral concept and the legal concept have come apart, which one should govern a museum's historical vocabulary, and does it matter that the legal definition's edges are themselves partly judge-made rather than textual (the tribunals have had to gloss how much of a group must be targeted before "in part" is satisfied)? Is the list of protected groups doing real conceptual work, or is it a 1948 compromise that we have no reason to treat as a map of what deserves the name — and if the latter, what stops the word from being stretched until it means nothing? I would also like the memo to face an awkward asymmetry rather than step around it: the same programme's forced sterilisations look very much like the act of imposing measures intended to prevent births within the group, so if the group requirement is the only thing standing between this history and the word genocide, we should say that plainly instead of implying that the acts themselves are different in kind. And I want a serious answer to the colleagues who say the dispute over the label is a distraction, including an honest reckoning with the possibility that they are right.

For what it is worth, I lean toward using the word, with a note in the catalogue explaining the gap between the legal and the historical senses, but I do not want a memo that ratifies my preference; if the better case is for the neutral wording, I will take it. Please end with a short appendix, written as fairly as I can ask for, setting out the strongest version of whichever position you end up recommending against, so that the minority can see their own argument stated at full strength rather than in caricature. And tell me which single consideration you would actually stake the decision on, as distinct from the ones that merely sound good in a meeting.
