I teach a first-year "ethics of everyday life" unit and want a decision-tree handout that sorts students by what they actually think about whether the little conventions we live by -- tipping, queueing, jaywalking -- are moral obligations at all, and whether a visitor has to follow the local versions of them. Please build the tree from the logical space rather than from a list of names: first split on whether these conventions carry any moral weight (versus being pure etiquette or prudence), and if they do, split again on what's supposed to carry the weight -- bare coordination (queueing), fairness to whoever relies on it (the server who lives on tips), or the disrespect expressed by opting out -- then a seperate branch for the visitor, on whether an outsider is bound by the local practice as such or only by the part of it that's independently justified. Name the leaf positions where there's a name and mark them 'unnamed' where there isn't. Use these as the diagnostic nodes, each phrased as a question a student could answer without knowing which position it leads to: tipping a server in a place where tipping is rude, queueing when the queue is empty and nobody's scrambling for anything, jaywalking on a dead street at 3am, and standing on the wrong side of an escalator in a city where everyone stands one way. Say which combinations of answers are unoccupied and whether that's because the view is incoherent or just unexplored, and flag any two positions the tree can't really tell apart. Aim for ten to fourteen leaves, and keep it short enough to fit on one side of A4.
