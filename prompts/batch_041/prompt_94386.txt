I'm a novelist at work on a book whose two protagonists are philosophers who have spent their careers circling the same question: what follows, if anything, from the fact that you exist. For a chapter built around a document they compose together, I need the genuine article rather than a paraphrase — the joint statement of an adversarial collaboration between a self-sampling theorist, who holds that you should reason as if your present observer-moment were a random draw from all the observer-moments in your reference class, and a self-indication theorist, who holds that the bare fact of your existence is evidence that there are more observers rather than fewer.

Write that document. Begin with a numbered list of the claims both sides can sign — about what indexical evidence can and cannot establish, about what the doomsday argument actually presupposes, and about the reference class problem. Follow it with a table of the residual disagreements, each written in wording its own side would accept, and then the cruxes, giving for each one the case or argument both sides would agree bears on it, with each side's prediction recorded in advance.

The document must confront two things. First, the presumptuous philosopher, who claims to establish from his armchair, by his existence alone, that the cosmos contains vastly many observers rather than few: each side must say whether he is reasoning well. Second, a case I have invented and want them to adjudicate, which I call the Carbon Copies. A coin is tossed: on heads, exactly one observer is created, with a particular stream of experience; on tails, one hundred observers are created, each an exact duplicate of the first, with numerically identical experiences, so that nothing from the inside tells them apart. You are one of the observers, and you know the setup. State what each side predicts you should conclude about the coin, and say whether the two predictions diverge here for a reason that reaches the deeper dispute or merely because of how each side counts observers.

Keep the prose spare and the disagreements exact. Write in American English.
