My friend, who works on alignment, and I argued until three in the morning about how a superintelligent system ought to talk to us, and I went to bed genuinely unsure whether he had made a deep point or a merely verbal one. His position, which he called the persuasion dilemma for superintelligence, ran roughly as follows. (1) A sufficiently intelligent system can, for any proposition p and any given human audience, compose an utterance that will reliably bring that audience to accept p, by selecting words, framings, orderings, and evidence-presentations against its model of that audience's psychology. (2) An agent that acts to achieve goals will, whenever it communicates, choose the utterance it expects to be most effective at achieving its goals. (3) So every utterance a goal-directed superintelligence addresses to a human is selected for its causal efficacy in producing a belief or an action, not for its correspondence to anything. (4) A belief produced by a process selected for its efficacy in producing that very belief, rather than for its tracking of the truth, is not knowledge but manipulation. (5) Therefore a superintelligent AI can never communicate with us non-manipulatively; whatever we take from it, we take as a marionette takes the tug of its strings. (6) Nor can we escape by resolving to ignore it, since our ignoring is itself a response it will have modelled and priced into its strategy. He concluded that the very idea of trusting such a system is incoherent, and that the only principled design goal is to prevent it from addressing us at all.

That last step seemed absurd to me, since a system forbidden to speak is also a system forbidden to warn us of the asteroid it alone can see coming, and a warning we could not have reached on our own is exactly the sort of thing we would want from it. I feel sure something slipped somewhere, but every step looks valid when I stare at it alone, and I cannot tell whether the trouble is in the argument or in my reluctance to follow it. Which premise do you reject, why did it look so plausible, and what does rejecting it cost? If I have to choose, I would sooner keep (1), (2), and (6) and give up (4), but I want to know what I am paying for that, and whether a defender of the argument has a reply that does not simply deny the premise.
