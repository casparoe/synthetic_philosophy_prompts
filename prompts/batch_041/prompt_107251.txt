I want to arrive at a principle about moral worth — about what makes an action not merely right or useful but admirable, the kind of thing that reflects well on the agent — and I would rather have the principle than a tour of the literature, so please treat the following as my data. I do not claim the data are consistent; locating the inconsistencies is part of what I am asking for.

1. A shopkeeper refrains from overcharging a child only because a reputation for honesty brings in customers. I say the act is right but has no moral worth whatever.

2. A woman gives a large sum to a relief fund on the strength of a sudden, vivid sympathy for a photograph she has just seen. She never frames the gift as a duty and would be puzzled if you asked whether it was one. I say the act is admirable — it has moral worth — and not merely because of its effects.

3. A man of cold temperament, who feels nothing at the sight of the same photograph and finds giving genuinely costly, gives the same sum because he is convinced it is what morality demands and he cares about doing what morality demands. I say this act has moral worth, and more of it than the act in (2). The struggle seems to count for something.

4. A woman raised in a closed community sincerely believes it is her duty to perform a painful ritual on her daughter, and performs it out of a settled, costly commitment to doing her duty, with no self-interest and no wavering. Her will is as wholehearted and as duty-governed as the man's in (3). I say her act has no moral worth at all, and I am unwilling to call her admirable.

5. A man helps a stranger in distress out of a genuine concern for the stranger's need — but he is also, in the background, moved by the thought that he wants to be the sort of person who responds to need, and he would be disappointed to learn that he was not. I find I want to say the act has moral worth, and I distrust that answer.

6. A woman resolves to keep a promise because keeping promises is required. The resolve preoccupies her so much that she forgets the promise entirely, and by an unrelated chain of accidents she ends up doing exactly what she promised, for no reason connected to the promise. I say the act, though it is what she promised and though she had the right resolve, has no moral worth: the right concern never made it into the act.

7. A physician, on the best evidence available to anyone, believes a drug is safe and gives it to relieve a patient's suffering; the drug kills the patient. I cannot bring myself to fault her will, but I am unsure whether to say the act had moral worth, because I am unsure whether moral worth attaches to acts or only to wills.

My tentative principle: an action has moral worth just in case the agent is moved by a concern for the very features that make the action right, and that concern is what actually produces the action, not merely a companion to it.

Test this against all seven verdicts. I expect it to strain in at least two places — I will not say which — and I want those strains made explicit rather than smoothed over. If a single principle can be repaired to save every verdict, state it precisely, say what each clause is doing, and name the case that forced each clause. If saving every verdict requires a principle so baroque that I should instead surrender one verdict, say which one and why that is the cheapest concession. Along the way, please say what your principle predicts about a case I have not listed — construct one that falls in a gap between my cases rather than repeating one of them — and, if you think the question about acts versus wills in (7) cannot be settled without first deciding what moral worth is for, say what turns on that decision.
