I am the teaching assistant for a ninety-minute graduate seminar session in an interdisciplinary MA program on freedom and aggregation. The students come from philosophy, economics, and law; they can handle a formal argument if it is motivated, but they tend to treat Sen's liberal paradox as either a technical curiosity or a political Rorschach test. They have already read a textbook chapter that states the theorem and gives the standard Prude/Lewd example with the banned novel, and they have also read a contemporary piece on algorithmic personalization and filter bubbles. My job is to make the paradox feel like a live problem rather than a parlor trick, and to connect it to the broader theme of the seminar if that can be done without forcing it.

Please plan the explanation for me. I need the order in which the components must be introduced and why—what has to be in place before the theorem can even be stated so that it does not look like a trick, and which piece of formalism is essential versus which can be left as a black box. I need the one example that should carry the whole thing: what it must have and lack, and whether it should be the standard Prude/Lewd case (and if so, how to frame it so it does not become a debate about pornography) or something else. I have been toying with a party-hosting case: two roommates, Alex and Blair, must decide whether Alex hosts a party, Blair hosts, or no party happens. Alex would rather Blair host than host himself, but he would rather host than have no party; Blair would rather no party happen than host, but if there is a party she would rather host than let Alex host. Both prefer Blair hosts to Alex hosts. I think this gives the same structure without the sexual content, but I am not sure it is clean, and I do not know whether it preserves the right kind of preference. I also do not want the session to end with the students thinking the paradox is an artifact of meddlesome preferences. Can you either design an example where the other-regarding preferences are not meddlesome—where each person's preference about the other's choice is tied to their own well-being—or explain how to address the objection if it comes up? I need a map of the misconceptions the students will predictably bring or form—that it is just Arrow's theorem again, that it shows liberalism is incoherent, that it only arises because of nosey preferences, that the liberal condition is about legal rights rather than a decisiveness condition, that the Pareto condition is about efficiency rather than unanimity, that the contradiction is a cycle rather than a direct clash, that it requires more than two people, that it requires irrational preferences—and exactly where in the sequence I should head each one off. I need one question I can ask at the end to see whether they got it, and I need to know what I should openly refuse to explain at this level and say so. Finally, if there is a way to connect the paradox to the algorithmic personalization material without distorting it, tell me where in the sequence that connection belongs and what it should and should not be used to claim.
