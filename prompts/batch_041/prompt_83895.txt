At my high school, the counseling office runs an annual workshop on generational differences in the workplace. The handout it distributes contains a chart that assigns a range of birth years to each generation — the Silent Generation, the Baby Boomers, Generation X, the Millennials, and Generation Z — lists a handful of shared traits for each, and offers advice on how to manage members of each. The school is otherwise careful to discourage generalizing about people by race, gender, or class, and our harassment policy explicitly forbids treating a student as a representative of a group. I am puzzled by the coexistence of the two, and I am puzzled by the chart itself: its birth-year boundaries differ from those of other charts I have seen, the traits assigned to the Millennials seem to have changed over the past decade even though the people in question have not, and several of the traits assigned to Generation Z — ease with technology, anxiety, a preference for flexibility — would have sounded equally plausible applied to teenagers in 1995 or in 1965.

Please treat the handout as evidence about what its authors believe, and reconstruct the theory of generations it presupposes. Does it presuppose that a birth-year band picks out a real kind, a group whose members share something that explains their traits and behavior, or only that the labels are convenient ways of sorting people? Which rival views does it rule out? In particular, does the chart require that a generation be constituted by a shared formative experience rather than by birth years, and could the practice survive on the view that a generation is nothing but a label that each era invents for its young? I would also like to know whether the parts of the practice are consistent with one another: can the same office coherently distribute this chart and enforce a rule against treating people as representatives of their groups, or is age being treated as a special case, and if so, what would justify the exception?

Finally, distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with, and say where it is philosophically underdetermined, so that several theories of generations would license the same handout. Then tell me what would have to change in the workshop if its presupposed view were replaced by the view that generations are only labels, and, conversely, what would have to change in the school's anti-stereotyping rule if the chart's view were taken seriously. The chart also cannot tell whether its descriptions capture differences between generations or merely differences between ages, and I would like you to say what follows for the practice if it cannot distinguish the two.
