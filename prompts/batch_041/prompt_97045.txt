I'm working through Schopenhauer's The World as Will and Idea and I've gotten stuck on the bit near the end of the third book where he says music is special. As I understand him: the other arts give us access to the will only indirectly, by way of the Platonic Ideas, which are the will's objectifications at various grades. Music, though, supposedly skips the Ideas entirely and is a direct copy of the will itself — he even talks as if the world could be called embodied music, and music could go on existing if the world didn't. I get why that's a striking claim, but I can't make it hang together. If the will is one, timeless and blind, how can a temporal sequence of tones, with harmony and melody, be a copy of it? And how can music be a direct copy of the will when he also insists the Ideas are the only adequate objectivity of the will? Then there's the stuff about melody being like the history of the will's striving — is that the same claim or a separate one? Help me untangle what work "copy" is doing here, and whether his view of music actually fits his overall aesthetics or is basically an exception he never integrates.
