I chair the signage and wayfinding standards panel of a metropolitan transport authority, and I have to circulate a written opinion before Thursday's sitting. A foundry has submitted a new typeface for our tram fleet, and one panel member objects to three of its letterforms: a single-storey 'a' whose bowl is closed by a stroke that doubles as the stem, a 'g' with no descender loop, and an 'r' cut down to a stem and a single angled arm. He says these are not letters at all but private marks, and that our design standard, which requires lettering to be 'recognisable at first sight to a traveller who has never seen the face before', therefore rules them out. The foundry answers that nobody identifies a letter in isolation: a shape that does the work of an 'a' in every word it appears in simply is an 'a', and the standard should be read that way. Our difficulty is that the same standard requires the approved face to serve the authority's embossed signage as well, where blind and partially sighted travellers meet individual raised letters one at a time, with no surrounding word to help them. Quoting the standard back at us settles nothing, since its wording is precisely what is in dispute.

Please write the opinion. It must recommend a decision — approve, reject, or approve subject to conditions — with the conditions and a date attached to it, and it must state what the panel should treat as the test for whether a shape counts as a given letter, defending that test rather than assuming it. Give my colleague's objection its strongest form before answering it, set out the strongest consideration against your own recommendation and explain why it does not carry the day, and say what should happen to the wording of the standard itself, since the same dispute will recur at the next submission. Do not cite type-design literature or appeal to the authority of designers; the panel needs reasons it can argue with.

Format: exactly five numbered points, each one or two sentences, with no preamble, no heading and no closing paragraph. The first point is the recommendation itself, with its conditions and its date; the remaining four carry the reasoning and the follow-up.
