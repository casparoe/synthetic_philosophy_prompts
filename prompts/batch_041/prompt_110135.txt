My old PhD supervisor has strong views about what counts as a unit of selection, and I'm trying to work out what she'd say about a case from a paper I'm reviewing. Her verdicts so far, as best I can reconstuct them: a resistance gene riding on a plasmid that hops between unrelated bacteria — a unit of selection, obviously. A transposon copying itself around a genome at its host's expense — yes, a unit. An ordinary rabbit — yes. A honeybee colony founded by a swarm — yes, she'll happily call the colony a unit. A clone of aspen spreading out from a single root system — yes. A lichen — yes. A herd of zebras that happend to be grazing together — no, that's just a crowd. The species Zebra, taken as a whole — no. The mixed bacteria in a bucket of pond water, taken as a group — no.

The case I'm stuck on is a clover plant and the rhizobia in the little pink nodules on its roots. The plant builds the nodules and pumps sugar into them, the bacteria pull nitrogen out of the air and hand it over, and neither does well on its own — but the plant picks its bacteria up fresh from the soil every generation; they don't come down through the seed.

Would she call that composite a unit of selection, or just two lineages temporarily in bed together? What I'm really after is the rule she seems to be running, at least one other rule that fits every verdict above just as well, what each of those predicts about the clover, which one you'd bet on and why, and a case that would actually tell them apart — somewhere the two rules come apart and we could just ask her.
