I retired from a physiology department about six years ago (vision, mostly — I spent a career on the retina and early cortex) and since then I've been reading my way into philosophy of perception, badly and with a lot of enthusiasm. I've written a short piece that I think might be worth something, but I've lost all perspective on it, so I'd like you to tell me honestly where it's wrong.

The gist. Everyone in this literature now talks about "cognitive penetration" — the idea that what you believe or know or expect can reach down and change what you actually see, not just what you're willing to say you see. My claim is that nearly all the striking demonstrations collapse into one of two much duller things. First, attention. If you cue me towards one of two things, my attention goes there, and attention genuinely changes appearance — that part I don't doubt, there's good psychophysics on it. But attention isn't cognition, so calling that penetration is just a category error dressed up as an empirical result. Second, response criterion. Knowing what the object is changes what I'm prepared to report, not what I see, and my old field solved this problem decades ago: signal detection theory seperates sensitivity from criterion, and if you only measure the criterion shift you've shown nothing about perception. Almost none of these papers report sensitivity measures at all. They ask people to adjust a patch until it matches, or to rate something, and then treat the rating as a window onto experience.

The interesting residue, I think, is the memory-colour sort of case — if I remember right, people report bananas as yellower than the patches that actually match them — plus learned categories, birders and colours and so on. But I want to argue that these are better described as perceptual learning, a slow reshaping of the sensory machinery over years, than as a belief reaching in and turning a dial in the moment. So I propose splitting the topic in two: synchronic penetration, where a thought alters a percept on the spot, which I suspect is empty, and diachronic penetration, where training rewires the visual system, which I suspect is real but is not really what anyone means by the word.

Three things I want from you. Is the signal-detection point as decisive as I think, or does it prove too much — couldn't you use it to dismiss attention effects as well, and if so have I sawed off the branch I'm sitting on? Is the diachronic/synchronic split a real distinction or just a relabelling that lets me keep the interesting cases while denying the label? And am I fooling myself that this is a contribution — I have a nagging feeling someone made exactly this objection twenty years ago and I've simply never found it. Be blunt about the accuracy, I can take it.

Please write it as continuous prose — no headings, no numbered points, no bullet lists, no bold, just an email. And in British English, if you don't mind; I've lived here long enough that the other spelling looks like a typo to me now.
