A couple of colleagues of mine have turned a lunchtime betting game into a standing argument, and they've asked me to referee. The game: you start with $100. In each round you may stake any fraction of your current bankroll on a coin that comes up heads with probability 0.6; if it's heads you win the amount staked, if it's tails you lose it. You may play as many rounds as you like, stopping whenever you want, and you keep whatever you finish with.

Anna argues: "You should stake exactly 20% of your bankroll every round. That's the growth-optimal fraction — it maximizes the long-run growth rate of your wealth, and every other fraction either grows more slowly or is outright reckless. A player staking 40% will almost surely end up behind a player staking 20%, if they play long enough."

Ben replies: "Growth rate isn't the thing anyone is obliged to maximize; expected utility is. If you're risk-neutral you should stake everything, every round. If you're risk-averse, the right fraction depends on your utility function, and there's no reason it has to be 20%. 'Maximize the growth rate' is just maximizing the expected logarithm of wealth — that is, assuming logarithmic utility. It's a preference, not a theorem."

Anna: "It isn't a preference. Growth rate is objective. Over many rounds the 20% player's wealth almost surely overtakes the 40% player's, for any sufficiently long horizon. That's a fact about the game, not about tastes."

Ben: "That 'almost surely' hides an assumption that you care about the very long run, or the limit. Over any fixed finite number of rounds the 40% player has strictly higher expected wealth, and there's a genuine chance he ends up ahead. Worse: suppose I tell you that you must reach $500 and that anything less is worth nothing to you. Then the 'growth-optimal' 20% can be strictly worse than a wilder bet. So growth-optimal betting can't be universally rational."

Anna: "The goal case is a different problem, and the fact that it has a different answer shows nothing about the ordinary case, where you just want to end up as well off as possible and have no fixed target."

They've asked me to settle it and I've given up. Please adjudicate move by move: say who is right about which specific point, and identify what each of them is missing. Then give a JSON object with the fields `overall_verdict` (a few sentences on who is more right overall and why) and `what_both_miss` (a few sentences on the crux neither of them has put their finger on).
