I have been trying to work out whether a certain reconstruction of Kant's position on lying is really as secure as it looks, and I would value a careful map of its exposure. The reconstruction I have in mind moves the ground of the prohibition away from the person addressed: the wrong of a lie, on this reading, is not that it violates any right of the particular hearer to be told the truth — not his interest, not any injury done to him — but that it violates a duty of veracity owed to rational nature as such, and in the first instance to the humanity in one's own person. On this view the murderer at the door is not a counterexample at all, because the argument from his supposed right to the truth was never the load-bearing argument; if the duty is owed to reason rather than to him, his murderous intention is simply beside the point, and nothing he does can release me from it. I like this because it seems to keep the prohibition unconditional while showing why the familiar reply, that he has forfeited any claim on the truth, is irrelevant rather than decisive.

What I want is an honest account of what would count against it, ordered from the genuinely decisive to the merely awkward. Which consequences are unacceptable if they turn out to follow — for example, if grounding the duty in the humanity of my own person makes the content of what I say, and the identity of the person I address, wholly irrelevant to the wrong, is that an elegant feature of the view or a reductio of it? Which cases should embarrass it, if any — the lie told to a tyrant, the lie that injures no one and benefits no one, the misleading truth uttered in order to deceive, the lie told to someone who cannot understand it? And does the position, as I have stated it, quietly smuggle exceptions back in, so that it collapses into the moderate view that lying is permitted precisely when the hearer has no claim on the truth; if so, is there a formulation that avoids the collapse, or is the collapse evidence that the absolutism was only ever a narrowing of what counts as a lie?

I am less interested in the standard objections to Kant than in what someone who holds this exact position ought to concede, so please tell me which of the defeaters the view's defenders would themselves grant and which they would dismiss as question-begging, and whether any result from elsewhere — about the nature of assertion, about the conditions under which a claim or a right is forfeited, about the distinction between lying and other forms of deception — bears on it. Say also what the position predicts that its rivals do not, and, in the end, whether it risks anything at all or has been insulated by its qualifications; if it has been insulated, tell me plainly whether that is a defect. What would move me most is a case or a consequence that the view cannot absorb without ceasing to be the view.
