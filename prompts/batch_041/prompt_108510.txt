Six months ago I moved from systems neuroscience into a group that builds detectors for anomalous behavior in neural networks — what the field calls mechanistic anomaly detection: detectors meant to fire when a model's output is produced by a mechanism other than the one you would expect, a backdoor or a shortcut rather than the computation you thought you were studying. In neuroscience, when two circuits were said to implement the same computation, there was at least a shared vocabulary for arguing about it — cell types, connectivity, response profiles. Here the same dispute returns every week and never resolves, and it has become my problem: a paper of mine came back with a referee's single sentence saying that I had shown two circuits behave alike but not that they are the same mechanism, and I could not say what further evidence would have settled it.

The dispute takes these forms. Two models, one small and one large, agree on every input we have tried, yet the components you must damage to break the behavior differ between them. One model solves the task one way on short inputs and another way on long ones, with identical outputs. Two training runs from different initializations end in behaviorally equivalent solutions whose internal structure differs, and a third places the "same" circuit elsewhere entirely. A detector fires on one of a pair and not the other; half the group reads that as the detector working and half as the detector failing, and that disagreement turns out to be the same disagreement about identity.

My hypothesis, which I would like tested rather than accepted, is that this is three quarrels braided into one: a verbal one about what "mechanism" ought to mean; a substantive one about which identity criterion actually predicts what we care about — whether a detector generalizes, whether an intervention transfers, whether an explanation travels to a new model; and a case of people answering different questions under one heading, one about explanation, one about prediction, one about control. I suspect the third does most of the work, and that the impasse is no sign the question is ill-posed.

Diagnose it. Say which parts are verbal, which are substantive and in principle empirical, and which are people talking past one another; identify the hidden factual or formal question if there is one; and tell me what would count as progress — a distinction, an experiment, a criterion, or a change of question — or argue that none should be expected. Ground the diagnosis in the cases above rather than in generalities. Write it as continuous prose, with no headings, bullet points, or tables, as you would a long letter.
