Two cases, both about who gets to speak for someone who has changed, and I have not been able to find a name for what the pair of them shows.

Case one. A man in his early sixties, fully competent, signs a directive declining hospitalization, ventilation, and artificial feeding if he should ever reach a point where he can no longer live independently or manage his own affairs. Ten years on he is in that state: demented, in a care home, still recognizes his daughter, still enjoys visitors. He develops pneumonia. The directive is produced and followed, and when the daughter protests that her father is sitting right there and wants to be treated, the reply is that the man she is pointing at is not the man who signed, and the man who signed is the one whose autonomy is being honored.

Case two. A man in the same position, whose directive is older, and who is still legally competent, though severely depressed after his wife's death. He asks for the document to be set aside and for everything to be done. He is refused: the depression is said to be doing the asking, and the earlier document governs. Here the self who is present and speaking is the one ruled out as not really him.

In the first case the earlier self wins; in the second the later self wins; and in both, the decision goes to whichever self the people around the bed are willing to call the real one. The question of which moment in a life has authority looks like it is doing the work, and I do not believe it is. What presents itself as a temporal rule seems to be a substantive judgment about which version of the person we endorse, wearing the costume of a temporal rule, and nobody involved has to notice that a substitution has been made.

I know the standard apparatus here — Ulysses contracts, precommitment, the long dispute over whether the later self is the same person as the earlier one and so can be bound or wronged. Those seem to be about defeating momentary weakness, or about personal identity, and neither is quite what I am pointing at. The adaptive-preference worry comes closer, but it reaches only case one; the depressed man is disqualified on other grounds.

Is there an established name for this, and if there is, I want the boundary rather than the label: what it covers that ordinary self-binding does not, where the line falls between a self that is not competent to speak for the person and a self that is competent but not treated as authoritative, and whether the same structure turns up outside medicine — in the twenty-two-year-old whose commitment the thirty-five-year-old wants out of, and in the onlookers who insist that only one of them was the real her. If nothing in circulation fits, coin something, but define it against the nearest neighbours.
