I spent last night arguing with a friend about when it's rational to stop looking and take something good enough, and I came away with clear verdicts on the cases below but no principle that fits them. I'd like you to find one.

My verdicts. Grabbing the first jar of pasta sauce that looks acceptable, without comparing the rest of the shelf: fine. Signing the first apartment that meets all my criteria, without waiting to see what else comes on the market: fine. Taking the first job offer that clears my bar, in a field where your first job largely determines which jobs you can later get: not fine; I owe myself more looking. A doctor settling on the first diagnosis that fits the symptoms and stopping the workup: fine when the condition is benign and the treatment is cheap, not fine when a rare fatal condition is still live and one more test would rule it out. Marrying the first person who meets my standards rather than seeing who else is out there: fine. Picking a research problem on the grounds that it looks workable, without surveying the whole field: fine. Picking a statistical method the same way, when an afternoon of reading would have turned up a better-established one: not fine.

Two things I can't reconcile. The apartment and the job look like the same choice — stop at the first acceptable option, without knowing what else is out there — and I want to treat them differently. And stopping at the pasta sauce seems fine even though checking one more shelf is nearly free and would probably get me a better jar, which appears to contradict the obvious principle that you should keep looking as long as the expected improvement exceeds the cost of looking.

I also have a hunch I want tested: stop when the expected improvement from further search is small compared to the cost of searching, with both measured against what actually matters to me. And a related case: a friend who always takes the first option above a fixed threshold, in every domain, never computing whether more looking would pay off. I think adopting that blanket rule is fine, even though in most particular cases it does worse than case-by-case optimization would.

Please answer in American English, in these sections and order: "Bottom line" (about 100 words, stating the principle you'd defend and which of my verdicts it sacrifices); "The verdicts, restated" (about 100 words); "Candidate principles" (about 350 words, stating two or three precisely, with the case that motivates each clause); "Predictions on cases I haven't listed" (about 300 words, at least three new cases); "What I have to give up" (about 150 words, saying which of my verdicts the best principle cannot keep, and whether I should drop the verdict or complicate the principle); and "Loose ends" (about 100 words).
