I built the following argument out of the standard fink and mask cases, and it ends somewhere I cannot follow. By a fink I mean a device that removes an object's disposition at the very moment the stimulus arrives — a glass that is fragile, is struck, and does not break because the fink has already dismantled its structure. By a mask I mean an external condition that blocks the manifestation while leaving the disposition intact — the same glass, packed in foam, still fragile, still not breaking. By a mimic I mean the reverse of a fink: a contraption that produces the manifestation without the disposition, so that the glass shatters when struck although it was never fragile. The argument:

(1) If a disposition ascription entailed the corresponding conditional — the object would manifest if stimulated — then nothing could have the disposition and fail to manifest under the stimulus.
(2) Finks and masks are precisely that: disposition present, stimulus present, no manifestation.
(3) So a disposition ascription entails no such conditional.
(4) If the conditional entailed the disposition, then nothing could satisfy the conditional and lack the disposition.
(5) Mimics are precisely that: no disposition, stimulus present, manifestation occurs.
(6) So the conditional entails no disposition either.
(7) Nothing in (1)–(6) turns on anything peculiar to dispositions; the same construction applies to any subjunctive conditional, since for any claim of the form 'were A, B would be' one can describe an interfering condition under which A holds and B does not.
(8) So no subjunctive conditional is true.
(9) But 'were I to release this glass, it would fall' is true, and I stake my fingers on such claims every day.

I want to keep (2) and (5) — those cases look real to me — and I will not give up (9). Tell me which step to reject, why it looked compelling in the first place, and what rejecting it costs. If the repair is that the conditional should have been stated with more packed into its antecedent, say exactly what has to go in, whether the result is circular or trivial, and what becomes of the reductive ambition that made the conditional analysis attractive.
