I teach an upper-level metaphysics seminar and I'm putting together the final. I want one essay question on Gödel's modal ontological argument — the argument as it's usually laid out, with the positivity axioms, the Godlike definition, the essence definition, necessary existence, the works. I'll paste below the version I use in lecture so the question is self-contained.

The trouble is that every year the good students can produce the derivation from memory and the weak ones write "S5 plus the possibility premise, QED" and I can't tell them apart. So I want the question to turn on something they can't have seen before. Here's a little parody I cooked up and I want the question built around it:

Call a property "shabby" if it is the negation of a positive property. Now run the argument with "shabby" in place of "positive" throughout. Two of the axioms transfer cleanly: if a shabby property necessarily implies another property then that property is shabby too, and for every property exactly one of it and its negation is shabby. Take the axiom that says the Godlike property is positive and replace it with: the property of being a "wretch" — the being that has all shabby properties — is shabby. And take the axiom that necessary existence is positive and replace it with: necessary *non*-existence is shabby. Now the derivation goes through verbatim and we get, in every possible world, a being that has every shabby property and exists necessarily — a necessarily existing wretch. So Gödel's argument proves too much.

I want the question to make them (a) say whether the transfer of each axiom really is clean, and if not, exactly which one breaks and why, and (b) decide whether the wretch argument is a genuine parity of reasoning with Gödel's or a disanalogy, and defend the verdict. Don't let them end on "both sides have a point." They should be able to do the whole thing from the argument as stated, no extra reading.

Please give me the essay question, a model answer of around 700 words, and a marking scheme: five or six must-hit points with the marks for each (out of 20), plus a line on how students typically botch each one.
