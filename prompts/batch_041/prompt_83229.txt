I'm a PhD student in philosophy working on decision theory and the epistemology of coordination under uncertainty, and I want your credence in a fairly specific claim — the kind sometimes floated under the heading of evidential cooperation in large worlds. I care much more about the decomposition than about the number itself, and I am deliberately abstracting away from how the idea is usually formalized, so please reason about the version I stipulate rather than about any particular author's.

Here is the setup. Suppose the universe is spatially very large and contains many agents causally isolated from us. Because these agents confront problems structurally identical to ours, and because they have been selected — by whatever process — for solving such problems well, their decision procedures and ours are correlated even though no causal signal passes between us; the dependence is evidential or logical rather than causal. Call the thesis that this dependence licenses paying local costs to benefit those distant agents evidential cooperation. The intuition is that if my adopting a cooperative policy is evidence that a distant agent adopts a cooperative policy, and their policy benefits me, then I may have reason to cooperate even at a local cost, and the more such agents there are, the larger the stake.

The proposition I want a number on, between 0 and 1: for an agent like us in a universe of that kind, evidential cooperation is rationally required, in the sense that a policy of cooperating on these grounds maximizes expected value under the correct theory of decision.

Please decompose the credence into at least the following, with one line of justification for each term and then the combination: (1) whether the correlation is a genuine evidential dependence rather than an artifact of self-locating or indexical confusion; (2) whether evidential or functional decision theory is correct rather than causal decision theory, noting that if the correlation has a common cause it is a further question whether causal reasoning endorses cooperation; (3) whether the sign of the correlation is cooperative rather than competitive, that is, whether structurally similar rational agents converge on mutual benefit or on mutual defection; (4) whether the expected gains, summed over a large universe, actually exceed the local costs; (5) whether our radical uncertainty about distant agents' procedures and payoffs should discount the whole argument; and (6) anything else you think belongs. Then tell me which term your final number is most sensitive to, and what argument or piece of evidence would move it most.
