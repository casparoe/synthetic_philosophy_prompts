I run a small robotics group — six people, one tabletop manipulator, a fixed compute budget, and no appetite for training a foundation model from scratch — and we have talked ourselves into organizing the next year of work around what Turing says about learning machines: make the child, not the adult, and then educate it. The trouble is that the instruction to educate it is not yet a decision procedure, and the decisions arrive weekly. At our Monday review we have to settle how much of a task to demonstrate before letting the machine attempt it, when to accept a behavior that works but that none of us can explain, when to add the next skill rather than consolidate the present one, when a piece of unasked-for cleverness is exactly the thing we were after and when it is the machine quietly solving a different problem from the one we set, and when to admit that a stage has failed. Please act as a philosopher of mind who knows the history of machine learning as well as the current practice of training learning systems, and write us the procedure we can actually run at that meeting: the questions in the order we should ask them, what counts as a sufficient answer at each step, where the procedure terminates in a no, and — just as important — where it terminates by handing the matter to a person because the remaining question is one of values rather than of fact. Two things I want to survive the translation, because they are what make this more than ordinary engineering. First, that the teacher's inability to specify the solution in advance is a feature of the method and not a defect of it, so a stage in which we have simply written the behavior ourselves is not evidence of progress; I take this to be the point of the child-machine suggestion in "Computing Machinery and Intelligence" (Mind, 1950), and it is also the thing the objection Turing reports from Lady Lovelace puts its finger on. Second, that education has to do two jobs that pull against each other — getting the pupil to do what we want, and getting it to do things we never thought to ask for — and that the two failure modes are different: a pupil that only ever does what it is told has learned nothing worth having, while a pupil given free rein will optimize for something we did not intend. We are calling these discipline and initiative internally; treat the labels as ours, and feel free to argue that the distinction, or the way we have drawn it, does not hold up.

Think it through carefully before you commit to anything, and show me the joints in the reasoning rather than a tidy document. Begin with a short paragraph, in your own words, setting out what you take the relevant Turing material to commit us to and what it leaves open, marking clearly any point where you are unsure rather than asserting it. Then give the procedure itself: a numbered sequence of questions, the evidence required to answer each, the stop rules, and the places where a human — in practice my safety officer, who has to sign off before the machine goes anywhere near people — must decide rather than check. The machine is headed for a six-month pilot in a care home, so anything it does unasked has consequences, and the colleague who teaches it has about four hours a week to spend in the loop; the procedure has to be runnable inside those limits and by people who are engineers rather than philosophers. Then run it on three cases: (a) the machine has learned to load the dishwasher tray by a route none of us can describe, and it works except that it occasionally cracks a glass; (b) the machine has begun doing something we never asked for that looks useful — it has started sorting the cutlery drawer while idle — and my safety officer wants it switched off; and (c) the case I am least looking forward to, in which the procedure tells me that our last three stages were not education at all, because we engineered the behavior ourselves and then described it as something the machine had learned. At least one of these should come out in a way I will not like, and I would rather you said so plainly than softened it. Finish by telling me what the procedure cannot do — the questions it will not settle, the cases where it will misfire, and the judgments it leaves with us. We are a UK-based group, so please write the whole answer in British English, and do not bother with citations, reading lists or references; I have the texts, and what I need from you is the procedure.
