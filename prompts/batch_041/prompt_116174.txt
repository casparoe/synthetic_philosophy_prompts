I'm drafting the criteria section of a paper on the prospects for a naturalistic account of mental content, and I've realised that my co-author and I have spent a month arguing past each other because we each hold candidate theories to a different set of demands. Before we go further I want the specification written down on its own terms: what any theory of representation for cognitive systems has to deliver, set out so that it could serve as a checklist for someone who is not a philosopher — a comparative psychologist deciding whether to describe a bird's memory for a cache site as representing a location, or an engineer asking whether the internal map in her robot genuinely represents the room. Fix the purpose there, at a theory good enough to license content attributions in ordinary scientific and engineering practice, rather than one good enough only for a seminar.

The requirements should each be motivated by a case, and I would rather they be cases a reader can assess on the spot than ones that presuppose the literature. So work in at least these: a frog that snaps at anything small, dark and moving, edible or not, and whose visual machinery is the same whether a fly or a pellet is in view; a bacterium that reaches the oxygen-poor sediment by swimming along the earth's magnetic field lines with a chain of magnetite crystals inside it, a trick that gets it to the right depth only because of a contingent feature of the planet; a gland that secretes a compound in exact proportion to blood glucose but that nothing downstream ever reads, so that only an endocrinologist with a syringe notices the correlation; a bird that learns to find its caches by a coloured plastic flag that no member of its species has encountered before; a neural culture grown in a dish and coupled to a robot arm, selected over many generations in simulation for seeking a light source, so that it has a selection history but no evolution and no designer who knew what he was doing; and a state that appears to be at once a description of how things stand and an instruction to act, so that one cannot decide whether it says that a predator is above or says to climb. For each requirement, give the case that forces it, one line on why it forces it, and a verdict on whether it is a genuine constraint on any theory or merely a working assumption of one camp — and if the latter, say which camp. I also want the distinctions the list must preserve kept separate and explicit: content against mere indication or symptom, content against reference, representing against being disposed to respond, original against derived intentionality, error against malfunction, and determinate against indeterminate content.

Then the part I actually care about: say which requirements cannot all be met at once. I suspect at least one such set exists, and I want the argument rather than a shrug — the candidates worth testing are the trio of naturalistic determination, determinate content, and the possibility of error; the pair of compositional content and content fixed by a state's function in the whole system; and the pair of content being fixed by history and content being needed to explain the behaviour of a creature that has no history. Rank the tensions by what they cost, say what a theory must surrender for each, and tell me which single requirement, if enforced, eliminates the most otherwise respectable contenders. Finish with a short paragraph scoring the main families — covariance or information-based accounts, teleological accounts, functional-role accounts, and interpreter- or practice-based accounts — against the list, two or three sentences each; I will do the longer treatment myself.
