For an independent study in my high school, I have been reading about Grice’s theory of meaning and conversation, and I find myself convinced by a strong version of it. The view I hold is that speaker meaning can be fully analyzed in terms of reflexive intentions: a speaker means something by an utterance when they intend to produce a certain effect in an audience by means of the audience’s recognition of that very intention. I also hold that conversational implicature is fully explained by the cooperative principle and the conversational maxims: what a speaker implicates is what they must be taken to intend in light of the assumption that they are observing the maxims, or deliberately flouting them. I am not interested in a general survey of pragmatics. I want to know what would count against this package. What cases, arguments, or theoretical results should move me? I am especially interested in whether the account can handle cases that seem to involve meaning without intention (a slip of the tongue, a symptom, a natural sign) and implicature without intention (a speaker who inadvertently conveys something). What about conventional implicature, which seems to attach to particular words rather than to conversational maxims? What about the distinction between what is said and what is implicated—does the Gricean program require a stable, intention-independent notion of what is said, and if so, is that notion defensible? And what should I make of the charge that the analysis is circular, since the intention is to produce an effect by means of the recognition of that intention? I would like these defeaters ranked from fatal to merely awkward. Which would a committed Gricean concede as counting against the view, and which would they dismiss as a misunderstanding? Has the view been qualified into unfalsifiability, or does it still risk something? Finally, which of these objections do you think I should find most persuasive, and which should I be able to answer without giving up the core claim?
