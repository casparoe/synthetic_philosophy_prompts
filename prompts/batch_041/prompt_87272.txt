I am organizing a reading-group session for an interdisciplinary seminar on games and ethics, and I keep running into the same obstacle: everyone in the room argues about particular cases — whether it is wrong to torture an NPC for amusement, whether a mod that lets you humiliate a character is protected expression, whether the developers of a companion AI that pleads with you not to shut it down have done something wrong, whether a player who grieves a fictional character's death is being irrational — without ever agreeing on what an answer to the question "do video-game characters matter morally?" would even have to accomplish. Before we debate another case, I want the specification written down: the desiderata that any adequate theory of the moral status, or the moral insignificance, of video-game characters must meet. Assume we want a theory good enough to guide real disputes about games, not one that merely catalogs intuitions.

Please give me the requirements list, and for each requirement the concrete case or consideration that motivates it — the NPC torture scenario, the character modeled on a real and identifiable person, the companion AI whose scripted distress is indistinguishable from the genuine article, the player who reports real grief, the designer who inserts degrading content for its own sake, and the fact that the same act of "killing" is trivial inside a game and monstrous outside one — together with a statement of why that is a genuine requirement rather than a prejudice of one camp. Tell me which distinctions the theory cannot collapse (the player's feelings versus the character's interests; what may be depicted versus what may be done; a character's fictional nature versus a real person's likeness) and which results from elsewhere in philosophy or the human sciences — that fictional entities have no welfare, that real conduct can nonetheless be wrong — the theory must not contradict. Flag any subset of the requirements you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied, since that, more than any scorecard, is what I intend to bring to the group. Finally, tell me which single requirement does the most to exclude the accounts people actually defend — whether that is the view that it is all just fiction, the view that locates the wrong entirely in the player's character, or the view that takes the appearance of suffering at face value — and you may skip scoring the contenders one by one; I will do that in the session.
