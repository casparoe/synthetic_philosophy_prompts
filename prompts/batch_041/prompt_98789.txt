My friend and I spent most of a weekend arguing about the discursive dilemma — she is a database engineer who has lately become fascinated by social choice theory — and we reached the point where neither of us could restate the other's position without distorting it, so I would like a written adjudication rather than a continuation of the shouting. Let me set out the disagreement as charitably as I can. You know the standard shape of the puzzle: an agenda of logically connected propositions, say 'the defendant committed the act', 'the defendant acted with intent', and 'the defendant is liable', where liability holds just in case the first two do; each voter's individual judgment set is internally consistent, yet propositionwise majority voting can yield a collectively inconsistent set. And the familiar escape routes — deciding only the premises and deriving the conclusion, deciding only the conclusion and ignoring the premises, or restricting the agenda so that majorities cannot collide — each appear to purchase consistency at the price of some basic fairness condition such as anonymity, neutrality, or responsiveness.

My friend's contention is that this entire framing mistakes a concurrency problem for a fairness problem. She treats the group's judgment set as a database that must satisfy integrity constraints, and each voter as a transaction that individually leaves the database consistent; the dilemma, on her view, is simply what happens when those transactions interleave badly. Her proposed remedy is what she calls a commit scheduler: fix an order over the propositions in advance and settle them one at a time, where each proposition is decided by majority vote but only among the options that remain consistent with the propositions already settled. She insists that this is what real committees actually do when they agree on the facts before voting on liability, that the order can itself be fixed by a prior and independent decision, and that the impossibility results bite only if one stubbornly insists on treating every proposition symmetrically. I think she is smuggling in a dictatorship of whoever gets to fix the order, and that any vote on the order will itself be a judgment about the agenda and so collapse back into the same trap.

Please write an essay adjudicating this. Assess whether the commit-scheduler proposal actually dissolves the discursive dilemma or merely relocates it — in particular, whether the ordering can be chosen without reintroducing the very problem it is meant to solve, whether settling some propositions first amounts to a hidden priority granted to certain reasons or certain voters, and whether any version of the sequencing idea survives scrutiny once those worries are pressed. Do not end on a non-committal note: reach a verdict about who has the better of the argument, and justify it.
