I'm a novelist — three books, none of them philosophy — but I did a PhD in metaphysics years ago before I defected, and my old supervisor has finally guilt-tripped me into giving a paper at the department's Thursday work-in-progress seminar next month. The paper grew out of something I got obsessed with while writing the last book: I kept needing to say what a character is really like underneath what they actually do, and every way of saying it fell apart in the same three places. So the thesis I'm defending is this — a disposition isn't individuated by its causal basis, and it isn't individuated by its manifestation either, but by its whole manifestation profile: the full pattern of what it yields across interfering conditions, so that how a thing behaves when it's masked, or when the trigger gets finked away at the last moment, is part of what the disposition is rather than noise around it. A brittle glass, on this picture, is a different disposition from a glass that's just as brittle but permanently screened off from ever shattering; and a coward who is never given the chance to be a coward has, strictly speaking, a different disposition from a coward in a war. I think that's right and that it does real work, but I have a horrible feeling its the kind of view that sounds profound for about ten minutes and then eats itself.

The room is going to be: four or five hard-nosed analytic metaphysicians who grew up on the conditional analysis and treat finks and masks as clever counterexamples to be quarantined rather than as clues; two people who work on mechanistic explanation in the philosophy of biology and care a great deal about what 'causal basis' is even supposed to mean; one dispositional essentialist who thinks properties just are powers and will hear 'manifestation profile' as a way of smuggling Humean regularities back in through the window; and a visiting psychologist who does causal modelling and will want to know what any of this has to do with invariance under intervention. None of them will have read anything I've written, and half of them think the whole dispositions cottage industry is a waste of a good afternoon, so I need to be ready for the eye-roll as much as for the argument. Can you put together a Q&A prep sheet for me? What I want is the objections I'll actually get from this room — ordered by how likely each one is and how much damage it would do if it landed, phrased the way each of them would phrase it and not the way a sympathetic commentator would — with a two-minute reply for each, a note on where my reply is weakest and what I should simply concede up front rather than try to defend, and a clear sense of which single objection has to fail for the paper to survive the hour. Please include the one I'm hoping nobody asks, because I'm fairly sure I know what it is and I've been avoiding thinking about it. Don't bother with a reading list or citations — I know the literature well enough, and I'd rather not have a fake bibliography — I just need the questions and what to say back. British spelling throughout, by the way; this is going to a UK department.
