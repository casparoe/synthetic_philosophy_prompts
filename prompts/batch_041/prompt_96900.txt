I teach a second-year undergraduate metaphysics course and next term I have a 75-minute slot on material constitution that I keep botching. Last year I did the statue and the lump on the whiteboard, took a show of hands on whether they're one thing or two, then talked at them about the options for forty minutes. Fine, they wrote it down, they'll remember none of it. A colleague reckons the trouble is that a show of hands is too cheap -- everyone already knows which way they'd vote before they walk in, so nothing is at stake and nothing gets discovered. What I want instead is for them to have to make the call over and over, under rules they didn't choose, and to notice that they keep wanting to change the rule at exactly one point in the sequence and not at the others.

Here's what I've sketched, and where I get stuck. Eighteen students, so six teams of three, each with a lump of plasticine and two ledger sheets they have to keep in parallel: a Works ledger for works of art, and a Materials ledger for the stuff those works are made of. I deal commission cards one at a time -- form the lump into a figure; squash it flat; recast it as something completely different; stick on a new limb; cut a limb off and put it in a drawer; take that drawer limb out again and build a second work with it while the first one is still sitting there; grind the whole thing to powder; press the powder back into a lump; divide the lump and make two works from it. After every card each team writes down how many works exist, how many material things exist, and which of the entries already on their lists have gone out of existence. Then I score them for bookkeeping consistency: one birth and at most one death per entry; nothing comes from nothing; and no two entries may be made of the same bit of plasticine or sit in the same place at the same time. I also want two rival persistence rules in play so they collide -- one where a thing survives as long as it keeps enough of its original parts and no rival thing gets built out of those same parts, and one where it survives as long as the shape or the function is preserved. The bit I can't solve is the ending. I want them to have to honour two claims at once: an insurer who only pays out if a work of art was destroyed, and a supplier who invoices for material that was returned and reused. If the work went out of existence but the material didn't, both claims are good, and I want them to feel how strange it is that both can be paid without ever counting a single thing on the table twice. I'd also like them to come away seeing that the two ledgers can't both stay consistent with their own rules -- something has to give, and that what gives is usually decided silently.

Could you design this properly and write it up for me to run, please? I'm not after a handout for the students, I want the facilitator's version. Use these sections, in this order: Setup (room, materials, teams, how the 75 minutes are carved up, roughly 120 words); The run of play (the cards in order, the ledger and scoring rules, what the teams physically do, and a note on which card is meant to produce which bit of confusion, roughly 500 words); The squeeze at the end (how to stage the two-claims finish so it lands as a genuine bind rather than a puzzle with a right answer, roughly 150 words); Debrief (mapping what happened back onto the philosophy -- moment by moment, not a lecture outline, roughly 300 words); What will go wrong the first time (roughly 200 words); and What the activity can't reach, i.e. which part of the puzzle I'll have to supply from the front of the room because the game can't produce it (roughly 120 words). Keep it runnable on a normal seminar budget, no money and no props I'd have to order in.
