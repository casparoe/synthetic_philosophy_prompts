After a career in experimental physics, I have spent my retirement reading philosophy of action, and I have run into a tension I cannot resolve unaided. Hyperbolic discounting is standardly offered as the behavioral-economic model of weakness of will: the agent prefers the larger-later reward, then reverses that preference as the smaller-sooner reward comes within reach. But on the classical understanding, weakness of will requires that the agent act against their own all-things-considered judgment at the very moment of acting, not merely that the judgment has changed between one moment and the next. If that is right, the discounting model captures inconstancy of preference rather than incontinence, and the two come apart precisely where the philosophically interesting cases lie.

Please explain how these two notions are meant to relate. I would like to understand (a) whether diachronic preference reversal — the agent's ranking shifting over time — is the same phenomenon as acting against one's present judgment, or a distinct one that merely tends to accompany it; (b) whether the familiar distinction between naive and sophisticated discounters, those who fail to anticipate their own future reversals versus those who anticipate them and precommit against them, corresponds to the traditional distinction between the incontinent and the continent person, or instead cuts across it; and (c) what the discounting framework can say about the case in which the agent's judgment itself is bent by the proximity of the reward, which I take some philosophers to classify as a defect of judgment rather than of will. I am after the conceptual geography, with the cases that force each distinction, rather than a catalogue of positions.
