I recently joined the board of a small land trust, and we are about to decide whether to rewild a sixty-acre former hay meadow or keep it in low-intensity agriculture. Sitting in those meetings, I realized that no one on the board has a defensible view about whether either option leaves the animals on that land better or worse off, and I want to turn that unease into a six-month research proposal for a grant from an animal-welfare funder.

My half-formed idea is this. The wild-animal-welfare debate has mostly asked how much suffering nature contains, but the live decision for a land manager is comparative: does destroying or restoring habitat reduce or increase it? The usual assumption that habitat destruction reduces wild animal suffering may rest on a mistake, because it tends to count the animals that are removed and ignore three things. First, what happens to displaced animals, which often die slowly of starvation, exposure, or predation rather than instantly. Second, the shift in species composition and body size: disturbed land often supports dense populations of short-lived small animals with very high juvenile mortality, while intact habitat may support fewer, longer-lived animals. Third, the difference between an acute pulse of deaths and a chronic background of suffering. I suspect the field is running together two things that should be kept apart: the instantaneous amount of suffering present on a hectare, which is a stock, and the rate at which suffering is produced there over time, which is a flux. I want to rebuild the comparison around a counterfactual, asking about the welfare of the animals that would occupy the land under each management regime rather than only the ones there now.

Please turn this into a proposal of about two pages, with the following sections, in this order and roughly these lengths:

1. Question — one paragraph stating the question so that it has a determinate answer, and naming the two or three claims the project will test.
2. Why it is open — about half a page on why the existing positions do not settle it, including what the standard argument that destruction reduces suffering assumes and where that assumption could fail.
3. Approach — about three-quarters of a page, month by month, describing the conceptual work I would actually do: how I would define the stock and flux measures, how I would handle the counterfactual and the replacement problem, and why a philosophical rather than empirical project can make progress here.
4. What a result looks like — a short paragraph on what a finished answer would look like and which way the argument could come out.
5. What would count as failure — a few sentences on what would show that the project had failed or was too big for six months.
6. Why fund it — a short paragraph aimed at a program officer, on what a land trust or conservation group could do differently with the answer.

Do not include citations or a reading list; I will add those myself. Write it in American English.
