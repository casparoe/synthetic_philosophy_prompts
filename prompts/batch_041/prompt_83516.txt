At a reading group I attend, two members argued for most of a session about whether the simulation argument should dampen fanaticism about the far future — where fanaticism means the willingness to choose a minute chance of an enormous payoff over a large and secure but finite one. Both accept that most of the value at stake lies in the far future; they disagree about what the possibility that we are simulated does to the case for pursuing it.

Ana argued that it dampens the case. If there is a substantial chance that our world is a simulation, the futures we take ourselves to be shaping are not what they appear: the simulators may shut the simulation down, or run it on a whim, or have their own timeline in which our far future is a footnote. Discounting the far-future payoff by that chance lowers its expected value, and once the numbers come down, chasing the long shot is less compelling than securing ordinary near-term goods.

Ben replied that the discount is a red herring. Fanaticism is a claim about the ranking of options, and a factor that applies to every far-future option alike cannot change a ranking. If the far-future option wins by a wide margin in the unsimulated case, multiplying both sides by the same probability leaves it winning by the same margin; dampening requires an uneven discount, and the simulation hypothesis applied bluntly supplies none.

Ana answered that the discount is uneven. Some interventions are aimed precisely at what happens in the simulators' world — at whether the simulation continues, or at what they do with us — and those are discounted little or not at all, whereas interventions that assume we occupy the base level of reality are discounted heavily. That reorders the options rather than merely rescaling them.

Ben countered that this only relocates the fanaticism: a bet on influencing the simulators has probabilities even less tractable than the original, and if the simulators' world is itself simulated, such bets are stacked on an infinite regress. Ana replied that an infinite stack of simulators does not shrink the payoff; it multiplies it.

Please adjudicate move by move. Say who is right about what, identify what each of them is missing, and say who has the better of the exchange overall.
