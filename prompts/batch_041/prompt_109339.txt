I'm a PhD student in philosophy and next week it's my turn to run the session on James and Clifford for our ethics-of-belief reading group — 14 people, mostly grad students, 90 minutes, a room with movable chairs and a whiteboard, and no budget for anything. I really don't want to do the thing where I summarize the two positions for twenty minutes and then we all agree that Clifford is too demanding and James is a bit slippery, because everybody already knows how that conversation goes. What I want is a structured activity where they have to put their epistemic money where their mouth is, so that the disagreement shows up as a felt tension rather than a diagram on the board. My rough idea, which you should feel free to throw out entirely if you've got something better: everyone gets a private evidence dossier about some hypothesis that matters, and we run two rounds. Round one they just say, privately, whether they believe the hypothesis. Round two they have to take a costly action that only makes sense if it's true — commit the patient to the surgery, sign the retraction, wire the money, book the boat — and then defend it to someone who has less evidence than they do. Then in the debrief I reveal that half the dossiers were rigged so that the evidence was in principle obtainable and the inquiry could have been deferred (Cliffordian conditions, and I keep thinking about his shipowner here), while the other half were rigged so the evidence is genuinely unavailable to them and the moment for action won't wait (Jamesian conditions). The thing I'm most excited about, and the reason I'd like the rules built so that it actually surfaces rather than just being something I announce at the end: in that second half of the room they should notice that they couldn't just believe the hypothesis by deciding to, even when the option was live and forced and momentous. All they can do is adopt it and act on it, which is a different mental act. So the whole will-to-believe framing comes under pressure in a way I find more interesting than the standard objection that you can't believe at will — it suggests James might be better read as defending a policy of acceptance than a power of belief. I'd like the activity to make that belief-vs-acceptance distinction (the one where acceptance is voluntary and belief isn't) feel like it's doing real work, not just get name-dropped by me in the last five minutes.
