At a dinner last week, two friends of mine — a nurse who works on a surgical ward and a trainee solicitor — fell into a long argument about a case the nurse had been told about at work, and I have been turning it over ever since without being able to settle it. The case is this. A woman with a chronic but not dangerous condition agrees to a procedure that will very probably relieve her symptoms. The surgeon explains the procedure and its benefit, discloses the rare serious risks (including a small risk of death), invites questions, and asks her to sign; she says she understands and wants to go ahead. What he does not mention is that roughly a third of patients come away with a permanent and conspicuous change — not harmful, but the sort of thing she would notice every day — because he believes telling her would put her off a procedure he is confident is in her interest. She has it, learns afterwards what he left out, and says she never consented to that.

The nurse says she is right: consent is agreement to an act under a description, and the description she agreed to simply omitted a feature of the act that mattered to her, so her yes was a yes to something else. The solicitor says that proves too much — no one ever agrees under a complete description, and there is always some true fact that, had it been disclosed, would have changed someone's mind, so if "she would have refused had she known" invalidates consent then hardly any consent survives; the test has to be objective and bounded, and the surgeon did disclose the risks that matter. The nurse replies that the objective test is precisely what is in dispute, so invoking it begs the question, and that her counterfactual was never "any withheld fact" but a fact about the very act being agreed to, known to the surgeon and known by him to be something she would treat as decision-relevant — strategic withholding, not mere omission. The solicitor concedes that the strategic point has force, but says she is now smuggling in a requirement about the consenter's mental state that consent does not carry: consent is something one does — a communication — and its validity turns on whether it was competent, informed and voluntary, not on whether the decision that followed matched what the agent would have preferred. He adds a case of his own: had the surgeon disclosed the change but buried it in a forty-page form she never read, we would surely still say she consented, which shows that the adequacy of the disclosure and the validity of the consent come apart. The nurse answers that the buried-in-the-form case shows the opposite — that the content of the act can be defective even when the act is performed — and that his "consent is a communication" view cannot explain why a yes extracted at knifepoint is no consent at all, since the communication there was perfectly in order.

I stayed out of it, and I honestly do not know who has the better of it. Could you adjudicate the exchange as it actually went, move by move rather than by declaring a winner — for each move, say whether it is clearly mistaken, defensible, or clearly right, and why — and then say what each of them is missing that neither has noticed? I would also like you to test a framework the nurse floated late in the evening, which the solicitor did not engage with: that consent has two separate ledgers, one for its validity (competence, information, voluntariness) and one for whether the consenter was wronged, and that these can come apart, so that there can be valid consent to a wronging, and a wronging without any defect in the consent. Say whether that distinction does any real work in this case or merely relabels the dispute, and whether it survives the cases each side raised. Please keep the verdict grounded in the arguments as made rather than in what you take the law or the literature to say.
