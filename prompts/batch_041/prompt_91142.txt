I teach one ninety-minute session in an upper-level undergraduate seminar on consequentialism and the aggregation of harms. There are fourteen students, a whiteboard, and a room that gets uncomfortably warm by the end of the hour. They have the standard utilitarian machinery down cold and have started treating it as a settled instrument, which is precisely the complacency I want to disturb. What I want is a runnable activity that makes them feel the pull of the puzzle usually posed as a choice between one person being tortured for fifty years and an astronomical number of people each receiving a single dust speck in the eye — and, more importantly, that makes them feel what each escape from the puzzle costs. The shape I have in mind, though you should improve it wherever it is weak, is a public commitment followed by probes. The group must settle on one number T: the number of people who would have to receive the speck before they would prefer that to the torture, with the understanding that below T they choose the torture and at or above T they choose the specks. One number, written on the board, signed by everyone who voted for it, monotone — no answering that it depends, no declaring the question ill-formed, no splitting the difference between two thresholds. Only after the number is committed to and displayed do the probes begin, and I want the probes to be the part they remember at the end of term. If T is finite, they must defend it when T is astronomically large; if they refuse to give a finite T, they must say whether they would turn down a trade in which an astronomical number of people are spared a speck of dust in exchange for shaving a single day off the torture. Then a revision round in which T may be changed, on the condition that whoever moves it must say aloud which intuition the group is abandoning in order to move it.

Beyond that skeleton, give me the whole apparatus: rules, materials (I have index cards, a whiteboard, and a stack of cheap tokens; no money, no projector), a role structure if roles genuinely help, a minute-by-minute timeline for the ninety minutes, and a decision scheme with no cash payoff. I would like at least one phase in which the group's own T is applied to something real rather than hypothetical — the obvious candidate being how the last stretch of the session is spent, with everyone doing a short dull task versus one volunteer doing a long one, the numbers scaled to their T and the group obliged to carry out whichever option its threshold selects; keep whatever you choose harmless, reversible, and something nobody can be conscripted into. I also want a debrief that maps specific moments of the activity onto the parts of the problem: what happened when they fixed T, what happened at each probe, what the revision cost them, and where the aggregating answer and the non-aggregating answer each get their grip and where each starts to look monstrous. Assume no lecture beforehand — the structure should do the work, and they should not hear the standard name of the puzzle or the standard labels for the two families of answers until the debrief. Do not build any of it around named authors, readings, or positions; I want them to have constructed the positions before anyone tells them the positions have names. Finally, tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle each failure: I am expecting at least one student to insist that a dust speck is not a harm at all, so the trade is free; another to point out that a speck in the eye could cause a fatal accident; a deadlock if the threshold has to be unanimous; and a filibuster about whether a finite threshold is even expressible. End with an honest note on what this activity cannot reproduce and what I will therefore have to supply myself. Say how confident you are that the design will produce the experience I described rather than a brisk forty minutes of arguing about whether dust specks count as harms, and single out the one design decision you are least sure of and what you would change if it fails in the room.
