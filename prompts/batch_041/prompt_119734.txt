I hold that the separateness-of-persons objection is decisive against aggregative utilitarianism: because each person's good is the good of a distinct subject, sums across persons answer to no single standpoint from which a loss to one could be outweighed by a gain to another, so the interpersonal trade-offs classical utilitarianism licenses are not justified by the values supposed to justify them.

Tell me what would count against that. I don't want a survey of the literature's objections; I want the exposure of the position mapped, with evidence and arguments sorted by how decisive they would be. Which of the following would its defenders themselves have to concede counts against them, and which could they dismiss as question-begging or beside the point: (1) that the objection proves too much, since the self at fifty is no less separate from the self at twenty than one person is from another, so it indicts prudence as well; (2) that a reductionist view of personal identity, on which persons are not separately existing entities but overlapping streams of experience, dissolves the separateness the objection needs, so the objection stands or falls with a contested metaphysics of the subject; (3) that it proves too little, leaving prioritarianism, rights-based constraints, and ex ante or expected-value aggregation untouched, since those also weigh goods across persons; and (4) a case in which two persons irreversibly fuse into one — may we then aggregate their pre-fusion welfare with the post-fusion person's, and is the objection's answer a prediction or an embarrassment?

Rank these from fatal to merely awkward, say explicitly what a committed objector should concede, and give me a definite verdict on how much of the objection survives — including whether it is an argument against aggregation or a restatement of the anti-aggregation intuition it is meant to support, and whether, if the latter, that is a problem for a view of this kind.
