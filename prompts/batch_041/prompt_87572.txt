I am a retired physical chemist. My working life was spent on statistical mechanics and, latterly, on the statistical analysis of noisy measurements, so I am at home with ensembles and phase-space distributions, microstates, coarse-graining, latent variables, and the distinction between a system's state and our information about it. I came to philosophy late and am now reading my way into the foundations of quantum mechanics. I keep encountering the question of whether the wavefunction is real, and the labels psi-ontic and psi-epistemic, and I find myself translating both sides into the Gibbs-versus-Boltzmann idiom I know: a distribution over microstates, on the one hand, and the microstate of an individual system, on the other. I am fairly sure the translation is too quick, but I cannot locate the error.

I would like two things.

First, a systematic translation. Take the psi-ontic position and the psi-epistemic position in turn, and restate each as faithfully as you can in my vocabulary — ensembles and distributions over underlying states, microstates, latent variables, coarse-graining, the Gibbs and Boltzmann readings of a statistical-mechanical state, preparation and measurement as operations on a distribution. For each position separately, tell me what carries over without loss, what I must distort in order to make it fit, and what in the position has no expression at all in my vocabulary.

Second, the residue report, which is the part I actually want. Please treat the leftover as evidence rather than as noise, and address three things specifically.

(i) When the psi-epistemic view is stated as the claim that the wavefunction is a probability distribution over some underlying states of the system, does that statement carry commitments my statistical-mechanical analogy does not supply — for instance, restrictions on which distributions are admissible, or on how the distributions of two systems must be related when the systems are prepared independently and then composed? I want to know whether the analogy is doing real work here or only sounding familiar.

(ii) I am told that the no-go theorem of Pusey, Barrett and Rudolph rules out a broad class of such readings: roughly, that if distinct pure quantum states correspond to probability distributions over a common underlying state space, then those distributions must not overlap, on pain of abandoning the independence of separately prepared systems. Classical statistical mechanics, as I understand it, faces no such constraint — distributions over phase space overlap freely. Is this a difference of substance or merely of formalism; and if it is of substance, which classical assumption is the one quantum mechanics denies?

(iii) Is the ontic/epistemic distinction as philosophers of physics draw it the same cut as my microstate/distribution distinction, or a different cut that I am conflating with mine? If the latter, tell me what the real cut is.

Finally, adjudicate my instinct: is the debate over the reality of the wavefunction simply Gibbs versus Boltzmann in new notation, is it that plus something more, or is the resemblance superficial and misleading? If my background is misleading me, say which piece of it is responsible and what it is leading me to miss.
