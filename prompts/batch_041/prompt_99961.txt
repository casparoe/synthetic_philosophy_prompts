So I've been arguing with a guy in my cohort for like six months now about AI and whether leaning on it wrecks your understanding of stuff, and we've basically gotten to the point where we're just repeating ourselves at each other. I want to write up the doc we should've written ages ago — an adversarial-collaboration statement we could both actually put our names on. Can you draft it?

Quick setup. I'm the pessimist: I think when you outsource the actual reasoning to a model you lose something that matters — not just the skill, but a kind of understanding that's part of what makes a belief genuinely *yours* rather than something you're parroting. He's the optimist: this is just the latest in a long line of cognitive scaffolding (writing, calculators, indexes, other people), and hand-wringing about "real understanding" is nostalgia dressed up as epistemology. Call them the erosion camp and the scaffolding camp if that helps.

The document should have: (1) a numbered list of claims we can both sign, about what understanding is, what's actually at stake, and what neither of us is claiming; (2) the real disagreements, each phrased so whoever holds it would accept the wording of their own side; (3) the cruxes — the sub-questions where, if it got settled, one of us would actually have to move; and (4) for each crux, a concrete test or case we'd both agree bears on it, with each side's prediction written down in advance.

Two things I'd like you to build in, because I think they're where the action actually is and we keep skating past them. First, a distinction I've been pushing: procedural grasp (you can produce the right moves) vs structural grasp (you grasp why the moves hang together and what would change if a premise changed). I claim AI-dependence mainly costs you the second; he claims the second was always thin and mostly reconstructed on demand anyway. Second, a candidate test we might both accept: take some AI-assisted derivation or solution that someone supposedly "understands," perturb a premise or a parameter, and see whether they can say what breaks and why without the model. Does that even count as evidence about understanding? What would each of us predict, and would a positive result actually move either of us, or would we both just explain it away?

Also, be honest about whether part of our fight is just verbal — e.g., if we mean different things by "understanding," or if he's talking about the person while I'm really talking about the person-plus-tool system. Say which part is terminological and which is a seperate real disagreement underneath. And don't let the agreed claims be mush; they should rule some things out. I definately don't want a list of truisms everyone already nods at.

Format: numbered agreed claims, then a disagreements table (disagreement / neutral statement of each side / crux / test / each side's prediction). Write it as the joint doc itself, not a report about it. And don't cite papers or give me a reading list — just reason it out.
