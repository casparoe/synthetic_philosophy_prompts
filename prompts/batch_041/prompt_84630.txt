You're a philosopher of language who also actually translates poetry, so you know both the theory and the bleeding. Here's a case I keep getting stuck on, and I want a verdict, not a shrug.

Imagine a twelve-line poem addressed to a dying friend. Every line has seven syllables; the rhymes run abab; the last word of each line, read straight down the page, spells the friend's name; and the whole thing also works backwards — read the lines bottom to top, it turns into a goodbye. Take away any one of those and you have a different poem. Now translate it into English, where you cannot have all four at once. Hofstadter, in Le Ton beau de Marot (1997) — the whole book is basically dozens of attempts at one little Renaissance French poem by Clément Marot — would say the job is to make the English *do* what the original does, not to say what the original says. But that tells me which failures are forgivable; it dosen't tell me what to do when they conflict. And Robert Alter, reviewing the book, pushed back that Hofstadter's poem was a charming trifle whose charm was inseparable from its form, and that he never got at the fact that literature is a way of imagining the world through words rather than a juggling of verbal patterns.

So: when the constraints and the sense can't both survive, which gets sacrificed, and is there a principled rule here or just taste in a lab coat? Does "preserving the form" even pick out a well-defined thing, or is it whatever the translator happens to care about? And what does "the same poem" mean across two languages at all?

Before you commit, spend a little time listing the different ways a form can carry meaning — sound, shape on the page, repeatability, what it makes the reader do — then argue it through and land somewhere. Open with your verdict in a sentence or two, no throat-clearing, and defend it, including a call on my invented poem. Don't finish on "it depends." Under 700 words.
