You are a philosopher of practical rationality with a strong background in decision theory. I want a careful conceptual explanation, not a historical survey, of the so-called Buridan's ass problem — the alleged paralysis of an agent facing two options it regards as exactly equally good. Start by brainstorming the main ways the problem can be stated, then say which of them, if any, poses a genuine puzzle for a theory of rational choice. In particular, address the distinction between having no reason to prefer one option and having no reason to act at all; whether an agent can choose without a preference, or whether the act of choosing itself reveals or creates one; whether deliberate randomization (flipping a coin) is ever instrumentally justified when the options are symmetric, as opposed to merely permissible as a tie-breaker; and what decision theory should say about an agent that stalls indefinitely. I am especially interested in whether the paradox dissolves once we separate questions about the existence of a reason, the strength of a preference, and the rationality of an act, and in what the puzzle reveals about the ideal of a complete preference ordering — whether a real agent can ever literally be indifferent between two options it knows everything relevant about. Aim for an intuitive explanation a smart non-specialist could follow, but do not flatten the technical distinctions. Answer in American English.
