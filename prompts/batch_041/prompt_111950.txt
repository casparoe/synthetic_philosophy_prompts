I want to take that seriously for one session but in a disciplined way. The problem is obvious: he's writing in a world of cabinet wars, dynastic marriages and hired troops, and basically nothing on the modern menu of things states do to each other existed in 1795. So this is a prediction job rather than a history-of-philosophy job: I want your best guess at what Kant would have thought, and then, kept separate, how much of that guess is really his text talking versus us.

So for each of the following, give me a verdict — approve, disapprove, and a rough 0–1 score if that helps you be precise — plus the part of the essay you're leaning on. He's got material on standing armies, on war debts and the credit system, on not interfering by force in another state's constitution, on the difference between a voluntary federation of free states and one single world state, on hospitality to strangers, and on what you're not allowed to do to an enemy even while you're fighting them, which I think is the most interesting one for the modern cases. The list: (a) nuclear deterrence and mutually assured destruction; (b) targeted killing of individuals by drone, including a state's own citizens abroad; (c) covert election interference and disinformation aimed at another country's voters; (d) economic sanctions and asset freezes used as a substitute for shooting; (e) long-term arms sales and military aid to a state that's currently fighting a war; (f) the EU as a partial realization of his federation; (g) climate-driven migration, and what hospitality even means when an entire island nation goes under. And if you have room, the question that keeps derailing us: his whole peace rests on republics, commerce and a voluntary league, and he's dead set against a single world state. Does that structure survive contact with nukes and cyber, or does the edifice need rebuilding from the ground up?
