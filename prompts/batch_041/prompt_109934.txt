For most of my working life I was an agricultural entomologist — thirty-odd years of scouting, threshold counts, resistance monitoring, and the unglamorous business of deciding when a field had to be sprayed and with what — and since I retired I have been reading moral philosophy with far more enthusiasm than discipline. I have discovered, to my annoyance, that my verdicts about killing animals in the field do not hang together, and I suspect the incoherence is not merely the usual gap between theory and practice. I would like you to find the principle, or the small family of principles, that best reconstructs what I actually think, to state it precisely, and then to tell me honestly which of my verdicts it forces me to abandon. I am a consequentialist by temperament, I have no religious convictions to lean on, and I do not believe that an insect's suffering amounts to much — I mention that because it seems to make my verdicts harder rather than easier to explain.

Here is what I think, going by my gut. Aphids above the economic threshold on a crop, a systemic insecticide applied on schedule, and a good deal of collateral mortality among ground beetles and spiders: fine, and I would do it again. The same field sprayed for a blemish that costs the grower nothing worth counting and endangers no one's health: not fine, though the killing is no greater than in the first case — it feels cheap rather than wicked, and I notice I cannot say why that should matter. Fogging an entire town for mosquitoes in the middle of a dengue outbreak, knowing perfectly well that it kills bees and butterflies over a wide area: fine, and I would argue for it at a public meeting. Fogging that same town because the mosquitoes are ruining the evening barbecues and nobody is sick: not fine. Poison bait in a granary: fine. Broadcast anticoagulant bait along field margins in winter, where the owls and foxes that take the dying rodents will bleed out themselves: not fine — I would call it a scandal, even though the target is a vertebrate and I claim to care about vertebrates more than about insects. A bumblebee nest in the compost heap: leave it alone; killing it would be a wrong, not a nuisance. A wasp nest under the eaves of the shed beside the same compost heap: I would knock it down without a second thought, and the two verdicts sit side by side in me without any friction at all, which I take to be diagnostic of something. The sterile insect technique — releasing irradiated males so that the population dwindles by infertility rather than poison — strikes me as the morally cleanest method ever devised, even though it kills slowly, indirectly, and wholesale; I would take it over a spray every time. A parasitoid that specializes on the target pest: fine. A generalist predator released against the same pest, which will also take native insects I never intended to touch: not fine, even when it works. Honeybees I keep in hives, and I would be furious with anyone who sprayed them; a feral colony in a hollow tree on my land I would leave in peace; a feral colony in the wall of my house I would have removed, and I would pay extra to have them removed alive rather than killed — though not an unlimited amount extra.

My own crude formulation runs something like this: killing is warranted when it protects health, food, or a structure, and unwarranted when it protects only comfort or appearance; and whatever the aim, one should use the least harmful method that will do the job. I can already see it strain in three places. The fogging verdict satisfies the first clause but plainly fails the second. The rodenticide verdict satisfies the first clause too, and I reject it anyway, which suggests I need something about who bears the cost rather than only how much harm is prevented. And the bumblebee and the wasp are as alike as two animals can be, so the difference between my verdicts can only be the service each renders me — which makes my insect ethics purely instrumental, and I am not certain I believe that, since the bumblebee verdict feels to me like a moral verdict and not merely a preference about gardens. Please tell me what principle, or set of principles, gets closest to my pattern, what it says about cases I have not listed — a gene drive that would drive a malaria mosquito to extinction, eradicating rats on an island to save nesting seabirds, drift from a neighbour's spray onto my ground, a protected rodent that eats a farmer's crop, spraying for a cosmetic blemish on a farm whose hedgerows are the only pollinator refuge for miles, and weeds, which I destroy by the acre without a flicker and would not know how to fit into any of this — and, if the only principle that saves every verdict I have given has to be elaborate, whether the elaborate version is worth having or is just a description of how I was raised.
