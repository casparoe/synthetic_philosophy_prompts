I'm a novelist, about 60,000 words into a book whose protagonist runs a card room in the desert, and I've been trying to get inside her head about money, so on Thursdays I play low-stakes hold'em with a retired pro named Dennis who has the patience of a saint right up until he doesn't. Last week I told him I'd finally figured out why you bet a fraction of your roll instead of all of it on a good hand, and he said, no you haven't, and then he worked out an argument for me on the back of a receipt that I still can't get out of my head. I've typed it up below. I'm pretty sure the conclusion is wrong — he thinks he's proved that the whole "think in ratios" business is incoherent — but every step looks to me like arithmetic, and that's what's keeping me up at night.

Here it is. (1) Over a life of bets, what happens to your money is that it gets multiplied by a sequence of factors, so what you end up with is the product of them. (2) The product is governed by the geometric mean of the factors, not their average: run the sequence long enough and, with probability one, your wealth tracks the geometric mean, while the arithmetic mean describes a crowd of copies of you rather than you. (3) So the rule for a bet is to take it if and only if it raises the geometric mean of your wealth — which is the same as raising the expected log of your wealth. (4) And this isn't a taste for safety, it's not "I happen to be a cautious guy." It's arithmetic. The expected-value maximizer and the log maximizer can sit at the same table, and with probability one the value maximizer ends up behind, so the log maximizer isn't risk-averse, he's right, and the other guy is wrong the way you're wrong when you add a column of numbers up wrong. (5) Now the twist. Take a bet with a one-in-three chance of tripling your roll and a two-in-three chance of halving it: positive expected value, negative expected log, so the ratio-thinker passes. Fine, I'd pass too. (6) But take a bet that pays you a million dollars with probability 99.9% and takes everything you own with probability 0.1% — and suppose everything you own is a used car and a laptop. Positive expected value, obviously, but also some chance of ending up at zero, and the log of zero is minus infinity, so the expected log is minus infinity, so the ratio-thinker passes on that one too. (7) And that goes for any bet with any chance of ruin at all, however small, so the rule forbids all of them: it forbids staking your used car and your laptop for a 99.9% shot at a million dollars, which is free money by any standard I recognize. (8) A rule that tells you to turn that down isn't a rule of rationality, it's a superstition. (9) So the ratio rule proves too much, and either it isn't the fundamental rule or rationality forbids bets that are obviously fine. (He calls the whole thing betting Kelly, for what it's worth, and says the name doesn't matter.)

So — which step do I throw out, and what does throwing it out cost me? I'd definately like to keep (1) and (2), they look like arithmetic to me, and I'd like to keep the bit where Dennis isn't just a scaredy-cat for only betting a fifth of his roll. My gut says I'm going to have to give up something I actually like, so tell me what it is and price it for me, because I have a character who needs a worldview and I can't have her holding one that eats itself.
