I'm a second-year PhD student in philosophy, working mainly on the metaphysics of time and the philosophy of mathematics, and I've been roped into contributing a short satirical piece to a departmental anthology whose loose theme is philosophers doing customer service. My contribution is going to be a mock customer-support exchange — an email or live-chat thread — set at a company that sells a novelty desk lamp billed as performing a supertask. The product copy claims the lamp toggles on and off infinitely many times in the first two minutes after you press the button, in the style of Thomson's lamp, and the whole thing is pitched to gadget buyers as a 'Zeno-series smart lamp' with a 'completed-infinity' mode. I want roughly 650 words, formatted as a back-and-forth transcript with a header line or two of order details at the top, written completely deadpan: no narrator, no winking, and no one in the thread aware that they are inside a philosophy joke.

The buyer should be a slightly pedantic but genuinely reasonable customer who wants to return the lamp because, two minutes after pressing the button, it is neither on nor off and he cannot get the app to display a state; the support agent should be relentlessly courteous and keep reaching for the resolutions that a competent but philosophically impatient person actually reaches for — that the lamp must be either on or off because a lamp cannot be neither, that its final state is 'just the limit' of the sequence of its states, that this is a known edge case which has been escalated to Tier 2, that other customers have not reported the problem. The customer, without turning into a lecturer, should press the objections that a philosopher of mathematics would recognise as the real ones: that the sequence of on/off states has no limit, so 'the limit' names nothing here; that the setup fixes the lamp's state at every moment before the two-minute mark and says nothing about the mark itself; that insisting it must be on or off simply assumes the very thing in dispute, namely that the description determines a final state; and that the absence of a contradiction in the story is not the same as the story's having an answer. I'd like the exchange to end with a resolution that resolves nothing — a refund offered on a technicality, say, or a replacement unit — so that a reader who knows the puzzle can see the indeterminacy is real and a reader who doesn't can still follow the comedy of a warranty department trying to adjudicate a supertask.

The thing I'm really trying to satirise is the habit, common in pop-science writing and in engineering-minded treatments of the puzzle, of assuming the lamp has a determinate answer that some clever piece of mathematics will supply; keep the exaggeration anchored to that rather than inventing fresh absurdities, so that someone who has thought hard about supertasks finds it fair rather than embarrassing. Please don't have either party cite literature or name philosophers — it should read as an ordinary company thread — and keep the maths at the level of 'the sequence doesn't converge' rather than introducing any actual analysis.
