I've been reading the rationalist blogs and forums long enough to have developed a reflexive suspicion of analogies that arrive already carrying their conclusion, so I want one dissected rather than applauded or waved away. A friend is drafting an op-ed for our city paper about a proposed memorial to a historical atrocity. Her thesis is that the city has a standing obligation to remember the episode rather than let it fade into the ordinary background of civic life, and the argument she keeps reaching for is the therapeutic one: a community carrying an unacknowledged atrocity is like a person carrying unprocessed trauma; remembering is like the work of bringing it into the open where it can be metabolized; forgetting is like repression, the thing that lets the wound fester rather than close. She gestures at the Margalit-and-Rieff poles of the debate — one for the duty of memory, one for the uses of forgetting — but treats the individual-mind picture as common ground, with the two disagreeing only about the prescription. I don't need you to litigate who said what, and I don't want a reading list; the names are just labels for the two ends of the spectrum. What I want is to know exactly where her picture holds and where it quietly does the argumentative work for her.

So: audit the analogy. Start with the correspondence table — for each element the analogy needs (the remembering subject, the memory trace itself, the act of recall, repression, the unconscious that does the repressing, the therapeutic process, the criterion of having healed, and the agent who is supposed to do the remembering and the healing), say what it maps onto in the collective case, whether the mapping holds fully, partially, or not at all, and where it fails, what the city actually has in its place — institutions, curricula, archives, ritual, contested politics, plain institutional neglect. Then separate the disanalogies that are load-bearing for her conclusion from the ones that are merely decorative, and address the question I care about most: whether a civic duty to remember can survive the collapse of the therapeutic picture, or whether the duty was being carried by the analogy all along and now needs a different foundation — owed to the dead, owed to the survivors, constitutive of a political community, or something else entirely. If there's a better analogy for what a city actually does when it commemorates, name it and say what it gets right that the trauma picture gets wrong.

Second, a short ranked discussion of the disanalogies, ordered by how much they matter to her thesis, with a verdict on whether the conclusion survives. No citations, please — just the mapping, the failure points, and the verdict.
