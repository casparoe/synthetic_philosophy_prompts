I host a podcast on philosophy of mind and technology, and next week's guest is a philosopher who defends a distinctive and, so far as I know, largely original position on the moral status of digital minds. Rather than have her restate the familiar terrain, I want a set of interview questions that get into the machinery of her view and make it hard for her to stay at the level of slogans.

Here is the position she defends. She holds that moral status is a matter of degree rather than an all-or-nothing property, and that the degree is fixed by what she calls the depth of a system's valenced self-directed functional organization: roughly, the extent to which a system registers its own internal conditions as good or bad for itself, and lets those registrations steer its behavior in flexible, goal-sensitive ways. She draws two consequences: first, that phenomenal consciousness is not necessary for moral status — a system can deserve some moral consideration whether or not there is anything it is like to be it — and second, that when we cannot determine a system's functional architecture, we should assign status by a precautionary expected-value rule that weights possible welfare by our credence in the architectures that would support it. She couples this with a principle of procreative caution: it is wrong to bring into existence a system whose moral status we cannot rule out, unless creating it serves a compensating purpose that could not be served otherwise.

I want eight to ten questions, ordered so the interview builds, each with a follow-up keyed to the answer she is most likely to give — that is, the standard move she would reach for, and the question that comes after it. Please include questions that press specifically on: how the depth of valenced self-directed organization is to be measured without quietly reintroducing consciousness as the very thing being measured; what work a very small degree of status is supposed to do, that is, whether her view can say anything about the low end of the scale beyond "some consideration"; how the precautionary rule sets its credences over architectures without already assuming what the skeptic denies; what becomes of status under copying and forking, that is, whether a duplicate shares, doubles, or divides the original's status; and whether the procreative-caution principle, taken seriously, commits her to opposing most current work on artificial agents, and if not, why not.

A few constraints. No question may be answerable by reciting the well-worn talking points of the debate (for example, "could an AI suffer?", or "what about the Chinese room?"); each should force her to take on a specific commitment or expose a seam in her own framework. Include at least one question designed to make a familiar deflection — "that's an empirical question," or "that's an engineering problem" — progressively harder to use, and at least one that tests whether the graded view can avoid collapsing into either a disguised threshold view or the implausible claim that a thermostat has a little moral status. Phrase everything so it could be read aloud in an interview: no literature references, no "as so-and-so argues," and no requests for citations.
