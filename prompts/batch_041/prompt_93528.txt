I am a novelist at work on a book set largely on a trading floor, and I have reached the part of the research where I need to stop reading around the subject and commit to a mechanism. My characters have to trade — a novel in which nobody trades has no plot — but I want the trading to be honest, which means I need to know which failures of the no-trade argument are doing real work in actual markets and which are decorative. I know the Milgrom–Stokey result well enough to state its premises in my own words: a common prior, common knowledge of the structure by which information is acquired, expected-utility maximisation, an initial allocation that is already Pareto efficient, and no noise traders or other non-rational interference. The conclusion, roughly, is that even with private information no one can profitably trade, because any offer reveals the offerer's information and gets priced in before it is accepted.

I have drawn up seven candidate leaks in that argument, and I want them ranked by how much of the observed volume in real markets each one explains. Please treat "explains observed volume" as the sole dimension of ranking, and do not hedge by saying that in reality all of them operate at once — I know they do; I am asking which carries the most weight.

(a) Divergent priors that are not merely different posteriors. Traders hold genuinely different generative models of the world, not different updates from a shared one, and neither model is derivable from the other by conditioning on evidence both accept.

(b) Opacity of the information-acquisition structure. Nobody knows who is informed, how well, or why, so the inference from "he is offering" to "he must know something I don't" has nowhere to land.

(c) Trade as a signal with many admissible motives. The offerer might be hedging, raising cash, satisfying a mandate, rebalancing to an index, harvesting a tax loss, or unwinding an earlier hedge; the offer is compatible with so many private states that it conveys almost nothing about the one that matters.

(d) Career and agency motives. The trader's utility runs over her own compensation, survival, and relative standing rather than over the portfolio's terminal value, and she trades in part because being seen to trade is rewarded.

(e) Benchmark-relative evaluation. The relevant reference point is an index or a peer group rather than the endowment, so an allocation can be Pareto efficient in consumption terms and still intolerable relative to the benchmark.

(f) Process utility. Agents take direct satisfaction from holding a view and acting on it — the position is a way of being right in public — so the act of trading has value apart from its payoff.

(g) Bounded depth of reasoning. The inference from an offer to the offerer's information requires iterated reasoning of a depth that real traders do not carry out; the regress stops after a step or two.

I have also included one item that I suspect is not a leak at all: (h) the thought that the theorem fails because some traders know more than others. I would like you to tell me whether I am right that this dissolves.

Please answer in the following sections, in this order and roughly these lengths:

The short answer — about 100 words. Name the single leak that does the most explanatory work, and say which one I should make the engine of the novel if I want the trading to be both dramatically live and defensible.

The ranking — about 600 words. Rank all seven of (a) through (g) from most to least explanatory, grouping them into tiers if you find that more honest than a strict order, and give each entry two to four sentences on what it buys and where it breaks down. Be willing to put things in the same tier, but do not put everything in the same tier.

The item that is not a leak — about 150 words. Take (h) and say whether it dissolves, and if so, why the theorem's critics keep reaching for it anyway.

What I would have to change in the setup — about 150 words. Identify the smallest revision to the theorem's premises that would make real trading volume unsurprising, and say what the revision costs the theorem's intended point about the epistemology of markets.
