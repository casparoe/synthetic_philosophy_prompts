I have been reading rationalist blogs and decision-theory forums for years, and I keep returning to Hofstadter's superrationality as the standard rationalist answer to the one-shot Prisoner's Dilemma. I want to understand how it compares to a much older universalization principle, Kant's formula of universal law, and I want the comparison to be tested against the two games Hofstadter used to develop the idea: the Platonia Dilemma and the Luring Lottery. I will describe both games so that the comparison does not depend on having the original texts at hand.

In the Platonia Dilemma, twenty people are each invited to send in a telegram. If exactly one telegram arrives, that sender wins a very large prize. If two or more arrive, no one wins anything. If none arrive, no one wins anything. The participants cannot communicate, cannot identify one another, and cannot agree to share the prize afterward. In the Luring Lottery, a single prize is divided equally among everyone who enters, and each participant may enter as many times as they wish; every additional entry dilutes everyone's share, including the entrant's own.

Kant's formula of universal law says to act only on that maxim through which you can at the same time will that it become a universal law. Hofstadter's superrationality says, roughly, to choose the policy that maximizes expected utility on the assumption that all agents who are relevantly similar to you and facing the same problem will adopt the same policy. I am interested in the precise points of agreement and divergence between these two principles, not in a generic summary of either.

Please address the following. First, what does each principle recommend in the Platonia Dilemma? Does Kant's test forbid sending the telegram, permit it, or leave the question underdetermined? Does superrationality recommend sending, not sending, or a mixed strategy in which each participant sends with some probability? If superrationality recommends a mixed strategy, does Kant's test have the resources to evaluate a maxim of randomizing, or is randomization a blind spot for it? Second, what does each principle recommend in the Luring Lottery? Does Kant's test forbid entering more than once, or entering at all? Does superrationality recommend a particular number of entries, and if so, how does it derive that number? Third, where do the two principles come apart most sharply? I am especially interested in cases where a maxim can be universalized in Kant's sense without contradiction but where universal adoption would still be collectively self-defeating, and in cases where superrationality's symmetry assumption seems to do work that Kant's test does not license. Fourth, does superrationality amount to a formalized, utility-maximizing version of Kant's universal law test, or does it depend on assumptions Kant would reject, such as the identity of the reasoners, the symmetry of the problem, or the propriety of expected-utility maximization as the test of a maxim? Conversely, does Kant's test capture a distinction between contradiction in conception and mere bad collective consequences that superrationality flattens?

Finally, take a stand. If you had to choose one of the two as the better reconstruction of the intuition that cooperation in a one-shot symmetric dilemma is rational, which would you choose, and what would you have to give up in choosing it? I would rather have a reasoned verdict than a survey of positions.
