I'm doing the opening remarks at a two-day workshop my department's hosting on when a legendary tradition stops being "just a legend" and starts getting treated as history — the three running cases are the Trojan War, King Arthur, and the Exodus, and honestly the whole workshop exists becuase of my late colleague. She spent her last decade arguing that "did it happen?" is the wrong question and that we should instead be sorting traditions by whether they're even candidates for history, and she had a little three-part test she'd run them through. First, the hole test: is there an event-shaped gap in the material record — an anomaly, a destruction layer, a suspicious silence — that the story could plausibly be the shadow of? Second, the anchor test: is there a place or an institution with a standing reason to keep the story alive and roughly the right way — a sanctuary, a throne, a festival, a scribal school — so that the transmission isn't just an accident? Third, the awkwardness test: does the story carry details that are inconvenient for the people telling it, bits a later inventor would have had every reason to trim out? She thought traditions that score on those are "history-eligible" even when we could never confirm them, and that the ones that only score because they're flattering or tidy are doing a different kind of work altogether. I don't want to trot her test out at the top and then give a generic talk about myth vs. history; I want the remarks actually built on it, moving through the three cases and letting the test do the structural work.

Here's the tricky part, and it's why I'm asking rather than just drafting it myself. The room is mixed and I'm nervous about it. Maybe half are ancient historians and archaeologists who lean minimalist and will flinch if I sound like I'm rehabilitating anything; there are medievalists and a couple of Arthur people who are perfectly happy to treat the whole thing as literature; and then we've invited a local interfaith group and some members of a congregation for the Exodus session, for whom this isn't a puzzle about evidence at all, it's their story, and I am not going to stand up there and tell them it didn't happen. So the speech has to hold all of that at once — honest with the sceptics without being cruel to the believers — and her framework needs to be the thing that lets me do it, not just a fig leaf over the disagreement. Keep it to something I can actually say out loud in six or seven minutes, warm but not mushy, and no jokes that punch down at anyone who's in the room.

Also, please be straight with me about which parts of this are settled among people who actually know the material and which are live fights — especially on the Exodus, where my sense is that the field is more divided than a short talk can show, and I don't want a contested position coming out of my mouth sounding like the consensus. Give me the response in three named sections, in this order: "The remarks" — the speech itself, around 800 words; "Settled vs. contested" — around 200 words separating what's consensus from what's disputed; and "What I softened, and why" — around 150 words on the calls I made for this particular room.
