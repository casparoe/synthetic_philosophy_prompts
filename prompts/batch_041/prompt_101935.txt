So my best friend is getting married next month and she's asked me to give the toast, and I've managed to get myself into a bit of a bind. She's a philosophy PhD student who works on Nāgārjuna — emptiness, the tetralemma, the four corners, all of it — and I've spent enough time around her that I've picked up the vocabulary and, honestly, half-belive it. Her fiancé is a marine biologist who is lovely and completely allergic to philosophy. Maybe a third of the room will be her department (analytic types who think Madhyamaka is either trivially true or self-refuting), and the rest is family who just want something warm and short. Here's the bind: every draft I write wants to say something like "you two are perfect for each other, you're really one," or "you're two people who chose each other," or some blend of the two — and all of those sound to me like exactly the kind of claims about svabhāva (inherent, independent existence) that she'd tell me to knock off. But I also don't want to shrug and say "well, nothing has a nature, so nothing matters," because that's the nihilist misreading she's always complaining about, and it would also be a terrible toast.

So here's what I want from you: a five-minute toast, warm, genuinely built for that mixed room, with the four corners of the tetralemma doing the actual structural work rather than just being name-dropped. I had this idea of four little toasts, one for each corner — they're one; they're two; they're both; they're neither — each of which gets gently undercut as I go, and then a last line that isn't a metaphysical claim at all. The conventional-truth side of things has to carry the emotional weight: the point should come out as the marriage being real and precious precisely because it isn't a fixed essence, not in spite of it. Please don't let me get tangled in whether "everything is empty" refutes itself — I know that's a live objection but I don't want the toast dying on that hill — and don't let it turn into a lecture. If you can work in one small joke that the philosophers and the family can both laugh at, even better. And it has to end with something I can actually raise a glass to.
