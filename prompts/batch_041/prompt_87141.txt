Most legal systems descended from Roman law have to answer a question that is more metaphysical than it looks: when one person makes a new thing out of materials belonging to another — pressing someone else's grapes into wine, painting a picture on someone else's canvas, building with someone else's bricks, weaving someone else's thread into a garment, or mixing two owners' grain — who owns the result? The doctrine, usually called accession, resolves this by sorting the inputs into a principal thing and an accessory that has been merged into it, and awarding the product to the owner of the principal. Which input counts as principal is settled by a shifting list of tests — economic value, bulk, whose labor it was, and, most characteristically, physical identity, under which the principal is whatever gives the finished thing its name while the accessory is whatever has lost its identity in the other. The verdicts are casuistic: paint on another's panel, write on another's parchment, weave with another's thread, mix two owners' grain, and you get four different rules rather than one. Roman jurists themselves arrived at no consistent principle and decided these cases one at a time.

I want to know what conception of objects this body of practice presupposes. Concretely: does it assume a settled answer to the question of when several things come together to make a single new thing, and of whether the things that went into it survive inside it as parts or are destroyed in the making? The recurrent image of an accessory whose identity is lost in the principal suggests that composition sometimes swallows its inputs and sometimes does not; is that a stable position, or does the doctrine slide between two incompatible pictures of what a finished object is? Please separate what the practice strictly requires from what it merely accommodates comfortably, and identify where it is underdetermined — whether one account of composition could underwrite all of the doctrine's verdicts, or whether the casuistry is itself evidence that no single account is being applied. Then consider two replacements for the implicit view. On the first, composition never occurs: there are only basic particles, and no such thing as the wine, the painting, or the house, only arrangements of simples. On the second, composition always occurs: any plurality of things whatsoever makes something. For each replacement, say which parts of the doctrine would have to be rewritten and which would be left standing.
