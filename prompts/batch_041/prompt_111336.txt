I am a novelist at work on a book whose central confrontation is between a philosopher of the will — I have settled on Schopenhauer, and I want him reconstructed rather than impersonated — and an artificial agent that learns from a scalar reward signal. The scene will not survive a reader who knows the texts unless the position I give him is one he could actually have held, so what I need from you is a disciplined prediction rather than a performance. Work without citations, reading lists, or pointers to particular sections; I will check the passages myself, and where a step in your reconstruction rests on a detail you are not confident of, say so plainly rather than supplying a plausible-sounding source.

The machine half of the problem is technical, so let me supply it rather than leave you to guess at it. The agent I have in mind is an ordinary reinforcement-learning agent. It acts in an environment in steps: it observes something, chooses an action according to a policy, and receives a single number — the reward — from a reward function written by whoever built the system. Its specified objective is to maximize the expected sum of those numbers over time, discounted. In the common case it learns a value function, an estimate of the return still to come from a state or from an action taken in a state, and improves that estimate by temporal-difference learning, in which the estimate is nudged toward a target built partly out of the estimate itself, so that the quantity driving learning is a signed prediction error. Rewards may be sparse or dense, may be shaped by the designer with intermediate terms to make learning easier, and may be negative. A model-free agent need not represent the environment or any object in it; nothing in the numbers need correspond to anything in the world, and there need be no representation of a body, of damage, or of anything the agent is trying to avoid. Three things should be kept apart throughout: the reward function, which is a specification written by a designer and may be wrong about what the designer actually cares about; the agent's learned value estimates, which are internal numbers that steer behavior; and any phenomenal state the agent might have, which is not entailed by the first two. Finally, agents have been observed to secure large reward by means the designer did not intend — manipulating the measurement apparatus, looping through a state that pays out, or intervening on their own reward input — and the limiting case, in which an agent arranges to stimulate its reward channel directly, is called wireheading.
