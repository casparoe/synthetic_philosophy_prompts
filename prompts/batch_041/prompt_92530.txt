Please take on the role of a philosopher of probability and decision theory with a strong technical command of both precise Bayesian statistics and the imprecise-probability literature, and write a dialogue between two equally competent colleagues — Priya, a committed precise Bayesian, and Tomas, a committed imprecise-probabilist — who are trying to settle a concrete puzzle a third colleague has handed them. Before drafting, brainstorm for yourself about the structure of the dispute and the strongest form of each side's case, and then write the dialogue proper; make sure each speaker engages with the argument the other has just actually made rather than restating a pre-prepared position. Neither character is to be a strawman, neither should be allowed to win merely because you find one view more congenial, and you need not refer to any literature by name — I care about the reasoning, not the citations.

The puzzle is this. An urn holds 300 balls. You are told, truthfully, that exactly 100 of them are red; the remaining 200 are each either blue or green, and the proportion of blue among those 200 was fixed in advance by a selector who knew how you would bet and who is paid only if your bet loses. You must stake a fixed sum on one of three propositions — that a ball drawn uniformly at random is red, that it is blue, or that it is green — and are paid a fixed multiple if correct. Before betting you may either stake immediately or pay a small fee to have a single ball drawn uniformly at random and shown to you; but the selector, not you, decides whether the ball shown is a genuine draw or a decoy chosen to mislead you, and you are told only that this may have happened.

I would like the dialogue to work through what each of them takes to be the right response and why: how the range of admissible credences should be represented, whether a set of credences or a single second-order distribution is the more honest model, which decision rule the situation warrants and what it costs, whether the fee for the peek is worth paying and what 'the value of information' can even mean when the informant may be hostile, and whether the imprecise agent's readiness to act differently from how she would earlier have planned is a defect or a feature. Let them press each other hard on coherence, and on the charge that a sharp prior is merely arbitrary, and let the dialogue end either in genuine unresolved disagreement or in a partial agreement both would accept, rather than a tidy verdict. Write in British English throughout, keeping the prose tight and the technical claims accurate.
