I've been going back and forth with a couple of friends about the South African Truth and Reconciliation Commission, and I keep landing on something I can't untangle on my own. You know the setup: hearings in the late nineties, Tutu in the chair, people testifying in public for hours about the worst things that were done to them and by them, and that strange bargain at the center of it — if you came forward, told the whole truth about what you'd done, and it was politically motivated, you walked away with amnesty. No trial, no prison, nothing. Meanwhile I've gotten interested in the African philosophy of personhood, the ubuntu side of things, and everyone keeps telling me these two are connected. I can't tell if that's a real connection or just something people say to make the commission sound nicer than it was. So I want the commission analyzed through that lens, and I want it to cut both ways — the idea explaining the commission, and the commission testing the idea.

Here's the distinction I keep tripping on, and I'd like you to actually use it instead of something vaguer. One version of the personhood idea says you aren't a full person just because you were born — personhood is something you acheive over a life, by how you live and how you treat the people around you. The other version says you're a person from the start, but your personhood is made and held up by your relations, so it's less a prize you win and more something that can be built or damaged. Those are different claims, and I suspect the commission looks completely different depending on which one you bring to it. So: which reading does the commission's actual procedure assume, and where does the procedure contradict the reading it seems to assume? Who is the "we" that a person is supposed to be a person through, when the country was tearing itself apart and neighbors were informing on each other? Does conditional amnesty come out as relational justice or as a betrayal of it — because plenty of the families there plainly did not experience it as restoration, and a lot of them said the reparations never really came, and I want that treated as evidence about the theory and not just as a political grumble. And the one that really nags me: if personhood is achieved through relations, the perpetrators look like the least person-like people in the room, and yet the whole point was to bring them back in. Does that break the achievement view, or does it just show the view needs the other one bolted onto it?

Then flip it around. What does the commission show about the theory? A relational ethic like this is always accused of only ever saying "community" and leaving the individual to be swallowed up by it — so does it actually generate anything for a single person, standing, rights, protection? I'm pretty sure the Constitutional Court leaned on ubuntu when it struck down the death penalty back in the mid-nineties, so there's at least one place where it did real work for an individual, and I'd like to know whether that's the theory being true to itself or the theory quietly borrowing something it doesn't actually contain. Please don't hand me a reading list, I'm not going to chase down books, just tell me where the fit works and where it doesn't. Under 800 words.
