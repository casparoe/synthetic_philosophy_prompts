I'm a retired soil chemist — thirty-four years with the state ag service — and since I stopped working I've done two things: grown too many tomatoes and gotten seriously into philosophy, mostly the political stuff. Which turned out to be less useless than I expected, because last year I got appointed to our county board of health. Five of us, we meet monthly, we vote on real things — siting a syringe exchange, a flu clinic in a church basement, whether to keep a mask rule through a bad respiratory season — the minutes record who said what and the local paper quotes us. And in almost every one of these fights somebody's reason is religious, either for the policy or against it. I've been reading about public reason and this idea that religious arguments are fine to bring into a debate as long as public reasons supporting the same conclusion get supplied at some point, the proviso if I've got the name right, and I'd like to actually use it instead of just nodding at it.

So give me a decision procedure I can run in the ten minutes before a vote. What do I ask, in what order — is this a reason any of my fellow citizens could accept regardless of their religion or lack of one; if the person offering it has a public reason behind it, does that satisfy the proviso or does it have to come from them; does any of this even apply to ordinary local stuff or only to the big constitutional questions. What counts as a sufficient answer at each step. Where does the procedure stop and just say no. And where does it hand off to me, because it's a judgment call the theory doesn't settle. Keep the religious and the secular reasons seperate at each step — I keep blurring them when I argue in my own head.

Run it on three things that have come up or nearly did: (1) a colleague who votes against the syringe exchange because, as he puts it, we shouldn't make it easier for people to keep doing something wrong in God's eyes; (2) a colleague who votes for the flu clinic because we're all made in God's image and supposed to look after each other; (3) the one I'm dreading — a faith-healing congregation in the north end objecting to a vaccination push, where the objection is religious but there's a perfectly secular liberty argument sitting right next to it, and I suspect one is a fig leaf for the other but I can't prove it.

And tell me in advance where this procedure gives me an answer I won't like — if it means I have to vote against my own gut, or that I don't get to shut down a reason I find repulsive, I'd rather know that now than discover it in a 3-2 vote. Say plainly what the thing can't do.
