Ok so I'm in a philosophy of science / metaphysics module and we've hit the bit about how you can ever say what something is really made of when some of the relevant information is unreachable. Before we start comparing answers I want the marking scheme, as it were. Write me the desiderata — the full spec — that any theory of identifying a system's ontology when part of the information about it is inaccessible has to meet.

Here's the flavour of case I mean, so the requirements aren't floating in the abstract. Picture a sealed box we can probe but never open. It answers only some kinds of probe and is simply blind to others — those inputs vanish, they leave no trace at all. Everything we can ever record is a finite, noisy stream of readings. Now two rival inventories of what's inside it — different 'furniture', different things that exist — both predict every reading we will ever get, and no experiment separates them. A third inventory posits the same stuff as the first but reshuffles which item is which: a systematic relabelling that keeps all the numbers identical. And a colleague describes the same insides using a completely different vocabulary that looks like it's about fields and processes rather than things and objects.

I want the requirements list for a theory of this. What it has to deliver, with the case behind each requirement: which phenomena it must account for, which cases it must get right, which distinctions it must not collapse (e.g. between 'there's no fact of the matter here' and 'there's a fact we just can't reach'; between being empirically equivalent and being literally the same claim), and which results or constraints it can't contradict. Flag any pair of requirements you think can't be satisfied at the same time — that's the bit I most want. And tell me which single requirement definately knocks out the most popular approaches. Then a short scorecard of the usual contenders (indeterminacy-of-reference style arguments, proxy-function / permutation tricks, structural realism, Ramsey-style reconstructions) against the list — but keep the weight on the criteria, I'll argue about the scoring in the seminar.
