I sit on the editorial board of a mid-size philosophy journal (the name doesn't matter) and Thursday we finally have to settle our style rule for slurs. Right now it's whatever the copyeditor feels like that morning, and we've got two submissions stuck because of it: one is by a Black philosopher arguing that reclaiming a particular slur is politically and linguistically coherent, and she uses it unstarred in her title and all the way through; the other is a history-of-medicine piece quoting a nineteenth-century physician's case notes, and the quote contains a different slur, this one aimed at disabled people. There's also a third paper by a white grad student who argues, in the course of making a point about how a word's meaning gets fixed by its history, that he isn't using the slur derogatorily when he uses it. The board is split. One camp says print it: asterisks and "the n-word"-style substitutions are a kind of cowardice, they distort the scholarship, and you can't argue about reclamation while refusing to write the word down. The other camp says the harm is in the word itself, so printing it makes us complicit no matter who's writing, and we should use the euphemism form uniformly. There's a sub-fight about whether the answer changes when the author belongs to the group the slur targets, and whether it changes between the title/abstract and the body text, and someone raised whether the euphemism itself starts to function as a slur once everyone knows what it's standing in for. One colleague keeps saying the insult "isn't asserted, it just rides along" -- she means it survives under negation and in conditionals, so it has to be part of what the word means rather than just a rude speaker's effect; another says that only shows the content is presupposed or backgrounded, and backgrounded content can be cancelled or blocked by context, which is exactly what reclamation is supposed to be doing. I honestly can't tell whether those two are disagreeing about the linguistics or about the politics, and I suspect it matters for the rule.

Draft the opinion I'll circulate Wednesday night. Take a side -- I want an actual rule with actual wording, not a menu of options with "considerations on both sides" -- and be honest about the strongest thing the other side has going for it. Keep it under two pages, in these sections and this order: "Bottom line" (three or four sentences: the rule itself, plus the motion I should put to the board and how I should expect the vote to go); "Why print in full" (the best case for the permissive rule, one paragraph, and make it the best version of itself -- the argument is about what euphemising does to the very texts we're publishing and to the people whose language is being managed, not about edginess); "Why euphemise" (the best case for the restrictive rule, one paragraph, and don't strawman it either -- the strong version is about harm to readers and about what the journal's practices signal to the communities involved, not about protecting delicate sensibilities); "The rule I'd adopt" (exact wording, then how it handles titles and abstracts vs body text, quotations from historical sources, authors who are members of the targeted group, and what we do with the grad student's paper); and "What would change my mind" (short). Assume we can't make the authors rewrite -- the vote is accept as is, accept conditional on style changes, or reject.
