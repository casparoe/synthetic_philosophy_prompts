I'm a novelist, about two thirds through a book with a narrator who turns out to have invented most of what you've been reading, and I keep going back to Atonement because McEwan got there first and better, and I want to think about it as more than craft. The setup: the whole novel we've read is Briony's novel, and in the last section she tells us straight out that the 1940 reunion she gave Cecilia and Robbie never happened — Robbie died of septicaemia on the beach at Dunkirk, Cecilia was killed when a bomb hit Balham Underground during the Blitz, and the happy bit was her invention. So the book asserts P and then asserts that P was made up. That looks structurally like a liar sentence to me, except the sentence doing the unmasking is also inside the book, which is exactly the move that gets you the revenge problem when you try to fix the paradox by shoving the truth predicate up to a higher level. So my questions. Is Atonement a liar, a truth-teller, or neither — because those two come apart in the fixed-point way of thinking about groundedness, and I want to know which one she's built. Does Briony's last section count as the ground that makes everything before it ungrounded, or does the ground itself go loose once you notice it's fiction too? And does the whole thing collapse because it's a novel — nothing in it is asserted, so maybe the paradox never even gets off the runway, and I'd genuinely like to know if that's a cheap escape or the right answer.
