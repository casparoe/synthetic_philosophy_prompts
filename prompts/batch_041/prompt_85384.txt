I am preparing a seminar on Goethe's political thought and want to make his Egmont the central text rather than the usual excerpts from the conversations and the campaign memoirs. The difficulty is that the play gets read in one of two ways — as a straightforward hymn to liberty, or as a character tragedy about a charming man who cannot take politics seriously — and neither reading puts any real pressure on Goethe's own political position.

What I want is a reading of Egmont through the lens of Goethe's practical and theoretical politics: the ducal minister who spent decades administering a small German state, who distrusted revolution and political abstraction, who held that reform must come gradually and from above, and whose sympathies lay with settled burgher life rather than with political enthusiasm. The play stages precisely the confrontation his politics had to manage. Alba speaks for order, and his argument is uncomfortably close to the remark Goethe is reported to have made late in life, in a secondhand report whose provenance is contested — that he would rather commit an injustice than endure disorder. Egmont speaks for inherited liberties, personal loyalty, and the charm of the exceptional individual, but Goethe gives him no political program whatsoever; he is negligent where Orange is prudent, and the play is honest about what that negligence costs. Meanwhile the Brussels guildsmen, whose lives are the thing actually at stake, are drawn warmly and as politically inert. So where does the play come down?

Please address several things. First, whether the play vindicates Alba's logic even as it condemns him, or whether Goethe supplies something that escapes the dilemma altogether. Second, what to make of the fact that Goethe names one of Margaret of Parma's advisers Machiavell and gives him the flavor of the historical Machiavelli — is the play a meditation on The Prince, and if so, whose Prince is Egmont? Third, the final act: the prison vision in which Clärchen appears and Egmont's death is transfigured into a victory for the cause of liberty. That ending was written in Italy, at the close of a twelve-year composition, before 1789; it is an apotheosis rather than a political argument. Does it resolve the play's politics or evade them, and how does it sit with the Goethe who, a few years later, watched the French Revolution with distaste and wrote a bourgeois epic about German townsfolk and its refugees?

I also want the honest counter-reading: where Egmont resists being treated as political theory at all, where its real interest is the psychology of a man who trusts his own person more than any constitution, and where the political frame distorts the play.

End by saying what the play contributes that a treatise could not — whether it dramatizes a dilemma about order, freedom, and the price of each that Goethe's own maxims flatten, and whether the play understands more than its author did.
