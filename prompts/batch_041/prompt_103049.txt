I was at a graduate seminar last week where two students, Marta and Dev, got into an argument that ran well past the end of the session, and the rest of us never got a clean verdict. I would like one, move by move.

Marta opened with the pessimistic meta-induction. The historical record, she said, is a graveyard: theory after theory that was empirically successful and central to research was later abandoned as false in its core claims — the luminiferous ether, phlogiston, caloric, and Newtonian mechanics itself, which survives only as a limiting case of something else. If success were a reliable sign of truth, she said, that correlation should show up in the record. It does not. So we should expect our own best theories to be false in their central claims too, and realism — the thesis that our successful theories are at least approximately true — is a bad bet.

Dev's first reply was that the sample is biased. The roll-call is drawn almost entirely from episodes of revolutionary change, and it counts as 'abandoned' theories that were superseded by successors which retained their predictive content. Restricted to mature theories, and to what those theories actually predicted rather than to the metaphysical gloss their authors put on them, retention is high. He added that a theory being superseded is not the same as its working posits being shown false, and that Marta's conclusion needs both a reference class and a probability threshold; realism may only require that the posits doing the predictive work are more likely than not to be retained, which is a far weaker claim than the one the induction refutes.

Marta said this is hindsight. You can sort theories into mature and immature only by seeing which ones survived; if 'mature' means 'the ones whose posits were retained', the reply is circular. And even granting the distinction, the realist has to say which posits are the working ones at the time, not after the fact, or the position makes no difference to what anyone should believe.

Dev then proposed a forward-looking criterion, which he called the derivation test. A posit earns credibility to the extent that it is indispensable to the derivation of the theory's novel, risky predictions: if you cannot get the predictions out without it, you have a live reason to think it is doing real work rather than bookkeeping. That test, he said, is available in advance, and it excludes the ether — the ether was posited as a medium, but the predictions that made the theory successful did not depend on its mechanical properties.

Marta's last move was that the test is either empty or not about truth. 'Indispensable to the derivation' is relative to how the theory is formulated, and a theory can be reformulated so that the same predictions follow from different posits; so the test lets you keep whichever posits you were fond of, provided you write the derivation accordingly. And if it is only a criterion of economy in our representations, it is a fact about us, not about what exists. Dev answered that she had now made 'indispensable' do all the work while refusing to say what it means; she answered that he had made 'working posit' do all the work while refusing to say how it is fixed at the time. They went around this three more times without either conceding anything.

Please adjudicate. Go move by move and say who is right about each specific claim — the induction itself, the bias charge, the hindsight charge, the derivation test, and the circularity charge against it — rating each from clearly mistaken to clearly right, with a brief justification for each rating. Then say what both of them are missing, and who has the better of the exchange overall.
