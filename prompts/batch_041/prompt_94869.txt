I'm in the last stretch of a graduate seminar on constitutional theory, and the whole second half of the course has circled one analogy: that a political community can bind its future self the way Ulysses had himself lashed to the mast. My final assignment is to stage a dialogue between two people who disagree about whether that analogy can bear the weight we place on entrenched constitutional provisions, and I'd like you to write it. The first interlocutor defends what I'll call the binding view: she holds that a constitution's hardest-to-amend clauses are genuine Ulysses contracts, justified because they shield the community from its own foreseeable bouts of panic, factional capture, and short-horizon self-interest. The second is a critic who thinks the analogy collapses the moment the bound party is a collective rather than a single agent, and that entrenchment is therefore either an incoherent kind of self-binding or a respectable-looking way for one generation to govern another. Both should be first-rate -- no strawmen, no cheap point-scoring -- and each should answer the other's actual argument rather than gliding past it.

To keep the exchange from becoming a recitation of the usual positions, please have them argue over a concrete device I've been toying with. Call it a ratchet clause: it would let a legislature add a constitutional right by ordinary majority, but require a two-thirds majority in two successive parliaments plus a confirmatory referendum to repeal one. I want the defender to say what the ratchet buys over ordinary entrenchment, and why it isn't just a Ulysses contract with the mast welded into place; I want the critic to press on whether a rule that makes expansion easy and contraction hard can be justified to the generation that inherits it, and whether it is a neutral procedural device or a substantive political commitment in disguise. Somewhere in the exchange the critic should raise the objection that the binding view must itself be precommitted -- that the community has to be bound now in order to be bindable later -- and the defender should offer a real reply rather than a concession.

Finally, have them work through the case I keep getting stuck on: a right that entered the constitution through the ratchet's easy path -- say, a sweeping right of personal control over one's data -- which a later generation, after a technological upheaval nobody anticipated, overwhelmingly wants to narrow or repeal. Does the ratchet make that generation's considered judgment hostage to a passing majority's enthusiasm, or is that precisely the weakness the clause was built to resist? Let the critic float a concrete alternative, such as periodic review by a randomly selected citizens' assembly with a lower repeal threshold, so the defender has something specific to resist rather than a vague worry. End the dialogue with each of them naming the one consideration that would actually move them, so I can see where the disagreement is load-bearing and where it is merely temperament.
