I work in admin at an NHS trust and I've somehow ended up on two seperate committees, one for the transplant unit and one that signs off the paperwork for the Phase I trials unit we run on the same site. The more time I spend with the two sets of rules the less I can see how they came out of the same building.

Trials side: healthy volunteers get paid. There's a fee schedule on the website — so much per screening visit, so much per night in the unit, more if there's a biopsy, more again if they have to leave a cannula in all day, plus a completion bonus. A fair few of them do it as a job. When I asked the ethics lot why this is allowed they said the money is for your time and the inconvenience, not for the risk, and that it only becomes a problem if the sum is so large it turns into an undue inducement in its own right.

Transplant side: we'll take a kidney out of you and give it to somebody else, and if anyone offers the donor fifty quid they've committed a criminal offence. The reason everyone gives is that it would exploit poor people — nobody would do it unless they were desperate, so the consent wouldn't be worth anything.

But that's the same worry we have about the trial volunteers, and there we treat it as a recruitment problem: no adverts in job centres, a nurse reads the consent form out loud, off you go. Here it's treated as a reason for the criminal law. And taking a kidney out causes the donor a hell of a lot of time and inconvenience, so if the money really is for the time and inconvenience I can't see where the line is meant to fall. One of the registrars reckons it's wage versus price — the volunteer sells their time, the donor would be selling the thing itself — but I'm definately not convinced there's a difference there.

So what do the two practices actually presuppose? Is there one view about bodies and money behind both, or two views that just happen to share a car park? If it's two, which one is doing the real work — is the objection to money changing hands at all, or to it changing hands with someone poor, or to something about how the deal is structured? And is paying for time and inconvenience a real distinction or just a phrase that lets us run a market while telling ourselves we aren't? Tell me which bits are genuinely presupposed and which could be swapped out without the practice changing, and where the whole thing is underdetermined — I suspect several different theories would license what we do. Then say what you'd change to make the trust consistent, and whether that's a tweak or whether the whole thing falls over.
