A small interdisciplinary group has three years of funding and three members — a philosopher of mathematics, a historian of early modern philosophy, and a sociologist of science — and it has committed to a single theme: the intellectual significance of Hobbes's work in and around geometry, including his long public quarrel with Wallis, his repeated attempts on the classical construction problems, and the place he assigned to mathematical method in his philosophy as a whole. The funders do not want a biography, an edition, or a narrative survey; they want a defended research agenda.

Identify the main open questions in this area and rank them by how much they deserve the group's three years. For each, say what the question is, why it is upstream of the others (or why it is not), how tractable it is given this particular combination of skills, and what would be lost if it were left undone. Then state the criteria you are using — importance, tractability, neglectedness, how many other questions depend on the answer, and whatever else you take to matter — and be explicit about how those criteria trade off against one another, and about what evidence or argument over the next few years would move a question up or down the ranking.

Address these specific matters along the way: which questions about the episode are genuinely open and which are settled but repeatedly re-litigated; whether the interest of Hobbes's mathematics lies mainly in its internal errors, in its bearing on his metaphysics and political philosophy, or in what it reveals about the social organization of mathematical knowledge; and whether the group should aim chiefly at an explanatory account of how a formidably intelligent thinker can be systematically and self-confidently wrong in a formal discipline, or at something else. Also flag the questions you consider overrated — those whose answers, even if decisively settled, would change little — and say why.
