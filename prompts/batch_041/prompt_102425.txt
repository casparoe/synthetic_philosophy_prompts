I've been helping a small press put together the eleventh edition of its house style manual, and I'd like to include a specimen entry that does for a whole family of default assumptions what Douglas Hofstadter's "A Person Paper on Purity in Language" (collected in *Metamagical Themas*, 1985) did for gender: transpose the linguistic convention rather than denounce it, so that the reader's own revulsion at the transposed usage carries the argument. What I have in mind is a single entry — "The Unmarked Case," or something of the kind — written in the flat, confident register of a real style manual, with numbered rules and worked examples, from a world in which the convention has been extended across several axes of defaulting at once rather than just the one Hofstadter chose. So: not the race-for-gender substitution he performed, and none of his examples, but a manual that has decided, on grounds of mere consistency, that wherever English leaves a default group unmarked it will now mark that group explicitly, and that applies the ruling uniformly to gender and to at least two further axes of defaulting that you select — say ability, species, age, native-speaker status, or handedness. The examples should be real asymmetries of English rather than inventions: unmarked and marked pairs like bachelor and spinster, host and hostess, governor and governess; asymmetric titles such as Mr. against Miss and Mrs.; the occupational terms that quietly default to one group; the habit of bolting a marker onto the non-default case (the female surgeon, the male nurse); the pronoun that is supposed to be generic and isn't. I want a linguist to laugh at the accuracy rather than wince at a made-up datum, so keep the morphology honest even where the ruling is absurd.

The piece should run about 650 to 750 words, formatted as the manual entry itself: a short preamble in the manual's voice, then numbered rules with examples, and at least one in-universe footnote or cross-reference to a neighbouring section, which is usually where the best joke lives. It has to stay deadpan — no rule may name the joke, no narrator may step out to explain that this is a satire, and the manual must never concede that it is doing anything but tidying up inconsistencies. The comedy should come from the guide's own justifications: the beauty and poetry of the inherited forms, the appeal to etymology, the assurance that the unmarked usage was never meant to exclude anyone, the claim that the explicitly marked form is simply more precise. A reader who has never heard of the Person Paper should still feel what is being ironized, and a reader who knows the literature should see that the author has understood it. Two further constraints. First, let the manual's logic run away from it in at least one place — a rule that begins as a harmless consistency measure and, followed out, produces something monstrous (a pronoun paradigm, a system of honorifics, a rule for compounds) — and let the manual report the monstrosity without flinching. Second, the accumulated effect across the axes should be that one notices the general shape of an unmarked default, not merely the gender case; by the end, the "neutral" term should look like a decision that was made all along. The target is a practice, not a person — the habit of treating one group's traits as the human baseline, and the style-guide genre that polices it — so if the entry quotes an advocate, invent one rather than putting the argument in the mouth of a named living writer.
