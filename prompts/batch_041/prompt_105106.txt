I teach an advanced undergraduate seminar on induction and concept formation. There are sixteen students, the session is ninety minutes, and most of them have already met Hume's problem of induction and Goodman's grue in lecture and can recite the standard responses. What they have never done is try to induce a concept themselves from a handful of examples under conditions where the examples genuinely underdetermine the answer, so that they can feel the pull of a rule that fits everything in front of them and the vertigo of realizing that fitting is not the same as being right. I want to build the session around Bongard-style problems, in which a set of figures is divided into two groups and the solver must find the concept that separates them, and I want the concept to be discovered in the doing rather than announced.

Design the activity. Give me the rules, the materials, the timeline, and the debrief, with the following requirements.

Materials and construction: everything must be buildable by me in an afternoon without artistic skill, so specify how I compose the figure sets from a small fixed library of shapes, dots, lines, and enclosures, and how many problems I need. For each problem, state the intended concept in plain words, and for the one problem that carries the session, state both the intended concept and the simpler feature-count rule that fits all twelve training cards equally well, together with a held-out card on which the two rules disagree. Build the set so that a majority of teams will adopt the simple rule and be wrong on the held-out card, and so that the correct concept, once seen, feels obvious and they cannot reconstruct how they missed it.

Structure: an individual warm-up on one easy problem; then teams of three or four; then the reveal with the held-out card; then a construction round in which each team authors a problem and a rival team must recover its concept. The construction round is essential, because I want them to run into the gap between possessing a concept and being able to transmit it in twelve cards, and to notice how easily a fair-looking problem turns out to have several admissible readings.

Incentives: a scoring scheme that does not need real money, rewarding teams for committing to a concept after as few examples as possible and penalizing confident generalizations that fail on the held-out card, so that asking for more examples is a real cost rather than a free move. Tell me how the bids or scores should work, and how to keep the competition from swamping the point.

Debrief: map each phase onto the conceptual content I am trying to convey. I want them to leave seeing the difference between a hypothesis that accommodates the data and one that is projectible; the role of a prior bias toward simple or natural generalizations, and the fact that this bias is doing the work the examples cannot; the idea that solving these problems involves a change in how the figure is represented rather than a search over the original features; and the temptation of a grue-style alternative that matches every card shown and predicts something absurd for the next one, which I want planted deliberately so that someone can raise it and we can see why it feels like cheating. Hold back the vocabulary until the debrief; they should not hear the names of the positions until they have felt the problem.

Then tell me, honestly, what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this, how to handle each failure, and which features of the underlying idea the activity cannot reproduce and that I will have to supply myself in the closing minutes.
