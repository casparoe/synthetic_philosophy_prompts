I teach a seminar on the concept of corruption, and I am trying to move my students past the legalistic definition according to which corruption is simply the abuse of entrusted public office for private gain. Next week I want to use Coppola’s The Godfather—the first film, not the trilogy as a whole—as a sustained case study. The film is usually discussed as a family tragedy or a gangster epic, but I think it is also a remarkable thought experiment about patronage, gift-exchange, and obligation. Vito Corleone’s world runs on requests made at a wedding, favors granted and remembered, debts of gratitude, and the currency of respect; the state’s law is present mostly as an external force that the Corleones either evade or purchase. The arc of the film turns on Michael, who begins as a war hero determined to stay outside the family business and ends as the most efficient and least merciful practitioner of its logic. I want a reading that takes both the film and the philosophical debate seriously: where does the film illuminate the difference between corruption, clientelism, and gift-exchange, and where does it resist being reduced to an illustration of any one of them?

Please bring to bear the following distinctions, which I have been using in class. First, corruption as abuse of public office presupposes a distinction between public role and private interest that the Corleone world does not quite recognize. Second, corruption as a systemic mode of political exchange—clientelism, patronage, favor-banking—may look less like a deviation from a moral order and more like an alternative moral order, with its own norms of reciprocity, loyalty, and honor. Third, corruption as a moral-psychological process: the way Michael’s initial decency is not simply destroyed but converted into a capacity for cold calculation, so that by the baptism sequence he is literally renouncing Satan while ordering murders. I am especially interested in whether the film’s judgment of the Corleones depends on a Western, liberal, impersonal-state standard, and whether a viewer who took amoral familism or patron-client exchange as a functional and even dignified way of life would see the same film differently. Does the film have the resources to criticize the Corleones from within their own moral economy, or does it smuggle in the state’s perspective?

I am not asking for a survey of the literature or a bibliography; I know the relevant debates and want a critical reading. Please focus on particular scenes—the wedding sequence and its requests, the horse’s head, the meeting with Sollozzo, the restaurant killing, and the baptism montage—and say what each shows about the relation between gift, obligation, violence, and corruption. Conclude by saying what The Godfather adds to the philosophical debate that a definitional argument about corruption cannot: does it make us revise the concept, or does it show the limits of any concept that tries to be culturally neutral? I would also like a brief note on how to frame the discussion for students who are inclined to see the Corleones as simply criminals and therefore not a serious test case.
