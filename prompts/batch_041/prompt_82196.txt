I have been arguing with a friend for the better part of a week about the ontological argument, and we have reached the kind of stalemate where each of us can predict the other's next move. His position is the familiar one, that the argument tries to smuggle existence into a concept and that this is a trick you can perform with anything — you cannot define a thing into existence, so the whole family of arguments dies at the first step. My position is that this objection has no purchase on the modal version, which never treats existence as a feature a concept might have more or less of; it works entirely with what must be true and what might be. As I reconstruct it from memory — and I am sure I am mangling the details, so please do not treat my formulation as the target — the modal argument moves in something like this shape. Some properties are perfections, meaning it is better to have them than to lack them. Whatever a perfection entails is itself a perfection. Being God-like, that is, having every perfection, is itself a perfection. Necessary existence is among the perfections, or follows from them. From this, and from the modal principle that whatever is possibly necessary is necessary, the conclusion is supposed to follow. What I want from you is not an adjudication of that argument. What I want is a designed case.

Construct a thought experiment in which an ordinary person, reasoning in ordinary language, is led by a small set of axioms about what is good to have into a conclusion that everyone, including the person, finds absurd. The constraints are the interesting part, and I care about them more than about elegance. First, the setting must be realistic: a person comparing job offers, ranking tenants, drafting a rule for a housing cooperative, choosing between two surgeries, something of that order. No perfect islands, no maximally great pizzas, no fables, and no mention of God, theism, or religion anywhere in the story itself. Second, the case must be formally parallel to the modal argument rather than a caricature of it, so that a defender cannot wave it away as a different argument wearing a costume. Since the story cannot use the word necessity, the modal element has to be carried by an ordinary notion — unavoidability, being so no matter what, being the option that could not have been otherwise — and you should say explicitly which ordinary notion you are using as the analog. Third, each of the person's premises must be individually acceptable to a sensible reader; the absurdity has to emerge only from their combination, and never from a premise that is obviously false on its face. Fourth, the absurd conclusion must not be absurd merely because it is surprising. It must be something the person has independent, everyday reasons to reject, so that the case has teeth rather than just shock value. Fifth and most important, the case must be built so that exactly one premise is isolable. By that I mean the following test: deleting that premise blocks the absurd conclusion; restoring it brings the conclusion back; and deleting any one of the other premises individually does not block it, or at least does not block it in the way the target premise does. Show me that derivation, not just the verdict, because I want to check that nothing else is quietly doing the work. If you find, in the course of building it, that a different premise than the one you expected is the load-bearing one, then say so and rebuild the case around it — the specification is that one premise be isolable, not which one. The point of the exercise, in case it affects your choices, is to test whether the modal machinery transfers to cases where the conclusion is plainly unacceptable; a case that simply announces the absurd conclusion, or that reaches it by stipulating a weird axiom nobody would grant, fails the exercise and I would rather have a harder case that works.

After the scenario, do the work in full. State the person's axioms, give the derivation, and then give the verdict of three positions on whether the parody succeeds. The first is the classical critic who thinks existence is not a predicate and that the whole family of arguments is dead before modality enters the picture: he must say what he thinks the parody adds, and whether he can use it or must reject it as beside the point. The second is the modal defender, who will want to say the parody is invalid or trades on an equivocation: he must say exactly where it breaks, and you must then ask whether the same move is available to him in his own case. The third is someone who thinks the modal argument may be sound but that the parody reveals a primitive notion doing illicit work: he must say what has to be given up, and by whom. For each of the three, tell me what they would have to surrender to hold their line. Then finish with the honest accounting: which single feature of the case is doing the work, what happens to the case if that feature is removed, and how the modal defender would attack the construction. I do not want a survey of the literature, and I do not want citations, author names, or reading lists — describe positions rather than name-checking them, and I will fill in the references myself. Write it in continuous prose, no headings, no bullet points, no bold, no tables, the way you would write a long letter to someone who asked you a hard question. American English, please.
