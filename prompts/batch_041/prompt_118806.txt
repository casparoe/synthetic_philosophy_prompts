A friend of mine who reads a great deal of popular philosophy recently sent me his summary of what he calls the spectrum argument, and he is unusually confident about it. In his telling, the argument begins with two items whose ordering nobody disputes — one is plainly better than the other — and then threads a long sequence of intermediate cases between them, each differing from its neighbour by a hair. Because no one can point to the precise step at which the tiny differences stop mattering, he says, every small step must count as an improvement; and by the time the chain reaches its far end you have argued your way from something wonderful to something plainly awful. On his reading, this proves that 'better than' is not transitive, that value comparisons simply do not obey the logic of ordinary relations; and, further, that many things cannot be ranked against one another at all, since the chain leaves us with pairs of options in which neither is better than the other and neither is worse, so that a choice between them is ultimately a matter of taste. He concludes that this is why serious ethicists have given up on the idea of a single scale of betterness, and that anyone who still speaks of 'the best option' is confused.

I am not sure how much of this to accept, and I have two suspicions I would like you to test. The first is that an argument which reaches an absurd conclusion by assuming transitivity is not the same thing as an argument which establishes that transitivity fails: the interest of a paradox lies in the fact that something has to be surrendered, and I would want to know which premises are actually in play and which one each party to the dispute gives up. The second is a half-remembered footnote to the effect that the transitivity of 'better than' follows from the transitivity of 'at least as good as' — if x is better than y and y is better than z, then x is at least as good as z, and were z at least as good as x we should have z at least as good as y, contradicting that y is better than z — which suggests to me that anyone who denies the transitivity of 'better than' is committed to something considerably stranger about 'at least as good as'. (I am a graduate student in economics, so orderings and representation theorems are familiar ground to me; the ethics is not.) I also cannot see where his talk of options that 'cannot be ranked' comes from, since that strikes me as a different phenomenon from a chain that loops back on itself, and I suspect he has run two debates together.

Please go through his summary claim by claim and tell me what someone working on this would accept, what is a familiar simplification that a careful expositor would flag, and what is a genuine misrepresentation — and then say plainly what the argument does and does not establish. I should also like to know how the main responses differ from one another (denying one of the steps, denying transitivity, accepting the conclusion), what the most effective reply to my friend would be from someone who keeps transitivity, and whether anything true in his summary supports his closing claim that there is no fact of the matter about which of two options is better. I do not need citations or a reading list; I want to understand the shape of the disagreement well enough to argue about it.
