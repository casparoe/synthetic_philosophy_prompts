I sit on the state textbook adoption committee that has to choose the eleventh-grade U.S. history text, and we vote a week from Thursday; I'm one of the two swing votes, so the short written opinion I circulate beforehand will probably decide it. Our two finalists are usually described around here as the 1619 book and the 1776 book: the first builds its narrative around slavery and its afterlives as constitutive of the country, the second around the founding ideals and reads the failures as departures from a promise later generations kept. I need the opinion to be usable — a recommendation the committee can act on, with reasons, not a lament that both books are imperfect.

What has made this harder is that the public comment has not stayed inside the usual frame. One parents' group asks us to require that the book's treatment of Japanese colonial rule in Korea — annexation, forced labor, the women and girls forced into sexual servitude for the Japanese military — be substantially expanded, on the argument that a text which teaches students to look hard at their own country's crimes cannot treat another country's as a paragraph. A second group, including families from the same community, objects that this is not American history, that page budget is finite, and that the passage as drafted reads as advocacy; a third simply asks for "balance" on everything, founding included. My colleague's proposed fix is procedural: adopt whichever book is stronger on the founding documents, then require the publisher to substitute a teacher-edited source packet for the stretches the committee finds tendentious, with students graded on adjudicating among the sources rather than on reaching the right conclusion. I'm skeptical of that, but I have not been able to say precisely why.

Please draft the opinion. It should recommend a concrete course of action, give the reasoning behind it, and state plainly the strongest argument on the side I end up rejecting — I would rather lose an argument in the memo than pretend it doesn't exist. One thing I'd ask you to be alert to: I have been treating this as a contest between two narratives, and I have a nagging sense that the real question is what a textbook is *for* — an instrument of civic formation, a record of what happened, or a training ground in a discipline — and that the vocabulary of the current fight hides that. If the nagging sense is confused, or if a written opinion is simply the wrong document for this body to produce, say so plainly rather than writing around it.
