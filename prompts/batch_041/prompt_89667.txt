I am a philosopher of mind, and for two years now a colleague in my department and I have run a small reading group on functionalism and the multiple-realizability argument; the group has quietly become a running argument between the two of us, and I have taken to keeping notes on her verdicts the way one keeps notes on a chess opponent's openings. She is finishing a book on the metaphysics of mentality, and I am to be her commentator at a workshop next term, so I would like to know in advance where she is likely to come down on a case her manuscript does not discuss. What she has committed herself to so far is this. A bimetallic thermostat has no belief about the temperature of the room. A bacterium swimming up a glucose gradient is exquisitely sensitive to where the sugar is, but has no belief about where the sugar is. A hand-authored lookup table that maps every input it will ever receive onto an output has no beliefs at all. A honeybee that has learned to associate a coloured card with sugar, and that hours later, in an apparatus it has never seen, flies to the colour, does believe that the sugar is there. A food-caching bird that re-hides its caches when it was watched while hiding them, and leaves them alone otherwise, believes that the observer may take them. And a patient with blindsight who can reliably report the orientation of a bar in her blind field when forced to guess, but who insists she is only guessing and cannot put the information to any other use, has no belief about the bar.

Now the case I care about. A team of engineers has hand-built and hand-tuned — with no learning and no evolutionary process anywhere in its history — a silicon system whose internal states are richly integrated: they are sensitive to many features of its surroundings at once, they are revised as the situation changes, and they are put to use across a wide and open-ended range of its behaviour, much as the bee's states are. Nothing about the system is biological, and nothing about it was acquired by training.

Please tell me what verdict she will give — does the system believe anything? — and be explicit about the rule she is running, since I suspect that more than one rule fits everything she has said. Lay out the two or three most plausible candidates, say what each of them predicts about this case, give me your best guess with your reasons, and tell me which further case would do the most to separate whichever candidates survive; I am especially interested in whether she can distinguish the contribution of the functional organization from the contribution of the system's substrate and its history, since that is the crux of the chapter she is writing. If her earlier verdicts genuinely leave the matter open, say so rather than manufacturing a confident prediction.
