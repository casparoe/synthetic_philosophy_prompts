I'm a journalist who covers philosophy and the sciences of reasoning, and I'm drafting a short essay on Lewis Carroll's tortoise: the dialogue in which the tortoise grants two premises and the conditional that links them to a conclusion, then declines to draw the conclusion until that conditional is itself entered as a further premise, and so on without end. I'd like you to act as a philosopher of logic and epistemology and give me detailed comments on the draft below. Please think carefully before responding, and begin by restating what you take my argument to be, so I can tell whether it has landed.

My draft, in three moves.

First, the regress does not show that deduction cannot be justified; it shows that the tortoise has run together two different things, having a reason and having a premise. A premise is an item in a derivation, something you can write on a line. A reason is a consideration that bears on a question. When the tortoise grants the premises and the conditional and then asks why it should accept the conclusion, it is demanding that its reasons be converted into further premises. But the conversion always loses something: the moment the rule of inference is written down as a premise, it becomes one more thing that must be related to the conclusion by a rule, and that rule is not itself written down. The regress is fed by treating a reason as though it were a premise, which is a category mistake rather than a genuine gap in the justification.

Second, the tortoise's demand proves too much, which is why it should be refused. If accepting a proposition as a reason required a further premise licensing its use, then no premise could ever be accepted in the first place, since accepting it would already require inferring from it. So the demand, if honored, would not merely leave deduction unjustified but would empty out reasoning altogether. Since we plainly do reason, the demand must be rejected on pain of a general skepticism about reasons, not just about deduction.

Third, what survives is a positive lesson: the justification of deduction has to be practical rather than propositional. We are entitled to infer not because we hold a premise that guarantees the transition, but because inferring is a practice we are trained into and hold one another to. I try to head off the objection that this simply concedes that deduction is unjustified by arguing that justification for a practice means something different from justification for a belief — though I suspect this is the weakest part of the piece.

I'd like comments of three kinds. On accuracy: is the reason-versus-premise diagnosis of the tortoise right, or is there a more direct reply I'm missing, and does the proves-too-much argument hold, or is there an asymmetry between the tortoise's demand and the demand for a premise that I've overlooked? On interest: has this been said before, and if so, what would I need to add to make the essay worth publishing? On the specific worry above: is the appeal to a practice a genuine position or just a dodge, and what is the strongest objection to it? Where you think a claim is wrong, tell me which premise to drop and what the replacement should be.
