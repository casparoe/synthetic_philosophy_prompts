I've talked myself into Dennett's real-patterns picture and I want you to try to talk me out of it. The picture: a pattern is real when there's a compact description of it that predicts better than a no-pattern baseline, and that's the whole story — no extra ingredient — so beliefs and desires come out as real as electrons, because the intentional stance compresses behavior the way the physical stance compresses trajectories. I'll take the further step myself: if compression plus predictive payoff is the entire criterion, the list of real patterns is open-ended, and the fact that we only have names for three stances is an accident of our interests rather than a fact about what's real.

So what would count against this? I want the actual defeaters, ranked from fatal to merely awkward, not the textbook complaints. Cases where the stance that compresses best is one I'd have to call a mere projection — say a system that's only predictable if you model it as trying to hold some ratio fixed, though nothing designed it, nothing has that function, and exactly one eccentric researcher cares whether it does. Arguments that the physical stance is privileged after all, because the design and intentional stances are in principle dispensable. The point that "compressible" only means anything relative to a chosen description language, so the whole thing slides into relativism. And tell me what a defender would happily concede versus have to bite off, plus whether the view has quietly made itself unfalsifiable — and if so, whether thats actually a problem for a claim at this level or just what a framework looks like.
