The setting: a 90-minute session in a master's-level seminar on rational choice, sixteen students, roughly half from economics and half from philosophy. They have all seen the standard urn comparison in a lecture — a known split against an unknown one, a bet on each, the usual pattern of preferences and the usual talk about ambiguity aversion. So the stock demonstration is dead on arrival. I need an activity that takes their familiarity as its starting point and then puts pressure on it. No real money; tokens, points, or a single end-of-session prize are fine, but nothing requiring cash handling. No computers: paper, index cards, and a projector.

What they should come away feeling, in order of priority. First, and essentially: that the disposition they have been calling ambiguity aversion is not a taste for the unknown, nor merely a preference for information, but the same disposition that governs their attitude toward an ordinary two-stage lottery whose first stage is perfectly known and perfectly fair. Concretely, a bet whose colour is settled by a fair coin choosing between two urns of identical, fully known composition is payoff-identical in every respect to a bet on either urn alone; anyone who prefers the one-stage bet is rejecting a reduction principle they would have endorsed on paper half an hour earlier. I want each student to locate their own two answers and see that they are one trait rather than two quirks, before anyone explains it. Second, if it fits without crowding the first: I would like them to find that their attitude does not shift when the unknown composition is presented as a fact somebody already knows versus a fact not yet settled at the time they bet. Build that in as a single extra condition if you can; if it would compromise the primary reveal, drop it and tell me why.

Deliver the design in full: rules, materials, payoff structure, a minute-by-minute timeline, what the facilitator does and says at each point — and, just as important, what the facilitator must not say — the order in which choices are elicited so that the reveal emerges from the students' own answers rather than an announcement, and how responses are collected and displayed so that the pattern becomes visible to the room. Then the debrief: map each moment onto the idea it conveys, separate what the activity shows from what it merely suggests, and be explicit about which feature of the idea the activity cannot reproduce and the facilitator must supply in words — for instance, whether the aversion is defensible, or whether the urn case is about ignorance at all. Finish with what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it: the likely confusions, the ways the payoff structure could make the wrong choices attractive for the wrong reasons, the students who will short-circuit the reveal by naming it early, and how to handle each. Be frank about which of these you cannot design around.
