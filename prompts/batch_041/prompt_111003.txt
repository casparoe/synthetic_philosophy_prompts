I sit on the nine-member early-release board of our state department of corrections, which meets twice a month and hears roughly six cases a session; I have to circulate a written opinion before Thursday, when the second item on the docket is the application of a prisoner I will call R.M. He is four years into a nine-year sentence for a series of burglaries and has applied under a statute the legislature passed two years ago. As written, the provision directs the board to release any eligible applicant who, at the time of the hearing, sincerely and wholeheartedly intends to complete a certified twelve-month substance-abuse program. The same provision states that the program is voluntary, that release is unconditional once granted, and that the board may not attach any condition, bond, reporting requirement, or supervision to the release. The bill's sponsor argued on the floor that the measure would test whether a person can be moved by a promise rather than a payoff, a remark that has since been cited at two of our hearings, both times by members arguing for denial.

R.M.'s file is genuinely difficult. He completed a ninety-day in-prison program with strong reports, and the psychologist who evaluated him describes his stated intention as sincere as far as such things can be assessed, while observing that every reason he gives for wanting to finish the program is something that release already delivers. Our counsel has circulated a memo arguing that the statute asks the board to do something incoherent: because the benefit is conferred at the very moment the intention is formed, nothing in R.M.'s post-release situation would give him any further reason to attend the program, and a person who sees that clearly cannot sincerely form the intention we are told to reward. Two members find this decisive and want to deny. Two want to grant, on the ground that people routinely keep resolutions after the reason for making them has lapsed, that this is simply what a promise to oneself is, and that denying R.M. release for a defect in the legislature's drafting punishes him for the state's confusion. A third group, led by a colleague whose judgment I respect, proposes reading the statute as impliedly permitting a self-imposed forfeitable bond, under which R.M. would post a sum of his own money that he forfeits if he leaves the program; she argues that this is the only construction that makes the scheme coherent, and that without it the board is merely deciding whether it likes R.M.'s face.

Please draft the written opinion I will circulate before Thursday's hearing. I need a concrete disposition of R.M.'s application, a position on whether the board should adopt the bond reading and whether doing so exceeds our authority under the statute, and a candid treatment of the strongest argument for whichever side I end up rejecting, since I will have to defend the opinion to colleagues who hold the other view and, if we deny, to the court of appeals, which has not yet construed this provision. Keep it to something I can read aloud in ten minutes. I am not looking for a survey of the literature on intention or a list of readings; I want the reasoning and a bottom line the board can act on.
