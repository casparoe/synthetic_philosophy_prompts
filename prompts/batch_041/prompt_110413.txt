Bit of background: I never studied philosophy properly, I just read alot on my own and I run a small study circle — about ten of us, mostly non-philosophers, a nurse, a physio, a couple of teachers — where we take turns setting and marking essays out of 20. Last block was on health and disease: the view that being ill is just your body running below the normal spec for the species, versus the view that "disease" is at least partly a value judgement about what's bad or unwanted. I've been obssessed with this for weeks. I get the shape of the disagreement fine, but every essay we wrote turned into a summary — everyone can say "the naturalist thinks this, the normativist thinks that" and then trail off. So for this block's final I want one essay question that you can't get through that way, aimed squarely at the thing I genuinely can't decide.

The thing I can't decide is this made-up case, and I want the question built around it. All humans have a quirk in how our joints form. Nothing is broken — the mechanism does exactly what it's built to do, and back when we lived the way we lived for most of our history it cost us nothing. Put us on hard floors and chairs and long commutes and by 45 the hips are wrecked: constant pain, can't work, some people end up in wheelchairs. Nearly everyone has the quirk, so it's as typical as having two arms. And there's now a cheap, safe pill you take from childhood that fixes it completely — in fact it leaves your joints better than any human joint has ever been, ancestral or modern. So: is the quirk a disease? Both of the usual naturalist tests (it's statistically normal, and it's the design working as designed) seem to say no, the harm seems to say yes, and the pill muddies whether giving it is treating something or just improving someone. My own half-baked fix was to say health is what you'd have in the environment we evolved in — a counterfactual baseline instead of a statistical one — but I can't tell if that's a real rescue or just a value judgement wearing a lab coat, and I want the question to make students say which it is and what that does to the two positions.

So: write me the exam question (put the case in the question itself, in plain language, so people can just read it — it's invented, so don't worry about whether it's realistic or whether anyone has written about something like it, and don't build the question on any particular author or paper, since we're offline here and nobody has notes), then a model answer of at most 600 words, then a marking scheme of five must-hit points worth 4 marks each, 20 total, each point with a one-line note on how people usually miss it. Assume 45 minutes, no notes, and make the model answer commit to a verdict rather than hedge. The scheme should make it obvious what a genuinely good answer has that a middling one doesn't, and if someone can score full marks just by defining the two positions cleanly, I've written a bad question. Keep the whole reply under about 1,100 words.
