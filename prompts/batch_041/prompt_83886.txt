I write a column on philanthropy and ideas, and I've spent the past few weeks embedded with a grantmaking outfit that does something unusual: it publishes the rubric by which it chooses among candidate causes. The rubric scores each cause on three axes — importance, understood as how much is at stake if the cause goes well or badly; tractability, understood as how much progress a marginal dollar buys; and neglectedness, understood as how thinly the cause is currently resourced — multiplies the three together into a single number, and then applies a fourth multiplier that the staff call comparative advantage, meant to capture whether this particular team, with its particular skills and networks, is unusually well placed to help. The staff are proud of the first three factors precisely because they take them to be objective: they are supposed to describe which causes matter most, not which causes this funder happens to favor. The longer I look at the rubric, though, the more the neglectedness term bothers me, and I have come to suspect that the comparative-advantage multiplier is less an addition to the framework than a confession about it.

I would like you to argue for a specific claim: that neglectedness, as it actually functions in frameworks of this kind, is not an independent dimension of cause quality at all, but a disguised estimate of the marginal value of the next unit of resources — and that because marginal value is always marginal for some actor with some alternative, neglectedness quietly imports agent-relative considerations into what is advertised as an agent-neutral ranking. On this view, the honest version of the rubric would drop neglectedness and ask a single question: what is the expected value of the next unit of resources, given what this actor would otherwise do with it? Argue that position as forcefully as you can. In the course of doing so, please do two things. First, identify a concrete pair of causes — real or invented, but described in enough detail to be judged — where the three-factor rubric ranks them one way and a direct marginal-value estimate ranks them the other, and say what this reveals about the neglectedness term. Second, explain what work, if any, neglectedness is doing that importance and tractability could not do on their own. Finally, tell me what would change your mind: which consideration, if it turned out to hold, would push you back toward the view that neglectedness is a genuine and agent-neutral dimension after all, and how confident you are that no such consideration holds.
