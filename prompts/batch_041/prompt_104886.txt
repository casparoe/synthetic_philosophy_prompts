I am preparing an application for a one-year research fellowship and need a proposal of roughly 2,500 to 3,000 words, together with a month-by-month work plan. I have a half-formed idea about Swiss direct democracy that I would like turned into a defensible project, and I would like you to write the proposal.

The idea concerns one of the less-discussed instruments of the Swiss system: the direct counter-proposal to a popular initiative. When a popular initiative reaches the ballot, the Federal Assembly may place its own competing constitutional amendment on the same subject alongside it. Voters then answer not one question but three: whether to accept the initiative, whether to accept the counter-proposal, and, if both are accepted, a tie-break question (the Stichfrage) asking which of the two should take precedence. A voter may therefore say yes to both proposals — a double yes — while using the tie-break to rank them against each other. If the tie-break produces a split result, with one proposal ahead on the popular vote and the other ahead on the cantonal vote, the winner is the proposal whose percentage share of the popular tie-break votes plus its percentage share of the cantonal tie-break votes is the larger sum.

My half-formed hypothesis is this. The tie-break question is the only point in the entire federal procedure at which a voter registers a comparative, ordinal judgment rather than a flat yes or no, and it is plausibly defended as a way of letting strength of preference enter an otherwise binary, majoritarian system without abandoning one person, one vote. I want to argue that this reading is both attractive and unstable. It is attractive because it appears to give preference intensity a formally clean home inside direct democracy. It is unstable because a voter who ranks both proposals above the status quo has an incentive to use the tie-break strategically — for instance, to throw weight behind whichever proposal they judge less likely to prevail on its own — so the aggregate tie-break result may track coordination among voters rather than the strength of anyone's attachment. If that is right, the instrument may measure something other than what its defenders believe it measures, and possibly something less normatively useful than intensity.

Please turn this into a proposal. In particular:

State the research question so that it has a determinate answer — something on the order of whether the tie-break question admits a coherent interpretation as a measure of preference intensity, and under what conditions its aggregate output actually tracks the quantity it is supposed to represent — rather than a vague invitation to examine the Swiss system.

Explain why the existing treatments leave the question open. My impression is that the standard defenses and criticisms of direct democracy turn on agenda-setting, turnout, campaign finance, or the bindingness of outcomes, and that none of them settles what the tie-break question is doing when it works as intended.

Lay out the approach and justify it against the obvious alternatives. I expect the project to be conceptual and formal rather than archival: model the three-question ballot as a social-choice rule; isolate the assumptions about voter psychology and about the aggregation of intensity that the intensity reading requires; test the mechanism against the usual desiderata, such as sincerity-proofness, respect for both the popular and the cantonal majorities, and resistance to manipulation; and compare it with the natural rivals — a single ranked ballot, a two-round runoff, and a Condorcet-style pairwise method — saying why the comparison is the right way into the problem rather than simply asserting that the Swiss design is superior or inferior.

Say what a result would look like and what would count as the project failing. I would rather have an honest account of what would falsify the hypothesis than a plan that can only succeed.

Give a realistic scope for a single year, flagging which component I should cut first if the argument grows beyond its container.
