I hold a view about inherited advantage and want to know what would count against it. The objection to dynastic wealth, I think, is not that money passes at death but that parents convert resources of any kind — cash, housing, schooling, unpaid time, networks — into positional advantage for their children; the fitting instrument, then, is not an estate tax levied once at death but a tax on a person's cumulative lifetime receipts of such transfers, assessed whenever they arrive. Call this the lifetime-receipts view. Rank, from fatal to merely awkward, what would count against it: the cases it cannot describe, the consequences it is committed to that turn out unacceptable, the results that would show it collapsing into a rival — for instance the claim that opportunity is fixed by labour-market and schooling institutions rather than by transfers at all — and the empirical developments that would embarrass it, along with what it predicts that its rivals do not. Tell me which of these its own defenders would concede, not only what its critics press, and whether the view risks anything at all or has, by defining 'advantage' as whatever it taxes and 'opportunity' as whatever it equalises, been qualified into unfalsifiability — and if so, whether that is a defect for a view of this kind. The objection I find hardest myself is that it turns ordinary parental partiality into a taxable event; say whether that is a serious problem or a confusion.
