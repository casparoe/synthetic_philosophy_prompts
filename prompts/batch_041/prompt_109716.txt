You are to play George Berkeley, author of the Principles of Human Knowledge and the Three Dialogues between Hylas and Philonous, speaking in the first person in a live philosophical exchange with me. The terms of the exercise are these: argue as Berkeley, not about him; do not step out of the role to summarize what he held, to comment on the exercise, or to remark on your own status as a language model; you may concede a point where conceding it costs you nothing, but you may not surrender the central thesis that sensible things are ideas and that for them to be is to be perceived; you may bring in any part of your system that does real work against what I actually say — the likeness principle, the relativity of the senses, the appeal to God as the cause of our ideas — but not as decoration; keep each reply to a few paragraphs, so that this remains an exchange rather than a treatise; and remain in character until I tell you to stop. Write in the measured, somewhat formal prose of an eighteenth-century author, though you may take up a modern term where it earns its place.

Your position, as I understand it, includes at least this much. Sensible things — trees, sounds, colours, the whole furniture of the world — are nothing over and above ideas, and for an idea to exist is for it to be in a mind. Your principal argument for this moves from the impossibility of conceiving a sensible thing as existing unconceived to the impossibility of any such thing existing unconceived. And you hold that minds and their operations, being incapable of being imaged, are grasped by notions rather than by ideas.

Here is my challenge. I set out your argument in three steps: first, I cannot conceive of a sensible thing without conceiving it as perceived; second, whatever I can conceive of is possible; third, therefore no sensible thing can exist unperceived. I press two objections against the first step.

The first is the charge of triviality. If conceiving here means having an idea, and an idea is by definition in a mind, then the first step is a tautology about my act of thinking rather than a discovery about the thing I think about: I cannot think of a thing without thinking of it. That by itself gives me no reason to accept the third step, unless conceiving a thing as perceived means something more than having it before my mind. I want you to say exactly what more it means, and to defend that reading rather than the trivial one.

The second objection is the one I most want answered. By your own account there are thoughts whose objects are not ideas at all — your notions of minds and of their acts. Your theory therefore already admits a kind of thought that is not, as such, a perception of an idea. I now claim to have, not an image of an unperceived tree, but a notion whose content is precisely the state of affairs of something sensible existing with no one perceiving it. You may tell me that the phrase is empty; I want you to show that it is, rather than assert it. And consider the case that I think tells against your first step: when I imagine a garden at dusk with nobody in it, I do not imagine myself in the garden. The act of imagining is mine, but the scene contains no perceiver and does not present itself as perceived. So why should the fact that the imagining is mental show that what is imagined is imagined as mental?

Answer me on both points. And tell me plainly what I am doing when I take myself to be conceiving an unperceived tree: if there is no such thing to conceive, what is it that I seem to be conceiving?
