I'm a novelist, and I've been reading my way through Aristotle's Politics and the Ethics for a book I'm working on, and last week two friends of mine got into a fight about it at my kitchen table that I can't stop thinking about.

Sam has been reading the Politics, Book I, and came in hot with the claim that Aristotle's whole attack on money-lending is incoherent. His argument: Aristotle says money is a thing we invented by agreement, that it's not natural but conventional, and that its value can be changed or abolished at will. Fine. But then he turns around and calls interest unnatural, and says the money-lender is breeding money from money, which he treats as a kind of perversion. Sam says you can't have it both ways. If money is just a token we made up to make exchange easier, it has no nature to violate, and calling a profit on it unnatural is just Aristotle's aristocratic squeamishness dressed up as metaphysics. He added that Aristotle even admits money's value fluctuates, which is exactly what you'd expect if it were purely conventional.

Rosa pushed back. She said Sam is confusing the token with the function. The convention, for Aristotle, is what we use as the standard, not what the standard is measuring. What money measures is need, or demand, and demand is not made up. Money was introduced to solve a real problem: unlike things, a house and a pair of shoes, can't be directly compared, so we need some common measure to make exchange possible without turning every trade into a haggle. Rosa said interest is unnatural not because money is sacred but because the lender treats the measure as if it were a good to be increased on its own, which breaks the function. She said Aristotle's example of the shoe having two uses, one proper and one for exchange, shows he's fine with exchange as such, just not with a use that consumes the point of the thing.

Sam said that's still a slide. Even if demand is real, the inference from money was introduced to do X to any particular use of money is against nature doesn't go through. A thing can have a function and still be used for other things. And he said Rosa's reading makes Aristotle sound like he thinks every profit on a loan is a perversion of a metaphysical purpose, which is a much weirder view than just saying he disliked usury. He also pointed out that the shoemaker in Aristotle's own example exchanges shoes for a house, and if that exchange is natural, the profit motive seems to be doing the same kind of work in both cases.

Rosa said no, the difference is that the shoemaker makes something, and the money-lender just rearranges claims on what other people made. Sam said that's a distinction between productive and unproductive labor, not a distinction about nature, and Aristotle doesn't have a clean version of it.

They went around like that for an hour and neither of them would move. I don't know the scholarship well enough to referee, and I don't want a reading list. Can you go through their exchange move by move and say who's right about what, where each of them is reading Aristotle correctly and where they're reading in something that isn't there, and what both of them are missing about the money-and-exchange stuff in the Politics and the Ethics? I'm less interested in who wins overall than in which specific claims hold up.
