I have been trying to determine what I believe about reference classes by attending to how I actually judge particular cases, and I want to know what principle, if any, my judgments express. The cases, with my verdicts:

1. A fair coin has just been tossed and covered. The chance that it is heads is one half. The narrowest class containing this toss is the class whose only member it is, and the frequency of heads in that class is zero or one. So either I am not following a narrowest-class rule, or something is overriding it.

2. A screening test comes back positive, and I take the relevant probability to be the rate of the disease among those who test positive — a class fixed by an effect of the very condition I am asking about. If I know more about myself, I narrow further, provided the data exist; if the narrower figure is unavailable, I stay where I am. Learning more moves me to narrower classes until the data run out.

3. A drug helps in a trial overall but not in patients with a particular genotype. That subgroup is small and the confidence interval wide, and I still take my chance of benefit to be the subgroup rate, because the genotype is known to affect how the drug is metabolized.

4. In another trial, someone has divided the patients twelve ways and found a twelve-patient subgroup in which the drug appears harmful, at a rate of one in four. I ignore it and use the trial-wide rate. Nothing singled out those twelve patients except that their outcome looked interesting.

5. I want to know whether a new antibiotic is dangerous, and I learn that seventy percent of the patients who died last year had been given it. I conclude nothing. The class is fixed by the outcome I am trying to predict, and the patients given the new drug were the sicker ones.

6. Among the patients on my ward, the non-smokers are likelier to have dementia than the smokers. I do not lower my estimate of smoking's contribution to dementia. Being on the ward is caused both by the illnesses that smoking brings and by dementia.

7. For the coin I say one half; for whether my brother will repay a loan I use what I know of my brother rather than the base rate among brothers who borrow. I take both answers to be correct, not merely useful.

Two principles have been proposed to me, and each is refuted by one of these cases. The first is that the right class is the one that screens off everything else I know: conditional on membership in it, the outcome is independent of the rest of my information. On the coin, the singleton class screens off everything, there being nothing else to screen off, and it yields zero or one; and the usual repair — that the class must be one there could be statistics about — does not help with case 4, which is a class with statistics. The second is that the class should be fixed by the causes of the outcome, everything upstream and nothing downstream, but the test result in case 2 is downstream.

What I want is the principle, or the small set of candidates, that captures these seven verdicts, stated clause by clause with the verdict that motivates each clause; an honest statement of which of the verdicts it cannot accommodate and of what the principle becomes if I abandon them; and its verdicts on three cases I have not put to you: a trial in which the drug is better overall but worse in my genotype subgroup with no known mechanism; case 5 again, with the exposure rate among the survivors supplied and equal to the rate among those who died; and a class fixed by an effect of the outcome that is neither the outcome itself nor a common effect of other things, such as patients whose illness happened to be caught early. Finally, tell me whether the principle that survives treats case 7's two answers as both correct or only as both reasonable to act on.
