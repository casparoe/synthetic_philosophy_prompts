I'm assembling materials for a graduate seminar on prediction and the social sciences, and I want to put Asimov's original Foundation trilogy next to Popper's case against historicism. My students will already know the books, so you needn't summarize them: Seldon's psychohistory, which deals in human masses statistically and deliberately leaves individual lives out of the calculation; the Seldon Plan and its recorded appearances at each crisis; the Foundation's survival on Terminus by way of religion and then trade; the fall of Trantor and the long imperial decline; and, at the hinge of the whole sequence, the Mule, the single figure the mathematics did not contain, whose conquest derails the schedule and whose defeat requires the hidden intervention of the Second Foundation. What I want from you is a sustained reading of the trilogy through the argument of Popper's The Poverty of Historicism and his related essays on the limits of social prediction — the claim that large-scale historical prophecy mistakes a trend for a law, that the future course of history turns on the growth of knowledge and that we cannot anticipate what we shall only come to know later, that a prediction can itself alter the event predicted (the Oedipus effect), and that explaining social outcomes as the execution of somebody's deliberate plan is a primitive and dangerous picture, one Popper treats as a secularized theology. Keep in view, too, his positive alternative: piecemeal social engineering against utopian engineering, and the logic of the situation by which he thinks we can make sense of historical action without invoking laws of development.

Where does the trilogy dramatize the historicist temptation, and where does it actually refute Popper? The Mule looks at first like a gift to his side — a singular, unrepeatable novelty that invalidates the prediction and forces the plan's guardians to improvise. But I don't want a reading that simply scores the novel for our team. Press the other way as well: the Second Foundation's clandestine steering of events is a conspiracy theory of society made true, and the confidence that the schedule can be restored — that Seldon was right even about the interruptions, and that his successors can quietly put history back on its rails — is a fiction in which history really does have a lawlike shape after all; and the fact that the Plan is hidden from the Foundationers themselves, working behind their backs while they are ignorant of the script, is not by itself a refutation of it. Then tell me honestly where the Popperian reading distorts the books, and where the honest critic has to concede that Asimov is writing a romance of science rather than a treatise, and that psychohistory may be doing narrative and emotional work — the pleasure of learning one's place in a comprehensible design — rather than doctrinal work. Along the way I'd like precision on three points: what exactly separates a trend from a law, and what psychohistory would have to be for it to count as a law of history in Popper's rejected sense; whether the Oedipus effect is ever genuinely staged in the trilogy (the secrecy surrounding the Plan, the Foundationers' ignorance of their own crisis) or is simply assumed away by the fiction; and what the books contribute to this debate that the argument alone does not — why historicist prophecy is so seductive, and what it costs to believe it. No quotations or page references, please; I care about the argument and the reading, not the citations.

End with a final line beginning "Verdict:" — one sentence stating whether the trilogy, taken as a whole, supports or undermines the thesis that history admits of the lawlike prediction Popper denies.
