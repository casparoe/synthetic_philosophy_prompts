Right, slightly annoying request incoming.

I read LessWrong / SSC / ACX and the rest of it far too much, and I've somehow ended up as the person who writes the long Stoicism post for our local meetup blog every year. This year I want to do one of those which-position-do-you-actually-hold things, but for the dichotomy of control, and I want it properly in the weeds rather than the usual mush.

Context in case it helps you see what I'm after: when people say focus on what you can control, they can mean at least five different things and nobody notices they're talking past each other. The classical Stoic split, as I read it, is between things that are up to us (eph' hemin) — basically our judgements, our assent, our impulses and desires — and everything else, including our own bodies, our reputations, and how our actions turn out. Then there's a weaker modern version where control means influence or degree of causal contribution, so an outcome can be partly up to you; and then there's the trichotomy you see around, sorting things into full control, partial control, none. Those all look like the same doctrine with different graphs, but I think they're actually different claims about what makes something mine in the first place. There's also the reading where the whole thing isn't a thesis about causation at all, but a rule about where to put your attention and your care, with the metaphysics deliberately left open.

So what I want is a decision tree, or a taxonomy if that fits better, for the blog post.

Requirements, roughly:

- Every branch node is a question an ordinary reader can answer without knowing any Stoic jargon and without being able to tell which leaf it leads to. Concrete cases, not do you think virtue is the only good.
- Build it from the logical space first — which combinations of answers are even coherent — and only then slot the actual positions into it, so I can see the empty slots.
- Leaves I definately want, or at least the claims they make: the strict classical reading (only assent and judgement are up to us, outcomes never are); the influence reading (up to us comes in degrees and tracks causal contribution); the attention-and-concern reading (control is a filter on what you care about, not a fact about causation); a compatibilist reading where assent is fully caused by your character plus the impression and is still up to us, because up to us means attributable to me rather than uncaused; a therapy-not-a-thesis reading where the doctrine is a training device you aren't meant to assert; the fatalist degenerate case where nothing you do changes anything so effort is pointless (everyone insists this isn't Stoicism, the text keeps getting read that way, I want it in as a leaf anyway); and the airport self-help version. If two of those turn out to be the same position wearing different coats, say so rather than pretending they're distinct.
- Cases I want the questions to run on: an archer who does everything right and misses; a leg being moved by someone else, or your values being edited mid-deliberation; a case where a huge effort moves a good outcome from 1% to 2%; whether to be upset about an election result; a chronic illness you can't improve; and, because I want to make the AI crowd at the meetup squirm, whether a model that reliably produces helpful answers has anything up to it, and what each leaf is forced to say about that.
- Each leaf gets a name, one line on the most awkward commitment it has to swallow, and a flag if the tree can't really seperate it from another leaf.
- Diagnose the unoccupied combinations: is nobody there because the view is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored?
- Cap it at eight or nine leaves, keep the whole thing under about 1200 words, and write it for people who've read the Sequences and no ancient philosophy, so assume they're fine with a technical distinction as long as you motivate it with a case first.
- No reading lists or citations, please — I'll do the references myself and I don't want to be fact-checking a bibliography in my own blog post. (I'm not a classicist either, so if I've mangled the historical bit, one line at the end is fine, but don't let that take over.)

Cheers. If the tree forces me to admit I'm secretly a fatalist, so be it.
