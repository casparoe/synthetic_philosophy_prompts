I have been puzzling over something on my own, without any training in this area, and I would rather you build me a worked example than explain the general problem in the abstract.

The puzzle is that I seem unable to hold three things at once. The first is that if every member of a group prefers one option to another, the group as a whole ought to prefer it too; it strikes me as absurd for a group to rank two options in the opposite order from all of its members. The second is that some choices are nobody else's business, so on those the person concerned should get their way no matter what the rest of the group wants. The third is that the group's overall ranking should at least be coherent: if it puts A above B and B above C, it should not then put C above A. Whenever I try to keep all three, something gives, and I want to see the smallest concrete case that makes this happen, spelled out completely.

Please construct that case to the following specifications.

- Use the smallest number of people and the smallest number of options that can possibly produce the collision, and state plainly why one fewer of either could not do the job.
- Keep the setting mundane and realistic — a household, a shared office, a small club. No fantastical devices, and no appeals to ideal observers or perfect reasoners.
- Make the protected personal choices genuinely small and private.
- The person who overrides someone else's private choice should be acting from an ordinary, decent-seeming concern rather than spite or a taste for meddling as such; I want to know whether the problem needs anyone to be nasty.

Present the scenario itself as a single short paragraph. Then list every person's full ranking of the options, from most to least preferred. Then do the check: show, step by step, which of the three requirements forces each link in the chain, and where exactly they collide.

After that, I want four things. First, identify the single ingredient whose removal or alteration makes the collision disappear, and say whether that fix is honest or whether it quietly abandons one of the three requirements. Second, give me the strongest case a defender of each requirement could make that one of the other two is really to blame — each complaint at full strength, not a strawman. Third, tell me whether the collision you built survives if everyone cares only about their own private business and nothing at all about anyone else's, and what your answer shows about whether the trouble is really caused by busybodies. Fourth, say what would have to be true of people's preferences, in ordinary life, for the collision to be a rare curiosity rather than a standing feature of group decisions.
