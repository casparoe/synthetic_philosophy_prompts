I am a fourth-year PhD student in bioethics, and for the past three years I have taught the death-and-dying unit in the paramedic program at our affiliated medical school. The program has asked me to deliver the address at this year's pinning ceremony, and I would like you to write it.

The room: about sixty graduating paramedics, their families, the faculty, and — the program now invites one each year — the family of a recent organ donor. Within a few months, some of these graduates will be the person standing in a stranger's living room at four in the morning who has to conclude that the resuscitation is over and that the patient is dead. Several have already had to do it as students, in the field or in the emergency department.

What I want the address to do is treat the determination of death as the graduates' working problem rather than as a seminar puzzle. They are entering a profession that runs on two standards at once. Under neurological criteria, a patient on a ventilator can be dead while warm and perfused, with a heart the transplant team will want; under circulatory criteria, the determination is made after a period of absent circulation short enough that the organs are still usable, which is why the relevant protocols specify minutes rather than hours. The two standards have to coexist in one hospital, on one shift, sometimes inside one family's week, and in both cases a person has to be declared dead while the body is still doing several of the things we associate with being alive. I want the address to be honest about that — that this is a determination made under a standard, not an observation — without stripping the graduates of the authority they need to say the sentence out loud and mean it. I have sat in that emergency department while a resident told a family their mother was dead and then, ten minutes later, called the transplant coordinator about a heart that was still beating; that scene is the reason I want this address to exist.

Two questions from my students should be its spine. From a student who watched a donation: if his heart was still beating when they took the organs, in what sense was he dead before they started? From a student who did a field termination: when we stop because the protocol says we may, have we decided that he is dead, or only that we are done trying? I answered both badly in class. The address should answer them in a way that a new graduate on a night shift can actually use.

Constraints. No citations, no names of scholars or landmark cases, no reading list, and no figures you would have to look up: you may refer to the waiting period that circulatory protocols require, but do not assert a particular number of minutes as though it were universal. Do not resolve whether neurological criteria are sound; I have colleagues who think they are not, and the donor's family will be three rows back, so the abstract version of that argument would be cruel in that room. Do not let the address become a lecture, and do not let the philosophy sit on top of the speech as decoration — it should be doing the work of deciding what can and cannot be said. No jokes about death, no religious consolation (the room is not religious and neither am I), no headings or bullet points; it has to be speakable aloud. Roughly one thousand words, ending with something the graduates can carry into a shift rather than with a conclusion about the metaphysics.
