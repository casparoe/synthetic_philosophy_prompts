Ok, phil of mind PhD student here, writing a chapter that touches Molyneux, and I keep running into sensory substitution stuff. You know the drill: a camera feeds a grid of electrodes on someone's tongue or back, or converts the image into a soundscape, and after weeks or months of training a blind user can reach out and grab a cup, tell a square from a triangle, dodge obstacles. Everyone describes the result as the person now seeing, or seeing with their tongue, and I think that description is smuggling in a whole theory nobody bothers to argue for.

So what does the practice presuppose? Does building and using one of these devices require that there be a shared spatial content -- the same square, the same layout -- that can ride on any channel, so that shape content is amodal and what makes something a case of seeing is the information it carries rather than the organ carrying it? Or is the weaker claim enough, that there's just an isomorphism between the stimulation pattern and the layout, which is compatible with the user never perceiving anything visual at all? And here's what bugs me: the months of training look like the empiricist answer to Molyneux, while the its-basically-seeing framing looks like the nativist one. Do those have to conflict, or is one of them just packaging?

I want the implicit theory reconstructed: what the practice strictly presupposes vs what it merely fits nicely with, which rival views of perceptual content it quietly rules out, and what would actually have to change in how the devices are built or pitched if you adopted a rival -- say, modality-specific content and no shared square. Keep it tight, I don't need a lit review.
