Right, so I'm a third-year PhD student and my methods chapter has metastasized into a sort of confession of faith, and I want a consistency check on it before my supervisor sees the thing. Here's roughly where I've landed about archives and what you can get out of them:

1. An archive isn't a window, it's an instrument. What gets written down, and what survives, tracks who had an intrest in recording, who had the staff and the shelves, and what the institution was trying to do to the people it recorded.
2. The interesting part is the silence. When a whole category of people only ever shows up as a tally in a column, that absence is itself a historical fact, and often a more revealing one than the entries are.
3. A silence only counts as evidence if I can name the mechanism — if I can say what would have had to be different for the record to exist. No mechanism, no silence; that's just a gap, and gaps are missing paper. I've been calling this the mechanism test in my notes.
4. Reading against the grain is legitimate. I can take a record written to manage people and use it to say things about the people being managed, including things the clerk would have denied.
5. I can't smuggle my own categories in. If the sources have no word for the thing I'm studying, the burden is on me to show I've found a phenomenon and not invented one.
6. And I don't think there's any getting outside it. There's no ground truth to check the archive against, just more documents, mine included.
