Write a parody of a municipal agency's FAQ page — the flat, courteous, faintly defensive document a city posts to explain a decision nobody likes — for the fictional Office of Contractualist Review, explaining why the long-planned Meridian Avenue light-rail extension has been canceled. The office administers a charter provision under which a policy may be adopted only if it can be justified to each affected resident individually, and which explicitly forbids "netting" — that is, offsetting the burden borne by one person against the benefits enjoyed by others. Aim for about 650 words, formatted as a numbered list of questions and answers, in American English.

The humor has to come from the philosophy rather than from bureaucratic slapstick. Contractualism, in the sense at issue, holds that an act or policy is wrong when it could not be licensed by any general principles that no one could reasonably reject — where the rejection is assessed person by person rather than in the aggregate, so that justification is owed to each individual rather than to a total. The difficulty this creates is the aggregation problem: how to handle a case in which a great many people each have a small complaint and one person has a large one, or in which a single resident's objection seems to have no principled limit. Let the FAQ get this machinery right — the difference between the strength of one person's complaint and the sum of everyone's gains, the refusal to let numbers do the work of argument, and the genuinely open question of whether the number of people who object, or who benefit, can ever matter, and if so how.

Let the office repeatedly discover that it has no principled place to stop: the resident who objects over noise, the one who objects over construction dust, the one who objects on principle to the very practice of measuring complaints, and the one who objects to the office's own existence. Include at least one question from a frustrated engineer asking why forty thousand projected riders do not simply outweigh one objector. The agency's answer should be honest that this is the hardest question for the framework, not something it can dispatch with a tidy reply. Do not let the piece smuggle in a solution, and do not let it suggest that anyone who takes the view seriously is merely confused. The target is the aggregation problem itself. A reader who knows the literature should find the objections familiar and fairly stated; a reader who does not should still be able to tell what is being mocked. Any quoted testimony should be attributed to invented residents, not to real people.
