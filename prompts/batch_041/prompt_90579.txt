I'm running a session for a medical humanities reading group next month — mostly clinicians, two anthropoligists, and nobody who's read much philosophy of medicine — and I want a one-page taxonomy they can actually argue over. It's the old question, but I dont want the usual three-name lineup: is being diseased a fact about a body, or a verdict about a life?

Build it as a decision tree, root to leaves, where each branch is a question somebody could answer without already knowing which position it leads to. Root it here: the exact same bodily state in two people, one devastated by it and one who couldn't care less — can they differ in whether they're diseased? Then make the tree do this work:

- Whether there's a value-free description of the state at all — can we say what a body is supposed to do without already deciding what's worth doing?
- If there is one, what fixes the "supposed to": what the part was selected to do, what's statistically typical, or something else? And if it's statistical, who picks the reference class — species, sex, age, occupation, population — and what happens when the reference class is itself a social product? That's where I want the tree to get uncomfortable.
- Whether the concept has to earn its keep: is "disease" meant to be a natural kind, or a practical kind defined by what we want it for (relieving suffering, allocating scarce care, excusing people from obligations, funding research)?
- Whether health is just the absence of disease or a positive state — capacity, resilience, adaptability.

Leaves I want to see: a hard naturalism where values never enter; a hybrid where there's a real dysfunction plus a value-laden harm requirement; a hard normativism where "disease" is a myth or a tool of control; a soft normativism or pragmatic pluralism; an eliminativism that wants thinner terms like injury or harm; and a positive-health view where disease is a failure of capacity. Add unnamed leaves wherever a combination of answers is coherent but nobody seems to hold it, and for each one say whether it's incoherent, unattractive, or just unexplored.

Every branch has to be answerable from a case, so use these and add others if you need them: near-sightedness; male-pattern baldness; a painless condition that's 80 percent heritable and shaves ten years off life expectancy; a state with no distress and no functional limitation whatsoever; a condition that everyone in one population has after forty and treats as ordinary aging, which one visiting doctor calls a disease; a condition defined entirely by what patients report, with no measurable marker; and a state that used to be classed as a disease and no longer is, or the reverse.

Flag any two leaves your tree can't actually separate — I suspect the hybrid and the soft normativism are going to be hard to pry apart, so say so instead of papering over it. No citations or reading lists, this isn't homework. Start the reply with the tree itself as an indented outline (branches as questions, leaves as positions with a one-line description each), then the commentary after it. Keep the whole thing under 900 words. American English, please.
