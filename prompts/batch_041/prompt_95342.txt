I chair the workgroup that maintains the diagnostic classification our health system uses, and every cycle we get a handful of proposals — to add a diagnosis, split one, retire one, or replace a category with a dimensional score — each backed by clinicians, researchers, or patient advocates. We currently decide by vote and by who argues hardest, and I want to replace that with a decision procedure grounded in what philosophy of science actually says about natural kinds and classification: the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops with a no, and where it hands the decision back to a person because the question is one of values rather than facts. The procedure has to distinguish a category that tracks a real causal structure — a cluster of properties that hang together and support prediction and intervention — from one that is merely a useful administrative convenience; it has to say when a dimension would serve better than a kind; and it has to handle the awkward case where classifying people changes the people being classified, so that the category's own membership shifts over time. Assume my members can follow an argument but have no training in philosophy, that we can spend at most a day on any single proposal, and that I will have to defend each outcome to the groups whose proposals we reject. Then run the procedure on three proposals: adding a diagnosis for prolonged grief after bereavement, splitting a single broad diagnosis of schizophrenia into several, and retiring a long-standing category that has become a catch-all. Include at least one case where the procedure returns an answer I will not like, and be explicit about what it cannot settle.
