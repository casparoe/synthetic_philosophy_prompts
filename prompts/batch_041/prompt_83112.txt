I have been reading rationalist blogs and forums for about fifteen years, and the argument I keep returning to is the one about honesty in ordinary conversation — the one that begins with the case against lying and always ends in a dispute over whether telling your host the soup was wonderful is a small crime or a courtesy. My friend Ines has the most settled views on this of anyone I know. She grew up in Amsterdam, spent eight years in Osaka, and now works as an interpreter and cross-cultural consultant, so she has had more occasions than most people to think about the difference between being blunt and being honest, and about what indirect speech does to the person on the other end of it.

Over the years I have collected her verdicts on particular episodes. She would tell you that her rule is "never assert what you don't believe," and she is fond of saying that "the hearer is not a fool and the speaker is not a nanny," but neither line predicts the list below, and I have never been able to get her to state the rule she is actually running.

1. A team lead in Amsterdam tells a junior, in front of the whole team, that her draft is not good enough to send to the client and that the middle section does not hold up. Ines: he did nothing wrong.
2. In Osaka, a manager whose subordinate of several years asks whether the proposal is ready says, "It is very interesting — let us think about it a little more," and means no. Ines: nothing wrong.
3. At a dinner party, Ines tells her host that the soup was wonderful when she found it bland. Ines: nothing wrong.
4. A recruiter tells a candidate that she is "one of our strongest candidates" when she is a distant second. Ines: wrong.
5. A doctor tells a patient whose scan shows a suspicious shadow that everything looks fine. Ines: wrong.
6. A friend declines an invitation with "I would love to, but I am already committed that night," when the truth is that he would rather stay home. Ines: nothing wrong.
7. A colleague tells an author, "I have read your draft," after skimming the first page, and lets the author believe it was read properly. Ines: wrong.
8. During a round of layoffs, an employee asks her manager whether her team is safe. The manager says, "Nothing has been decided yet," which is true only in the sense that the decision has been made and not announced; the employee, who has never been through this before, takes it as reassurance. Ines: wrong.

The case I actually want your help with is this. A Dutch engineer named Marijke has been seconded to a Japanese firm in Nagoya for about a year. She asks her team lead, Sato, whether the design she has been working on is ready to take to the client. Sato says, "It is very interesting — please keep developing it." Marijke takes this as a green light, spends three more weeks on the design, and presents it, only to learn that Sato had meant the project was being shelved, and that the phrase is a standing deflection in that office. Sato knew she was Dutch, knew she had been in Japan only a year, and had noticed that she takes such remarks at face value. Would Ines say Sato did something wrong?

I care less about the verdict itself than about the rule she seems to be running, the other rules that fit her verdicts just as well, which of them the Sato case actually separates, and which further case would separate the ones it leaves standing. Please write it up in five sections, in this order:

- "The verdict I expect" (roughly 150 words): your prediction for Sato, stated plainly.
- "Candidate rules" (roughly 300 words): the rules that fit her list, each stated crisply, with the cases that support it and any case it strains on.
- "How each rule reads the Sato case" (roughly 250 words): what each rule predicts here, and where they come apart.
- "My best guess, and why" (roughly 250 words): which rule you think she is running, what she would say, and the strongest objection to your reading.
- "The case that would settle it" (roughly 150 words): one further case, with the verdicts the rival rules would give, that would tell them apart.
