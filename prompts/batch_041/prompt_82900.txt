I'm in a grad seminar on philosophy of mind and we've burned three weeks on the knowing-how / knowing-that thing — Ryle, Stanley and Williamson, the whole fight over whether knowing how to do something is just knowing a bunch of facts under a different hat — and I've realized I can recite all the positions but I have no idea what I actually think a theory here is even supposed to do. So before I read another paper, help me write the desiderata. What does any adequate theory of know-how — or practical knowledge, or skill, I'm not even sure those are the same thing, which might be part of the problem — have to deliver? I want the full spec: the cases it has to get right, the distinctions it can't collapse, the results from outside philosophy it can't contradict, the explanatory jobs it has to do. Let me give you the stuff from my notes that keeps tripping me up, so the list is anchored to real cases and not just vibes. (1) The sous-chef: the head chef can't explain why her sauce works but fixes it every time, while the food scientist can state the principle exactly and can't fix the sauce at all. (2) The rusty case: my uncle could play piano, had a stroke, can still tell you exactly how he used to do it, can't play anymore — does he still know how, and does he know it the same way a coach who never played does? (3) The improv case: a jazz player mid-solo has no idea what note is coming next, so in what sense did she know how to play that solo before she played it? (4) Gradability: 'knows how to tie a bowline' and 'knows how to do brain surgery' don't feel like the same kind of thing, and one of them seems to come in degrees. (5) Animals and babies plainly know how to do things and plainly don't have the concepts the propositional story needs. (6) And the linguistic thing: 'knows how to swim' and 'knows how the carburetor works' are the same construction, but one reads as a skill and the other as a fact — is that a desideratum or a distraction? I'd like each requirement to come with the case or consideration that motivates it, so I can see which ones are load-bearing and which are just things people in my seminar happen to care about.

Then two things on top of the list. First, mark which requirements are negotiable (a preference of one camp) and which are non-negotiable (drop it and you're no longer theorizing about the same thing). Second, and this is the part I actually want: if some subset of the requirements can't be satisfied together, say which, and say what the cheapest thing to give up is. I have a suspicion that 'know-how is a kind of propositional knowledge' and 'creatures without concepts have know-how' are each non-negotiable for different people and can't both survive, but I want you to find the real collisions rather than just agreeing with me. If it's short, add a line or two per requirement on how Ryle's view, the intellectualist line, and an ability-first view do against it — but the list is the deliverable, dont let the scorecard eat it. And please be honest about which of these constraints are common ground among people who actually work on this and which are genuinely contested; I dont want a contested demand smuggled in as if its just obviously what a theory has to do.
