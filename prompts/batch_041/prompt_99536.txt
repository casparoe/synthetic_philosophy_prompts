I recently had a long and unusually heated argument with a close friend, and I have not been able to stop turning it over. The dispute began with something trivial: she keeps a diary in a shorthand that no one else can read, and I remarked that it was a private language of her own. She replied that Wittgenstein's private language argument shows that this is impossible—that a language used by a single person cannot be a language at all, and that a solitary castaway who invented names for the tides, the gulls, and his own moods would merely be making noises that felt meaningful to him. I had always understood the argument to be about something narrower, but I could not articulate the difference, and by the end of the evening I was half-convinced that any vocabulary used by only one person must be meaningless, including my own notes to myself.

So I want to ask: does Wittgenstein's private language argument really imply that a language used by a single person is impossible, and therefore that the castaway's names for the tides and gulls, or the diarist's shorthand, cannot have meaning? If the argument is instead directed at a more specific kind of privacy, what exactly is that kind, and why does it leave the solitary castaway's vocabulary for public things unproblematic while casting doubt on his words for his own sensations? I am not after a bibliography or a summary of the secondary literature; I want to understand the shape of the argument itself and where the confusion between a merely secret language and a genuinely private one actually lies.
