I have been asked to give the address at a modest commemorative ceremony next spring: the re-dedication, after restoration, of the war memorial in the English town where I grew up, with a delegation from our German twin town present. The memorial carries the names of forty-one local men killed between 1914 and 1918, and a smaller tablet, added later, for the German prisoners of war who died in a camp on the edge of the parish. The audience will be mixed. Some are descendants of the men named; some are local historians who will know exactly which controversies are being avoided and which are being fudged; the German delegation will be listening in a second language; and many will simply be there out of habit, respect, and the expectation of a certain kind of sentence. I have been asked for eight minutes.

I have spent the winter reading about the long-running quarrel over responsibility for 1914 — whether the war was willed by one power, whether it emerged from a system in which each state's mobilisation was a defensible response to the last, whether the timetables that made mobilisation irreversible meant that the final week of July was less a series of choices than a sequence of commitments none of which could be undone. I have come to suspect that the question of which nation was to blame may be malformed, and I want the address to make that thought do real work rather than merely assert it. What I find hardest to get across, and what I want you to get across for me, is a set of distinctions I can feel but cannot articulate:

- between a nation as a bearer of responsibility and a nation as a convenient fiction for a great many people who each did something;
- between the causal question (what made the war happen) and the moral question (who is answerable), and between backward-looking blame and forward-looking responsibility — the difference between asking who did it and asking what we, now, owe;
- between guilt, which attaches to acts and to persons, and the sort of responsibility that attaches to institutions and to inheritance — the thought that I may be answerable for something I did not do and could not have prevented, simply because it was done in my name.

The address must not do either of two things. It must not assign guilt to a nation as such, in the manner of the clause in the 1919 settlement that fixed responsibility on Germany and her allies; I take that to be a category mistake as well as a moral disaster. Nor may it retreat into the comfortable line that everyone was equally to blame, which is both false and, I think, a way of making the deaths meaningless. The difficulty is that the occasion is quietly asking for one or the other: the first in its older form, the second in its newer one.

There is an analogy I keep reaching for and distrust, which you may use, repair, or discard: a pile-up on a motorway in fog, in which no driver did anything unreasonable given what the driver ahead had just done, and yet the pile-up happened, and the insurers still have to decide, and we still feel that someone should answer for it. I am not sure how far it goes; if you use it, say where it breaks.

What I want back is the text of the address, about eight minutes when read slowly, in British English. It should stand on its own words: no quotations from poems, sermons, or documents, and no naming of historians or of works. It should make the distinctions above land for people who have never heard of them, without ever sounding like a lecture, and it should be honest about what is not known. It should end in a way that makes sense of my laying a wreath — that is, it should tell me what I am doing when I do it.
