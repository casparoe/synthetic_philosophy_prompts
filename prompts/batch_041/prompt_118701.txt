I'm in a fortnightly reading group that's nominally about the philosophy of probability and mostly turns into the same fight over and over. The fight is about the principle of indifference — split your credence evenly between possibilities your evidence doesn't distinguish between — and about what the old chord puzzle is supposed to do to it. You know the puzzle: a chord is drawn at random in a circle, and we ask how likely it is to come out longer than a side of the inscribed equilateral triangle. Fix the chord by its two endpoints and you get a third; fix it by a point on a radius, with the chord meeting that radius at right angles, and you get a half; fix it by its midpoint and you get a quarter — and every one of those readings sounds like the obvious way to be uniform. Half the group thinks this kills indifference, one person keeps saying it only shows that a random chord is an incomplete description, and I have a suspiscion that two or three people who sound like they disagree actually hold the same view in different words. I want to hand out a quiz and sort us out.

So: eight to ten questions, each one a concrete case or a claim with two or three possible answers, plus a scoring key that maps answer patterns onto positions, and a short note on which pairs of positions my key can't really seperate (I'd bet the specify-the-experiment people and the it's-only-fine-for-finite-cases people blur, and maybe the no-fact-of-the-matter people and the subjectivists do too, but tell me). No question should be anything like are you a subjectivist — every question has to be answerable by someone who has no idea where their answer lands. Cases I want in, at least: the chord puzzle itself, asked in a way that doesn't let the question is incomplete serve as a free pass (make them say what they think follows from the incompleteness); a ratio case where uniform over the ratio and uniform over its reciprocal disagree, like a wine and water mixture where all you know is that the ratio of one to the other is somewhere between a third and three; a factory case where the cubes coming off the line have side length under two centimetres, so uniform over side length and uniform over volume come apart; a conditioning case where the conditioned-on event has probability zero, so the answer depends on how you take the limit; and a case where no uniform distribution exists at all, like picking a positive whole number with every number equally likely. Plus one question that separates there is no fact of the matter from there is a fact of the matter but indifference can't reach it.

The leaves I need to be able to land on, in plain words rather than yours: (a) indifference is fine and the chord puzzle is solved by finding the right invariance — the problem never fixes the circle's size or where it sits, so a decent answer shouldn't change when you shrink the circle or move it, and that pins down the half; (b) indifference is fine, but only for finite or genuinely symmetric cases, and continuous cases need a physical assumption the maths can't supply; (c) a random chord is under-described, so each sampling procedure has its own correct answer and the puzzle dissolves, because the question is about experiments rather than about probability; (d) the question has no determinate answer and that isn't a defect of the question, there just is no fact about the random chord; (e) only coherence is required of credences, so indifference is never obligatory and never forbidden and the puzzle is a non-issue; (f) the classical line, that probability is the ratio of equally possible cases, so the puzzle only shows you counted the cases wrong. For each leaf, give me one line on what the position costs whoever holds it, and if the invariance move in (a) just relocates the arbitrariness rather than removing it — someone in my group says you merely get to pick your favourite invariance group — that should show up as a question too. Finally, if my list of leaves leaves a hole where a combination of answers ought to sit and nobody holds it, say where it is and whether the view is incoherent, unattractive or just unexplored.
