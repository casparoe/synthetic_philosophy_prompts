I'm one of the organizers of a small grad-student work-in-progress series attached to our philosophy of mind reading group, and next month we've got a visitor: a philosopher of cognitive science whose book manuscript is all about sphexishness. You probably know the anecdote — the digger wasp drags its paralyzed cricket to the burrow, goes inside to inspect the nest, and if you nudge the cricket a few inches away while she's in there, she comes back out, finds it, drags it to the entrance, and then inspects the nest all over again, and if you keep moving it she keeps resetting, supposedly forever. As I understand it the story reaches philosophy through Dennett quoting Wooldridge's 1963 book on the brain, and I think it's Hofstadter who coined the word sphexish for it, and apparently a philosopher named Keijzer has argued that the whole thing is a lab artifact, since the wasps do eventually adapt and re-inspecting a nest that somebody just disturbed might just be the sensible thing to do.

Her line, going by the abstract she sent us, is three claims stacked on top of each other. First, the wasp anecdote is rhetoric rather than data and should be retired. Second — the part I find interesting and a bit slippery — sphexishness was never really a property of the wasp anyway, it's a property of a description: to call a system sphexish is to say that some observer can compress its behavior into a small fixed rule-set, so that the system is invariant under changes the observer regards as goal-relevant. Third, the free-will upshot: determinism doesn't entail sphexishness, being rule-governed isn't unfreedom, what matters is whether the rules can be revised in light of failures that matter to the agent — except there's a regress, because any self-reviser revises by a rule it isn't simultaneously revising, so every agent has some sphexish core, and freedom comes in degrees and is relative to a level of description.

Can you help me draft 8 to 10 questions for the Q&A? Take the role of someone who knows the free will and animal cognition debates well and who isn't going to be impressed by hand-waving — I don't want her just restating the three claims. Before you write anything, brainstorm what her most likely reply to each pressure point is going to be, because I want each question to come with a follow-up already keyed to the answer she'll probably give, so I'm not left scrambling on the day. I want at least one question each on (a) whether making sphexishness description-relative doesn't just make it vacuous — if a clever enough observer can redescribe anything as a fixed routine, what work is the notion doing; (b) the regress, and specifically whether noticing your own sphexishness could itself turn out to be one of the subroutines, which is the objection I'd most like to watch her squirm on; (c) what freedom comes in degrees actually buys, and whether it quietly turns free will into an engineering question about architecture and computational capacity rather than a metaphysical one; and (d) the empirical point — if the wasp does eventually adapt after enough iterations, is that the wasp becoming less sphexish or just adapting very slowly, and does either answer save the anecdote. Then throw in one or two on whether the account can say anything about the felt difference between doing something out of habit and doing it deliberately, and one on whether rigid-in-the-sense-of-stereotyped and rigid-in-the-sense-of-deaf-to-reasons are being run together. No softball stuff like what first got her interested in wasps, and don't ask her to list who's written on this — I can't check references during the session anyway.
