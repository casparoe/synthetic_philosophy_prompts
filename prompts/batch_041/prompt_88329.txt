I'm prepping for a podcast episode next month and I'd rather not wing it. Act as a political philosopher who's also a genuinely good interviewer — the kind who reads the guest's work closely and asks the question they've been dreading rather than the one they've rehearsed. Think it through carefully before you write anything. For context, I teach political philosophy and I've done enough of these to know when I'm asking soft questions.

The guest defends work-related conditionality in the welfare state — job-search requirements, sanctions for missed appointments, the general idea that benefits can be tied to doing something. She's not a real person; I've stitched her together out of a position I keep running into, and I've tried to make her as strong as that position can be. She doesn't ground conditionality in desert, or in paternalism, or in what the taxpayer should have to fund. She grounds it in fair play, and she's precise about it. Her view, as best I can reconstruct it:

1. The welfare state is a cooperative scheme that produces benefits for its members. Those who accept those benefits take on an obligation to bear a fair share of the scheme's burdens — to do their part, whatever the scheme's rules define that as. The obligation is owed to the scheme and to the other participants, not to the government.
2. What counts as a fair share is settled over a whole life, not a pay period. Past contributions, unpaid care work, and the work a person could still do later all go into the accounting. So someone currently drawing more than they pay in isn't thereby a free rider.
3. Because the obligation runs to the scheme rather than to the labour market, it can be discharged in ways other than paid employment — caring for a dependent, say, or sustained work in a mutual-aid or volunteering arrangement. She thinks treating paid work as the only marker of contribution is a mistake even if you grant her premise.
4. So sanctions are legitimate in principle, but only against someone who refuses to do their part while the scheme is doing its part for them — and the scheme has to be able to justify its own terms to the people it binds, including the ones it sanctions.

Her standard move, whenever you point at a case where this lands badly — the carer told to take a supermarket job or lose her housing benefit, the man with a bad back who keeps failing assessments — is to say those are misapplications of the principle rather than counterexamples to it.

What I want: 8 to 10 questions, ordered so the pressure builds, each with a one-line follow-up keyed to the answer she's most likely to give. I want at least one question on each of the following, and I want the hard version, not the version you'd ask at a job talk:

- the fair-play premise itself. The standard objection to fair-play accounts of political obligation is about non-excludable benefits: law and order, public health, clean air — you recieve these whether or not you accept them, and receiving isn't accepting. She needs conditionality to make welfare excludable, and she needs acceptance to generate the obligation. Put the circularity to her squarely: doesn't the thing she's trying to justify presuppose itself?
- the whole-life accounting. What does it license? Retroactive clawbacks? Obligations that only become determinate at the very end, when it's too late to refuse them?
- the care-work move. If caring discharges the duty, is the money she's owed a benefit at all, or a wage? And if contribution has to be socially useful to count, how is her view not just desert wearing a different hat — and doesn't the undeserving poor end up being the same people either way?
- fraud. Fair play looks like it condemns benefit fraud far more cleanly than it condemns not working: the fraudster takes without doing their part while everyone else complies. Does she want that? If the principle's sharpest edge lands on fraudsters rather than on the workless, doesn't that quietly concede that the moralized category was doing the work all along?
- the public-justification clause. Is that a condition on the obligation or only on the legitimacy of the sanction? If the scheme fails the test, does the obligation evaporate — and if it does, hasn't she given away most of what she wanted?

Then a closing question I really care about: what would actually count as evidence or argument against the view, as opposed to evidence that someone misapplied it? I want to see whether anything is left of the principle once all the bad applications are subtracted.
