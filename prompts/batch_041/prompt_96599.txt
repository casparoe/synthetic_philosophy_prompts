I keep trying to forecast how long present-day political entities will last by looking at how long past ones did, and my gut verdicts won't settle into one rule. Forecasting how much longer the EU has, off the lifespan distribution of past confederations and leagues: fine, honestly the only thing I trust. Using "all sovereign states since 1815" as the reference class for a big empire: garbage, it's mostly microstates that wink out in a decade. But then "all great powers since 1500" for that same empire feels legit, even though I just said matching on size and status is what makes a class good. For a religion or a technology that's already survived 300 years, I think it's more likely to see another 100 (the Lindy thing). For a state that's already survived 300 years, I feel the reverse -- it's probably near the end, old empires get brittle. I can't hold both of those. Picking the reference class after I already know how the story turned out feels like cheating. And when the base rate says "these things almost never end peacefully" but I have a vivid inside-view story for why this time is different, I still lean on the base rate -- unless the story names a specific mechanism that would have to break, in which case I flip.

Is there one principle about reference classes, base rates and the outside view that gets all of this, or do I have to give one up? What does it say about a case I didn't list -- a 40-year-old defacto secessionist state, where the base rate for such entities is short but this one has now survived two generations? My own tentative rule is "pick the class defined by the causal forces you think are actually doing the work" -- test it against the cases and repair it where it breaks.
