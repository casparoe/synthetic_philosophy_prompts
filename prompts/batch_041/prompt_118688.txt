I have no training in philosophy, but I have been trying for some weeks to work out whether I have understood Galen Strawson's argument that nobody can ever be genuinely morally responsible for what they do, and I should be grateful for your help with a difficulty in it that I cannot get past.

As I have it, the argument runs something like this: I do what I do because of the sort of person I am; so if I am to be genuinely responsible for my actions, I must be genuinely responsible for the sort of person I am; but the sort of person I am was settled by my upbringing, my circumstances, my temperament and my constitution, none of which I chose; and the same question then arises about whatever settled those, and about whatever settled that, and so on backwards without end. So the responsibility never gets off the ground.

That strikes me as an argument about what causes what. It seems to say that my actions are caused by my character, and my character by things outside me, and that a chain of this kind is not the sort of thing that can make an action mine in the deep sense that deserving blame and praise would require. So the argument looks as though it depends on determinism being true — on my decisions being fixed in advance by what has gone before.

But I am told that this is a misunderstanding, and that Strawson himself insists the argument does not depend on determinism at all and would go through even if determinism were false. I cannot see how this can be so. If determinism is false, there is a gap: not everything going on in me when I decide is fixed by what came before, and what emerges from that gap is not simply the playing out of my history. Surely that gap is exactly where the freedom the argument denies would have to live? What I should like to ask, then, is whether the falsity of determinism brings the responsibility back; and if it does not, what precisely the argument is asking of me that indeterminacy fails to supply.

There is a smaller puzzle that may be the same point in another form. A friend of mine says that he made himself the man he is — that he left home at sixteen and set about remaking his own temper. I can see that this is true in an ordinary sense of the words. What I cannot see is why the argument does not treat it as the very thing it says is impossible, namely a man being the author of his own character. Is the difference merely one of degree, or is the argument demanding something that my friend's self-improvement, however real, does not provide?
