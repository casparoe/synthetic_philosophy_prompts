For three sessions running my reading group — mostly people who came to philosophy through rationalist blogs and never took a course in it — has failed to have the same argument about Molyneux's problem, and I have concluded that the problem should be our diagnostic instrument rather than our topic. What I want is a decision tree that takes a reader from the one commitment we all share, that a congenitally blind adult who gains sight is at least a coherent case worth reasoning about, to a named position on what such a person could do at first sight.

The historical question, as Locke prints it, asks whether, by his sight and before touching them, the man "could now distinguish and tell which is the globe, which the cube." Note that the verb is tell. My working hypothesis, which you should improve on or overturn, is that every position on the question can be located by decomposing it into three stages and asking which stage a given answer is really about:

1. Parsing. Can the newly sighted subject see a cube as a bounded, solid, spatially extended thing at all, or does he get an undifferentiated patchwork of colors and shadows?
2. Transfer. Granting parsing, is information available to touch matched to information available to sight, and by what means: a single amodal code both senses draw on, a learned translation between two codes, or nothing at all?
3. Report. Could a subject who has in fact done the matching name, point to, or otherwise indicate which object is which?

Build the tree so that each branch node is a question about one of these stages, phrased as a concrete case or claim that a reader can answer without knowing which leaf it leads to. Start from the shared commitment, not from the names.

Leaves I need at minimum: the empiricist answer on which he says no because the correlation between how a thing feels and how it looks has to be learned; the rationalist answer on which he could work it out by reasoning from a feature that sight and touch both present; the radical answer on which sight and touch share no common idea at all, so the question is not merely unanswerable in practice but misposed; the conditional answer on which two-dimensional shapes would be recognized at once while three-dimensional solids would not, or only by a blind geometer's inference; the sight-restoration answer on which any failure lies in the subject's inability to parse the visual array rather than in a cross-modal gap; the report-stage answer on which the subject may already have the information and lack only labels, concepts, or confidence; the process answer on which there is no first sight, since restoration unfolds over weeks and every test is contaminated by seeing that has already occurred; and one further leaf of your own invention if the space seems to demand it.

For each leaf, name the position (or write "unnamed" if no one has defended it), give its answer to the historical question, and add one sentence on its most awkward commitment. Where the tree leaves a combination of answers unoccupied, mark it and diagnose why: incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. If two leaves cannot be separated by any question you could put to a reader, flag the pair rather than manufacturing a distinction. Include at least two leaves that return the same answer to the historical question for different reasons, since that is the failure mode our group keeps hitting.

Two constraints. First, no citations: the group has no library access and I do not want a bibliography I cannot check, so if a leaf needs an empirical case, describe it generically — the old cataract-surgery reports, modern sight-restoration programs in which testing happens only after some postoperative seeing has already occurred, tactile-vision substitution devices — and attach no names or dates. Second, do not lean on the reader's knowing the historical figures; use a name as shorthand only where the label does real work, and make sure the tree still sorts someone who has never heard of any of them.

Audience: smart non-specialists who read a great deal of blog philosophy and will notice a branch that is doing no work. Length: eight to ten branch nodes, no more than nine leaves.

Open with the instrument itself — the tree, with each leaf's name, its answer, and its awkward commitment — and put your notes on unoccupied branches and inseparable pairs after it. If you have a single sentence of framing to offer, make it the tree's first line: which branch does the most diagnostic work. No preface, no restatement of this request, and no commentary on your process. Write in American English.
