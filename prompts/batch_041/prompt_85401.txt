I'm a systems engineer by training — my day job is distributed middleware: message buses, publish-subscribe brokers, shared-memory coherence, consensus protocols, and the occasional painful afternoon with a single-writer bus that turns out to have two writers. I've been sitting in on a cognitive science reading group, and the session that stuck with me was the one on global workspace theory and the related distinction between access consciousness and phenomenal consciousness. What I noticed is that the theory's own genealogy already lives in my vocabulary — it was inspired by blackboard architectures from early AI, where independent programs share a common data structure — and I keep wanting to push the translation much further than anyone in the room was willing to. So please push it for me, honestly and in detail.

Treat the global workspace as a publish-subscribe bus with a fixed-width broadcast channel. A coalition of producer modules wins a competition for the channel, the winning content is broadcast, and every subscriber — episodic memory, language, motor planning, the report system — can read it and act on it. Ignition becomes a threshold nonlinearity in the routing layer, so that a message either propagates globally or dies locally. The capacity limit becomes a fixed-size register file, or a bus that serializes because it can only carry one payload at a time. Attention becomes the scheduler deciding which producer gets the channel. And access consciousness becomes nothing more exotic than a content being globally readable and actually read by the modules that can use it. I'm fluent in this idiom and I'd like you to stay in it rather than drifting back into cognitive science prose the moment the mapping gets awkward. Then give me the residue report, and I want the unflattering version.

Which parts of the theory translate cleanly, which parts only survive because I've quietly imported assumptions I don't actually have, and which parts have no counterpart at all? I'm particularly suspicious of two joints. First, whether global availability is the same property as global use: a message that every module can read but no module does read seems, in my vocabulary, to be doing nothing at all, yet the theory seems to want that difference to matter. Second, whatever the phenomenal side of the distinction is supposed to add over the access side — the unity of a single conscious episode, the serial character of experience, the fact that there is something it is like — and whether that residue marks a real expressive gap in my vocabulary or merely a place where the original claim was less precise than it sounded. For each piece of residue, tell me which of those two it is. Finally, try one variant I haven't seen discussed anywhere: recast the workspace not as a broadcast bus but as a consensus protocol among replicated state machines that must agree on one serialized history, and tell me what the access/phenomenal distinction looks like under that reading, whether the two readings are genuinely different theories or two diagrams of the same machine, and which one better survives the residue test. Please write in American English and keep the technical register — I would much rather have the failures listed bluntly than smoothed into a graceful metaphor.
