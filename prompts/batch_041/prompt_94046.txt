My friend and I spent two hours yesterday arguing about the idealization of deliberation behind factored cognition, the one he calls HCH: a hard question goes to an idealized reasoner who may take as long as it likes and who may hand any subquestion to a fresh copy of itself — equally competent, equally honest, and with no memory of what the other copies were asked — the whole thing defined recursively, so that the top-level answer is just the limit of that tree of copies. He treats it as a substantive thesis about deliberation as such: that any answer worth having is reachable through a chain of individually easier questions, and therefore that the binding constraint on a mind is always the best decomposition it can state, never the sheer quantity of thinking it can do. I think the idealization is carrying more weight than that, and I want to know what it actually commits us to. Please set out the most important implications of taking it seriously — for what deliberation is, for how far to trust any mind (or group of people) that answers questions by decomposition, and for the idealization's own limits — and then say plainly which of us it supports.

Give the implications as a table with these columns: Implication | What it bears on (deliberation itself, an oversight practice, the idealization's limits, and so on) | Status (follows from the idealization as stated / follows only with one of its clean-up assumptions — equal competence, honesty, memorylessness / does not follow) | What it rules out. Close with two sentences naming which of the two positions the idealization actually favours and why.
