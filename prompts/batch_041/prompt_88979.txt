I'm preparing a departmental colloquium talk defending a deliberately narrow thesis about heritability: that a high within-population heritability estimate for a trait — whether from twin and adoption designs or from SNP-based methods, including the gap between the two — is consistent with, and under some models even predicts, large, low-cost, generalizable environmental effects on that trait, so that the familiar claim that heritability is not destiny is a quantitative claim with content rather than a truism. My argument leans on the norm-of-reaction picture, on the distinction between the heritability of a trait and the heritability of a between-group difference, and on treating heritability as a descriptive variance partition rather than a causal parameter. The audience is mixed: several sociologists and social epidemiologists who expect behavior genetics to smuggle in hereditarian premises, two quantitative geneticists who will think I am conceding too much to the critics, and one philosopher of science working on causation who will ask what quantity heritability is even supposed to measure.
