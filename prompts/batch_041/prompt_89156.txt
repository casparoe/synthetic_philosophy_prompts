I am producing a short interview series on the epistemology of conspiracy theorising, and my next guest is a philosopher who has spent the better part of a decade developing a position she calls the agency-prior thesis. Stated plainly, her claim is that the characteristic epistemic failure of conspiracy theorising lies neither in logical invalidity, nor in unfalsifiability, nor in any failure to respect the evidence the believer actually cites, but in a systematic distortion of the reasoner's prior over agency: an inflated antecedent probability assigned to coordinated intention, paired with a correspondingly deflated probability assigned to coincidence, independent convergence, and institutional drift. She treats this as a general and content-independent defect, and so rejects the particularist contention that the phrase names no single epistemic kind. She is also a deft interlocutor who typically grants her critic's premise and then relocates the disagreement, most often by insisting that the agency-prior is not directly inspectable and can only be inferred from the shape of a person's overall explanatory practice.

Draft eight to ten questions for the interview, together with, for each, the single follow-up keyed to the reply she is actually most likely to give — the move she will reach for, not the strongest reply available to her. At least one question should probe how the agency-prior thesis escapes collapsing into the particularism she rejects, given that the prior is legible only through the content of what someone believes; at least one should ask whether over-assigning agency is genuinely distinctive of conspiracy theorising or merely continuous with ordinary historical, forensic, and intelligence reasoning, where the same prior may be well earned; and at least one should ask what the thesis implies for conspiracy theorising that turns out to be true. Make two of the follow-ups do real work: one should press her to state a diagnostic criterion that a third party could apply without already knowing whether the theory in question is a conspiracy theory, and the other should force her either to defend the thesis as descriptive or to concede that it is doing normative work about how we ought to allocate explanatory trust. The questions should turn on the substance of the position; nothing about her biography, her influences, or how she came to the topic.
