I'm leading a ninety-minute session of a graduate philosophy reading group on dialetheism and paraconsistent logic. Everyone is at home in classical first-order logic; nobody has looked at a non-classical consequence relation. Don't give me the material — plan how I should present it. Lay out the order the pieces must come in and why each depends on the last: what has to be in place before the phrase "true contradiction" can mean anything to them, and what the opening move has to be so that "inconsistent but not trivial" lands as a real distinction rather than a dodge. Choose the single example that carries the whole session — it has to be a case where they can feel the pull of accepting the contradiction, not a case that merely decorates a formalism — and tell me what that example must have and must lack; in particular, argue me into or out of letting the Liar carry it, since the Liar is the one case they have all met before. Then map the misconceptions they will form and the precise point in the sequence at which each should be headed off. I expect at least these: that paraconsistent logic just is the claim that contradictions are true; that dropping explosion licenses anything; that dialetheism is relativism or a refusal to criticize; and that the law of non-contradiction has simply been denied — and tell me where the paraconsistency/dialetheism distinction itself has to be planted so the rest does not keep collapsing into it. Finish with one question I can put to the group that would reveal whether they have grasped the difference between rejecting explosion and rejecting contradictions, and with what I should openly refuse to cover in ninety minutes.
