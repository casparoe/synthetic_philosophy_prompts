I'm writing a dissertation chapter defending a version of the real-patterns view, and I need an honest map of its exposure. The position I want to hold is this: patterns are real — there is a fact of the matter about which patterns a system exhibits — but that fact is always relative to a stance, so the physical, design, and intentional stances do not compete for a single true description; each picks out patterns that are real, and a pattern counts as real when it is compressible and projectible rather than when it can be reduced to the physical stance. Tell me what would count against this, ranked from fatal to merely awkward, and go beyond the stock charge that it is instrumentalism in realist clothing. Three worries especially. First, a system that admits two equally compressible, equally projectible, mutually incompatible intentional-stance descriptions: can I say which is real, or must I say both are? Second, a colleague proposes to rescue the view by making pattern-reality a matter of degree, so that a thermostat's intentional pattern is real but less real than a person's; is that move available to me, or does it quietly surrender realism? Third, which of the defeaters you raise could a defender concede without abandoning the position? Close with an honest verdict on whether the view is specified loosely enough that nothing could count against it, and if it is, what minimal tightening would make it falsifiable.
