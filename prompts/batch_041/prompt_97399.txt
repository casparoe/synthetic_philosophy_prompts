I'm in a historiography seminar and I've got a distinction stuck in my head that I can't tell if its real or if I'm just redescribing the standard debate, so I want to compare it against the usual positions. The usual positions, roughly: Ranke wanting the historian to just show the past as it was and keep their own viewpoint out of it; Carr insisting you can't do that, because the historian is always selecting and arranging the past through present-day concerns; White going further and saying that imposing a narrative shape on the past is itself a way of giving it a meaning it doesn't carry on its own.

The distinction I keep wanting to draw is between two ways of cashing out "objective". Call the first fidelity: an account is objective to the extent it's answerable to the evidence, to the extent the sources are what's making it say what it says. Call the second invariance: an account is objective to the extent it's robust — to the extent it would come out roughly the same if a differently-situated historian, with different interests and a different narrative shape, had written it instead. These seem to pull apart in both directions. You could be scrupulously faithful to the sources and still write something so shaped by your own framing that no other historian would have produced it; and you could get a story that everyone in the field tells the same way, but only because they're all leaning on the same secondary account and nobody has gone back to the sources.

So compare the two for me. Is fidelity-vs-invariance a real distinction, or does it just collapse back into the old realism-vs-constructivism thing? If it's real, which one is the better ideal for history to aim at, and what does White's point about narrative form do to each of them — does it show that invariance is impossible, or does it actually make invariance the only ideal left standing once fidelity is off the table? Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give the reasoning, and skip the preamble.
