I help run a small volunteer heritage group in a town of about nine thousand people: we maintain a walking tour, a modest house museum, and a website where we publish short profiles of people and episodes from the town's past — a mill owner, a doctor, a suffragist, the family that ran the tannery, and so on. There are six of us, none of us a trained historian, and we put out roughly two profiles a month; the finished text is voted on by the whole group and then goes on a public website that school groups use, so whatever we print we have to be able to defend to a stranger and to each other. Lately every profile turns into the same argument, and it is starting to stall us. Some of us want the writing to say plainly when someone did something we would now call cruel or unjust — the mill owner who used child labour, the doctor who experimented on poor patients — and to name it in our own vocabulary, because otherwise the profiles read as flattering and the visiting classes get a sanitised story. Others of us say you cannot judge people by the standards of our time, that this is arrogant and anachronistic, and that a profile should describe what people did and believed without passing sentence. I have come to think both sides are talking past each other, partly because the phrase the standards of their time is doing far too much work — it makes it sound as though everyone in a given decade thought alike, when there were almost always people then who objected — and partly because we keep sliding between at least four different things: describing the past in present-day words and categories (was this child labour, or just children helping at the mill?), holding someone to present moral standards, actually blaming and condemning them as a person, and telling the story as though it were simply a march toward us.

I would like you to turn this into a procedure our writers can actually run on their own, in an evening, without a history degree — something closer to a checklist than an essay. Give me the questions to ask in order, what would count as a sufficient answer to each, the point at which the procedure stops and simply forbids us from making a particular claim, and the point at which it must hand the decision to a person because what remains is a judgement about values rather than a question about the past. Please build in the two tests I keep reaching for and never quite get right: first, whether the person lived up to standards they themselves professed, or whether there were people around them saying at the time that it was wrong; and second, whether the criticism would survive being applied to us by whoever comes next, given that some of what we now take for granted will look monstrous later. Then run the procedure on three cases so I can see how it behaves — the mill owner who employed children, the doctor who experimented on his patients, and one I care about where I suspect it will tell me something I do not want to hear: the town's celebrated benefactor, who gave us the library and the park and, it turns out, made his money in a way our own stated values should condemn. Tell me where it lands on each, which of the three is the genuine borderline, and, honestly, what the procedure cannot do — the parts that will still be argued about no matter how carefully we follow it, and the ways a tidy checklist of this kind could give us false confidence or quietly let us off the hook.
