I'm a philosophy prof — political philosophy, and I run a seminar on corruption every other year — and for the past three years I've also sat on the ethics panel of a mid-sized NGO that runs clinics and drug-supply chains in about ten countries. Twice a month a country director emails us with something they need settled before money moves, and we keep improvising and contradicting each other. Recent ones: the district health officer who personally signs off on our permits has invited the whole team to his daughter's wedding, and everyone knows a goat and an envelope are expected; a driver job is about to go to the regional director's nephew, who is honestly the best candidate; customs wants a fee that's on no schedule but is what everyone pays.

They've asked me to write a decision procedure that a country director with no philosophy training can run alone, in half an hour, and then run again next month on a different case and get a consistent answer. I need help writing it.

The hard part, which I want the procedure to face rather than dodge: our compliance code is stricter than US anti-bribery law, which still carves out small payments to speed up things an official already has to do, and basically in line with the UK's, which on paper carves out nothing. One collegue on the panel says that on the collective-action picture — corruption as a stable social equilibrium rather than a few greedy individuals — a strict code is just a tax on being the one honest operator, and that we'd be imposing standards on people who never got a say in them. The other says that argument is what every captured official would make, and that appealing to local standards is what you do when you want to keep the contract. Both of them are right about something, and I need a procedure, not another seminar.

What I want from you:

- The procedure itself, as questions in order: what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where it stops with a no, where it stops with a yes, and where it hands off to a person because what's left is a value question rather than a factual one.
- Say which distinctions from the philosophical literature on corruption have to survive the trip into a 30-minute checklist and which can be dropped. My hunch is that the load-bearing one is the difference between a rule aimed at improper influence on a decision and a rule aimed at any advantage to a public official, and that the timing-versus-outcome distinction is what makes the customs case tractable — but you tell me.
- Run it on all three cases, and make at least one of them come out in a way the country directors won't like.
- Where it misfires: the cases where it will give a confident answer that's probably wrong, the ones where it stalls, and what the director should do when it stalls.
- No citations or reading lists — I know the literature, the directors dont, and the thing has to stand on its own.

Keep it plain. Finish with a final line in exactly this form — \rule{...} — containing the single question in the procedure that does the most work, the one that, answered honestly, settles most cases, in under fifteen words.
