I'm a backend engineer with zero formal philosophy or econ background, but I've been going down a Bayesian rabbit hole ever since a coworker and I argued about whether two perfectly rational people can look at the same evidence and still disagree. That led me to Aumann's agreement theorem and the common prior assumption, and from there to the no-trade theorem, which as I understand it says something like: if two traders share a prior over the relevant states, and it's common knowledge that they're both rational and that they both know the initial allocation of goods, then no purely speculative trade can happen between them, because it can't be that both walk away thinking they got the better end of it. Which seems crazy given that the whole market for mortgage-backed securities and the CDOs stacked on top of them in the mid-2000s looks like two sophisticated parties each convinced the other was the sucker, and then 2008 happened.

What I want is an analysis of that episode through the lens of the common prior assumption, not the macro story (I know the macro story). The epistemic one. Who are the players and what does each side actually know; what does the theory say should have happened in that market; and then which of its assumptions failed, and in which direction. Was there a common prior about house prices and default correlations (the Gaussian copula stuff), or did the two sides genuinely have different priors? If they did differ, is that a counterexample to the Harsanyi doctrine that we should all share a prior because we share the same experiences -- or is the better reading that the prior was common but simply wrong, with the Great Moderation as the shared experience that produced it? And does it matter for the theorem that a lot of this trade was partly hedging rather than pure speculation?

Then please run it the other way, from the episode back to the theory. A friend of mine insists 2008 proves the common prior assumption is just empirically false, which is why the no-trade theorem is useless for real markets; another friend says the theorem was never a description of real markets and the real failure in 2008 was common knowledge of rationality, not the prior. Tell me which is closer and where each is wrong. I'd also like an honest account of what this framework just can't tell us here, and whether some neighboring idea -- heterogenous priors, higher-order uncertainty, whatever -- handles the case better. And please don't just give me the standard asymmetric-information line; differing priors and differing information are not the same thing, and I want to know whether the distinction actually matters for this case.
