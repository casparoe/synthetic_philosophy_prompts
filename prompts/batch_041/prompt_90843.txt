I cover the AI-safety world, and the arguement I keep hearing and re-hearing is the one about heuristic arguments. The shape is always the same: someone says a model can't be doing X, or a system won't be able to do Y, because pulling that off would mean solving a problem that's believed to be hard. So either the argument goes in a circle, or it quietly assumes the hard pieces are independent of one another. That assumption is what people in that world call the presumption of independence, and the fight is over what to do with it. One camp says: then formalize it. Give me a distribution, or a model of computation, in which the presumption comes out as a theorem, and stop hand-waving. The other camp says formalizing it is exactly the mistake, because write it down as a probabilistic assumption and you've traded a claim about what a computation can do for a claim about a distribution, which isn't the same thing and isn't something you get to just assert. This has been going in circles for a few years now, with alot of heat and no movement. I want a diagnosis, not a side. Is this a real disagreement about whether heuristic arguments are evidence, a verbal one about what formalizing is even supposed to mean, or two different questions wearing one name, one about the epistemology of informal reasoning and one about the structure of computational hardness? And what, if anything, would count as progress?
