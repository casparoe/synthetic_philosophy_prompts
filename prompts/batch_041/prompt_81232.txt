I'm putting together an episode for a student-run podcast on philosophy of the social sciences, and my guest is a philosopher who works on measurement and inference in the behavioural sciences -- not a household name, but she's exactly who I want on this. Her thing is the gender-equality paradox: the pattern where the richer and more gender-equal a country is, the bigger the sex gap in what people actually end up studying and doing (Nordic countries are the poster child; I have a vague memory that the flagship result is about top math students, and that somebody found the same thing for personality scores). Her line is that the regularity is probably real, but that almost nothing follows from it -- neither the popular inference that the differences must be natural, nor the political inference that we should stop worrying about representation. I'm sympathtic, but I want the episode to actually test her, not let her do the standard twenty-minute version of the argument.

Can you draft me ten-ish interview questions, in the order I should ask them, so that the pressure builds instead of me blowing my best question in the first five minutes? Each question should come with a short follow-up keyed to the answer she's most likely to give, since she's smooth and will deflect if I let her. I want at least a couple of questions on the inference to nature -- in particular I want her pressed on whether 'in more equal countries people are freer to do what they want' is even coherent as an explanation, given that those countries also differ from each other most in childcare, family policy and labour markets; and on whether the nativist reading needs assumptions about environments being uniform that egalitarian countries arguably violate (people keep telling me heritability claims need something like that, but I've never seen it laid out properly). Then I want her on the normative half: she says the paradox tells us nothing about justice -- fine, but I want to know whether that cuts against quotas and parity programmes or against the people who cite her in order to oppose them, and whether 'it's a preference, not a constraint' is a distinction she can really keep if the preference is formed under exactly the institutions we're asking about.

Also, three things I keep chewing on that I'd like her to have to deal with. First, the which-science-not-whether-science point: women in these countries seem to do fine in some fields and not others, and I can't tell whether that's a problem for the nature story or for the whole framing. Second, a generalisation test I invented over the weekend -- if freedom just lets real preferences show through, the same countries should look *more* gender-typed in hobbies, consumption, baby names and so on, and if they don't, does that sink the 'freedom reveals nature' story? Third, I read somewhere that in egalitarian countries the gender-typical job is often a perfectly decent living, so the oportunity cost of picking it is low, whereas in poorer countries women get pushed towards the high-return fields -- if that's right, the paradox might be about labour-market structure and not about psychology at all. I'd like one question that forces her to say which of these she thinks is doing the real explanatory work, and a closing question about what evidence would actually change her mind about the inference to nature, so the episode doesn't end on 'we need more research'.
