I'm a graduate student in political theory writing a seminar paper on whether public reason can bear the weight Rawls puts on it, and I want to test the theory against a real case rather than illustrate it with one. The case I've settled on is the Dover intelligent design controversy of 2004–2005, in the Dover Area School District in York County, Pennsylvania. I know the episode; what I need is a disciplined mapping in both directions, theory onto case and case back onto theory. Do not give me citations, a bibliography, or a reading list — I have those. I want the analysis itself, and I want it to be honest about where the theory runs out rather than forcing it to speak.

Work from the following facts, and please do not import extra historical detail or hedge about dates. In October 2004 the school board voted 6–3 to require that a statement be read aloud in ninth-grade biology classes presenting intelligent design as an alternative to evolution, and to place the textbook Of Pandas and People in school libraries as a reference. The district's biology teachers declined to read the statement, and administrators read it instead. Eleven parents sued; the case was tried without a jury before Judge John E. Jones III in late 2005, and in December 2005 he ruled the policy unconstitutional, finding that intelligent design is not science and cannot be separated from its creationist antecedents. All eight board members who stood for reelection that November were defeated. The textbook's earlier drafts had used creationist vocabulary, which was replaced with design vocabulary after the Supreme Court's 1987 ruling that creationism could not be taught in public schools. The board's stated rationale for the policy, both in the statement itself and at board meetings, was framed around critical thinking, academic freedom, and exposing students to alternatives, not around religion.

Use Rawls's political liberalism as the theory: reasonable pluralism, the liberal principle of legitimacy, the criterion of reciprocity, the duty of civility, public reason as it governs constitutional essentials and basic justice, and the proviso under the wide view of public reason. Treat the school board members as citizens exercising coercive political power over a constitutional essential — what is taught in a public school science class — and treat the biology teachers' refusal as a distinct locus of the problem, since they are public officials declining to lend their own speech to a justification they reject.

Structure the response in the following sections, in this order.

1. The case restated in public-reason terms. Roughly 400 words. Map the episode onto the theory's vocabulary: who the relevant agents are, what the constitutional essential at stake is, what counts as a reason offered in public, and what the criterion of reciprocity demands of the board. Be explicit about which strand of Rawls you are invoking at each step, because the content of the policy, the reasons offered for it, and the motives behind it are three different things, and the theory treats them differently.

2. What public reason explains. Roughly 300 words. Say what the theory gets right here: why the board's position is a paradigm case for the liberal principle of legitimacy, why the teachers' refusal is intelligible in public-reason terms, and what the theory predicts about the aftermath — including the electoral defeat of the board members and the fact that a court ruling settled the legal question without settling the underlying disagreement.

3. Where the mapping strains. Roughly 400 words. This is the part I care most about. The board's stated reasons were public in form. If public reason constrains only the reasons officials offer, the board's rationale passes a first look and the case may not be a violation at all; if it constrains the reasons that actually move them, the theory needs an account of sincerity it has not obviously supplied. Say which reading the proviso and the duty of civility actually require, whether the wide view can distinguish a genuine public justification from a strategic translation of a sectarian one, and what the court's reliance on purpose rather than content does to the analogy between judicial review and public reason. Also say whether the biology teachers' refusal is a case the theory handles well or one it never anticipated, since the standard picture concerns citizens and legislators, not officials asked to read a script they reject.

4. What Dover does to the theory. Roughly 400 words. Run the case back onto the theory. Does it confirm public reason, expose an anomaly, or present a case the theory never anticipated? If the theory survives, say what it has to concede. If it does not, say precisely which premise fails. Consider in particular whether the episode supports the narrow view over the wide view, whether it shows that public reason must be a constraint on coercion rather than on deliberation, and whether the fact that the dispute was resolved by a court rather than by the parties converging on shared reasons is a problem for the theory or exactly what the theory should expect.

5. Verdict. Roughly 150 words. State your own position plainly: does Dover vindicate public reason, embarrass it, or leave it where it was?

Keep the whole response under about 2,000 words, and do not pad with restatements of Rawls that I already know.
