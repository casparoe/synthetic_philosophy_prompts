I'm a third-year PhD student in philosophy of cognitive science, and I'll be running a single 90-minute session of our upper-level seminar on minds and machines this spring. The group is about fourteen people — mostly advanced undergraduates, with a few first-year graduate students — and I want to open the unit on non-human and pre-human cognition with an activity rather than a lecture. Specifically, I want a game that makes the group feel the force of the progression Dennett draws from Darwinian to Skinnerian to Popperian to Gregorian creatures, and above all the two transitions that tend to get nodded at rather than felt: the move from learning by trial and error in the world to running the trials inside the head, and the move from running them in one's own head to running them in a shared, external medium of tools and artifacts. I care less that they can recite the four labels than that they arrive at the distinctions from the inside and can then point to which of the four is doing the work in a given situation.

Please design the activity with the following constraints. Nothing should be explained up front; the four terms should be named only in the debrief, and ideally the labels should feel like a summary of what they just lived through rather than new information. The rules should be carried by a hidden world the facilitator keeps on a single page — simple enough to run from memory, opaque enough that it must be probed. Structure it as four rounds, one per kind, and make the nesting vivid: a later creature keeps the earlier capacities, so students should be able to see all four floors still present and to say which one is carrying the load at any moment (I like Dennett's image of a tower of generate-and-test here, and I'd like the activity to have the same stacked structure).

Give each round a specific job. The first round should be selection at the population level, not learning: individuals must be unable to improve within their own lifetime, and any improvement must come from reshuffling the population across generations. The second round should make error genuinely costly — a wrong move has to subtract something real from the player's score, so that the appetite for the third round is felt rather than asserted. The third round should let players test a move before making it, but on a limited budget and with a slightly unreliable oracle, so that they discover an inner model is a model: it can be wrong, and trusting it trades the cost of error for the risk of model error. The fourth round should locate the advantage in an external, transmissible artifact — a shared public medium that can be written to and inherited, so that a player entering late benefits from a store she did not build — and I want the payoff to come from the artifact itself rather than from teamwork or individual cleverness, which means you'll have to block ordinary verbal coordination and make the shared medium the only channel.

Deliverables: the full rules, a materials list using only cheap things (index cards, a whiteboard, counters), a payoff scheme that needs no real money, a minute-by-minute timeline for the 90 minutes, the hidden-world specification for the facilitator, and a debrief that maps specific moments in the game onto the four kinds and onto the economics of error that drives the progression. Then tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle each failure — I'd rather be warned than surprised. Finally, be frank about what the activity cannot reproduce and that I will have to supply in the debrief: I already suspect it cannot convey the evolutionary timescale of the first floor, and it presents the four levels synchronically, so the historical claim that these are stages in a lineage will have to come from me. Tell me what else is lost in translation. No reading or web access should be required of the students, and the whole thing should fit in one session with no prior preparation on their part.
