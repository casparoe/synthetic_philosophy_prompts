A few weeks ago I sat in on a session of a reading group that had been working through the philosophy of mind, and two of the regulars — I'll call them M and T — got into a dispute that never resolved, and the rest of us were not equipped to settle it. I'd like you to adjudicate it for me. I'm reporting it as a bystander; I have no stake in who turns out to be right, and I've tried to give both of them their strongest form.

M opened by claiming that introspection is simply not a reliable source of information about our own minds. M cited the experimental work in which people give confident explanations of why they chose or felt something, when the actual cause was something they were never aware of — the classic cases where the experimenter secretly manipulates the stimulus and the subject invents a plausible-sounding reason for a choice the subject never actually made. On M's view, when we report why we did something, we are usually confabulating, and we mistake the fluency of the explanation for accuracy.

T replied that this overreaches. Those studies target one narrow kind of report — our causal explanations of our own behavior — and say nothing about our access to our current experience. Nobody confabulates that they are in pain right now, T said; the feeling is simply present to the person having it. T added that the studies themselves rest on participants sincerely reporting their own attitudes and choices, so if self-report were worthless across the board, the experiments could not have shown anything.

M was unmoved, and widened the target. Even reports of present experience, M said, come apart from the states they purport to describe: people feel pain in limbs that are no longer there, report intense pain when there is no detectable tissue damage, and report relief when a placebo has changed nothing physiological. If the report and the state can dissociate in both directions, then the report is a poor instrument for the state, which is all "unreliable" was ever supposed to mean. M also pointed to the well-documented tendency to treat the mere act of introspecting as if it guaranteed accuracy — the sense that looking inward is transparent when it is not.

T answered that M is equivocating on what "reliable" is being asked to do. There is a difference, T said, between introspection as a measuring instrument that could in principle come apart from its target, and a person's sincere avowal of, say, being in pain. On one influential view, an avowal of that kind does not describe an inner object at all — it expresses the state rather than reporting it, the way a cry does. If that is right, T argued, then asking whether the avowal is "reliable" is a category mistake: reliability is a property of a method that tracks a target, and an expression is not tracking anything.

M called this a rescue by changing the subject. If avowals carry no information about the mind, M said, they are useless to the clinicians and psychologists who need to know what a person is actually feeling, and you have simply renamed the problem. And even granting the expressivist picture, M added, you still have to distinguish a sincere avowal from an insincere one, which means you are back to asking what the person has access to — so nothing has been gained.

T insisted the point is not verbal. First-person authority, T said, is a standing we grant to a person, a normative status, not a perceptual achievement; it explains why we do not demand evidence when someone says they are in pain, while we do demand evidence about their blood pressure, and it explains why we treat their word as settling the matter. Sincerity, on this view, is just the absence of deception, not the successful completion of a further inward inspection.

The group broke up without a verdict.
