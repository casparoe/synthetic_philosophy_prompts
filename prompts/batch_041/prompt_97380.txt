Write a pantoum — four quatrains in which the second and fourth lines of each stanza recur as the first and third lines of the next, with the closing stanza folding back into the opening lines — whose every line is a single micro-experience, so that the form's interlocking repetition enacts the combination problem: the lines keep handing themselves on to one another and returning, yet no line ever becomes the subject of the whole, and the poem loops back to its beginning instead of arriving at a unified "I." Let the endless circulation, not any argument, do the work; the reader should close the poem with the sense that the parts have been shuffled forever without ever summing into one experience. Work the two horns of the problem into the middle stanzas. If each micro-experience has its own subject, then their union must yield either one subject — and it is unclear whose, since the others seem simply lost — or several, in which case nothing has actually been combined. If instead they are qualities without owners, the resulting whole has the qualities but no one it is like something to be. Let one recurring line carry the puzzle of two qualitatively identical twinges, and whether their union is one red or two. It should be accurate enough that someone working on the combination problem recognizes both horns, and it should remain a poem rather than an argument in verse.
