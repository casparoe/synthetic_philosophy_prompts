I am a PhD student working on Nietzsche's essay on the uses and disadvantages of history for life, and I have built the following argument for a seminar. It troubles me because it seems to prove more than I can accept, and I cannot tell whether it is valid and simply overpriced, or whether one of the steps slides.

(1) Nietzsche holds that life requires a degree of forgetting and of the unhistorical, and that an excess of the historical sense paralyzes action.
(2) He nonetheless recommends history in the service of life, and distinguishes three modes: the monumental, which serves the person of action; the antiquarian, which serves the preserver; and the critical, which serves whoever must break with a past.
(3) A mode of history serves life only if it is the mode the present need calls for.
(4) But to know which mode the present need calls for, one must already grasp what the present life requires, and the capacity to grasp and transform one's situation is precisely the plastic power that the modes are supposed to supply.
(5) So the modes presuppose the very capacity they are meant to cultivate.
(6) Therefore anyone who lacks the plastic power cannot use history for life, and anyone who has it does not need history to acquire it.
(7) Hence history is either idle or harmful for life — which cannot be right, since Nietzsche plainly wants a disciplined use of it.

Where does this go wrong? If the argument is valid, tell me which premise is least costly to abandon and what abandoning it costs; if it is invalid, name the step and explain why it looked fine — in particular, whether (4) mistakes a precondition for a cause, and whether (3) through (5) quietly assume that the use of history for life can be measured by an output fixed independently of the modes themselves. Then say what your diagnosis implies for how the three modes should be individuated and whether they can be ranked against one another.
