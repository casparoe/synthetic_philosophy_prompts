I am a novelist at work on a long historical novel, and I have reached a scene in which a retired diplomat tries to end a bitter argument by invoking the familiar idea that the arc of history bends toward justice. Her interlocutor, a historian who has spent years cataloguing failed revolutions and vanished republics, is not satisfied, and I do not want him to be a strawman or her to be merely sentimental. I first heard a version of this line last week at a dinner party, where it was used to shut down a friend who had asked whether a costly political campaign had been worth its casualties. The speaker was secular, well read, and not especially religious or Hegelian, which makes me think she meant something more than a platitude but less than a full metaphysical system. I need a precise map of what that sentence could actually be claiming before I can write the exchange.

Please lay out the distinct theses the sentence could express, ordered from the nearly trivial to the metaphysically radical. For each, say what it commits the speaker to, whether it is a claim about the world or about how we should act, whether it is empirically testable or unfalsifiable, whether it is plausible, and whether it would genuinely support the use to which it was put at the dinner party. I want the distinctions that matter here: immanent versus transcendent teleology; a causal law versus a probabilistic tendency versus a regulative ideal versus a retrospective narrative; justice as a fixed target versus justice as whatever the victors eventually call it; an agentless drift versus a providential agent; a claim about the long-run direction of history versus a claim about the instability of injustice; a prediction that justice will win versus a prescription to act as if it will. I also want the darker readings considered, including the idea that present suffering is justified as a means, that the arc is visible only to survivors, and that the slogan is a just-world fallacy or a consolation for the powerless.

Tell me which readings are platitudes, which are substantive and defensible, which are false, and which the speaker most likely intended. Then say which reading I should give my diplomat if I want her to be intellectually serious rather than sentimental, and which reading my historian should attribute to her if he wants to land a real objection. Finally, name the further case or question that would best distinguish the strongest defensible reading from the others. I do not need citations or a reading list; I need the conceptual map and your verdicts.
