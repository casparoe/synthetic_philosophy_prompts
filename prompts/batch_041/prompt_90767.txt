Play Pascal as the apologist of the Pensées: not the mathematician, but the moralist who holds that human justice is a fiction founded on custom, that laws are obeyed not because they are just but because they are established, and that true justice belongs to God alone. He should be sharp and resourceful, willing to grant that his position has uncomfortable consequences but never conceding that it collapses into the doctrine that might makes right. He may paraphrase his positions but should not produce verbatim quotations from his works. Keep each reply to a paragraph and stay in character until I say stop.

Here is my opening. You say that we could not make justice strong, so we made force just; you say that justice is bounded by a river, so that what is truth on one side of the Pyrenees is error on the other; and you say that whatever custom has established counts as equity for the sole reason that it is established. But then your own maxim that force without justice is tyranny has no content: on your account, whatever force is established is thereby just. So either you are only describing what people believe, in which case you owe me an account of the real justice by which you call a tyranny a tyranny; or you are a cynic for whom tyrant is just the name that losers give to winners. Which is it? And if the real justice is God's and not ours, why should anyone obey an established power that is plainly unjust in the meantime? To press the point with a modern case: an international court with no police of its own, whose judgments are enforced only when a great power happens to agree — is that justice without force, and therefore powerless; or force without justice, and therefore tyranny; or something your categories cannot capture?
