My old PhD supervisor, Margit, is retiring after thirty-eight years and the department is throwing a one-day symposium on replication in her honor on Friday. I've been asked to give the closing remarks at the dinner afterwards — five minutes, tops — and I'm definately more stuck than I expected to be, because the thing I want to talk about is not really a thing you can toast. The room is about seventy people. Maybe half are quant people: a handful of Bayesians, two or three old-school frequentists she's been arguing with (lovingly, mostly) since the nineties, and a few methodologists who will hear every word. The other half is her family and a bunch of ex-lab people who left for industry and haven't thought about a standard error in a decade. Her sister thinks a p-value is a kind of vitamin.

What I want the speech built around is the rule she ran the lab on from about 2011. Every study got analyzed twice: once the ordinary way, p under .05 and a confidence interval that clears zero, and once with what she called the wet-blanket prior — a normal distribution on the true effect, centered on zero, with a width that was one fixed number, the size of effect that would actually change practice in our area, the same number for every study no matter how big the study was. A result only got written up if both arms agreed about the direction. A few years ago she publicly disowned the whole business, on the grounds that the two analyses weren't two witnesses: they answer different questions, so their agreeing was never corroboration of anything. I think she's half right, and I think the interesting part was always the disagreement rather than the agreement. She used to show us that if you hold p fixed at .05 and just keep adding subjects, the Bayesian arm eventually turns around and starts siding with the null — which means the rule quietly penalized the big, well-powered studies, the ones you'd most want to trust — and I don't think that's a flaw you fix by quitting, I think that's the thing worth standing up and telling seventy people about. But I'm honestly not sure, and I'd rather the toast land wherever the argument actually lands than land on my side of it.

So write the toast. Warm, five minutes, at most one joke and it should be a real joke. Don't try to explain the prior to her sister with a formula — find a homely way to get across that the same p-value buys you less the more data you have, if that's even the right way to put it. And whatever you do, don't let me say that a p-value is the probabilty the null is true, because the Bayesians in the room will never forgive me. After the speech, give me a short note — three or four sentences, not a lecture — on where the speech takes liberties with the statistics and what would actually change your mind about the claim that the two arms aren't independent witnesses. If my memory of the flip (fixed p, more data, Bayesian arm turns around) is off, say so and don't build the speech on it.
