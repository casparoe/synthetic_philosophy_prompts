ok so I have to do a case analysis for my philosophy of science class and I picked a weird one. In Turing's 1950 paper where he introduces the imitation game (the thing everyone now calls the Turing test), he goes through a long list of objections that people might raise, and one of them is about ESP. Not as a joke either. He says basically that the statistical evidence for telepathy is actually hard to just wave away, and that this is a real problem for his test, because if the interrogator can read minds then the interrogator might be able to tell which one is the machine no matter how well the machine plays. His fix is that the game should be played in a room that blocks telepathy. I'm pretty sure that's really in there, my teacher says it is, and he also seems to think the ESP objection is one of the stronger ones on his list, which is wild.

What I want is an analysis of this whole episode through the lens of the underdetermination idea, the Duhem-Quine thing where no hypothesis is ever tested by itself, you always test the hypothesis plus a pile of auxiliary assumptions, so when a prediction fails you never know for sure which piece to blame. And the related worry that sometimes the auxiliary you'd have to blame is the very thing the experiment is supposed to be figuring out, which makes the test circular.

So map it out. What exactly is the hypothesis being tested in the imitation game, what auxiliary assumptions does the test need to work at all, and where does telepathy sit in that structure, is it an auxiliary assumption of the test itself or is it a rival claim about the world that just happens to break the test? Then the fix. Does the telepathy-proof room actually solve the problem or does it just move it somewhere else? To build a room that blocks telepathy you have to already know something about how telepathy works, that it's the kind of thing that can be blocked, that it has some medium or mechanism, that it doesn't just pass through walls. But the people who think telepathy is real don't agree on any of that, some of them think it's exactly the kind of thing that wouldn't be blocked by a wall. So has Turing assumed the conclusion he needs? Or is there a reading where the room is a legitimate experimental control, like shielding a detector from background radiation, and the assumption he's making is no worse than the assumptions you make in any experiment?

And then the direction I actually care about: what does this episode show about underdetermination? Does it support the thesis, or is it a case where the auxiliary in question (telepathy) is a normal empirical claim that could in principle be tested on its own, so the "you can always blame an auxiliary" worry is less scary than it sounds? Also is there a real difference between a confound you control for and a confound you assume away, and which one is Turing doing?

One more thing I keep thinking about: if the interrogator might be using telepathy, doesn't the game stop being a test of the machine and become a test of the interrogator? Does that break the test, or does it just change what the test is measuring? I don't need citations or a reading list, just the analysis.
