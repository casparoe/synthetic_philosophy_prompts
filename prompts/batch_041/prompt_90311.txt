I'm taking a third-year seminar on philosophical method and we've spent the last two weeks on thought experiments, and there's an analogy a guy in my discussion group keeps leaning on that I can't quite argue against. His line is that a thought experiment is just an experiment: the lab is your head, the apparatus is the scenario you describe, and the intuition you have at the end is the reading on the dial. He says that's not a cute comparison, it's litterally the same kind of thing, and he draws two conclusions from it. First, a robust intuition is data — when people say thought experiments are just rhetoric or persuasion, they're confusing a method with its abuses. Second, when the intuition doesn't show up, or shows up differently depending on how the case is worded, or on whether you ask someone in their second language, that's a failed replication: the fix is a better-designed case, not giving up on the method. He uses this to say the trolley problem industry is fine and just under-replicated, and that anyone who says our intuitions are just artifacts of the vignette has misunderstood what an experiment even is.

I'd rather audit this than just grumble at him. Can you lay out the mapping the analogy needs — what plays the role of the apparatus, the materials, the control condition, the measurement, the prediction, the surprise, the replication, the lab notebook — and then tell me where it breaks? I care especially about the disanalogies that actually bear on his two conclusions: is there anything in the experiment picture that his version quietly imports (intervention, data that could have come out otherwise, calibration, the possibility that the apparatus itself is misbehaving) that a scenario plus an intuition just doesn't have? And does "intuitions are data" survive once those are separated out, or was the analogy definately carrying the conclusion the whole time?

One more thing, because I don't want to just swap one picture for another. Someone else in the seminar said thought experiments are more like optical illusions — they exploit a bug in your cognitive wiring and the reading you get is the illusion itself. Does that one do any better, or does it prove too much, since we don't throw out perception because of illusions? And if neither picture holds up, is there an analogy that keeps what's genuinely right in the experiment one — that some thought experiments really do surprise us and really do constrain a theory — without dragging in the parts that don't transfer?
