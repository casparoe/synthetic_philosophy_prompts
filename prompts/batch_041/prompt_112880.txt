I chair the patient safety review board at a mid-sized hospital — nine of us, we meet once a month for 90 minutes, and lately more and more of the cases that come to us involve tools that make their own calls. A sepsis alert that goes quiet for eleven days after a vendor update and stops firing for patients on a certain drug, while a resident cross-covering fourteen patients that night trusts the silence and doesn't escalate on the numbers alone. A compounding robot that reads a new supplier's barcode into the wrong field and puts the wrong concentration in an IV, because the pharmacist who used to double-check everything was told the robot was validated as the second check. A triage chatbot that files a chest pain complaint as low priority because the patient wrote in a dialect the model barely saw in training. Nobody in that room has a philosophy background and we are not getting one. The engineer says the model did exactly what it was specified to do, the clinician says they were told to trust the output, the informatics lead says they signed off on a spec that didn't cover this, and the vendor's contract says it's not their problem. I've read enough to know there's a name for the hole we keep falling into, and that the usual move is to seperate being on the hook from being at fault, but I can't figure out how to make that do any real work at 8am on a Tuesday.

So what I want is a procedure we can actually run in the room: the questions to ask in order, what counts as a good enough answer to each, the point where it stops with a no, and the point where it hands the decision to a person because the question is values and not facts. Hard constraints, because general counsel won't sign off otherwise: every finding has to land in exactly one of three buckets — (1) a named person acted below a standard they could reasonably have been held to, (2) no one is at fault but a named person has to answer for it and own the fix, or (3) nobody can own it. The state's adverse event reporting form has a field for responsible party and none isn't an option we can pick without a fight, so the procedure needs to say what we write there in each case, and it has to survive cases where the logs are missing and cases where the party that looks responsible is a vendor we can't discipline, a regulator, or a decision this board itself made two years ago. It also has to be something a family can read without it sounding like we're covering for ourselves, and something nine people with different loyalties can't game in the room.

Then run it on three cases. One: the sepsis alert, where the vendor's release notes mentioned the change on page 40 of 41 and our informatics lead didn't flag it. Two: the robot and the pharmacist, where the pharmacist is beloved, did exactly what the validation told them to do, and everyone's instinct is to say the vendor is on the hook even though the contract says otherwise. Three: the chatbot and the dialect issue, where the fix is a change in clinical practice the nurses will hate and that we probably can't staff. I want at least one of these to come out somewhere I don't like — ideally the one that points at this board's own sign-off two years ago for not requiring a monitoring plan. Finish by telling me honestly where the procedure breaks: which of the questions are values calls dressed up as tests, which distinctions from the theory didn't survive the simplification, and the cases it genuinely can't settle where a human has to decide. American English please.
