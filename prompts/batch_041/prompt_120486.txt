I am a novelist. The novels I write are of an unfashionable kind: they take for granted that people have characters, that character is what pressure reveals, and that a reader's interest in a person is an interest in what that person is disposed to do across many different circumstances. For the past year I have been reading the social-psychological literature usually called situationism, and it has worked on me like a slow poison. The famous demonstrations I know by now — the seminary students who walked past a man slumped in a doorway because they had been told they were late; the dime left in a telephone's coin-return slot that apparently decided whether a stranger's scattered papers got picked up; the ordinary people who went on administering what they believed were painful shocks because a man in a lab coat asked them to — and I am not disturbed by any one of them. I am disturbed by the cumulative claim: that consistency of behavior across situations is so weak that the ordinary vocabulary of character — generous, cruel, cowardly, loyal — does no predictive or explanatory work whatever, and that what we call character is a story told afterwards about conduct that was actually produced by the situation.

I do not want a survey of the literature, and I have no use for a reading list. I want your credence, to two decimal places, in one proposition:

P: There exist robust dispositional traits of the kind that both virtue ethics and realistic literary characterization presuppose — traits that (i) predict an individual's behavior, at least moderately, across a range of situations, and (ii) are the right unit for explaining why that individual acted as she did on a particular occasion.

Give me the number, then the decomposition that produces it. In particular I want a probability and one sentence of justification for each of the following, and then the arithmetic that combines them:

1. The empirical term: that the anti-trait reading of the evidence is correct — that the low correlations found in single-occasion experiments are not substantially an artifact of the designs themselves. Consider at least: one-shot observation of strangers, which is close to the least favorable design imaginable for detecting a trait; the difference between strong situations, which leave little room for individual variation, and weak ones, which leave a great deal; aggregation over many occasions, which is where the same person's average behavior begins to look reliably like itself; and the fact that some of these classic studies have not replicated cleanly.

2. The inference term: given that people are moderately consistent, that the consistency is better explained by stable dispositions internal to the agent than by stable environments — the roles, routines, and recurring predicaments that a person's life is made of — or by the longitudinal finding that people's standing relative to one another on broad traits is fairly stable across decades.

3. The conceptual term: that the character/situation distinction is a real distinction rather than a verbal one. My own worry here is that situationism is a purely negative thesis with no positive theory of situations; that the agent's construal of a situation may itself be a stable, trait-like thing, in which case the two sides of the supposed dichotomy are the same kind of thing under different names; and that a theory which can absorb any behavior as the situation is not falsifiable.

4. The ethical term: that what survives terms 1–3 is enough for the central claims of virtue ethics — enough for the claim that a life can be organized around the virtues, and that the fully virtuous person is more than a regulative ideal.

5. The novelist's term: that it is enough for realistic characterization — that there is no viable way of writing persons as morally intelligible and morally interesting once terms 1–3 are granted.

Then tell me how you combine these, and where you think they are not independent (I suspect 4 and 5 are not); say which single term your final number is most sensitive to; and name the one piece of evidence or argument that would move it by more than 0.05.

Be committed. I would rather have your actual view, held and defended, than a map of positions with the choice left to me. If you think the proposition is ambiguous in a way that matters, choose the best reading, say which you chose, and answer under it. And since I am asking for two decimal places, tell me which of your numbers is the one you would least want to defend in public.
