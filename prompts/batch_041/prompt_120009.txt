I'm a backend engineer, no philosophy training, and I've been arguing with a coworker about whether an AI could ever do everything a human mathematician can do. He handed me this and I can't break it, but I don't fully buy it either. (1) Suppose there's some computer program P that captures everything I can prove about arithmetic: whatever I can prove it can prove, and nothing it proves is something I couldn't prove. (2) Assume P is consistent, i.e. it never proves a falsehood. (3) Then by Gödel's first incompleteness theorem there's a sentence G in the language of arithmetic that P can't prove but that is true (it basically says "P can't prove me"). (4) I can walk through the reasoning that gets me to G: I see that P can't prove it, and I see that it's true, so I've proved something P can't prove. (5) So P isn't me after all, contradicting (1). (6) Since P was arbitrary, no program is me, so human mathematical reasoning isn't mechanical. (I think this is the Lucas-Penrose argument? not sure which of them is which.)

Where does this break? My gut says it's somewhere around 3 and 4, partly because the thing seems to prove way too much - it looks like the same move would let me settle the consistency of any system anyone hands me, which would make me some kind of consistency oracle, and I'm pretty sure I'm not that. I also cant shake the feeling that if I'm wrong about my own consistency I'd never be able to tell, and this argument assumes I'd know. But if I drop 4 I lose the whole conclusion, and I'd like to keep 6 if it's affordable. So: which numbered step do I reject, why did it look fine, and what does rejecting it cost me?
