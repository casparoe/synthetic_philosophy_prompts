A seminar reconstruction of practical-reason internalism has led me to a conclusion I cannot accept, and I would like help locating the fault. The view I am trying to state is that a person has a reason to act only if the action bears a suitable relation to that person's existing motivations. The reconstruction runs as follows.

(1) A has a reason to φ if and only if A could arrive at the judgment that he should φ by sound deliberation from the elements already present in his subjective motivational set.
(2) Sound deliberation is deliberation free of factual error and logical error; it is reasoning from what one already wants.
(3) Reasoning from what one already wants can eliminate, combine, rank, or specify existing desires, but it cannot generate a basic desire that was not already present in some form.
(4) Hence, if nothing in A's motivational set would be served by φ-ing, sound deliberation cannot yield the judgment that he should φ.
(5) Hence, by (1) and (4), A has no reason to φ.
(6) Now consider A: a man raised to want, above all else, to spend his life counting blades of grass, and otherwise indifferent. He is fully informed and guilty of no factual or logical error, and no desire of his whatever would be served by his helping a drowning stranger.
(7) By (5), A has no reason to help — not merely no strong reason, but no reason at all.
(8) But this seems wrong. A is not simply differently motivated from us; he is missing something, and his indifference counts against him as a rational agent.

The damage is not confined to (8). If the argument holds, then any claim of the form "there is a reason for A to φ" that does not trace back to A's motivations comes out false, and criticism of the indifferent person can be only causal pressure rather than rational criticism.

I would like to keep (1) if I can, and I certainly want to keep (8). Which step should go, and what does giving it up cost? Explain why the step you reject looked so plausible, and then price the alternatives: I want to know what the thoroughgoing instrumentalist, the internalist who tries to preserve the deliberative condition, and the externalist each have to concede in order to escape the argument. Please commit to a particular diagnosis rather than laying out the options and leaving the choice to me; if more than one repair is available, say which you would take and why the others are worse.
