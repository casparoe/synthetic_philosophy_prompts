Bit of an odd request for you. I got appointed to our water district's long-range planning committee a few months ago — my reward for showing up to too many board meetings — and now I'm stuck in an argument I don't have the tools for.

We have a 30-year supply plan going to a vote next month, and my colleague Dale, who's a good engineer and reads alot, has decided the whole exercise is conceptually confused. He says it's Popper's argument, roughly like this:

(1) What actually happens in 2045 will turn mostly on what people know and can do by then that nobody knows now — cheap desalination, some new irrigation or reuse process, who knows.
(2) Nobody can predict what we'll know later. If you could write down today what the big discovery of 2045 will be, it wouldn't be a discovery — you'd already have it.
(3) So the things that will actually matter in 30 years are exactly the things we can't predict now.
(4) A plan is just a prediction with a budget attached. If the prediction can't be made, the plan is a fiction, and a harmful one, because it locks us into pipes and reservoirs and bond payments we'll regret.
(5) So kill the 30-year plan and do 3-year rolling plans.
(6) And in general there's no such thing as a law of social change. You can describe history after the fact but you can't forecast it, so any social 'science' that claims to is fooling itself.

Here's my problem. (1) and (2) seem to me flatly, obviously true, and I'd rather keep them than give up on planning. But the conclusion strikes me as absurd, and when I push back Dale just says I'm attached to the plan because I helped write it. For what it's worth, our bond covenants require a 30-year demand forecast, so "just do 3-year plans" isn't even a thing we can do — but I don't want to win on that technicality, I want to know if he's right.

So: which step do I give up, and whatever I give up, what does it cost me? I want the actual bill, not just "oh, Popper was more subtle than that." And if you can, tell me what the honest position is for someone who accepts (1) and (2) and still thinks long-range planning is worth doing, because I have to say something coherent at this meeting and I'm not going to stand there quoting a book I havent read.
