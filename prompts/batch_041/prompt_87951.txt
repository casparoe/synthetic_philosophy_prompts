I'm in a methods seminar where every single example of Goodhart's law is either Soviet nail factories or Wells Fargo, and I'm bored of it, so I want to work through something from my own job instead. I work in the finance office of a regional health system -- nine hospitals, mostly rural, we're the biggest employer in three counties -- and about a third of our revenue now comes through a state Medicaid quality-withhold program, so I know the scoring rules better then I'd like to. The shape of it: the state holds back 2% of our base payments and returns it only if our composite quality score clears a threshold. The composite is built from fourteen measures, almost all of them process measures we report ourselves out of the EHR -- screening rates, follow-up within seven days, med rec on discharge, that kind of thing. There's a cap so that no single measure can count for more than 15% of the total. The benchmarks are frozen for three years at roughly the 60th percentile of where everyone sat before the program started. And there's a rule everyone calls the "too good to be true" rule: score in the top 2% two quarters running and you get an automatic audit, and if the auditors decide your documentation practices are inconsistent with the spirit of the measure, you forfeit the whole bonus. The scores are public, the local paper ranks the hospitals every year, and our CEO's comp is tied to ours.

What I can't stop chewing on is what all of this assumes philosophically. Take the too-good-to-be-true rule: to be suspicious of a score that's too high is to assume there's something the score is supposed to be tracking that isn't just the score itself, otherwise how could you be too good at it? But then the payment formula turns around and treats the score as the thing that matters, full stop. So does the program presuppose that quality is a real quantity out there that the measures approximate, or that quality just is whatever the measures say, or neither -- that the measures are levers that drag quality along behind them whether or not they track anything? I also want to know what the 15% cap assumes. Capping each measure implies the fourteen are substitutes for each other and that quality is a weighted sum, an additive kind of value, but freezing the benchmarks implies quality is positional -- you're good insofar as you're ahead of your peers -- and those two dont obviously sit together. And underneath all of it, what does it assume about us? That we're honest reporters who happen to respond to incentives, or strategic maximizers the state has to assume are gaming it?

Reconstruct whatever theory the whole arrangement presupposes, tell me which parts the practice genuinely can't do without versus which parts it could shed and still work, and be straight with me about where it's underdetermined -- I suspect two or three different theories of measurement would license the exact same rulebook, but I can't tell which. Finish with a last line beginning "Verdict:" naming, in one sentence, the single commitment the program can least afford to give up.
