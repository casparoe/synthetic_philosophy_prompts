I keep a list of verdicts about when a life goes well for the person living it, and I would like to know what principle, if any, they add up to.

(1) A woman's closest friend secretly despises her; she never learns this and dies believing the friendship mutual. I judge her life no worse for it: the contempt never entered her experience. (2) A novelist dies believing his last book a masterpiece; it is in fact derivative and forgotten within a year. I judge his life went better than if he had known the truth. (3) A man spends his life in a machine whose experiences are indistinguishable from those of a successful, loved, and admired life. I judge his life went worse than that real life would have. (4) A woman whose single ambition was to be a mother cannot conceive, adopts, and is wholly fulfilled by the child. I judge her life to have gone well, the ambition notwithstanding. (5) A man secures everything he set out to secure—career, marriage, reputation—and reports feeling hollow throughout. I judge that, for him, it went well all the same.

Cases (1) and (2) suggest that facts a person never encounters leave her well-being untouched; case (3) seems to say the opposite, since the machine's whole trouble is a fact its occupant never encounters. Case (4) treats a thwarted desire as harmless, while case (5) treats a satisfied life as insufficient—so desire cannot be the whole story either.

Is there a single account of well-being that delivers all five verdicts, or must I surrender one of them? If so, which, and what does the resulting principle say about a case I have not listed: a man who is content and reports wanting nothing, but only because a stroke has left him unable to want anything else? Please state the principle precisely, note the case that motivates each clause, and say honestly which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate. Keep your answer under 500 words.
