I am in the lower sixth and am writing an extended project on whether animals think and feel, and I have reached the point where I need to know what my own position actually amounts to. I have been keeping a list of cases and my verdicts on them, and I would like you to work out what principle, if any, holds them together.

I began from Descartes, because his is the view that makes my verdicts look inconsistent. He holds that animals are machines: that they neither think nor feel, and that their cries and writhings are no more than the working of their parts. He offers two tests. The first is language: a creature that cannot use words or signs of its own devising to make its thoughts known to others is not thinking. The second is that animals manage some things as well as or better than we do, but never from knowledge; they act from the arrangement of their organs, as a clock keeps time. I think he is right about the spirit of the first test and wrong about almost everything he draws from it, and I need to know whether that combination is stable.

My verdicts:

1. Our dog whines at the door to be let out. I do not think this is language or thought; it is a signal, closer to a doorbell than to a request.

2. My dog, who had never seen a puzzle toy before, worked out within a minute that she had to tip the box onto its side to get the ball out, after trying four or five other approaches. I think that is genuine problem-solving, not mechanism.

3. A honeybee returning from a good patch of flowers dances in a way that tells the others the direction and distance. However intricate it is, and however much it resembles what my dog did, I do not think the bee knows anything. I would call it mechanism.

4. Our old cat, when she was ill, stopped eating and hid under the bed, and would let me sit with her but not touch her. I think she was suffering, in the sense that matters, and that Descartes was simply wrong. What convinced me was not only the behaviour: it was that she behaved as I do when I am ill, and she is made of the same sort of stuff as I am.

5. An octopus unscrews a jar from the inside. I think that is thinking, of some sort.

6. A grey parrot answers questions about the number and colour of the objects on a tray. I do not think that is language, though I cannot say what is missing.

7. A human infant of six months, with no language and no general reasoning, plainly thinks and feels. Descartes agrees with me here, but only because he thinks the infant has a soul, which I do not believe.

8. Someone with advanced dementia, who has lost language and general reasoning, still thinks and feels, and it would be monstrous to say otherwise. This is where the language test, if I keep it, seems to cost me something.

9. A robot that behaved exactly as my dog does — same movements, same noises, same apparent attachments — would feel nothing. I am quite sure of this, and I cannot say why, since in the cat's case it was the behaviour that convinced me.

10. My biology teacher distinguishes nociception, the reflex that pulls a hand off a hotplate, from the experience of pain, and says the first can occur without the second. Applying that, I think a mouse caught in a trap feels pain; applying it to a fly that avoids the swatter, I do not. I cannot give an account of where the line falls.

What I want to know is whether a principle gets all of these, or most of them. I have a tentative one of my own: that Descartes's language test tracks something real about thought, so the bee and the parrot fail it and the dog's whine is not a request; but that feeling is not settled by language at all, and turns instead on the creature being made of the right sort of stuff, which is why the cat and the mouse count and the robot does not. That principle seems to make my verdicts on the box and the jar turn on a matter of degree of flexibility, which I am not sure I want, and it makes my verdict on the bee hard to defend, since her dance is flexible in its own way.

Please state the principle, or the small set of competing principles, that best captures these verdicts, precisely enough that I can see what each clause is doing and which case forces it. Then apply it to cases I have not listed — a crow bending a piece of wire into a hook, a fish on a line, a dog who hides food so that another dog will not find it, a machine that passes every test one can put to it — and tell me honestly which of my ten verdicts the principle cannot accommodate, and whether I should give up the principle or the verdict. I would rather be told which of my verdicts has to go than be told that everything is a matter of degree; and if a simpler principle that sacrifices one verdict is preferable to a complicated one that saves all ten, please say so and say which verdict I should sacrifice.
