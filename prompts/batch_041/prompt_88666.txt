I spent about 35 years as a field ecologist before I retired, and for the last couple of years I've been reading philosophy pretty much full time, and I've gotten myself stuck on something I can't think my way out of.

There's this whole debate about what you're "allowed" to assert — the norm of assertion, the rule that's supposed to govern when it's OK to just come out and flat-out say that something is the case. And there seem to be four or five serious candidates, each with defenders. One camp says you should only assert what you know. Another says knowledge is too strong and truth is enough — you can assert a true thing even if you don't know it. Another says what matters is justified belief, or maybe just belief/honesty. And each camp has its pet examples meant to sink the others: the lottery cases, where you're sure your ticket lost but supposedly can't assert it; the cases where someone says something true but only by luck; the odd sentences where a person says something and then tacks on that they don't know it, or don't even believe it. Then someone patches their rule to survive the counterexample and a new counterexample shows up. It's whack-a-mole.

And as far as I can tell this has been running for decades with nobody moving. Everyone keeps retreating to their own intuitions about the invented cases.

Here's my hypothesis, and I definately expect to be told it's confused. I suspect this isn't really a disagreement about assertion at all. Three possibilities I keep going back and forth on. (a) It's a verbal dispute — the camps are just using the word "assertion" for slightly different acts: saying what you believe, vs. putting something forward as knowledge, vs. committing yourself to defend it. (b) The camps are answering different questions under one heading — one is describing what's going on in a person's head when they make a claim, another is stating a normative rule, another is really about the social function of claiming (what it licenses other people to do: rely on you, resent you if you turn out wrong). (c) It's a disagreement about method — whose intuitions about made-up cases count, or whether case-intuitions should decide this at all.

There's also something that bugs me alot as a scientist. In my field nobody actually follows "only assert what you know." We assert best-supported hypotheses flat-out all the time, and the real norm is more like "assert what the evidence licenses you to and flag your uncertainty" — and that's clearly weaker than knowledge and doesn't fit truth or plain belief either. So maybe the philosophers have quietly picked out one register of assertion (the confident everyday flat-out claim) and generalized from it, when asserting is really something that comes in degrees and varies by community. If that's right it might explain a lot of the stalemate.

What I want is a diagnosis, not a verdict on which norm wins. Using what each side actually does — the examples they lean on, what they say a "norm" is even for, what they'd count as a counterexample, whether they think they're describing a rule we already follow or prescribing one — tell me: is this a substantive disagreement, a verbal one, or several different questions wearing one word? Is a hidden question about the function of assertion doing the work? And given your diagnosis, what would actually count as progress — a distinction, a new kind of case, some empirical work on how people actually use "know" and "assert", or just accepting it can't be settled? Be concrete, and tell me where I'm conflating things, because I'm sure I am.
