I'm running the next session of our reading group and I need a teaching plan, not a summary. The room: six or seven linguistics PhD students, two engineers from a lab that trains language models, nobody with any philosophy training, everyone comfortable with formal machinery. Topic: Bradleys regress, the one about relations and the unity of a proposition — the idea that if a relation is what ties two things together, then the relation itself has to be tied to the things it relates, and off it goes. They'll all nod at the first step and then, I predict, mostly fail to see why anyone ever lost sleep over it, so the plan has to make the problem feel forced rather than quaint.

What order do the pieces go in, and why does each depend on the one before? What single example carries the whole thing — what it has to have and what it can't have (no cat on the mat, and nothing that only bites because the relation is symmetric; I want them to feel the 'something has to glue it together' pull before I show that glue never helps)? What will they get wrong, and where in the sequence do I head each one off: 'so this just shows relations aren't real', 'this is the third-man argument with a new hat', 'an infinite regress is only a problem if you're scared of infinity', 'the relation unifies itself', and someone insisting it's a fact about language and not about the world. Also — the two engineers will definately want to know whether attention is the glue; if that comes up I'd like it to be the last five minutes and not a rescue.

Finish with one question I can put to the room that shows they've got the regress as a demand rather than a reductio, and tell me what I should openly refuse to go into at this level.
