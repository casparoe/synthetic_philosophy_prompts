And here's what one of them turned in:

---

Setup: an eccentric billionaire will put a million dollars in your bank account tomorrow morning if, at midnight tonight, you intend to drink a vial of toxin tomorrow afternoon. The toxin makes you violently ill for about a day but does no lasting damage. He has some way of telling whether you actually formed the intention at midnight. The catch is that you don't have to drink it: the money is yours hours before the drinking is supposed to happen, and you're perfectly free to change your mind.

The reason this is a puzzle and not just a cost-benefit calculation is that the payoff is attached to a mental state rather than to an action. If he were paying you for drinking, you'd just weigh a day of misery against a million dollars and get on with it. But intending isn't like raising your arm — you can't just do it on command, and you can't intend to do something you're sure you won't do. And you're sure you won't drink, because by the afternoon the money is already in the bank and there's nothing left to gain. So at midnight it looks like the intention is simply unavailable, even though it's worth a million dollars.

So the obstacle seems to be your own reasoning. If you could stop yourself from thinking ahead — if you could form the intention at midnight without running the rest of the calculation — you'd get the money and keep your health. That's the sense in which the puzzle is really about whether you can choose your own intentions.

Kavka's own answer, as I read it, is that you should go ahead and drink the toxin. Once you've genuinely formed the intention at midnight, the question at 3pm isn't what maximizes your payoff from scratch; it's what's consistent with the commitment you've already made, and the answer to that is to drink. That's what makes the puzzle interesting for theories that treat intentions as nothing more than predictions about your own future behaviour: on that picture, forming an intention can't have any rational grip on what you later do.

I think the puzzle shows that rational choice theory is just incoherent. You have decisive reason to have the intention (a million dollars) and decisive reason not to drink (a day of misery for nothing), and a fully rational agent can see both, so the intention it has decisive reason to have is one it can't have. The only escape is to be deliberately a bit irrational — to cultivate a kind of self-deception and not follow the reasoning all the way through.
