I am writing in defense of a lexical-threshold negative utilitarianism: the view that disvalue is not merely weighed more heavily than value but ranks above it in kind, so that suffering outweighs pleasure once it passes some threshold, and no finite quantity of pleasure compensates for suffering beyond it. Before I argue for it, I want the specification. Write the desiderata that any theory of the commensurability of pleasure and pain would have to meet to be adequate for adjudicating this dispute: the cases it must get right, the distinctions it may not collapse (for instance between the intensity and the duration of an experience, or between a bad life and a bad moment inside an otherwise good one), and the structural constraints on value it cannot violate. For each requirement, give the consideration that motivates it and mark it as non-negotiable or as an artifact of one camp's commitments. I care most about two things. First, which requirements cannot be jointly satisfied — in particular, whether lexical priority for disvalue can survive the continuity and transitivity conditions that decision-theoretic treatments of value typically impose, and what would have to be surrendered on either side. Second, which single requirement excludes the most otherwise plausible views, and why. Include among the candidates the requirement that the theory be action-guiding under empirical uncertainty, and the requirement that the lexical ranking attach to moments of experience rather than to whole lives; say what each would demand. I know the standard positions, so skip the scorecard.
