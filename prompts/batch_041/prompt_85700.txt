I teach a grad seminar on Dennett and AI, and I'm so tired of teh counterfeit-people discourse. You know the one: Dennett's suggestion that making AI that can pass as human should be illegal, like counterfeiting money, because it corrodes trust. Every term a student writes a paper that just repeats it. I want an assignment that makes them feel the weirdness of the proposal instead of reciting it.

So: write me a mock FAQ, deadpan, from the Office of Authentic Persons at a mid-tier university that has actually adopted a Certified Genuine Person policy for all campus interactions. Around 700-900 words. Voice: bureaucratic, cheerful, a little menacing. It should cover how you prove you're not a chatbot (and why the test keeps changing), what happens if you fail, the appeal process, whether the office tests for consciousness (it doesn't, and it gets huffy about why), why the real test is whether you can be treated as a person under Dennett's intentional stance -- i.e., whether it's useful to predict and explain you that way -- and what that means for the people who fail, plus a sidebar on the black market in human credentials. The jokes should come from getting the Dennettian machinery right: if there's no inner Cartesian theater -- no little screen in the head where the real person sits -- what exactly is the counterfeit supposed to be a copy of? The satire should land the objections -- the metaphor smuggles in a mint that doesn't exist, the policy punishes the appearance of personhood rather than deception, and the people who fail the test are usually the ones already treated as less than persons -- without ever stating them. Don't make it a hit piece on Dennett; target the proposal and the compliance culture it would breed. No direct quotations from Dennett or anyone else; paraphrase if you need to.

If the whole premise is confused -- if Dennett's view doesn't actually support a certification regime, or if the intentional stance makes the very idea of a counterfeit person a category error rather than a policy problem -- say so up front in a couple of sentences and don't write the FAQ. If the task as posed can't be done well -- if the FAQ form flattens the philosophy into a skit -- say that too. I'd rather be told. If you do write it, add a short note at the end on where it takes liberties with Dennett's actual view or leans on a contested reading. Last line, one sentence, in the form Verdict: <...>.
