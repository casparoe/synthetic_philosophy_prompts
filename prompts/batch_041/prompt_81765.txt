So I've been reading alot about evidential cooperation in large worlds lately, and I think I've basically been convinced, but I want to stress-test it before I start actually making life decisions on the basis of it. The view, as I've got it, goes something like: when the world is big enough — lots of agents, many of them causally unreachable, some of them possibly running the same decision procedure as me, some of them maybe even being 'copies' of me in a computational sense — the evidential component of my action's expected value stops being a rounding error and starts to dominate. Defecting in some local one-shot prisoner's dilemma isn't just a bad local move; it's evidence about what all the other instantiations of my decision procedure do in structurally similar spots, and if there are astronomically many of those, then a tiny correlation multiplied by a huge number of them can swamp the causal payoff of the defection. So the recommendation is to cooperate with distant and isolated agents, to reason more like an updateless/functional-decision-theory agent than a plain causal one, and generally to treat "what does my action say about agents like me" as the thing worth maximizing. I'm fairly sure I've seen versions of this argued with actual formal machinery, but I can't reproduce the details and I don't want to just trust the vibe of the sequence I read.

What I want from you is the honest case against it: what would count against this view? Not just "causal decision theory disagrees," I know that. I mean which observations, arguments, intuitions, or formal results should actually move someone who's already sold on evidential decision theory and already thinks the world is large. Some specific worries I'd like your verdict on: (a) the reference-class problem — if there's no non-arbitrary way to fix who my 'correlates' even are, does the view just become indeterminate rather than false, and is that a bug or is that the whole game? (b) the astronomical payoff — if the evidential term can be arbitrarily large, does the view collapse into a Pascal's-mugging-style machine that can be used to justify anything, and if so does that mean no ordinary evidence could ever count against it? (c) the smoking-lesion-style objection, i.e. cases where my action is evidence of a bad outcome without causing it — does the same evidential reasoning that gets me cooperation also get me obviously absurd conclusions, and if so is that a reductio or just a bullet to bite? And (d) whether the whole thing secretly leans on a contentious theory of what makes two physical computations 'the same computation,' such that the correlation is really an artifact of a formalism rather than a fact about the world.

Sort the defeaters by how decisive they'd be, from "this should make you abandon the view" down to "this is merely awkward," and tell me which ones a committed defender would themselves concede as counting against them versus which they'd wave away. Then the meta-question: has the view been qualified into unfalsifiability — "cooperate unless the correlation is negligible, or the payoff is finite, or the reference class is ill-defined" — and if so, is that actually a problem for a view of this kind, or is that just what any sufficiently general decision-theoretic thesis ends up looking like? I'd especially like to know what this view predicts that its rivals don't (plain causal decision theory, or a more modest "evidential considerations matter, but only locally"), since that's what would let me tell whether it's really earning its keep or whether I've just been pattern-matched into agreeing with it.
