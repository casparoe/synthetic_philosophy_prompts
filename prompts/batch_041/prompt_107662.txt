I spent 35 years as a microbiologist before I retired, and I've been reading philosophy of mind for the last four. Here's the thing that's been bugging me since about year two. In my old field, when we said a bacterium senses the sugar and swims up the gradient, everyone understood that was shorthand for a methylation cascade with a crude sort of memory wired into it. Nobody thought the bug was having an experience or making a choice. And as the chemistry got filled in, the words didn't get replaced by anything better — they just quietly stopped being part of the explanation. So I've ended up thinking the same thing will happen to believes and wants. Not reduced, just dropped, the way vital force and protoplasm got dropped. I gather this is called eliminative materialism.

What I want isn't a defense of it. I want a map of what would count against it, sorted — from things that would actually sink it to things that would only be awkward. Specifically: which observations, arguments, or results should move me? Which of those would the eliminativists themselves grant are problems, as opposed to the cheap ones their critics like to recycle? And what does the view predict that its rivals don't — has anything like it already happened somewhere else?

I'll tell you what I'd find most convincing, so you can weigh it. The thing that definately keeps me up is this. A belief can be wrong. A belief that the sugar is to the north can be false, and it can be false because of the world and not because of the brain — the world fails to match up with it. A neuron fires or it doesn't, and there's no such thing as a neuron firing at something wrongly. If that asymmetry is real, then no amount of mechanism reproduces what belief-talk does, and I'd have to give up the whole thing. But I suspect I'm confusing two different things. Tell me if I am, and if I'm not, tell me exactly what it costs me.

Second thing. I've noticed that people who defend this sort of view always seem to end up saying they only mean the propositional attitudes, or that it's a prediction about where psychology will go in fifty years, or that the coarse vocabulary is a useful fiction, like the center of mass. At what point does that stop being a view that could be false? And is that a problem for a view of this kind, or only for people who insist everything be falsifiable?

Last thing. Here's a test I've been trying on myself, tell me whether its any good: a term gets eliminated rather than reduced when the successor theory keeps every prediction, never needs the old word to state its own results, and the old word turns out not to have been naming a real kind at all, not even a fuzzy one. By that test, is belief more like caloric (gone) or more like solid (coarse but still true of tables)? And whichever way you answer, what evidence would flip it?
