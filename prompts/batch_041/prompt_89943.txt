I've long been partial to Wigner's claim that mathematics is unreasonably effective in the natural sciences — that structures developed for internal mathematical reasons keep turning out to describe the physical world, and that this fit is a fact about the world rather than an artifact of how we carve up our intellectual activities. I've also been working with a test for what counts as evidence for it: the mathematics must have been developed before contact with the domain, and must have yielded a prediction that the domain's own practitioners had no independent route to. What would count against the position, and against that test? Rank the defeaters by how decisive they are — the selection-effect worry that we sort activities into mathematics and science only after the fact, so the correlation is guaranteed; the celebrated cases that fail one or both clauses of my test; the rival explanations that account for the same data with fewer commitments; and the suspicion that the claim has been qualified until nothing could disconfirm it. Say which of these a sympathetic defender would concede as real costs and which they would rightly wave off, what the position predicts that its rivals do not, and whether its being well insulated is a defect or just what a thesis at this level of generality looks like. I would be most moved by a case where the mathematics genuinely came first and the prediction stuck.
