Q3 (10 points). In a two-person bargaining problem, S is the set of feasible utility pairs and d = (d1, d2) is the disagreement point. (a) Define the Kalai-Smorodinsky solution, including what the ideal (utopia) point is. (b) State the axioms that characterize the KS solution and say how they differ from the axioms that characterize the Nash solution. (c) Give a numerical example where the two solutions pick different agreements and say what the difference means.

His answer:

(a) The KS solution gives each player the same fraction of what they could have gotten. Formally, for each player i let u_i* be the highest utility player i can get in a feasible agreement where the other player gets at least their disagreement payoff; u* = (u1*, u2*) is the ideal point (sometimes called the utopia point). Draw the straight line from d to u*; the KS solution is the point where that line leaves S, i.e. where it hits the Pareto frontier. Equivalently, at the KS point the two players' gains over the disagreement point are the same proportion of their ideal gains: (x1 - d1)/(u1* - d1) = (x2 - d2)/(u2* - d2).

(b) The characterizing axioms are Pareto optimality, symmetry (if you swap the players you swap the answer), invariance to positive affine transformations of the utility functions, and a monotonicity axiom. The first three are shared with Nash, and the difference is the last one. Nash's characterization uses independence of irrelevant alternatives (IIA): if the point the solution picks in a big problem is still available in a smaller problem, it's still the answer to the smaller problem, so options nobody picks can't matter. Kalai and Smorodinsky thought that was wrong, because an option that's great for one player and terrible for the other gives the first player something to appeal to, so they dropped IIA and used monotonicity instead: if the feasible set grows in a way that raises one player's best attainable payoff while the other player's best attainable payoff stays the same, the first player's payoff at the solution can't go down. Since monotonicity is a strictly stronger requirement than IIA, the KS solution satisfies IIA as well, so really K&S added a requirement rather than giving one up.

(c) Nash picks the feasible point maximising the product of the gains over disagreement, (x1 - d1)(x2 - d2). Example: d = (0,0), player 1 can get at most 1, player 2 can get at most 1.5, feasible set x + y ≤ 2. The ideal point is (1, 1.5), and the line from (0,0) to (1, 1.5) meets the boundary x + y = 2 at (1, 1), so KS gives each player half the surplus, 1 each. Nash's product rule gives (1, 1) here too. When the problem is symmetric both rules agree (if both players could get at most 1, both give (0.5, 0.5)), so the KS solution is the version of the fairness idea that a player with worse options can defend.
