I have been reading a trade book on rationality whose chapter on the lottery paradox and the preface paradox argues that the requirement that a rational agent's beliefs be consistent is simply too strong. Here is the substance of its exposition, as best I can reproduce it:

'A fair lottery with a thousand tickets and one guaranteed winner. For each ticket, the odds that it loses are 999 to 1, so you are entitled to believe of each ticket that it will lose. But you are equally entitled to believe that some ticket will win, since the lottery is fair and must produce a winner. Rationality therefore licenses you to believe a set of things that cannot all be true. The preface paradox is the same argument in everyday dress: an author who has checked her manuscript carefully is entitled to believe every claim in it, and is equally entitled, on the strength of her experience of her own fallibility, to believe that at least one of those claims is false. She too is licensed to hold a contradictory set of beliefs. What both cases show is that the real test of rationality is whether each belief is well supported on its own, and that consistency is a regulative ideal we should give up on: the rational lottery player believes of every ticket that it will lose, and the rational author believes every sentence of her book while also believing that the book contains an error. This is why the paradoxes matter so much for epistemology — they refute the idea that there is any such thing as a logic of belief, since deductive logic tells us that from an inconsistent set of beliefs anything at all follows.'

The setup strikes me as broadly right, and I am inclined to accept the conclusion. But two things nag at me. First, I do not see how someone who believes contradictory things can still be called rational. Second, if anything at all follows from inconsistent beliefs, does the author not end up believing everything — including the negation of every sentence in her book?

Please work through the passage claim by claim and tell me what an epistemologist would accept, what is a standard simplification, and what is actually mistaken. I would especially like to know: (a) whether the two paradoxes really are the same argument, and whether it matters how many tickets the lottery has or how long the book is; (b) whether the claim that one is entitled to believe of each ticket that it will lose is something the paradoxes establish or something they call into question; (c) whether my two worries are well founded, and how a defender of the give-up-consistency line would answer them; and (d) what becomes of all this if we replace all-or-nothing belief with degrees of belief — does the paradox survive the translation, and if it does not, is that a solution or an evasion?
