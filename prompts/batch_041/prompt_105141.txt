I retired last year from a career in field geology, and to my wife's mild alarm I've filled the time since with philosophy — medical ethics in particular, because they still let me sit as the lay member on the clinical ethics committee at the university hospital where I worked for thirty years. My whole job there, as far as I can tell, is to ask the dumb question out loud, which turns out to be useful more often than you'd think.

Here's the thing I need help with. Over the last few years our committee has kept siding with what I've started calling the earlier self — the person somebody used to be back when they were still lucid — against the person they are now. Three cases stick. A man in the early stages of Alzheimer's had signed a very specific directive refusing CPR and antibiotics; by the time he got pnuemonia he was quite demented, but he seemed content and gave no clear instruction either way, and we refused the antibiotics anyway, honoring the directive. A woman with bipolar disorder had left a psychiatric advance directive refusing hospitalization; she arrived manic and refused treatment, and we treated her against her current refusal on the strength of the directive. And a woman with advanced dementia had refused a feeding tube years earlier; by now she said, in a flat and unconvincing way, that she wanted to live, and we declined the tube, judging that wish to be an artifact of the disease. So the pattern reads: once the person is no longer really the same person, we let the old one win.

Now the new case, and it's the one that's got me stuck. A woman in her fifties with chronic kidney failure — no cognitive impairment whatsoever, sharp as a tack. Fifteen years ago, during a bad stretch of her life, she signed a directive refusing dialysis and ventilation if she ever became dependent on them. She is now entirely herself, and she has changed her mind completely: she remarried, she wants the dialysis, she wants everything done. No dementia, no mania, no rupture of any kind — the same woman, with new values, and the change is one she'd call fully her own. My younger colleagues say the old directive binds her, that taking a person seriously means holding her to her own earlier decision. I'm not so sure, and I suspect we've been quietly running a rule about identity that simply doesn't reach this case. So: predict what our committee will decide if it stays consistent with itself; tell me which rule it's actually been running, and I'd bet there's more than one that fits all three precedents — maybe one about psychological continuity, one that just says a competent decision always binds, one about whether the present wish can be trusted; say what each of those predicts for the dialysis case; give me your best guess and why; name the single further case that would best tell the candidate rules apart; and tell me plainly what principle we'd be adopting if we went either way. Keep it under 600 words, and please write in American English.
