I write smart contracts for a living — not a researcher, just someone who ships escrow contracts, commit-reveal schemes, multisigs, hash-locked conditional payments, and who's been burned enough by MEV, reentrancy and deployment ordering that I think in terms of deployed bytecode, verified source, gas, storage slots, and the question "what can this thing actually enforce, and against whom". My formal game theory is thin: I know Nash equilibrium as "nobody wants to unilaterally change what they're doing", and that's about it. So when a friend in a theory group described a construction to me (he called it program equilibrium) I could half-follow it but I can't shake the feeling that he was describing something I already build, or something I can't build at all. The setup as I understood it: two players don't pick moves, they each submit a *program*; both programs go to a referee, which runs each one with the other program as input, and the resulting outputs are played in some base game (his example was a one-shot prisoner's dilemma); the programs are allowed to read each other's source code, or at least to compare it to their own. An equilibrium is then a pair of programs where neither player wants to swap in different code knowing what the other submitted. The payoff is that you get mutual cooperation in a one-shot PD with no repetition and nobody enforcing anything, via a recipe like "if the opponent's program is textually identical to me, cooperate, otherwise defect" — a clone cooperates, anything else gets defected on, and since mutual defection is worse for me than mutual cooperation, deviating doesn't pay. He also mentioned a folk-theorem-flavoured result: exactly the payoffs that are feasible and better-than-minimax for each player are achievable, the same condition as in repeated games but without the repetition. And he mentioned the standard complaint, that the whole thing is fragile — if one player's copy has an extra space character in it, both programs defect and you're back to mutual defection.

What I want from you: restate that construction, as faithfully as you can, in the vocabulary I actually use. Go piece by piece rather than in one blob — what the player's program corresponds to (a contract? a deployed bytecode hash? a constructor argument? a signed message? a commitment?), what the referee corresponds to, what "reading the opponent's source" corresponds to, what the equilibrium condition corresponds to, what the clone recipe corresponds to, and what the folk-theorem result corresponds to. Then give me the residue report: which of those translations are clean, which only work if you fudge something and what exactly you had to fudge, and which parts of the construction have no counterpart at all in how contracts work. I'm especially suspicious of one spot, so please answer it directly rather than hedging: the theory seems to buy cooperation *without* an enforcer, whereas basically everything I build is a way of borrowing the chain's enforcement, so I suspect the mapping inverts at exactly the point that matters — the referee in his story has no power except running the programs, and the EVM in my story is precisely the thing doing the enforcing. If that's right, tell me what the inversion costs, and if it isn't, tell me why not.

Two more specific things I want in the report. First, the theory seems to assume both programs are handed over and then run together, and that a program can inspect the other one for free; on chain I can't simulate arbitary code without paying for it, I can't call into a contract that hasn't been deployed yet, and whoever deploys second gets to read the first one — so I want to know which of the results survive contact with deployment ordering and cost, and whether "hash-committed to code identical to mine" is the real counterpart of the clone recipe or a cheap imitation of it. Second, the whitespace fragility landed weirdly hard for me, because I've hit its exact on-chain analogue: two contracts with identical source can compile to different bytecode (compiler version, settings, the metadata that gets appended), so "same code" and "same hash" come apart in ways that bite me in practice — tell me whether that makes the construction more fragile than he let on, or whether it's the opposite, i.e. the fragility is a feature because it's what makes deviation detectable. And don't just tell me in general that translations lose something — make a call on each bit of residue: is it a gap in my vocabulary, or is it a sign that program equilibrium is doing less work than it looks like? If the honest answer is that my framing quietly presupposes the very thing (a trusted, enforcing referee) the construction was designed to do without, say so, and tell me what the construction is actually buying that a smart contract isn't.
