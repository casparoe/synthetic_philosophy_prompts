I teach a graduate seminar on the ethics of memory, and this term my students and I have been trying to make sense of a pattern in how institutions handle contested commemorations — a pattern that strikes me as philosophically unstable, and I would like your help in saying precisely why. When the object is a bronze figure on a plinth, the dominant response among the institutions I have compared notes with is not removal but retrofitting: the statue stays where it is, and a new plaque, an interpretive panel, or a commissioned counterwork is installed beside or around it. When the object is a building's name — a donor's or a founder's name over a library door, on a lecture hall, on an endowed chair — the dominant response is removal, with the name struck and nothing put in its place. When the object is a street name, the response is increasingly additive: a number of cities now permit a block to be co-named, so that an honorary second name is added to a sign that keeps its official one, and the old honoree and the new one share the plaque. And in a couple of planning documents I have read, there is a fourth device that seems to me the most interesting of all: the sunset monument, built with an expiration date, subject to review, renewal, or demolition after a fixed term of years, on the model of a license rather than a deed.

I am not asking which of these responses is the right one. I am asking what each presupposes, and whether they can be held together. What conception of commemoration must be in play for a plaque to neutralize a statue but not to neutralize a name, and for an added name to neutralize a street sign that a removed name could not neutralize? Do these practices commit their users to the view that a monument is an act of honor, an act of assertion, an act of the community's self-definition, or merely an instrument for producing certain effects in passersby — and which of those views does each device quietly rule out? Where the devices seem to conflict, is the conflict real, or is there a single underlying theory that would license all four, perhaps because the differences track something other than the metaphysics of commemoration, such as the reversibility of the act, the identity of the agent who performed it, or the audience it reaches? I would like you to reconstruct the implicit theory as carefully as you can, to distinguish what these practices strictly presuppose from what they merely fit comfortably with, to tell me where they are philosophically underdetermined so that several theories would license the same behavior, and finally to say what would have to change in our commemorative practice if we abandoned that implicit theory for a rival one — say, for a view on which public commemoration is a promise rather than a claim, or on which it is nothing but a matter of who controls the visible surface of the city. Please write your answer in American English.
