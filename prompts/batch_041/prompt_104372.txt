Write me a letter from a home-insurance claims adjuster denying a claim, around 600 words. Heres the premise: the insurance company exists in a world where Ash'arite occasionalism is simply the official metaphysics — taught in schools, assumed in court, printed on the back of the policy. So nothing in the house caused the fire. The faulty wiring didn't do it, the candle didn't do it, the oxygen didn't do it; God creates the burning at the instant the flame meets the cotton, and the flame is only the occasion, the thing He customarily pairs it with. The adjuster has to deny the claim in the flat, polite, slightly apologetic register of a real denial letter — date, policy number, "Dear Mr. ___, we have reviewed your claim," the whole thing — and never once wink at the reader. No smugness, no "of course we all know." She believes it. Everyone believes it.

The funny has to come from the metaphysics being right, not from being quirky. Things I want her to trip over: the policy's "custom and habit" clause, which lets the company price risk off observed frequencies while denying that any of those frequencies reflect a real necessary connection; the fact that the company's actuaries are basically Humeans with worse theology; the atomist bit, where accidents are recreated each instant, so the house that was insured on Tuesday is not, strictly speaking, the same house that burned on Wednesday — she should have to work around this and find it slightly annoying rather than dramatic; the kasb business, where the homeowner's negligence is genuinely his and genuinely blameable but still not causally productive of anything, so the adjuster has to affirm his fault and deny his efficacy in the same paragraph; a line excluding miracles from underwriting, since a miracle is not logically impossible, only off the customary schedule, and the company won't price that; and a closing note that since God is the sole agent there's no party left to subrogate against, so the file is closed. She should also, without comment, recommend the insured keep taking ordinary precautions, since God customarily doesn't create safety for people who do nothing.

The standard objections to occasionalism should land on their own without anyone stating them — the collapse of natural science into a list of habits, God as the direct author of every house fire and every atrocity, the fact that the occasionalist still locks his door. A specialist should recognize someone who knows the debate; a general reader should just find it funny. About 600 words, one letter, no numbered clauses.
