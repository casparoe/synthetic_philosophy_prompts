I'm a philospohy professor — philosophy of science by training, but I keep getting dragged into historiography, and now I've got a paper I have to defend in front of people who do this for a living.

The paper's line is this: the Middle Ages / Renaissance / Scientific Revolution triad doesn't describe anything in the past. It's a filing system we built after the fact and then forgot we built, and the work it actually does is comparative and ideological — it fixes where Europe's 'modernity' begins and turns everything before it and outside it into a waiting room. My positive claim is that we shouldn't go looking for better periods; we should stop treating periods as explanatory units at all and do problem-based, scale-based history instead. I'm not saying nobody in 1500 felt like something new was happening. I'm saying the boundaries are ours.

Next month it's a workshop and the room is stacked in a very particular way. Two early modernists whose entire careers live inside 'the Scientific Revolution' and who are going to hear this as me saying their subfield is fake. A medievalist who basically agrees with me but will come at me from the other side, because I'm still letting 'the Middle Ages' sit there as a coherent backdrop. A global historian who thinks the whole triad is a Eurocentric export and wants to know what my alternative does for the parts of the world that were never medieval to begin with. And one philosopher of science who's going to ask whether 'real' is even the right predicate to be applying to a period.

So draft me a Q&A prep sheet: the objections in the order they'll actually come, phrased the way each of those four whould phrase them, which ones actually do damage and which are just reflex, a two-minute reply for each, what I should concede in the first thirty seconds, where each of my replies is soft, and the one question I'm hoping nobody asks. Don't cite anything at me or tell me who said what — I know the literature, I want the argumentative structure.
