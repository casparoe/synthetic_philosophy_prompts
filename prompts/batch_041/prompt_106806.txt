I'm putting together a reading group on decision theory and we're using the old Appointment in Samarra story as a literary companion to the Death in Damascus problem. You probably know the tale: a servant in Baghdad sees Death in the marketplace, Death makes a threatening gesture, the servant borrows a horse and gallops off to Samarra to escape, and then Death tells the master she was surprised to see the servant in Baghdad because she had an appointment with him in Samarra that night. I want a reading of the story through the decision theory, not just a summary. Does the servant's flight look rational under evidential decision theory, or is he making exactly the fatalist mistake that causal decision theory warns about? Or is the story rigged so that any action leads to the same outcome, and the real question is whether he could have known that? Take the story seriously as a piece of philosophical fiction, not just an illustration. Say what it dramatizes that the decision theory thought experiment leaves out -- the panic, the feeling of being trapped, the fact that the prediction is about him specifically and not some abstract agent. And where does the story resist the reading? Maybe the servant's flight isn't a decision under uncertainty at all but something more like a self-fulfilling prophecy, or maybe the story is a revenge fantasy about the servant being punished for trying to outsmart fate. I don't need citations or references, just a careful reading that takes both the story and the decision theory seriously.
