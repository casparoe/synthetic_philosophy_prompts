You are a game theorist and philosopher of practical rationality, and I want you to act as a diagnostician rather than a summariser. Before you answer, think the problem through carefully and set out what each move in the argument is doing and which assumption is load-bearing; only then tell me where it fails. I do not want a literature review or a reading list — I want your own reasoning, in British English.

Some background, so that we are working with the same toy model. Game theorists have a construction they call program equilibrium. Two agents play a one-shot Prisoner's Dilemma, but instead of choosing cooperate or defect directly, each is modelled as submitting a program that takes the other player's program as input and returns an action. Because my program can be written as 'cooperate if the other program cooperates with mine, otherwise defect', and yours can be written the same way, mutual cooperation is an equilibrium of the meta-game — even though the underlying game is played exactly once, with no repetition, no reputation and no third party to enforce anything.

A colleague has pushed this much further, and I cannot decide whether the resulting argument is sound or merely seductive.

(1) In the model above, two agents achieve mutual cooperation in a one-shot Prisoner's Dilemma.
(2) Nothing does the work except the agents' mutual access to one another's decision procedures; no repetition, reputation, contract or external enforcement is required.
(3) Therefore any two agents who can anticipate each other's choices well enough to condition on them can secure any outcome they both prefer to the one they would otherwise reach.
(4) People can very often anticipate each other's choices: we know how our friends, colleagues and rivals reason, and we know what they expect of us.
(5) So ordinary human interaction is already largely a matter of program equilibrium, and the rational policy is to become the sort of agent whose cooperation is predictable to those one deals with.
(6) It follows that the one-shot, no-commitment, purely self-interested encounter — the state of nature, the anonymous market transaction, the collective action problem — is a fiction, since no agent's procedure is in principle unpredictable to the other party.
(7) And so the standard explanation of failed cooperation — that self-interested agents in one-shot settings defect — never explains any actual failure of cooperation. Something else is always doing the work.

I am quite happy to give up (5) through (7); what I should like to keep, if I can, is (1) and (2). Tell me which step you reject, why that step looked so plausible in the first place, and what rejecting it costs — in particular, what I would then have to say about why cooperation between strangers ever succeeds, and whether anything survives of the claim that program equilibrium is a general model of rational cooperation rather than an idealised special case.
