Alright, I've been defending a Leibniz-flavoured package in my seminar for a few weeks now and its started to feel like I'm getting away with something. Before I write it up I want an audit. Here's the package:

(1) Nothing is just so. Every truth — including the truth about which world exists — has a sufficient reason why it's this way and not some other way. No brute facts, not even at the bottom.

(2) God is essentially good, and his goodness inclines him to create, so the world he actually makes has to be the best one there could be — best overall, everything counted, not best by some parochial measure like most pleasure or least pain.

(3) The creatures in it are genuinely free: when I choose, I could have done otherwise, and nothing outside me settles the choice. It's my choice in the strong sense, not just the sense that I did what I wanted.

(4) Evil isn't a thing God manufactured and inserted into the world. It's a privation — the absence of a good that ought to be there. A hole in the cloth, not a thread of a different colour.

(5) Where God permits a particular evil, it's because that evil belongs to the package: the greater goods in this world are compossible with those evils and not without them, so you can't lift the evil out and keep the good. That's what makes best of all possible worlds more than a bumper sticker.

(6) God's choice to make this world was free — his goodness leans him toward the best without forcing him, he wasn't cornered into it.

My seminar-mates keep telling me these can't all be true and I keep not seeing where the knife goes in. Sort it out for me: which of these actually pull against each other, which pairs only look like they clash, and for the real ones rank them by severity, tell me the cheapest patch for each, and say what the patch costs me. I want an audit, not a general lecture on theodicy — tell me what has to give, not what Leibniz definately meant.
