Ok so I'm running a session on decision theory for a mixed group, about 18 undergrads, mostly philosophy with a few econ people, 75 minutes in a normal seminar room. I don't want to do another lecture on Newcomb's problem, I want them to actually run into the thing first. The thing I care about is the Death-in-Damascus loop, but I don't want to name it or even describe it up front — I want them to walk into it and only get told what it was called afterwards.

Quick reminder of the case if its fuzzy for you: you're in Damascus, Death tells you it's coming for you tomorrow, you panic and flee to Aleppo, and then it turns out you were always going to die in Aleppo — that's why Death was surprised to see you in Damascus the day before. The bite is that whichever city you pick, that turns out to be the fatal one, so "ok then I'll pick the other one" just slides you into the same spot, over and over, and there's no choice that survives you thinking it through. I want the room to physically run that loop, not hear about it.

So design me the activity. I need to be able to actually run the predictor at the front of the room and have it be reliably accurate — I'm happy for that accuracy to be built into the rules in some way, but it should feel like a real prediction to them at least for the first round, and it needs to survive me doing it live with 18 people, not just in theory. Give me the rules, the materials, what I say and when, a payoff scheme that doesn't need real money (tokens, a scoreboard, whatever), and a timeline I can hold to in 75 minutes.

I especially want the structure to force the flip on them: whatever they commit to in round one should come back and make round two feel like it argues for the other side, and round three should feel like it argues back, so they notice they're going in circles and there's no resting place. If some of them try to beat it by randomizing (coin, die, rock-paper-scissors) don't shut that down — I want that to happen, it's exactly the interesting move, but I need to know how to harvest it in the debrief. Same with the ones who'll say "fine, I just won't play" or "I'll go to a third city" — tell me whether to pre-empt that in the rules or let it happen and use it.

Then the debrief: map the moments of the game onto the actual decision-theory distinctions, especially the split between "my choice causes the outcome" and "my choice is just evidence about the outcome," and the awkward fact that when every option looks equally (un)attractive the theory has to add an extra rule just to stop deliberating. Don't assume they've read anything.

Also be straight with me about two things: what predictably goes wrong the first time I run this and how to handle it in the moment, and which part of the idea the activity honestly can't reproduce so I know what I have to supply myself with my own voice rather than pretending the game did it.
