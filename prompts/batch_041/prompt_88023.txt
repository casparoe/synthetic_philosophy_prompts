I am a third-year undergraduate and the current president of my department's student society. In three weeks the department will be absorbed into a newly created School of Humanities: the merger preserves our name and our faculty, but from next fall the school will admit our students, hold our budget, and award our degrees. The university is holding a final reception for the department, and I have been asked to give the closing remarks — roughly four minutes, to about sixty people. Some of them are faculty and graduate students who will catch every technical allusion; the rest are alumni, staff, and family who will catch none.

I am taking a course on vagueness this term, and it has left me unable to deliver the obvious speech. The obvious speech says either that the department ends tonight or that it survives in the new school, and I do not think either is the sort of thing one can simply assert. On the view I find most convincing, a sentence about a vague matter is true just in case it is true on every admissible way of sharpening the vague term, false just in case it is false on every such sharpening, and otherwise neither true nor false — which is why "the department will exist next year or it will not" still comes out true even though neither of its disjuncts does. My closest friend in the same course holds the opposing view: that there is a sharp fact about exactly when a collection of people and practices stops being a department, and that we are merely unable to know it. I want remarks that both of us could sign off on, sentence by sentence.

Please write the speech. Every sentence should be true on every admissible sharpening of "our department": nothing that depends on choosing a boundary, nothing that treats tonight as a death, nothing that treats the new school as a straightforward continuation. It must nonetheless work as a warm, ordinary farewell, with no trace of a seminar in it, and it should end on a line I can raise a glass to. Then add a short note for me rather than for the audience, listing the four or five things I most wanted to say and had to cut or rewrite, and what each of them would have committed me to.
