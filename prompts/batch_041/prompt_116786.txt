I am a high school junior and the student representative on my district's technology advisory committee, and I have a meeting next week for which I need to prepare.

Since the start of the semester, the school-issued laptops have been running a monitoring program the district bought from a vendor. It reads what students write in their school accounts and raises a "concern flag" when the language matches certain patterns. Flags are supposed to go to a counselor for review. The program also assigns each flag a category by itself, and under a rule our IT director wrote, anything in the top category automatically sends a notice to the student's parents. The notice is generated from a template and goes out whether or not a person has looked at the flag first.

A student in my grade wrote a first-person short story for English about a character who wants to disappear. The program put it in the top category. The counselor who normally reviews flags was out that week and had arranged coverage. The principal, following the procedure he was given, pulled the student out of class and sent the template notice to the parents before any counselor had read the story. The parents were badly frightened. The student was taken out of class in front of everyone, and the story was forwarded to the district office. Since then the student has refused to write anything personal for school and has asked to transfer. The family has hired a lawyer.

Everyone involved can explain why they did what they did. The vendor says the system performed exactly as designed and that the flags were always labeled advisory. The IT director says the top-category rule exists because a genuine warning sign was missed last year. The principal says he followed the procedure he was given and had no way to know the flag was about fiction. The counselor says she was absent and had arranged coverage. The teacher says she had no idea the software was reading the assignment. Nobody intended to hurt anyone. The contract caps what the vendor owes us, the district cannot afford to replace the system this year, and our committee can only recommend anyway — the school board decides. So I am not asking for a legal answer.

What I want is help thinking it through. I keep separating in my head the question of who caused this from the question of who should have to answer for it, and I cannot tell whether that separation is doing any real work. I also have a proposal I want you to be honest about rather than encouraging: I want to recommend that the committee formally accept responsibility for what happened and keep a public record of it, even though no member of the committee did anything wrong. A friend told me this is meaningless — that you cannot take responsibility for something you did not do — and I do not have an answer for him.
