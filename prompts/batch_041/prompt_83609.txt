I write about the foundations of machine learning and statistics, and for some time I've been trying to understand why the argument over whether algorithmic information theory justifies Occam's razor has run for more than half a century without either camp giving ground. I want a diagnosis of the disagreement, not a verdict on who is right.

Here is how the two sides look from where I sit. One camp points to the universal prior and the invariance theorem: for any computable source, a predictor that weights hypotheses by their description length converges to the truth, and the choice of reference machine supposedly cannot matter because any two universal machines agree up to an additive constant. From this they conclude that preferring short descriptions is a rational guide to truth — that simplicity, cashed out as compressibility, is genuinely truth-conducive. The other camp answers that the convergence is only asymptotic, that the additive constant is unbounded and can swamp everything at any finite amount of data, that at every realistic sample size the choice of machine is doing all the explanatory work, that the measure is uncomputable and therefore cannot be what any actual inference is using, and that defining simplicity relative to a machine we have no independent reason to trust makes the whole justification circular. Some in this second camp also press the no-free-lunch point: a bias toward simple sources can only help if the world is itself biased toward them, which looks like an empirical premise smuggled in as a theorem.

My own suspicion — and I want you to treat it as a hypothesis to test rather than a conclusion — is that the two sides are answering different questions under a single heading. One is asking whether an idealized, computationally unbounded agent's beliefs must converge; the other is asking whether a bounded agent's actual practice is warranted. If that is right, then Occam's razor is naming two distinct claims: that nature is simple, and that we should proceed as though it were.

I also want you to consider a distinction I've been mulling, which may or may not be doing real work. There is the simplicity of a hypothesis — the description length of a model — and there is the simplicity of the source, the description length of the process that actually generated the data. These can come apart: a very short hypothesis can be about a very complicated source, and a long hypothesis can be about a simple one. If the debate keeps conflating the two, that alone might explain why the camps talk past each other. Tell me whether it does, or whether the distinction collapses on inspection.

What I need from you is a diagnosis with reasons: is this a substantive disagreement, a verbal one about the word "simplicity," a hidden formal question that a theorem could settle, a disagreement about which cases count as the data (toy computable sources versus the messy inferential practice of actual science), or a clash of starting intuitions about what a justification is even supposed to deliver — an external guarantee about the world or a self-consistent norm for inquiry? Then tell me what would count as progress given your diagnosis: a distinction, a formal result, a change of question, or an honest argument that no progress should be expected and why. Please engage the positions as I've stated them rather than surveying the field, and do not give me citations or a reading list — I'll track those down myself.
