I teach a graduate seminar in the philosophy of history, and I have reached the point of suspecting that the criterion of testimony I have been teaching for fifteen years is a bundle of habits wearing a criterion's name. I would like you to reconstruct the principle, or the small set of principles, that actually generates the verdicts below, test it against cases I have not considered, and be blunt about which of my verdicts it cannot save.

My working formulation has been this: a report about the remote past warrants belief to the degree that it would have been unlikely to have been made unless it were true. I no longer trust it. The cases are types rather than episodes from one period, which is how I use them in class.

1. The terms of a treaty survive only in the chancery record of the losing party, which sets out its own king's concessions in detail. I treat this as strong evidence for the terms.

2. Two chronicles, written a generation apart, give the same casualty figures and the same sequence of maneuvers, but both plainly descend from a single court bulletin composed just after the campaign. I do not count them as two witnesses; my confidence is barely above what it would be with one.

3. My main evidence for the route an army took is a chronicler whose dates are systematically wrong by a decade and whose manuscript contains a charter I regard as forged. I trust him on the route.

4. A hostile annalist reports that the enemy king committed an atrocity against a third people. I discount this heavily.

5. A community maintains a cycle of songs and a set of knotted cords recording a coastal inundation, and the inundation corresponds to a geological event dated independently. I accept the inundation and reject the migration narrative that the same cycle attaches to it.

6. The official records of a kingdom, otherwise detailed for a given decade, are silent about a battle that later tradition places in that decade and region. I take the silence as evidence that no such battle occurred.

7. A document composed two centuries later than it purports to be, and in my view a forgery, contains incidental references to administrative titles and place names that archaeology confirms. I use it as evidence for the titles and place names, not for the events it narrates.

8. A king's monumental inscription attributes his defeat to his own impiety and to the abandonment of the gods. It is against his interest, yet the genre makes such self-abasement a standard move, and I do not treat the inscription as strong evidence that the defeat occurred.

9. A single late source reports that a famous man's mother was a slave. Nothing corroborates it. I believe it, on the ground that nobody in that society invented genealogies that lowered a family's standing.

I take 1 and 9 to be the same principle, and 8 to be that principle failing in the same form. I take 2 and 5 to pull in opposite directions, since 2 says that two chains sharing an origin count as one while 5 says that a single chain, well anchored from outside, can carry a fact across many centuries. Cases 3 and 7 both say that reliability is local. Case 6 says that missing evidence counts when it was to be expected.

What I want from you:

- A precise statement of the principle or principles that best fit these verdicts, with each clause tied to the case that forces it.
- Your verdicts, on the principle you propose, for at least two further cases of your own construction, one of which should be a case where the principle's two halves seem to point different ways.
- An honest accounting of which of my verdicts the principle cannot accommodate. Where something has to go, I would rather keep 1, 2, 6, and 9 and surrender one of the others; tell me which surrender is cheapest and why.
- A verdict on the case I keep going back and forth on: an oral genealogy of eight generations, recited on formal occasions, which agrees with a written king-list at three points and diverges at the rest, and which the community treats as a record of descent rather than of events.

No citations or reading list, please; I know the literature well enough to place your answer, and what I want is the structure of the principle. Where your answer turns on something historians and philosophers of historiography disagree about, say so plainly and separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested, rather than presenting a contested position as the consensus.
