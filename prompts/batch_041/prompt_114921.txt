I am designing a session for a reading group of graduate students in linguistics and cognitive science. They are comfortable with formal semantics at the level of possible-worlds intensions, but they have had almost no exposure to modal metaphysics, and several of them have told me they think necessary just means true by definition and a posteriori just means empirical and therefore contingent. The topic is rigid designation and the necessary a posteriori, and I want to teach it through a case I have invented rather than the usual morning star and evening star example, because I worry that the usual example has become a slogan they can repeat without understanding. Here is my case: a community has two names, Dawn and Dusk, for what turns out to be a single planet; Dawn is used for the bright object visible before sunrise, Dusk for the bright object visible after sunset; no one suspects they are the same object until a telescope reveals the identity. After the discovery, the community says Dawn is Dusk. Plan how I should explain the idea, not the idea itself.

Give me the order in which the pieces must be introduced and why each depends on the previous one: what has to come first for the case to do its work—what must they already understand about names, objects, possible worlds, and the difference between how a proposition is known and whether it could have been false? What does the case need to include, and what should it deliberately leave out, so that the necessity does not look like a trick? Which misconceptions will they form at each stage—for example, that rigid designation means the object exists in every possible world, that the identity is necessary only after we discover it, that a posteriori truths are contingent, or that the necessity is a fact about our language rather than about the planet—and exactly where in the sequence should I head each one off? Finally, give me one question I can put to the group at the end that would show whether they have grasped the distinction rather than merely memorized the vocabulary, and tell me what I should openly refuse to cover at this level (for instance, the necessary a posteriori for natural kinds, the de dicto and de re distinction, or the controversy over essentialism) and why refusing is better than a rushed gesture. Please write your answer in American English.
