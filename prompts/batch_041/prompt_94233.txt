I teach an upper-level undergraduate seminar on meaning and reference — fourteen students, one ninety-minute session. Design a runnable in-class activity that makes them feel the force of semantic externalism — the claim that the reference of a kind term is fixed by the world rather than by anything inside the speaker's head — without my ever naming the view or describing Putnam's Twin Earth before the activity ends. Structure it as two rounds sharing one architecture. In each round, split the room into two groups that believe they are two offices of a single firm which have communicated only in writing. Give each group a sealed dossier describing the stock it handles: in the first round the dossiers specify a natural kind by its composition (make the two compositions differ while stipulating that every observable, testable property is identical); in the second round they specify an institutional kind whose conditions of application diverge between the two offices. Each pair of offices must negotiate a single shared label and a single written guarantee for the good, then separately report whether they agree about what they have been handling. Arrange the rules so that they will believe they agree and the twist lands only when I open both dossiers at the end. Include roles, materials, a minute-by-minute timeline for the ninety minutes, the exact wording of the instructions I give each group, and a debrief that maps each moment of the game onto the externalist claim and onto the contrast between natural-kind and social/institutional terms. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle it, and be explicit about which feature of externalism the activity cannot reproduce and that I will have to supply in the debrief. In the debrief material, separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who work on this; in particular, do not present any contested position about how far externalism extends to normative or institutional terms as though it were the consensus.
