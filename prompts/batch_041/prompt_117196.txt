Last weekend my flatmate and I finally sat down to divide the contents of the flat we are giving up, and what should have been an hour with a spreadsheet turned into the longest argument we have had in four years — not about the record player, which we settled in ninety seconds, but about what it is to divide anything fairly. He kept returning to a picture he could not quite name: you begin at the point you would be at if you agreed on nothing, draw a straight line to the point at which each of you got everything he could possibly want — a point that is obviously not on offer to both of you at once — and take the place where that line runs into the edge of what is jointly possible. On his view the two of you should always gain in the same proportion as the line does, and the whole answer must survive being re-denominated: hours for objects, money for hours. I kept returning to the thought that we should take the division that makes the product of our gains as large as possible, and, more stubbornly, that the appearance of some new thing that neither of us ends up taking ought not to move the terms at all — I have since learned that this last insistence is called independence of irrelevant alternatives — whereas his line seemed to shift the moment anything new came into view. It was only the following morning, over the washing-up, that I realised he had been reciting the Kalai–Smorodinsky solution and I had been reciting Nash's, and that we had not been arguing about the flat at all. We were both in the same game theory course last year, which is presumably why neither of us could let it go.

I am closing the reading group's term with a poem on bargaining, and I want the form to carry the argument rather than merely gesture at it. So: sixteen to twenty-four lines, in the voices of the two of them as they divide what they had together, advancing in paired lines — one thing for each of them per pair — with the pairs holding a fixed proportion between their two shares as the poem travels from the point of maximum disagreement (nothing agreed, the solicitors, the spreadsheet) towards the ideal in which each of them gets everything they could ask for. The poem must stop exactly where that trajectory meets the boundary of what is jointly possible: the final pair should be the last pair that can both be honoured, and the pair that would have come next must be left unwritten, in such a way that a reader feels it is impossible rather than merely absent.

Three further conditions, because these are the properties I want a game theorist in the audience to recognise without my having to say so: the two of them must be interchangeable, so that swapping their names leaves the poem's shape untouched; nothing may depend on a unit, so that the poem reads identically whether the goods are being counted as objects, as hours or as money; and no pair may take back what an earlier pair granted, so that the shares only ever grow. Somewhere near the middle, let something come into view that neither of them ends up taking — a thing one of them could have had, and will not — and let it move the terms anyway, since that is precisely the moment at which the poem stops being Nash's. A short prose note afterwards, mapping the disagreement point, the ideal point, the boundary and those four properties onto the poem's parts, would help me hand it out; but the poem has to do the work on its own for someone who has never seen the axioms.
