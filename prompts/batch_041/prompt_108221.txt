My friend Devon has been cornering people at parties with an argument he claims proves he can do ornithology from his sofa, and none of us can find the bad step. He got the setup from a philosphy of science class — Hempel, ravens, all that — but he's pushed it further than the lecture did, so I don't trust my own read. Here it is roughly as he writes it:

(1) If two hypotheses are logically equivalent, then any evidence that confirms one confirms the other to exactly the same degree.
(2) Seeing a thing that is both F and G confirms 'every F is G'.
(3) 'Every raven is black' is logically equivalent to 'every non-black thing is a non-raven'.
(4) So a white shoe — non-black, and not a raven — confirms that every raven is black.
(5) How much an observation confirms a hypothesis depends on the two of them alone: on their contents and the logical relations between them. It can't depend on how the observation was obtained, or on what the observer already believed or already knew.
(6) Devon has seen white walls, a red sock, a grey cat, green apples, a beige carpet. Each of those is non-black and not a raven, so by (4) each confirms that all ravens are black; by (5) each adds its fixed bit, whether he went out looking or was just sitting there, and whether or not he already knew perfectly well that a sock is not a raven.
(7) Bits accumulate. A few thousand sightings like that and he has strong evidence that every raven is black. Same trick for 'every elephant is grey' (the green apples are non-grey and not elephants), for 'every swan is white', and so on — he can rack up strong evidence for a whole shelf of claims about the world without getting up.
(8) That's absurd. You can't do zoology from an armchair, and he'd be the first to admit he has never had a single piece of information about ravens.
(9) So one of (1)-(5) is false.

His roommate thinks this is easy: the shoe does confirm, just by an amount so small it's beneath notice, so the trouble is in (7) and there's nothing to see. Devon says fine — but then the size of that amount depends on what he already believed about how common ravens are among non-black things, and on whether he was sampling objects at random or had deliberately picked out something non-black to look at, and that's (5) being thrown out, not (7). He wants to know what giving up (5) costs. And if we keep (5), he wants to know which of (1)-(4) goes instead, and what that costs. I'd hate to loose (1) or (3) — those look like straight logic — and (5) has a lot going for it too, so I can't tell who's right.

Can you sort it out? Start with your verdict in the first sentence — which step goes, and what giving it up costs — then defend it step by step, and say what each of the other candidate steps would cost if it were the one to go. Don't restate the argument back to me and don't warm up first.
