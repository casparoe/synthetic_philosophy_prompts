I have no academic training in philosophy or economics — what I know about this I have picked up from reading and from arguing — but for the past year I have been part of a small monthly discussion group in which two members have been having the same argument about effective altruism, and I would like to write up the document they ought to have produced instead of having it a thirteenth time.

The first is a committed effective altruist. She gives away a large share of her income, chose her career partly for how much good it would let her do, and holds that the movement's basic method is sound: compare causes by how much good they do per unit of money or work, go where the numbers are best, and improve the numbers rather than abandon them. The second accepts her starting point — that everyone's interests count equally, that effectiveness matters, that good intentions are no guarantee of good results — but holds that the method quietly rigs its own answers: that it selects for what can be counted and against what cannot, that earning to give lets a person buy a clear conscience while doing a job that may itself do harm, and that a movement financed by a handful of wealthy donors and framed as private charity will crowd out the slower institutional and political work that changes things at the root.

What I want from you is the joint statement these two could both put their names to. Specifically:

1. A numbered list of claims both would sign — about what matters morally, about how much variation there really is between causes, about the proper role and the limits of measurement, and about what neither of them is claiming.
2. Their remaining disagreements, set out one at a time, each written so that the person who holds it would accept the wording as a fair statement of her own view rather than a caricature. A table would be fine.
3. The cruxes: the sub-questions such that settling one would actually move at least one of them. My own guess is that one is whether there is a robust core of interventions whose superiority survives the harshest plausible correction for the effects we cannot measure, and another is whether earning to give really adds money to the pool that would not otherwise have been given at all — but you should tell me which cruxes you think are genuine and which are red herrings.
4. For each crux, the case, comparison, or piece of reasoning that both of them would agree bears on it, with each side's prediction written down in advance.

One case I would like the statement to confront directly, because my two friends agree it is relevant and then draw opposite lessons from it: a funder announces that it will give ten million dollars to whichever of two proposals it judges better. The first is a proven health programme with solid trial evidence behind it and a well-established cost per life saved. The second is a long, unglamorous effort to change a procurement rule, which the few people who understand the field believe would save more lives per dollar, but whose results will not be visible for a decade and will never be cleanly attributable to anyone. I want each of them to say in advance what they predict — what the funder will in fact choose, and what the right choice is — and what would have to be true for them to change their minds.

Finally, if you conclude that part of their quarrel is really about words rather than substance — for instance about whether effective altruism names a method or a movement — say which part, and why. Please write the statement itself, not advice about how to write one.
