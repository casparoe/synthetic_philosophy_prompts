Last winter I sat through a dinner argument between two friends — Vera and Hal, both of whom read more philosophy than is good for them — that ran until the small hours and that neither of them won, and I've been turning it over ever since. I've spent enough time lurking in the rationalist blogosphere to recognize the shape of the dispute, but I stayed out of it and I honestly can't tell which of them came out ahead. The case they were fighting about was this: Vera is about to make a single blind draw from a sealed urn that everyone agrees holds fifty red and fifty blue balls, thoroughly mixed, so that the objective chance of red on this draw is one half. On the table, however, is a second fact — a forecasting process that Vera has tracked for years has just announced blue, and in situations like this one it has been right about ninety percent of the time. The narrow question is what Vera's credence in red should be; the wider one, which is what they actually cared about, is whether the forecaster's output is even the sort of thing that is permitted to move her credence off the chance. Hal opened by saying her credence should be one half and that this follows from what it means to have the concept of chance at all: deference is constitutive, so a "chance" one doesn't defer to simply isn't a chance, and since the chance here is stipulated at one half, the forecaster's output must be inadmissible and set aside. Vera called that circular — you cannot define chance by its normative role and then use the definition to quarantine unwelcome evidence, since rationality demands the total evidence, and on the total evidence her credence in red is closer to a tenth. Hal came back that if inadmissibility is that cheap to invoke, the concept of objective chance does no independent work at all; you've collapsed chance into subjective credence and thrown away exactly the distinction that physics and experimental design depend on. Vera granted that chance is mind-independent but denied that mind-independence is a rule about credence: a mind-independent fact can be a poor guide, and the very fact that the deference principle has always needed an admissibility clause shows that deference was never unconditional. Hal replied that the admissibility clause is not an escape hatch but a constraint — admissibility is fixed by what an observer could know at the time without knowing the outcome — so the forecaster either reads the outcome, in which case it is inadmissible, or it tracks a genuine feature of the setup, in which case the chance was never one half to begin with; there is no stable middle. Vera then offered her sharpest point: the forecaster is probably not reading the outcome at all but detecting a common cause, some hidden feature of the urn or of the draw that also shapes what comes out, and if so the chance really isn't one half and Hal ought to welcome the correction rather than resist it. Hal closed by insisting that if there is such a common cause, the stipulated chance of one half was simply false or ill-posed, so Vera's example is not a case of evidence overriding chance but of the chance being other than advertised.

Please adjudicate this move by move — go through Hal's constitutive claim, Vera's circularity charge, Hal's no-independent-work point, Vera's mind-independence-versus-deference distinction, Hal's analysis of the admissibility clause, Vera's common-cause suggestion, and Hal's rejoinder that the common cause case is really just a mis-specified chance — and for each say whether it is clearly mistaken, defensible, or clearly right, with a short justification, rather than simply declaring a winner. Then tell me, overall, which of them has the better position and why, and — most usefully — what both of them are missing: in particular, whether their quarrel is really about the content of the deference principle or about something upstream, like what fixes the reference class for a chance or whether a single draw can even have an objective chance in the first place. I'd like the verdicts to be as specific as the claims, so that if one of them is right about the concept and wrong about the case, or vice versa, that comes through.
