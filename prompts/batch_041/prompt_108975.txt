I've been reading Berkeley's economic writings — the Querist and the stuff on Ireland — and got into an argument with a mate of mine in the pub who's doing a PhD on credit theory. He thinks Berkeley's view that money is a kind of ticket or counter, and that real wealth is the power to command the labour and goods of others, is basically right but naive. I tried to defend it and ended up building an argument that I now can't decide whether it's a reductio of Berkeley or a reductio of my mate. Here it is:

(1) Money is not itself wealth; wealth is the power to command goods and labour. (2) Money is a ticket or counter that transfers a share of that power. (3) A ticket's value derives entirely from what it can be exchanged for. (4) So money's value derives entirely from what others will give for it. (5) What others will give for money depends on their expectations of what others will give for it, and so on. (6) So money's value is purely conventional, a self-referential matter of collective belief. (7) If money's value is purely conventional, then any object could serve as money and its value could be whatever we collectively decide. (8) If any object could serve as money and its value is whatever we decide, then a government could create unlimited wealth simply by issuing more tickets. (9) But issuing unlimited tickets doesn't create wealth; it just causes inflation. (10) So something in the ticket view is wrong: either money isn't merely a ticket, or its value isn't purely conventional.

My mate says the argument is valid and the culprit is (1), that Berkeley was wrong to separate money from wealth so sharply. I suspect there's a slide somewhere in the middle but I can't name it. I'd rather keep (1) and (2) if I can — they seem to capture something real about credit — but I want to know what I'd be paying. Which step do you reject, and what does rejecting it cost? If you think the argument is valid, tell me which premise is least costly to abandon and why.
