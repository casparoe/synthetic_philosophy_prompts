I am a retired atmospheric physicist and, since retiring, I have been reading philosophy of science and decision theory. I sit as a lay member on the national scientific advisory council. The council is revising its protocol for cases in which accredited experts disagree after examining the same evidence. Our current rule says that such disagreement is prima facie evidence that at least one expert is irrational, biased, or acting in bad faith, because if the experts had a common prior and their posterior probabilities were common knowledge, those posteriors would have to coincide. The chair now wants to apply the rule to an upcoming assessment of a low-probability, high-consequence risk: two careful experts have reached different conclusions, and the chair proposes to discount the dissenting expert's testimony on the ground that the disagreement itself shows he must have made an error. I am not persuaded, but I need to give the council a written opinion it can act on. Please draft that opinion. It should recommend whether to retain, revise, or abandon the rule, and set out a concrete procedure for the upcoming case with a clear deadline. It should engage the strongest version of the chair's position, explain what the rule assumes about common priors, common knowledge, and rationality, and say what follows when the disagreement concerns the choice of prior itself rather than the updating. It should also address whether the council can legitimately treat a dissenting expert's testimony as evidence of error without first establishing that the relevant assumptions hold. Please write in British English, and keep the opinion suitable for circulation to a non-specialist council.
