A friend and I spent most of yesterday evening arguing about silences in the archive: she holds that every gap in the record is somebody's decision, I hold that most gaps are the residue of routine practice and nobody's choice at all, and we could not settle it, because it cannot be settled from the armchair. I would like to run a session that lets students feel why it is so hard. I teach a graduate seminar in history and archival studies — sixteen students, eighty minutes, one room — and I want a two-stage activity. In the first stage, four groups each act as a different record-creating body in the same small town in the 1890s: a district office under pressure to produce revenue figures, a mutual-aid society under police surveillance, a church that keeps a register, and a household that keeps nothing. Working through a set of incoming events, each group must decide what gets written down and what does not; give them real pressures, a ledger with a fixed and partly unsuited format, and no hint that the exercise concerns silences. In the second stage, collect the ledgers and hand them to a different set of students, who must write a short account of the town's communal life from the ledgers alone, marking in the margin which claims are record and which are inference. Then the debrief: map what happened onto the several moments at which a silence can enter — the making or not making of a fact, selection, description and filing, and later use — and bring out the case I care most about, in which not being recorded is a deliberate tactic of survival rather than a deprivation, so that the second group's confident inference of 'no institutions' turns out to be a misreading of an absence that was chosen. Give me the roles, the pressures, the ledger format, the timeline, and the debrief questions; tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time, which I expect to be over-documentation among the record-keepers and smuggled-in outside knowledge among the historians; and be frank about what the activity cannot reproduce — that real silences do not announce themselves, and that my students will always get to see the withheld material, which no historian ever does.
