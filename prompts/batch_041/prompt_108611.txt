Our epistemology reading group has spent the term on two ideas that we have finally decided are the same idea wearing two hats. The first is the transparency of knowing: if you know something, nothing further is needed for you to know that you know it. The second is more ambitious — that some of our conditions are self-announcing, so that whenever one of them holds, you are in a position to know that it holds. We noticed that if knowing were self-announcing in that second way, the first would fall out of it for free, and then we spent three weeks trying to break the second one. Our running example has been the radiator in the seminar room. Somebody turns its dial a hair colder at every break, and by four o'clock we are all reaching for our coats; but nobody can say at which break the room became cold, and nobody, at the moment it happened, could have known that it had. The engine of the thing, as we reconstructed it, is that at each step along that chain you could so easily have been wrong that if you were right it was only by luck, and knowledge, we agreed, does not come by luck.

I would like to close the semester with a poem built on that radiator, and I would like the poem's form to do the arguing rather than its adjectives. Please write a pantoum, five quatrains. The form is the whole point: in a pantoum the second and fourth lines of each stanza return as the first and third of the next, and the last stanza closes the loop by handing the poem back its opening line. That interlocking recurrence is precisely the structure of the argument I want the poem to make. Let one recurring line be a claim to know something about the present case — "I know how this feels," or whatever you prefer — and another be the concession that it might be otherwise by a hair, and let each return find the line in a changed neighbourhood, so that words which were confident in the first stanza have gone hollow by the fourth. One of the recurring lines should be a question, and it should be harder to answer each time it comes round. The speaker is the person sitting in the room, not a philosopher commenting on them.

Two constraints. First, keep the technical vocabulary out: no "luminosity," no "KK," no "margin for error," no "safety," no names of philosophers. The form has to carry it. Second, do not resolve it. We broke up over whether what fails is knowledge or the assumption that knowing announces itself, and I want the poem to leave both readings standing — so the closing line should be the opening line again, and the reader should hear the difference. Someone who knows the argument should be able to point to the step where knowledge gives out; someone who doesn't should still find it a good poem.
