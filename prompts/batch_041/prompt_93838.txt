Take a comparison between two changes: for person A, a reduction of chronic pain from 6/10 to 3/10; for person B, recovery from moderate depression to a normal mood. Suppose we know only their ordinal preferences over health states, elicited under standard conditions, and that each prefers their own improvement to the status quo. I want your credence that there is a determinate fact of the matter about which change is the greater improvement in well-being. For precision, call the comparison determinate just in case it is representation-invariant: for every pair of utility functions representing A’s and B’s preferences respectively, each unique up to positive affine transformation, and for every bridge principle that maps each person’s utility scale into a common moral scale while respecting each person’s own preferences, the comparison yields the same verdict. Please give me a single number, then decompose it into the sub-questions you think it depends on: the probability that preferences are cardinally measurable in the relevant sense; the probability that a non-arbitrary bridge principle exists; the probability that, given such a principle, the comparison is invariant under permissible re-descriptions; the probability that well-being rather than preference satisfaction or objective goods is the correct object of comparison; and the probability that the class of such comparisons is large enough to guide a concrete policy choice. Assign a rough probability and one line of justification to each term, show how they combine, and tell me which term your final number is most sensitive to and what evidence or argument would move it most.
