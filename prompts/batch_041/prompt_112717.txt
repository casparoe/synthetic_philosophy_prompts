I'm giving the after-dinner speech at my local socialist group's annual dinner on Saturday and I need a hand. Setup, sorry it's long.

Six months ago our branch had a stupid, wonderful argument about whether to rename ourselves after John Stuart Mill. Long story. The upshot is we voted to do it, narrowly, and now the renaming dinner is Saturday and I — the branch's resident philosophy person, i.e. the one who owns too many books — have been asked to give the speech explaing why Mill. Half the room voted for it and is insufferably smug about it. The other half voted against, and they include Doreen, who's been a member since the sixties and thinks Mill is the original sellout, a man who'd rather lecture you about your soul than about your rent. She is waiting to be annoyed. There'll also be a dozen or so partners and friends there who aren't political at all and mainly want the food and the raffle.

What I want to do is use the occasion to actually put Mill's late views on socialism on the table, because everyone in that room knows him only as the On Liberty guy. What they don't know, and what I've slowly become convinced is the interesting bit, is roughly this:

- Late in life Mill basically conceded the socialists' critique of actually existing property. His line, as I read him, is that our property rules have never matched the principle that's supposed to justify property in the first place — that people get out roughly what they put in. Don't quote him at me, I'm not sure of the exact wording and neither are you, so put it in your own words.
- So he wasn't a fence-sitter about the ends. He thought the criticism was largely right.
- What he wouldn't do was pretend the hard part was over. His worry was the transition: a society that reorganised production around common ownership could get the economics right and still crush the thing he cared about most, individuality. And notice that his famous fear in On Liberty isn't the state, it's society — the majority, the neighbours, public opinion. A socialist Mill is a Mill who thinks the danger of socialism is conformity.
- He thought the practical road ran through cooperation, worker ownership, profit-sharing, experiments small enough to actually try, rather than one big rupture.

Here's my own gloss on that last point, use it if you like it: if the collective is your only employer, then dissent isn't dangerous, it's just expensive. You don't get arrested for disagreeing, you get quietly not rehired. That's the fear.

So the speech has to do two things at once, and this is the real ask. It has to be genuinely rousing — our chair wants people signing up for the door-knocking at the end — while not saying a single thing Mill would have winced at. No promising the outcome is inevitable. No pretending that agreeing on the destination settles anything about the route. And it can't be a lecture: no jargon, no hedging every clause. It should sound like someone who loves this stuff talking to neighbours over a plate of rice, not a seminar.

Take the role of a philosopher who also writes speeches for a living, and be careful here — this room will forgive a lot, but it won't forgive being condescended to.

Please write me that speech, about five or six minutes, so 800-900 words, meant to be spoken aloud.

A few things:
- Before you write anything, think it through and give me a short plan first: the emotional arc, where Doreen gets a fair hearing (or gets won over, if you can manage it honestly), and the one concrete image or bit of a story that carries the whole thing. Then write the speech.
- Give me three candidate opening lines, tell me which one you'd pick and why, and go from there.
- Use Doreen somewhere, and give me at least one moment where I concede something real to her side. A speech that wins the room by pretending there's no disagreement would bomb in this room.
- End with a specific ask, not a vague call to hope.
- Don't quote anyone and don't cite anything. I'm on my feet, not writing an essay.
- Aim it at people who are smart but tired and a bit drunk, not at philosophers.
