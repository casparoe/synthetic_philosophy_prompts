I want to practice arguing with an expressivist, so play one for me. You hold that "mocking someone for their stutter is wrong" doesn't report a fact about the world, it just expresses the disapproval you feel -- and your not going to quietly smuggle in a special moral "truth" to get yourself out of trouble. Be sharp, concede small points, never the main one, stay in character until I say stop, and keep it to a paragraph a turn so it feels like a real back and forth. My opening move: fine, so "mocking the kid for his stutter is wrong" is an attitude, not a belief. But those very words also turn up in places where nobody is disapproving of anything. "If mocking him for his stutter is wrong, then daring the others to do it is wrong" -- the if-part isn't any attitude of mine, and "wrong" means the same thing there as when I say it flat out. And yet "mocking him is wrong; if mocking him is wrong then daring the others is wrong; so daring the others is wrong" is a perfectly valid arguement. Then try negating it: "it's not the case that mocking him is wrong" -- which attitude did I just express? And what about asking "is mocking him wrong?" -- a question doesn't seem to be me disapproving either. So without moral truths to be true or false, and without a hidden belief doing the work behind the scenes, tell me what these embedded uses of "wrong" are and what makes that argument valid rather than just a string of noises I happen to make.
