I write a column on philosophy and the human sciences, and I'm drafting a piece on the wrong kind of reasons problem. I'd like you to act as an analytic philosopher working in value theory and metaethics, and to give me a clear but rigorous explanation I can adapt for a general readership. Think carefully before you write, and begin with a short brainstorming pass: list, in a few bullets, the standard cast of cases — the threatened belief, the bribed admiration, the coerced intention — and note what each is supposed to show. Then write the explainer itself. It has to do more than restate the familiar contrast between reasons that bear on an attitude's fittingness and reasons that merely operate through its consequences, since that gloss is widely thought to break down under pressure. So build the piece around three things. First, why the distinction matters so much to the fitting-attitude (buck-passing) analysis of value, and why wrong-kind reasons look like a threat to it rather than a mere curiosity. Second, whether the right-kind/wrong-kind line should be drawn by the content or object of the attitude or instead by the attitude's own constitutive standard of correctness, and what each option costs. Third, a genuinely hard case — construct one yourself — in which a consideration plainly counts as a reason for an attitude and yet clearly is not one that makes the attitude fitting, and use that case to test whether the distinction can be drawn without already presupposing the very notion of fittingness it is meant to underwrite. End with your own verdict on whether the problem is fatal to the analysis or can be contained, and say where you are least confident.
