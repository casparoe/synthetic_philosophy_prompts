I am a novelist, and I have been trying to settle an argument I keep having with a philosopher friend. It turns on an image I have used often, in conversation and, I confess, in a draft: the novelist and her characters. I know what my characters will do before they do it; I made them, and the traits out of which their choices issue; nothing in the book happens except by my arrangement. Yet inside the book they deliberate, they weigh reasons, and we hold them responsible, and it does not seem to us that my control over them makes their choices any less theirs. I have used this image to argue that a world in which everything is settled in advance — by a plan, a script, or a chain of causes — need not be a world without free will, and that the compatibilist is right.

How far does the image actually hold? Give me the correspondence first: what plays the part of the author, the manuscript, the character's reasons, the reader's verdict. Then tell me where the mapping is exact and where it slips, and which features of my side of the analogy I am quietly importing into the other side — an author who loves her characters, a text that is read rather than lived, a knowledge that is timeless rather than a link in a causal chain. Say whether it matters that my relation to my characters is one of authorship and foreknowledge rather than causal determination, since the argument I want to make is about determinism, and what becomes of the character's ability to have done otherwise when the only sense in which she could have is that I might have written it differently. Separate the disanalogies that matter for the intended conclusion from the ones that do not, tell me whether the compatibilist conclusion survives on the former, and whether an incompatibilist can simply reverse the image — the characters, after all, are not free; only the novelist is — without begging the question. If the image cannot carry the argument, what image could?
