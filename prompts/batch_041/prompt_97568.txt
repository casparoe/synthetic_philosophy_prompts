A friend and I argued for most of last night about the strategy-stealing argument, and he left still convinced it was a swindle: a proof that hands you a win while never showing you the winning move seems to him not to be knowledge at all. I would rather answer him with a poem than with another lecture.

Write a pantoum of four or five quatrains, in which lines 2 and 4 of each stanza return as lines 1 and 3 of the next and the closing stanza turns the poem's opening line back at the end. Let the recurrence do the argument's work instead of decorating it: a line first spoken by the defender of the second player should come back in the first player's mouth, since that is precisely what the thief does with a strategy he never earned. Keep the premises honest. The trick needs a game in which an extra move can never be a liability, so it holds where a spare stone is simply spare and collapses wherever a wasted tempo or a forced move hurts, as in zugzwang or misère play; and it needs a game with no draws, since otherwise all it establishes is that the second player cannot force a win. Let the poem be equally honest about what is not delivered: in chomp, where a move eats a block together with everything below and to the right of it and the poisoned corner must be left alone, the argument settles that the first player wins on any rectangular bar, yet in the infinite version of the game no winning first move is known at all. Give one recurring line the task of asking for that move, and never let the poem answer it. Someone who knows the argument should find the poem exact; someone who does not should still be able to follow it.
