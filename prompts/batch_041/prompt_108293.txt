My wife is a medievalist and I work on early modern natural philosophy, so we've been having the same argument at the dinner table for about two years now, and I want to actually settle it on paper. Her line is that 'the Middle Ages', 'the Renaissance' and 'the Scientific Revolution' are retrospective impositions -- buckets that got their modern shapes long after the fact, for the convenience of people who wanted a dark age to look back on, a golden age to claim, or a heroic break to take credit for -- and that when you get down into the actual record of what people were doing with nature, the boundaries dissolve into continuities. My line is that this is true as intellectual history and mostly irrelevent as history: whatever the labels' origins, something did change, on a scale you can see without squinting, and the fact that a boundary is fuzzy doesn't mean there's no boundary. We keep going around in circles, so I'd like the document we should have produced. Please draft the joint statement of an adversarial collaboration between these two positions -- a committed constructivist about the periods and a committed realist about them, both smart, both honest, neither one a strawman.

Numbered agreed-claims section first, then a disagreements table. In the agreed part I want what both could sign about: what it would even mean for a period to be a real thing; what these labels are actually used to do (periodize, teach, staff departments, sell books); what's uncontroversially true about scale and pace of change; and what neither side needs to deny -- that the constructivist isn't saying nothing changed, that the realist isn't claiming the boundaries are sharp or that contemporaries thought of themselves as living at a turning point. Then the disagreements, each written in wording its own side would accept, not a paraphrase the other side supplied. And I want the cruxes -- the sub-questions where settling one would actually move somebody -- with, for each, a test or body of evidence both sides agree would bear on it and each side's prediction recorded in advance. Things like: does a fixed corpus of Latin and vernacular works on nature, spanning the centuries on either side of the alleged break, show a step change in some measurable property or a slow drift -- and if it's a drift, does the constructivist win outright or only draw? Does the institutional record (universities, academies, journals, instruments, patronage) show anything sharper? Does self-conscious novelty talk cluster at the alleged turning points, and does that count as evidence of a break or just as evidence that people flatter their own age? Is there a version of the realist claim that survives even if every label turns out to be a nineteenth-century artifact, and a version of the constructivist claim that survives even if there was a genuine step change? And if part of the fight turns out to be verbal -- the two of them using 'period' to mean different things -- say which part and why. Keep it concrete enough that we could actually go run one of the tests, and skip the reading list; I don't need to be told who said what, I want the argument. Also, neither of us is only talking about Europe, so if the agreed section can say something about whether the same question even makes sense for other regions, do that.
