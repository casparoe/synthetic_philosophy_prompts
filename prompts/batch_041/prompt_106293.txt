I'm a novelist and I've got a story idea I can't shake, but I keep getting the philosophy of it slightly wrong in my head. So: write me a short one, 1500 words or so, set a few years from now, where a company sells what they call coherence therapy. You come in with all your half-formed and half-contradictory preferences -- want the job, want to leave the city, would rather not swap A for B, would rather not swap B for C, but would happily swap C for A -- and they run your answers through something that returns the nearest complete and transitive utility function that fits you, and after that you're supposed to live by it, on the grounds that anyone whose preferences don't hang together can be walked round a loop of trades until they've handed over money for nothing. What I actually want the story to be about is that the completion isn't unique. Loads of coherent utility functions fit the same person, so the service has to break the tie somehow -- a seed, the order you happened to fill the form in, something arbitrary -- and the customer knows that going in and signs anyway, because the alternative is being a walking money pump. First person, the customer's voice, ordinary and a bit flat. Nobody should be evil and nobody should get lobotomised; the eerie part is everyones behaving sensibly. And I'd like one beat where somebody points out she could simply refuse to trade, and she agrees, and it doesn't help her at all. If someone who's read the arguments about why rationality is supposed to demand complete preferences nods along, great, but a reader who's never heard of any of it should still follow what's happening.
