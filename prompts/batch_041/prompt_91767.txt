I run a small forecasting shop — eight analysts, we sell probability estimates to a handful of institutional clients (mostly insurers, a couple of funds), and our whole pitch is that we hand over a number with a range rather than a shrug. The recuring headache is the split: two analysts come back from the same question with numbers miles apart, 25% vs 65% on whether a merger closes, and I have to ship something by Friday. A colleague in the econ department told me there's a body of work that speaks directly to this, roughly: if two agents start from the same prior, are both fully rational, and it's common knowledge between them what probability each of them currently assigns, then they cannot rationally keep disagreeing — any disagreement that survives all of that has to be coming from different priors, or from one of those idealizations breaking down (they don't trust each other, they aren't perfectly rational, the 'common knowledge' bit was never actually achieved). I want to turn that into a procedure I can run on a live disagreement before a deadline, instead of just guessing who's smarter or splitting the difference because I'm tired.

You're a decision theorist who also designs decision processes inside real organizations, so you know the result cold and you also know how it falls apart with tired humans. Please start by brainstorming the distinctions that actually do the work here — evidence gaps vs prior gaps, the difference between each analyst merely knowing the other's number and that number being common knowledge, what 'fully rational' is carrying in the theorem, and where the idealization plainly fails for two people with egos and a Friday deadline. Then give me a checklist I can run: the steps in order, what counts as a sufficient answer at each one, when it says send them back to compare evidence and re-run, when it says the disagreement is now about priors rather than facts and here is the rule we apply to prior gaps, and where it has to stop and hand the call to me. A specific thing I want in there: a way to tell a prior gap from an evidence gap, which I assume means making each analyst decompose their number into what they'd have said before the new evidence and what they think the evidence implies — tell me if that's right, and what to do when they can't or won't decompose it. Also the in-room tests: whether the two of them would take opposite sides of a bet at their stated odds, what it means if one won't, and whether asking each to predict what the other will say next week adds anything or is just theatre.

Then run the whole thing on two cases: (a) two analysts who've both read the same trial data disagree on whether a drug gets approved, 30% vs 70%; (b) two analysts both say 20% but for completely different reasons, which I suspect is a different kind of problem wearing the same clothes. I'd rather you include the case where the procedure tells me something I don't want to hear. Finally, be straight with me about what the procedure can't do — especially the situation where the 'prior gap' is really a values gap, or a disagreement about which model of the world is right, where no further argument is going to close it — and tell me how to present the resulting number to a client without pretending we've resolved something we haven't. Concrete over pretty; I'd take a procedure with three visible holes over a elegant abstraction.
