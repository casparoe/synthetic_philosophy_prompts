I cover science and ideas for a general-interest magazine and I'm working on a piece about illusionism about consciousness — the view that the sense of there being something it's like to be us is a trick the brain plays on itself. A philosopher I interveiwed gave me his summary of the position and I want to check it before I put anything like it in print. Roughly what he said: illusionism is the eliminativist view that consciousness doesn't exist, so nobody actually has experiences; 'phenomenal consciousness' is just the label we slap on whatever we can't yet explain mechanistically, so once the neuroscience matures the hard problem will dissappear the way phlogiston did; talk of an 'illusion' implies there's a subject who's being fooled about their own inner life, which is why critics say the whole view refutes itself; the meta-problem is really just the hard problem restated, so illusionists are basically saying 'we don't like dualism'; and on this view it makes no difference whether a dog or a chatbot feels pain, since illusionism is at bottom a verbal thesis about how we talk. How much of this is fair to illusionism as its defenders actually hold it? Go point by point: what an illusionist would accept, what's a conflation of two claims they keep apart, what's a standard objection being presented as a consequence, and which of his downstream claims (dogs, chatbots, the hard problem evaporating) actually follow from anything true in the summary. I'm inclined to think the self-refutation line is too quick, but I don't know the literature well enough to say. I'm definately not after a reading list — I'll chase citations myself — I just want the audit.
