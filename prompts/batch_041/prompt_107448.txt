Our reading group has spent the term on the hierarchy of minds, and one of us has produced an argument that I can neither refute nor accept. He calls it the Substitution Argument. It runs as follows.

(1) The hierarchy is meant to be cumulative. A Darwinian creature is fixed by selection and cannot learn. A Skinnerian creature adds learning from the consequences of its own acts. A Popperian creature adds the capacity to try candidate acts against an internal model, so that they may fail there instead of in the world. A Gregorian creature adds tools, above all words, and so imports thinking tools it did not invent.

(2) Each addition can be described as a way of moving the cost of error one step back from the world. The Darwinian pays for error with its lineage; the Skinnerian with its own skin or its missed reward; the Popperian with a discarded hypothesis; the Gregorian with a discarded tool.

(3) But a cost that has merely been relocated is still the same cost, and a trial that has merely been relocated is still the same trial.

(4) And every level can be stated in a single vocabulary: generate variation, submit it to a test, retain what passes. Selection generates variants and retains the lineages that survive. Reinforcement generates acts and retains those that pay. Simulation generates hypotheses and retains those that survive the model. Language generates formulations and retains those a community can use.

(5) A vocabulary that describes all four levels is the vocabulary of the kinds of mind.

(6) Therefore the hierarchy ranks the medium in which variation is written and the speed at which it is tested, not the kind of thing the creature is doing. There is no principled difference between a mind that cannot do without the world and one that can.

I am willing to surrender (6) if I must, but I want to know the price, because I would rather keep the thought that the digger wasp and the physicist are not doing the same thing in different materials. Every premise looks true to me. Tell me which step to reject, and why that step looked true when it was not. If the argument is valid and the fault lies in a premise, tell me which premise is least costly to give up, and what the hierarchy looks like once it is gone.

One thing troubles me and I cannot make it into an objection. The same vocabulary seems to cover the immune system, a market, and the ordinary growth of a habit — and no one calls those minds. That makes me suspect the vocabulary rather than the hierarchy. But I cannot say what the vocabulary leaves out, and I cannot deny that, as far as it goes, the friend's description is accurate.
