I am a professor of philosophy, working mainly in political philosophy and social epistemology, and I would like your help with an argument that I witnessed at a departmental workshop last term and have not been able to settle to my own satisfaction. The two participants are colleagues of mine, both careful and both experienced; I was a bystander and am reporting their exchange rather than my own view, and I have tried to state their positions at least as strongly as they did.

The subject was a decision procedure of the following kind. Before a decision, two conditional prediction markets are opened on a single, pre-specified index of welfare, W. One market prices W conditional on policy P being adopted; the other prices W conditional on P not being adopted. If the first price exceeds the second, P is adopted. Contracts held on the branch that does not occur are refunded, since that branch's outcome is never observed. Neither colleague works on the empirical literature on prediction markets; the dispute was about whether the design is coherent on its face, as described.

Colleague A opened as follows.

(1) The decision rule compares a number that will be settled with a number that never will be. Because contracts on the unrealized branch are refunded, a trader who believes that price is wrong can neither profit by being right nor lose by being wrong. The price that drives the decision is therefore one that nothing corrects. This is not a measurement problem; it is a question whether the market has a well-defined question at all.

(2) Even granting that, W must be fixed in advance and must be invariant to the decision. But many of the decisions the scheme is meant to make are precisely decisions about what W should be, and whose welfare counts. If W can be revised by the procedure's own verdicts, the market prices a moving target. If it cannot, the scheme covers only a narrow class of questions, and the claims made for it are overstated.

(3) And if W is fixed outside the market, then the substantive choice, what to optimize, has already been made by ordinary politics. The market then executes a decision it did not make, and whoever controls W controls the market. The scheme does not replace politics; it conceals it.

Colleague B replied.

(4) A is conflating an unobserved quantity with an indeterminate one. What would have happened had P not been adopted is a determinate fact. Markets do not need a state of the world to be realized in order to price it; they need traders paid for accuracy, and accuracy can be paid for across a sequence of decisions, or by pricing the same index against a comparison unit not subject to the decision, whether another jurisdiction, another firm, or a control group. Then the counterfactual is realized somewhere and settled, and the objection dissolves.

(5) The incentive point is a point about contract design, not about the idea, and the repair is to change what the contract pays rather than to abandon the scheme. In any case, the unrealized-branch price is disciplined indirectly. A trader who thinks it is wrong can also take a position on the market for whether P will be adopted; the two positions together constitute a bet on the joint distribution, and the arbitrage relation among that price, the realized branch, and the probability of adoption constrains the number A says is unconstrained.

(6) As for the metric: every procedure takes some input as fixed. A legislature takes preferences as given; a court takes a constitution as given. Deciding at a higher level what a lower level will optimize is not circularity, it is the ordinary structure of a rule-governed practice.

A's last word was this.

(7) Fallibility is not the issue; the absence of a scoring rule is. A forecaster is scored against what happens; the conditional price on the branch that does not occur is scored against nothing. The arbitrage relation in (5) ties one number to the probability of adoption, but adoption is fixed by the very comparison in dispute, so the relation links an undisciplined number to another. And (6) relocates my point rather than answering it: if the higher level sets the metric, then the higher level is where the politics lives, and nothing has been said about how it is decided. As for (4), the comparison with cost-benefit analysis is inapt, because a cost-benefit analysis can be checked against outcomes after the fact, which is exactly what this design cannot do.

As the session broke up, A proposed a distinction that B rejected as spurious: between a defect that is constitutive, meaning the procedure cannot specify its own question, and a defect that is one of settlement, meaning the procedure's prices cannot be disciplined. A said his objection was of the first kind; B said there is no such kind, and that everything A called constitutive was a design choice.

What I want is an adjudication, not a judgment of the people. Go through moves (1) through (7) and rate each as clearly sound, sound in substance but misdescribed, or mistaken, justifying each rating briefly. Then answer four questions separately. First, is A's distinction between constitutive and settlement defects a real one, and does his objection fall on the constitutive side of it? Second, is B's argument in (6) sound, or does it concede A's point in a different vocabulary? Third, by the end, are the two still disagreeing about the same thing, and if not, what has the disagreement become? Fourth, what is each of them missing that the other sees? I am not asking for a literature survey, and I do not need citations; the question is whether this design, as described, is coherent.
