Here is the position I hold. When several agents face a genuinely identical problem — same options, same payoffs, same information, same reasoning capacity — the correct answer is the one that maximizes the outcome for all of them at once, because "what I should do" and "what each of them should do" are one question, not two correlated ones. So the causal reasoning that defects in a one-shot prisoner's dilemma is not merely suboptimal but confused: it treats my deliberation as an intervention on the world when it is a computation the others are running too. Hence I accept Hofstadter's answers in the Platonia case (a trillionaire offers a billion dollars to the sole person who sends a telegram by noon; no collusion allowed; send with probability 1/20) and in the Luring Lottery (prize divided by the total number of entries; roll a die with as many faces as expected responders and enter only on a 1), and I think the readers who mailed in googolplex entries were reasoning wrongly, not just greedily. I also lean toward a stronger claim: the argument survives approximate symmetry — if others are only somewhat likely to reach my conclusion, the superrational answer is still the one to give, and its correctness does not fade in proportion to that probability.
