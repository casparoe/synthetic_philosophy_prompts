Write a piece of sustained, deadpan satire in the form of an official guidance document issued by a fictional public agency that adjudicates existence claims: a caseworker's handbook, an internal procedure manual, or a published FAQ. Target length 650–800 words, divided into numbered sections, in the flat and unamused register of genuine administrative prose. No section may acknowledge that it is funny, no narrator may step outside the document, and no irony may be signposted. Any quoted voice must be an invented official of the agency; do not put words in the mouths of real mathematicians, and do not cite real works.

The conceit: the agency administers constructive mathematics as a matter of routine public business. Every claim that something exists must be filed with a witness; every either/or must be resolved into the disjunct actually established; and the document should present these as ordinary standards of evidence rather than as a philosophical commitment. The agency must never do anything a careful constructive mathematician would not do. Internal consistency matters more than laughs, and the technical material should be accurate — the humour has to come from the real content, not from generic bureaucratic absurdity.

The following should appear as humdrum procedural matters, stated correctly and without commentary: (i) the witness requirement for existential and disjunctive claims, together with the rule that a demonstration that a file cannot be shown to be empty confers no entitlement to anything in it; (ii) the absence of the law of excluded middle, framed not as a thesis but as a standing instruction against presuming that every question has an answer, so that each disjunct must be applied for separately; (iii) a translation or amnesty scheme through which classical results may be imported, with the consequence that what passes through receives a guarantee of immunity from refutation rather than a warrant — a certificate that a finding cannot be overturned, not that it holds; (iv) an expedited route for claims of the form that there is a number with a checkable property, under which the office simply searches and treats a search that never terminates as a defect in the application, noting in passing that some branch offices do not recognize this route; (v) a division between a no-frills branch, which assumes nothing beyond its own procedures, and a branch that additionally assumes that every function it handles is continuous, so that complaints about discontinuous behaviour are returned as ill-formed; (vi) the axiom of choice granted in one formulation and refused in another, with an explicit denial that this is a clerical inconsistency; (vii) the practice of issuing, with every approved application, a procedure the applicant can run, so that approval consists in having something to execute rather than in having established a fact.

The standard objections to constructive strictness — that it discards sound classical results, that it conflates what is true with what can be exhibited, that the demand for witnesses is unmotivated unless one already reads mathematics computationally — must be legible in the document's procedures and must never be stated in it.

Then, clearly separated from the fiction and labelled as commentary, add a note of 150–250 words with two parts. First, say where the piece takes liberties or leans on contested readings: in particular, whether the witness requirement belongs to intuitionistic logic itself or to the intended meanings of the connectives, whether the continuity assumption is part of intuitionism proper or an additional mathematical principle not licensed by the logic alone, and whether running intuitionism and constructive mathematics together as a single thing is a distortion. Second, say what would change your mind about the piece's fairness: which feature of the actual position, if it turned out to differ from how you have represented it, would make the parody a strawman, and where the satire is most likely to misfire for a reader who knows the material well.
