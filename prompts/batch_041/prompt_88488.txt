Please take the role of a philosopher of religion who works on the epistemology of theistic belief, and think carefully before you answer. I would like you to begin by identifying the assumptions my question takes for granted, and then to say whether the question is well posed or rests on a mistake.

Some context: I was raised in the church, drifted away in my twenties, and have lately been trying to work out what I actually believe — my wife and I just had our first child, and the question has stopped being abstract. In reading up on it, I keep meeting an argument that runs roughly like this: a God who loved us would not leave honest seekers unable to believe in him, since he would both want us to know him and be able to make himself known; yet many sincere people fail to believe; so no such God exists. I have also seen the phrase "nonresistant nonbelief" attached to this, which I take to mean people like me — not hostile to the idea, just unable to get there.

Here is what bothers me. The argument seems to assume that God is hiding — deliberately concealing himself — and that a good God would not do that. But I hide from my daughter constantly: I put the toy behind my back, I let her struggle with the puzzle, because handing her the answer would rob her of the finding. So if the argument comes down to "a loving God wouldn't play hide-and-seek," isn't it begging the question? It seems to assume that God should deal with us the way one deals with a toddler rather than the way one deals with a grown child. And if that is all there is to it, why is this treated as one of the stronger considerations for atheism rather than as a bad analogy?
