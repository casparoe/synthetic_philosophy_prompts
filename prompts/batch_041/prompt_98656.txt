For several years I have been trying to reverse-engineer the implicit theory of innateness held by a colleague of mine, whom I will call R, and I would like you to predict how she will rule on a new case and then to tell me what would genuinely settle the question.

R has spent a career adjudicating which human capacities are innate, and she has ruled on enough cases that I believe the rule she is operating can be recovered from them. Her settled verdicts, as faithfully as I can reconstruct them, are these. The constraint that leads a speaker to form a question by moving the auxiliary of the main clause rather than the first auxiliary in the sentence: innate. The preference of newborns for face-like over scrambled patterns: innate. The ability to read an alphabetic script: not innate. The capacity to acquire a first language at all, in either speech or sign: innate. The particular vocabulary of one's language: not innate. The ability to distinguish small quantities without counting, as in telling eight dots from sixteen: innate. The tendency to place the boundaries of one's color categories exactly where one's language places them: not innate. The grammatical structure that deaf children invent when they are raised with no accessible language model at all: innate. The species-typical song of a bird that develops it only after hearing conspecific song during a sensitive period: not innate.

The new case is a specific capacity that R has now been asked to rule on: the ability to hear the sounds of one's native language categorically — to hear a continuous physical continuum of vowel sounds as a small number of discrete native categories with sharp boundaries between them. In every speech community, children who develop normally end up with such categories, and the categories they end up with are constructed from the distribution of the sounds they hear.

I want four things. First, identify at least two distinct rules that fit all of R's settled verdicts equally well; I can see at least two, and I suspect they come apart on the new case. Second, for each rule, state what verdict it predicts for the new case and why. Third, give me your best guess as to which rule R is actually running, with your reasons, and in doing so separate carefully what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who work on this topic; do not present a contested reading as though it were the consensus. Fourth, name a further case, one in which the two criteria come apart in the opposite direction from the new case, that would cleanly tell the candidate rules apart, and say what each rule predicts there. If you think R would decline to give a flat verdict on the new case, say so and explain why.

I care far less about whether the capacity really is innate than about the shape of R's implicit theory and the case that would expose it.
