I run the capital-program analysis desk at a state department of transportation. Four times a year I sit with an appointed board that votes on whether to continue, rescope, or cancel projects that have gone over budget and behind schedule: six or eight on the docket, figures in the hundreds of millions, and in twelve years the board has never cancelled anything. Cancellation takes a two-thirds vote and a public hearing; continuation takes a simple majority. I owe each member a one-page recommendation by the morning of the meeting, I get about ninety minutes per project, and the cost and benefit figures I work from were produced by the project's own sponsors and, occasionally, by a consultant those sponsors hired. Two of the projects coming up are ones I helped design in a previous job.

I know the textbook instruction: the money is gone, so compare only what is left to spend against what is left to gain. That instruction is both true and useless. It says nothing about the half-built structure standing on the ground, nothing about what stopping triggers, and nothing about the reasons the board actually gives. The arguments I have to be able to answer, in the board's own words:

- "We've come too far to stop now."
- "Walking away means repaying ninety million dollars in federal funds and spending forty million to make the site safe."
- "The last third is cheaper than the first third; the hard ground is behind us."
- "It's ninety percent finished; stopping now would be madness."
- "If we pull out, the partner cities will never co-fund anything with us again."
- "We've already spent double our estimate, so we should assume the remainder will double too."
- "If we wouldn't start this today, we shouldn't finish it."

Some of these are reasons, some are rationalizations, and I cannot reliably tell which, which is the actual problem.

Write me the procedure. I want an ordered sequence of questions an analyst can run in ninety minutes and a board member with no finance background can follow: what has to be true for the review to end there, with a recommendation to stop; what counts as a sufficient answer at each step; and where the procedure must stop pretending and hand the matter to the board as a question of values rather than arithmetic. It has to tell me what to do when I do not trust the numbers. It has to be explicit about which of its steps are judgment dressed up as method. And it should say who ought to be running the review, given that the person running it is often the person who wanted the project in the first place. Sort the objections above at the end: which the procedure treats as genuine considerations, which as warning signs that I should look harder, and which as irrelevant — and if one of them turns out to be a real reason that the textbook line gets wrong, say so plainly. I don't want a reading list or a survey of what has been written about this; I want the thing itself, with the hard cases worked.

Run it on three projects. First, an interchange rebuild: sixty percent complete, six hundred and ten million spent against an original estimate of four hundred and eighty million, three hundred and ninety million to go; the independent estimate of the remaining benefit is three hundred and forty million; stopping triggers an eighty-five million federal repayment and thirty-five million in site restoration. My instinct is to continue, and I want to know whether the procedure reaches that conclusion for a defensible reason or merely permits it. Second, a commuter rail extension: twenty percent built, three hundred and ten million spent, eight hundred and eighty million to go, ridership forecasts collapsed and a bus line now serves the corridor, mothballing costs twenty-five million. My instinct is to kill it and I expect the board to refuse. Third, a hospital seismic retrofit: seventy-five percent complete, one hundred and eighty million spent, seventy million to go, but it completes a standard already superseded, and the same seventy million would build an outpatient clinic with a larger expected reduction in deaths over thirty years; securing the half-retrofitted building costs fifteen million. My instinct is to finish it, the public would riot at anything else, and I suspect a procedure that takes its own questions seriously will tell me to stop — if it does, state the argument in terms I could read aloud at a public hearing.

Finish by telling me what the procedure cannot do: the cases where it will misfire, and the ones where it will hand me a recommendation I am not competent to make alone.
