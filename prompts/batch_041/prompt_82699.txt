Ok so I'm a second-year PhD student sitting between archaeology and philosophy of science, and I have to submit a short proposal (2 pages, first person) for a small internal pilot grant — six months, no fieldwork money, basically just my own time and a library. I have a half-formed idea and I need you to turn it into a real proposal with an actual question in it.

The idea, roughly: everyone in my department talks as if you can read beliefs and institutions straight off the material record. The pots are uniform, so there was a shared standard; the burials are ranked, so there was a ranked society. But when I push on it, the inference always seems to have the same shape as the reverse inference problem people complain about in brain imaging: you see a signature, you say aha, that's the mark of a shared belief, and the whole thing turns on that signature being selective — on the belief producing the signature in a way that the boring non-belief explanations wouldn't also produce. I can't find anyone who has asked what selectivity could even mean in the archaeological case, where you don't get to run new trials and the record you're sampling from is itself a biased sample of the thing you're trying to infer.

So what I want to make precise: for which classes of material evidence does a signature raise the probability of "there was a collectively accepted norm, with enforcement" more than it raises the probability of "everyone just converged on the same practice for boring local reasons — raw material, labour costs, drift, copying without any norm at all"? Is there any signature of enforcement specifically — sanctioning costs leaving traces — that isn't equally predicted by the boring explanations? And if there isn't, that's a result too, I'd rather have that than a fake positive.

What I want from you in the proposal:

- The question stated so it has a determinate answer, not "can we infer beliefs from objects" (I know that's too vague) but something you can actually succeed or fail at in six months.
- Why the existing positions leave it open. There's plenty written about whether archaeology can be objective, and about middle-range theory, and I don't want to relitigate any of that — say why that literature doesn't settle this.
- The method: I'd work through a small number of concrete signature types (three or four, I'll pick the cases myself later) and for each one lay out the rival generating processes, what each of them predicts, and whether the signature is genuinely selective or merely "consistent with" the institution story. Say month by month what I'd actually be doing.
- Why this approach rather than the obvious alternative of just doing another case study. My hunch is that selectivity is a quantitative-ish notion, so it can be assessed case by case instead of in the abstract — tell me if that hunch survives contact with the proposal.
- What a result looks like: I think it's a table — signature type, rival hypotheses, whether any rival is differentially predicted — plus a defensible verdict on one or two signature types and a clear statement of which ones are simply non-identifiable from material evidence at all.
- What counts as failure. Honestly if every candidate signature turns out to be equally (un)predicted by the boring explanations, I get a negative result, and I need you to say whether that's a real contribution or just me failing to find a good case, and what I could add to keep it from being the second thing.
- Scope. Six months, no fieldwork, and I'll be honest: I can't do the Bayesian machinery properly, I'd be doing it qualitatively in prose. Tell me whether that sinks the whole thing or whether qualitative selectivity is enough for a pilot, and what I'd have to drop if it isn't.

Two constraints. Don't include a bibliography, reading list, or citations — refer to positions and arguments in plain terms and I'll add the references myself, I just want the argumentative skeleton. And keep it to two pages, first person, casual but professional enough that a committee won't wince. There's probably a typo or two in what I wrote above, ignore those.
