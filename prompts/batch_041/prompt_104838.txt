I'm putting together a unit on punishment for my ethics survey, and a colleague dumped a summary of the retributivism/consequentialism debate into our shared folder. It reads like one of those big handbook overviews — competent, confident, and I suspect a bit off in places, but I havent pinned down where. Here's the gist of it, more or less as written:

Consequentialism and retributivism are the two main theories of punishment, and theyre flatly opposed. Consequentialists justify punishment only by its good effects — deterrence, incapacitation, rehabilitation — so for them punishment is forward-looking: it earns its keep by making the future better. Retributivists justify it by the past: the offender deserves to suffer because they did wrong, and the suffering should match the wrong done, which is essentially the old lex talionis, an eye for an eye. Because retributivists get their justification from desert and consequentialists from outcomes, retributivism turns out to be a dressed-up form of vengeance — giving people the pain theyve earned — while consequentialism is really just a cost-benefit calculation. That's why a consequentialist would happily punish an innocent person whenever the numbers worked out, and why consequentialism cant be the correct theory of punishment.
