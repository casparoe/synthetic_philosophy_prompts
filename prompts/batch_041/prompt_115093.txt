I'm a teaching assistant for a graduate course on social choice, and I want to open the session on Arrow's theorem with a joke rather than a definition. The difficulty is that everything I can find is a "economists can't agree on where to eat" gag that would work just as well for any discipline, since the humour comes from the setting and not from the theorem. What I'm after is a joke whose punchline could not be understood, let alone enjoyed, by someone who doesn't know what the theorem actually says: that with at least three options and at least two voters, no procedure for turning individual rankings into a collective ranking can simultaneously satisfy unrestricted domain, the weak Pareto principle, independence of irrelevant alternatives, and non-dictatorship. It's fine — better, even — if the joke presupposes somewhat more than that, for instance the machinery of a decisive coalition in the proof, the way restricting everyone's preferences to a single left-right dimension rescues the median voter from a Condorcet cycle, or the thought that the obvious escape routes, such as letting voters report cardinal scores instead of rankings, each buy their consistency at a price. It just shouldn't turn on a pun on "impossible" or "dictator," and it shouldn't require the audience to recognise any particular name, work, or episode in the history of the field.

The setting I'd suggest, if it helps: a three-member departmental hiring committee choosing among three candidates, in which one member's ballot is not single-peaked with respect to any ordering of the candidates — which is enough to void the guarantee the chair keeps invoking when he proposes that the committee simply "take the median position," as though the candidates were arrayed along one axis. The joke may be a one-liner, a brief exchange, or an anecdote the chair retells at the next meeting, as you prefer, but it has to land for someone who has just worked through the proof for themselves; if the only people who would laugh are the ones who would laugh at any joke about committees, it isn't the joke I need.
