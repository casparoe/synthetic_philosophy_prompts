I'm a software engineer with no formal philosophy training, and I've been trying to work out what I actually believe about accepting things on other people's say-so. I have a set of fairly firm verdicts about particular cases, and I want to know whether one principle generates them or whether I'm quietly applying different rules in different places. I'm told there's a long-running dispute between people who hold that testimony justifies belief only when you have independent grounds to think the speaker reliable on that occasion, and people who hold that testimony is a basic source of justification, like perception, so that trust is the default and the burden falls on whoever wants to distrust. I haven't read that dispute and I'm not asking for a reading list or a summary of who said what. I want to know which position, if either, fits the verdicts below, and where my verdicts are simply inconsistent.

My verdicts:

1. A stranger at a station tells me the next train leaves from platform 4. I believe them. I have never met them, I know nothing about their reliability, and I could have looked at the board. Believing them seems entirely fine to me.
2. A colleague tells me a function in a third-party library is thread-safe. I believe them, though I've never tracked their accuracy on library semantics.
3. A vendor's whitepaper says their database sustains ten thousand writes per second on commodity hardware. I don't believe it at all until I run the benchmark myself. The speaker is a professional and may well be reporting honestly; I still think believing them would be a mistake.
4. A user reports that the export button crashes on Safari. I believe them immediately, even though users misdiagnose causes constantly and I've been burned by reports before.
5. A friend who is confidently wrong about most technical matters tells me a configuration flag defaults to true. I half-believe them, largely because I can settle it in ten seconds. If settling it took an afternoon, I wouldn't believe them at all.
6. The same friend tells me our former manager was pushed out in a dispute with the board. I don't believe them, and no amount of cheap checking would help, because there is nothing I could check.
7. My doctor tells me my fatigue is probably sleep apnea. I believe them, even though I can't assess their reasoning and my own reading pointed elsewhere.
8. Someone sincerely and in detail tells me they were abducted by aliens. I don't believe them.
9. Two hundred forum answers state the same obscure fact about a compiler. I discover they are all copies of one original answer, and my confidence falls back to what it was for that single answer.
10. Two hundred colleagues who don't talk to each other independently report the same intermittent bug. My confidence rises sharply.
11. I come to trust a blogger on database internals by reading their posts and finding them right on the parts I could check. So my confidence in their uncheckable claims rests on my having already trusted some of their claims. This seems legitimate to me, even though the first step can't be justified without the very thing I'm trying to justify.
12. A coding assistant tells me a library function silently truncates its input past a certain length. I don't believe it even provisionally; I treat the message as a search query and go read the source. From a colleague I would provisionally believe the same claim and perhaps verify later. My track record with the two is roughly comparable, so the asymmetry bothers me.

The tentative principle I'd offer is this: trust is the default, and the default is defeated when the speaker has a stake in my believing them, when the claim is extraordinary relative to what I already accept, or when being wrong would cost me a lot; and where verification is cheap, I stop caring about the speaker's reliability altogether. That principle gets case 3 and case 8 and case 1, but it seems to say nothing that distinguishes case 5 from case 6, and it gives me no account of case 11 or the asymmetry in case 12.

What I want: the principle, or the smallest family of clauses, stated precisely enough that I could apply it to a case I haven't thought of; an honest statement of which of my twelve verdicts the principle cannot accommodate and why; and its predictions for cases I haven't listed — a colleague who has been wrong twice before tells me something ordinary and uncheckable; an anonymous post that agrees with my prior turns out to trace back to one source; an assistant contradicts the documentation on a point I can't test by running code; someone who benefits from my believing them describes a meeting I missed; my own memory disagrees with a log file. I'd also like to know whether "cheap to check" belongs in a principle about justification at all, or is just a fact about what I'll bother to do, and whether the circular-looking step in case 11 is a real defect or ordinary inductive practice. If the verdicts can't be unified, say so plainly and tell me which one to give up.
