I'm midway through a course on Enlightenment political thought, and this week we read Rousseau's Letter to d'Alembert on the theatre — the long open letter he published in the 1750s against the suggestion, floated in the Encyclopédie's article on Geneva, that his native city ought to have a playhouse. I came away convinced that Rousseau is making a single sweeping moral argument, and the summary sheet our seminar circulated seemed to confirm it. It reads: 'Rousseau's Letter is a blanket attack on the theatre as such. He holds that the stage is inherently corrupting: actors earn their living by counterfeiting emotions, so the profession is degrading in itself, and plays teach audiences to take pleasure in vice while laughing at virtue — as he complains of Molière's Le Misanthrope, where the honest man is turned into a figure of fun and the smooth flatterer is vindicated. And because a theatre only ever mirrors and flatters an audience's existing tastes, never reforming them, Rousseau concludes that drama ought to be abolished everywhere; that is why he urges Geneva to reject the playhouse Voltaire was proposing to build there. Strip away the republican vocabulary and it is essentially the old religious case against the stage.' A friend assures me this is more or less how Rousseau is usually taught, but something about it feels off, and I don't know the text well enough to locate the problem. I am inclined to believe the summary, which is exactly why I want it checked before I lean on it in an essay.

Could you audit it for me claim by claim? I would like to know which parts a Rousseau specialist would accept as fair, which are common simplifications that a specialist would wince at, and which are simply wrong — and, where the summary misstates him, what the corrected version of his position actually is. Please also say what a defender of Rousseau would object to most strongly, whether the sharpest objections are to his conclusions or only to the summary's framing of them, and whether the final claim — that he wanted drama abolished everywhere — follows from anything accurate in the summary. I am especially unsure how much of his argument is a timeless moral verdict about the theatre as an art form and how much is a claim about a particular kind of society, and I would like the two kept apart rather than run together.
