Alright, I want an actual number out of you on this one, not a literature review.

You know the Gödelian anti-mechanist move (Lucas/Penrose): the sentences an idealized human mathematician would eventually accept as provable can't be enumerated by any Turing machine, because given any such machine you can construct its Gödel sentence, which the machine can't produce but which we can see is true. I know the usual complaints (soundness, idealization, a machine could just be inconsistent). What I want to know is whether there's a repaired version that survives, and I keep circleing one specific repair.

Drop soundness. Have the anti-mechanist claim only that we can recognize the consistency — or the soundness — of our own reasoning, at least for the fragment we're currently running. But that's a strange thing to claim. By Gödel's second theorem, a consistent system of the relevant kind can't prove its own consistency. So if we really do see that our reasoning is consistent, that seeing has to be mathematical knowledge that outruns proof — an insight into the soundness of one's own reasoning that isn't itself a theorem.

So: what's your credence that this repaired argument goes through? Decompose it into terms, a number and one line for each, then combine:

(a) that the target claim is even well-defined (is the idealized human mathematician a determinate thing, or does the idealization slide between different limits?);
(b) that the weakened assumption — recognizing consistency rather than assuming soundness — is genuinely weaker and doesn't sneak soundness back in;
(c) that seeing the consistency of my own reasoning is a real epistemic state a human can be in and a machine can't, rather than a restatement of the conclusion;
(d) that the regress doesn't eat it: if we can see the truth of our own Gödel sentence, a machine could be that extended system, and then there's a new Gödel sentence — does the anti-mechanist get off the treadmill or just take one more step?;
(e) that any of this carries over from the idealized case to actual human minds, or that it doesn't matter.

Then tell me which term your number is most sensitive to, what single thing — an argument, a formal result, a thought experiment — would move it most, and what would actually count as this debate resolving. Because I half suspect the real disagreement is about that last bit rather than about Godel.
