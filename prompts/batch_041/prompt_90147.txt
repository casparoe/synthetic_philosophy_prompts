I sit on the advisory board of a small foundation that has agreed to fund roughly eight philosophers and philosophically inclined cognitive scientists for three years to work on a deliberately narrow question: when, if ever, is it warranted to attribute propositional attitudes — belief above all — to a trained model, and what actually turns on doing so? The trustees' real interest is AI governance, but they have been persuaded that the conceptual groundwork is worth paying for, so the brief is theory rather than benchmarking. My job is to propose an ordering of the open questions in this area and defend it at the next board meeting, and I would far rather have the defence than the list.

To force the issue, I have been sorting the terrain into three registers, and I would like you to interrogate the sorting rather than simply accept it. In the first register, attitude-talk is purely predictive: saying that the model thinks the corridor is clear is shorthand for a compressible generalisation about its behaviour, and the only test is whether the shorthand earns its keep in prediction and control. In the second, it is diagnostic: the attribution is a claim about what is inside the network — some structure that a probe or an interpretability technique could in principle locate — and it is answerable to evidence about mechanism. In the third, it is deliberative: the attribution figures in our practical reasoning about the system, in judgements about deception, sycophancy and manipulation, in ascriptions of responsibility, and in decisions about deployment. I suspect these registers come apart more often than the literature admits, and that a good deal of confusion in the area is register-confusion in disguise. There is a wrinkle I keep returning to as well: because these systems are trained on text in which humans ascribe attitudes to one another, some of the behavioural evidence for an attribution is manufactured by the training process, and a model's self-report is not a report in the ordinary sense at all.

What I want from you is a prioritisation for those three years. Which open questions in this area most deserve the eight people, and in what order? Defend the ordering by the criteria you take to be the right ones — how far a question is upstream of others, how tractable it is for people whose training is in philosophy rather than ML engineering, how neglected it is, and how much difference its resolution would make to the governance questions the trustees actually care about — and be explicit about where those criteria pull apart and which you would sacrifice for which. Tell me which questions are upstream of which, which you would deprioritise precisely because settling them would not change much downstream, and what developments over the next two or three years — a result, a kind of model, a regulatory decision — would move an item up or down the list. Finally, tell me where the three-register sorting is doing genuine work and where it collapses into a distinction the field already has. I am not after a reading list; I want the agenda and the reasoning that would let me defend it. Please write in British English.
