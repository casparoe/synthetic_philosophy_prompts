I have been trying to reconstruct, for a graduate seminar, an argument that seems to show that rational markets cannot trade at all. It is meant to be a reading of the no-trade theorem, but it proves far too much. To make it as strong as possible, assume that the market price is common knowledge, that it is a rational expectations equilibrium price, and that it is epistemically dominant in the following sense: conditioning on the price is at least as informative about the asset's payoff as conditioning on any other public signal available in the market. Here is the argument.

1. A voluntary trade at price p occurs only if both parties expect to be better off than they would be without the trade; otherwise at least one would refuse.
2. If two traders have a common prior over the relevant states, and this is common knowledge, then any difference in their expected valuations of the asset, holding their risk preferences fixed, must be due to a difference in their private information.
3. In a market, each trader knows that the other is rational and knows that the other's willingness to trade is based on the other's information.
4. So if A offers to buy at p, B must treat A's offer as evidence that A's information makes the asset worth more than p; if B offers to sell at p, A must treat B's offer as evidence that B's information makes the asset worth less than p.
5. Therefore, at any p, the buyer's and seller's offers carry opposite information, and each trader's posterior is pulled toward the other's; in the limit, no p can leave both with positive expected gain.
6. This holds for any asset, any p, and any private information, provided the common prior and common knowledge of rationality assumptions hold.
7. Therefore, in any such market, no voluntary trade occurs.
8. Since trade does occur, either common priors are false or traders are not rational.
9. But common priors are required for Bayesian agents to avoid Dutch books, and traders are at least approximately rational. So the no-trade theorem shows that markets are epistemically incoherent, and prices cannot be interpreted as rational aggregates of private information.

I cannot accept step 7, and I am suspicious of the whole shape of the argument, but I also do not want to give up step 1 or the observation that trade is pervasive. Which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost? If there is a single slide, name it. If there are two, say which is primary. In particular, tell me whether the argument has misstated the scope of the no-trade theorem, whether it has conflated a claim about speculative trade with a claim about all voluntary exchange, and whether the epistemic-dominance assumption changes anything. I would like to know what each of the following would have to give up: a common-prior Bayesian, a heterogeneous-priors Bayesian, a rational-expectations theorist, and someone who thinks markets are mostly noise.
