Some context before the ask. I'm a backend engineer at a mid-sized software company, and for the past two months I've been on an internal working group writing the policy that governs how our assistant product answers users who ask it for moral or political advice — whether to report a colleague, whether to take a job, how to vote, that sort of thing. Two members of the group have been arguing past each other since the first meeting and the rest of us have quietly stopped following. The first (call him the aggregator) thinks the problem is one of combination: values are values held by individual people, so the policy has to be derived from some defensible way of merging everyone's preferences into a single ranking, and he keeps invoking the argument that an impartial chooser who didn't know which person she would turn out to be would pick the arrangement maximizing average welfare, which he takes to settle what serving everyone means. The second (call her the neutralist) says that is a category error: a liberal society contains a plurality of reasonable conceptions of the good and has no single ranking to discover, so a system with this much influence over people's choices has to stay neutral among those conceptions rather than manufacture a winner — and the aggregator's proposal, she says, launders one contested conception of the good, that more satisfied preferences is always better, into a neutral-sounding procedure. Each now accuses the other of begging the question, and I genuinely cannot tell how much of this is a real disagreement and how much is a fight about words. My own background is distributed systems; the nearest thing I have to philosophical training is a symbolic logic elective I took years ago.

Please draft the joint statement the two of them should have produced and could both sign. I want the claims they can agree on about what aligning a system to human values can and cannot mean, including claims about the limits of each other's framework; the residual disagreements, each written in wording its own side would accept as fair to it; and the cruxes — the sub-questions such that settling one would move at least one of them — with, for each crux, a case, argument, or observation that both agree bears on it and each side's prediction recorded in advance. Format it as a numbered agreed-claims section followed by a table of disagreements.

Three things I would like the document to come to grips with rather than skate over, because these are where our meetings actually stall. First, our product emits one output at a time, so some aggregation happens no matter whose policy we adopt; say whether that makes neutrality impossible, or merely relocates the aggregation into the design of the assistant, and whether both of them can accept that framing. Second, handle the inclusion question explicitly: whose preferences or conceptions count when we aggregate, or when we refuse to — the user, the people affected by the user, future people, non-human animals, and the possibility that the system's own dispositions should count for something. Third, have both sides say what would actually change their minds, and say plainly if any part of the dispute turns out to be terminological — for instance if neutrality means neutrality of justification to one of them and neutrality of effect to the other. Assume I cannot look anything up, and do not hand me a reading list; make the arguments self-contained.
