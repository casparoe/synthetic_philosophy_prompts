Most legal systems recognize a cause of action for "wrongful birth" while refusing one for "wrongful life," and I want to know what theory of the value of existence that split presupposes. The setup is familiar: a physician misreads a test or fails to disclose a prenatal risk, the parents would have avoided or terminated the pregnancy had they known, and a child is born with a serious disability. The parents may sue and recover, in many places, at least the extraordinary costs of caring for a child with that condition. The child, by contrast, generally cannot sue on the ground that but for the negligence she would never have existed and that her existence itself is the injury. The usual grounds for refusal are that existence cannot be compared with nonexistence, that life is a benefit whatever its impairments, or that no duty was owed to a person who did not yet exist. The pattern is not uniform, and the deviations are the interesting part. A few jurisdictions let the child recover, but only for objectively provable economic losses, never for the experience of having been born disabled rather than healthy. One constitutional court has held that a wrongful life claim is itself an affront to the equal dignity of disabled persons, while the same system continues to award parents the added maintenance costs of the child, insisting that the award says nothing about the value of the child's existence.

So: does this body of doctrine commit anyone to the view that coming into existence is never worse for the one who exists? Or does it commit only to a narrower claim about the incomparability of existence and nonexistence, or to no axiological claim at all, resting instead on a structure of duties and standing under which the physician owed a duty of information to the parents and none to the child? Benatar's asymmetry holds that existence can contain harms that nonexistence avoids, while nonexistence deprives no one of any benefit, so that bringing someone into the world can be wrong even if that person's life is worth living. Does the legal practice rule that view out, or is it compatible with it in ways its defenders have not noticed? Reconstruct the implicit theory of harm and value, separate what the doctrine strictly presupposes from what it merely fits, identify where it is underdetermined among rival views, and say what would have to change—in the standard of care, in prenatal screening, in the damages rules—if the asymmetry were treated as settled.
