I teach a graduate seminar on toleration and free expression, and I want to open the term with a satirical text rather than a philosophical one — something the students have to argue with before they have the vocabulary to do it comfortably. Please write it for me.

The text I have in mind is the official record of a single meeting of a fictional body: a standing committee on toleration, say, of a university, a professional association, or a press council. The choice of institution is yours, provided the committee reads as a real organ of governance rather than a thought experiment. Give me the minutes — agenda items, motions, votes, the chair's rulings, and two or three recorded objections. Around 800 words, deadpan throughout, in the flat register of a secretary with no opinions about what she is transcribing. No narrator's asides, no winking, and no character who steps outside the fiction to explain the paradox or to name the positions on the table.

The committee resolves the paradox of tolerance by adopting it as standing policy, and the comedy should come from watching that resolution do its work. Equip it with two or three charter provisions I can recognize: a last-resort clause requiring that any suppression be the least restrictive means available; a working definition of intolerance that reaches positions whose general adoption would foreseeably shrink the space for future toleration; and a procedural guarantee that an objection is heard and entered in the record before it is overruled. Then let the meeting take up four or five cases such a committee would actually face — for instance, a member of the committee's own constituency who argues that its studied neutrality is itself the intolerance; a complaint that turns on speech rather than conduct, where the charter's language is simply silent; an accusation of intolerance that must itself be assessed for intolerance; and a case in which the last-resort clause forces a choice between silencing a speaker and silencing the people who would answer him.

Two constraints. First, every decision must be defensible from the charter as written: no motion may be carried on the chair's authority alone, and no case may be resolved by an obvious cheat. Second, put the strongest arguments in the dissents, so that a reader feels the pull of the position that loses the vote. The piece should be funny to someone who has read the literature and legible to someone who has not: a reader who has never encountered the paradox should be able to reconstruct the rule from the minutes and to see why it is uncomfortable, and a reader who knows the terrain should recognize each case as a live position rather than a strawman. I would like the minutes to be in dialogue with two traditions without naming either — the liberal one, in which toleration is one virtue among others whose limits are set by reciprocity rather than by the content of anyone's beliefs, and the critical-theory one, in which the demand for tolerance in an unequal society is itself an exercise of power. Whichever way the committee's votes lean, the votes should carry the argument rather than the speeches.

End the meeting without resolution: a final item that would apply the charter to the committee's own composition or procedure, tabled pending a ruling on whether the motion is itself intolerant, recorded, and adjourned. Do not resolve the piece in favor of toleration or against it; if it commits to anything, let it be the thought that the rule cannot be applied without a prior decision about who is applying it, and let that be visible only in the pattern of the decisions.

Invent the members; give them plainly invented names, and do not model them on identifiable living people. No citations, epigraphs, or references to real documents are needed — I will supply the reading myself.
