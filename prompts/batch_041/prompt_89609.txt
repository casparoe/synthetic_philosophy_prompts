You are a philosopher working at the intersection of decision theory, the philosophy of practical rationality, and the microeconomics of investment under uncertainty. I am scoping a research agenda and will judge your answer on the quality of its reasoning rather than on the breadth of its coverage, so think carefully before you commit to anything.

Setting: a foundation can support roughly eight researchers for three years on the theory of instrumental rationality — conceptual and formal work, not new laboratory experiments. They have asked me to recommend where the marginal effort should go. I want to concentrate on a question the textbook treatment tends to flatten: is honoring sunk costs always irrational?

To make the question tractable, adopt the following stipulative taxonomy. Treat it as an instrument for this exercise: you may refine it, extend it, or argue that its joints are cut in the wrong place, but if you do, say so explicitly. A past and unrecoverable expenditure might influence a present decision through at least four distinct routes.

1. The evidential route. The fact that the investment was made is evidence about some relevant state — the quality of the project, the competence or resolve of the investor, or the beliefs of third parties — and so enters the decision through probabilities rather than through any accounting of gains and losses already booked.

2. The structural route. The investment has changed the feasible set or the marginal product of further effort — through complementarities, asset specificity, learning, or option value — so that the past expenditure matters only because it has altered what is now available.

3. The commitment route. Because the expenditure is observable, to others or to one's future self, continuing or abandoning it alters incentives, reputation, or the credibility of threats and promises.

4. The brute route. The past expenditure is treated as a reason in its own right — as something owed to the past, or as a loss that must be made good — which is the route the classic critique of the sunk cost fallacy targets.

Your task is to give me a defended research agenda, not a survey. Begin by brainstorming the space of candidate open questions, then narrow and commit to an ordered list of the four to six that most deserve the foundation's three years. For each, explain why it belongs where it does: whether it is upstream of other questions (and which), how tractable it is for philosophers and formal decision theorists as opposed to economists or psychologists, how neglected it is, and whether settling it would change any actual practice — institutional review, personal decision-making, firm governance, or public policy. State the criteria you are using and the trade-offs among them, and for each item say what argument or finding would move it up or down the ranking.

Two further things I want from you, woven into the ranking rather than appended. First, say which of the four routes is the most under-theorized, and whether the blanket verdict that honoring sunk costs is irrational survives once the evidential, structural, and commitment routes are taken seriously — or whether it survives only for the brute route, in which case say what theoretical work that leaves undone. Second, address how the unit of analysis changes the answer: does it matter whether the decision-maker is an individual, a firm, or a collective body such as a legislature that cannot cheaply reverse course, and can an act that looks irrational for one unit be rational for a larger one that holds a portfolio of such commitments?

Do not give me a bibliography, reading list, or citations; I want the argument itself, reconstructed from what you can reason out, and I would rather have a sharp ordering with clear costs than a comprehensive map.
