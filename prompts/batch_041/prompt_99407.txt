I'd like a precise, in-the-weeds explanation of the self-undermining objection to conciliationism about peer disagreement, together with the resources conciliationists have for answering it. In outline the objection is this: a conciliationist who applies her own view without exception will, upon meeting a steadfast peer, be required to conciliate toward steadfastness and thereby to abandon conciliationism, whereas a conciliationist who instead treats conciliationism itself as a fixed point exempt from the demand to conciliate owes us a principled reason for that exemption rather than an ad hoc one. I want the dilemma laid out with care — the exact assumptions it requires, the level at which the disagreement is supposed to bite, and whether the exemption is genuinely unprincipled or can instead be supported by a principled asymmetry between first-order and higher-order disagreement. Please give the response as exactly five numbered points, each a sentence or two, with no introduction and no conclusion. The five points should, in order: state the objection in its strongest form; identify the assumptions it depends on; give the most promising conciliationist reply; give the most promising rejoinder to that reply; and say what you take to be the crux on which the whole objection turns.
