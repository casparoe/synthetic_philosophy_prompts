I have no training in philosophy or in the history of science; I left school at sixteen and have spent about three years reading my way into this on my own, mostly through popular books and through a long argument with a friend of mine, a retired chemistry teacher who reads more widely than I do. The argument is about how one tells whether a scientific idea is still worth pursuing or is being kept alive out of habit. She gives verdicts, I forget them, so I have started keeping a list. A mutual friend who did study philosophy once remarked that the question is essentially Lakatos's, though I have never read him. What I have is her verdicts, and I want to know two things: what she will say about one further case, and whether her verdicts so far pin down a rule at all.

Her verdicts, as I have written them down:

(1) The 1989 claim that a simple laboratory cell had produced nuclear fusion at room temperature. When other laboratories failed to reproduce it, the original group and its defenders kept adding conditions — the effect appears only with this kind of metal, after this much charging, when you look for this particular signal — and none of those conditions predicted anything new that was afterwards found, while ordinary nuclear physics explained the failed replications and continued to make predictions of its own. Degenerating.

(2) A prediction, from small irregularities in the orbit of a planet already known, that a further planet would be found in a particular part of the sky; the planet was found near there. Progressive.

(3) A claim that failed a clean test, after which its few supporters drifted off to other work. Not degenerating, she says — dead. Degenerating, in her usage, is for programmes whose defenders stay and keep patching.

(4) The theory that light required a medium to travel through. As the evidence against it piled up, its defenders kept adding properties to the medium — it is dragged along by matter; it contracts bodies by exactly the right amount — while the rival account, which did without a medium altogether, predicted a new effect that was then observed. Degenerating.

Now the case I want her to rule on. Physicists were sure of a principle: that energy is never lost in a certain kind of nuclear decay. Then a decay was observed in which the energy did not add up — the electron came out with less than it should have, and with a different amount each time. Rather than give up the principle, one of them proposed that the missing energy was being carried off by a particle nobody had ever seen, a particle so indifferent to matter that it would pass through the Earth without noticing. It was treated at the time as a desperate repair, and there were respected people who thought the honest conclusion was that the principle simply fails in this decay. But it was not only a repair. It gave the new particle definite properties, and it implied that the particle could be caught with a large enough detector and would show up in other reactions too; those implications were later confirmed.

What will she say about it? I am less interested in her answer than in these questions: which rules fit her verdicts so far — I would be surprised if there were only one — what each of those rules predicts for this case, which rule she is most likely running and which of her earlier verdicts does the most to show it, and what further case, one she has not yet judged, would best tell those rules apart.
