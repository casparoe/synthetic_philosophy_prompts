For three years I have been writing a novel about the slow decline of a mercantile city-state, and the work has forced on me a question I would once have waved away as a philosopher's hobbyhorse: whether the explanation I give when I tell a reader why a character acted as he did is an explanation at all, or merely a story that leaves the reader feeling that he understands.

Let me state the claim I want a number for as precisely as I can. Call a *narrative explanation* of a historical event any account that makes the event intelligible by placing it in an ordered sequence — motives giving rise to choices, choices to consequences, the sequence arranged so that the ending throws light back on the beginning. Call a *covering-law explanation* any account that makes the event intelligible by exhibiting it as the conclusion of an argument from general regularities together with antecedent conditions. The claim is this: every narrative explanation that genuinely succeeds can in principle be restated as a covering-law explanation without loss of explanatory content — that the narrative form explains nothing that the regularities it gestures at do not already explain.

What is your credence that the claim is true? I want a number, then the reasoning that produces it, decomposed into the sub-questions the number depends on, each with a rough probability and a line of justification, and then the combination. At minimum I would expect you to price: the probability that explanation is a single natural kind at all, so that "explanatory content" is something one can lose or preserve across forms; the probability that the relevant regularities exist at the grain of individual conduct rather than only as statistical tendencies that do not entail any particular act; and the probability that the reduction fails because explanation is answer to a why-question and therefore interest-relative, leaving no interest-free content to be preserved. I keep two columns in my notebooks and have come to think the distinction between them is the crux: generalities that do load-bearing work in a scene, and narrative connective tissue that only keeps the reader oriented. Tell me which column you think a covering-law restatement is really reconstructing, and whether the other column is doing explanatory work or merely rhetorical work.

I put my own credence at about 0.3; a philosopher friend I argue with puts hers at about 0.75. So give me your number, tell me which sub-question it is most sensitive to, and then tell me which of the two of us is most likely mistaken about which sub-question — where our disagreement actually lives. Finish by naming the evidence or argument that would move your number most, and in which direction. I have no use for a reading list or for who said what first; I want the derivation.

Begin with your bottom line — the number — in the first sentence, then give the decomposition. No preamble, and do not restate my question.
