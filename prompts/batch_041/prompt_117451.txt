I'm a second-year PhD student in the history and philosophy of science, and there's a one-liner I keep running into that I've come to suspect is doing far more work than anyone who says it realises. It has turned up twice this month. First, in a seminar on scientific priority, a classmate waved away the whole Newton–Leibniz quarrel over the invention of the calculus with "if one of them hadn't got there, the other would have, so the priority question is just a distraction." Then, in a reading group on Darwin and Wallace, someone said that the fact that two men arrived independently at natural selection shows the discovery was bound to happen when it did — the discovery, not merely some discovery or other — and concluded that we therefore shouldn't care which of them gets the credit. I have a nagging feeling that these two remarks are not the same claim wearing the same words, and that neither is simply false; but I can't say what either of them actually amounts to, and whenever I try to push on it the conversation slides away. What I want from you is not a defence of the slogan but a menu.

Please lay out the distinct precise theses that "if he hadn't done it, someone else would have" could be expressing, ordered from the nearly trivial to the genuinely radical, with a verdict on each — platitude, substantive and defensible, or simply false. Separate the readings along the axes that seem to me to be doing the hidden work: what exactly is being quantified over (some person or other, some discovery or other, or this very discovery with this very content at this very moment); whether the claim is about the world (a kind of determinism about discovery), about our evidence (an inductive generalisation from how often multiple discovery seems to occur), or about explanation and credit (the individual doesn't explain anything, so deserves no credit for anything); and how each reading stands to the softer thought that "the time was ripe" — that the technical and social conditions made some such discovery likely, or even overdetermined. Two further points I'd like you to press on, because they are where I get stuck. First, is the counterfactual even well formed when the two "independent" discoveries were not, as far as I can tell, the same theory in every respect — and if the content isn't fixed, what exactly was supposed to be inevitable? Second, is there a hidden premise carrying you from "inevitable" to "the question of who did it doesn't matter," and is that inference valid? My instinct is that one could accept the strongest inevitability claim and still think it matters enormously who did the work, but I can't tell whether that instinct survives contact with the argument.

Don't hand me a reading list — I don't need citations, and if one of the readings has a standard label I'll take the label, but the carving is what I'm after. Tell me at the end which reading you think my classmates were actually invoking, in each of the two cases if they differ, and be explicit about your confidence and about what would change your mind: which consideration, turning out one way rather than the other, would push you to a different verdict on the central reading, and where in the analysis you think you're most likely to be wrong. Finish with a single line of the form "Verdict: <the reading that best supports the conclusion that priority doesn't matter, or 'none'>".
