You are a philosopher of biology whose work sits at the intersection of the philosophy of science and origins-of-life research, and you have been asked to advise a foundation that can fund roughly six postdoctoral researchers for four years on conceptual work — not laboratory work — bearing on the question of what life is and where its boundaries lie. The trustees are not philosophers; they want a defended agenda, not a survey. Think carefully before you answer, and begin by brainstorming the full space of open questions before you commit to a ranking.

The backdrop is that several borderline cases have become pressing at once: obligate intracellular parasites and giant viruses, whose genomes are stripped back to the point that they can no longer be said to sustain themselves; laboratory-built protocells that maintain themselves but reproduce only crudely; and engineered minimal cells whose every component is designed rather than evolved. Meanwhile a working definition that has circulated in astrobiology — life as a self-sustaining chemical system capable of Darwinian evolution — is doing a great deal of quiet work in how missions and funding panels are framed.

I want you to weigh three further cases that I have invented for this exercise, and to say explicitly how each should bear on the research agenda:

(a) a synthetic compartment that repairs itself and maintains its own internal gradients using an external energy source, but has no mechanism of heredity and cannot evolve;

(b) a lineage that possesses metabolism, heredity and heritable variation, but whose entire genome and machinery were designed, and which persists only through deliberate human propagation;

(c) an entity with a full genetic and metabolic apparatus that lies dormant for millennia and is revived only under laboratory conditions.

Which open conceptual questions — about the definition of life, about whether the word 'life' picks out a natural kind or a cluster concept, about the relationship between metabolism and heredity, about function and teleology in a designed entity, about the role that definitions ought to play in a science at all — matter most for a programme like this, and in what order? Please defend the ordering rather than merely listing: for each question, say why it is upstream of the others, how tractable it is for philosophers working without a wet lab, how neglected it is relative to the empirical work, and what would move it up or down the ranking over the next few years. Where a question is overrated — where settling it would change little downstream — say so and explain why. Conclude by identifying the two or three questions you would actually fund first, and the strongest objection to your own ranking.

Write in British English, in continuous prose, and keep the tone of a serious advisory memo.
