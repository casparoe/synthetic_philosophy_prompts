I belong to a small reading group, four economists, two philosophers and a computer scientist, that has now spent the better part of a year arguing about what the Gibbard–Satterthwaite theorem really establishes, and I have been asked to write the wrap-up note for our final meeting. We all state the theorem the same way and none of us disputes it: a social choice function that is defined on the unrestricted domain of strict ordinal preferences over at least three alternatives, takes a single winner as its value, and is onto and strategy-proof must be dictatorial. The fight is entirely about what follows from that. One faction treats it as the strategic twin of Arrow's theorem and draws a general lesson, namely that there is no non-dictatorial way to get people to report what they actually want, so the project of designing rules that elicit sincere preferences is a dead end and talk of choosing a better voting rule is wishful thinking. The other faction says this mistakes a scope-restricted theorem for a universal one and offers exits: restrict the domain and the impossibility evaporates, since with single-peaked preferences over a one-dimensional issue space the median rule is strategy-proof and has no dictator; let the mechanism allocate a divisible private good alongside the collective decision and you get dominant-strategy mechanisms that are neither dictatorial nor trivial; let the rule return a lottery rather than a single winner and strategy-proofness is available again; and in any case the theorem promises only that a profitable misreport exists, not that anyone can find it, since for several rules we actually care about computing a manipulation is intractable and in large markets the incentive to go looking for one is said to shrink toward nothing. The first faction has replies ready: the randomized rules that survive turn out to be lotteries over dictators and so buy appearance rather than substance; domain restrictions buy strategy-proofness only by assuming away the disagreement the rule was supposed to resolve; and the money-based mechanisms change the problem rather than solve it. A third position, held by one of the philosophers, is that the two factions never contradict each other, because one is asking whether sincere reporting can be an equilibrium of the game the rule induces while the other is asking whether sincerity is uniquely rational or privileged in any way, and that "no strategy-proof rule" and "here is a strategy-proof rule" can both be true once the domains and the notion of what is being chosen are made explicit.

What I want from you is a diagnosis of the dispute, not a survey of social choice theory and not a verdict on which faction is right about the mathematics. Is this a substantive disagreement about the significance of a theorem, a disagreement about scope that dissolves once the quantifiers are made explicit, two groups answering different questions under a single heading, or a genuine impasse in which the parties begin from different standards for what an impossibility result has to do to a research programme before it counts as a result? My own hypothesis, which I would like you to test rather than adopt, is that the first faction is not asserting a false theorem but making a claim about significance, that the second faction is answering a question about the reach of the theorem while believing it is answering the first faction's question about its lesson, and that the impasse is therefore partly verbal and partly a real disagreement about what counts as an escape from an impossibility. Tell me whether that hypothesis survives contact with the positions as I have described them, and where it fails.

Then tell me, given your diagnosis, what would actually move these people: a sharper statement of the theorem's scope, a formal result closing one of the alleged exits, a distinction between equilibrium and unique rationality, a change of question, or nothing at all, in which case say so and explain why no progress should be expected. Be explicit about which of the alleged escape routes are genuine and which are illusions, since my sense is that the diagnosis turns on that, and please skip the bibliography; I know the literature well enough, and what I need is the diagnosis.
