I teach an upper-level seminar on conditionals and probability — fourteen students, one ninety-minute session, all of them comfortable with conditional probability and expected value but with no measure theory. I want to run an in-class trading activity that makes them feel the force of the triviality result for indicative conditionals from the inside. The idea I want them to collide with, without ever being told it, is this: they will all accept that a conditional is the kind of thing that can be true or false, so that a ticket paying out on its truth can be bought and sold; and they will all accept that a fair price for such a ticket is the probability of the consequent given the antecedent. If both are held at once, their own posted prices can be turned against them until the price of if A then B is forced to equal the price of B alone, with the antecedent doing no work at all. The collision has to happen in the trading, not in anything I say; I want the name of the result spoken only once the post-mortem is under way.

Build the activity inside the following frame, because I do not want the familiar called-off-bet demonstration — I want a game the students have not seen. The underlying chance event is a single die roll with a publicly observed outcome. Two families of contracts trade. Plain contracts pay 1 if a specified event occurs and 0 otherwise. Conditional contracts pay 1 if the antecedent and consequent both occur, pay 0 if the antecedent occurs and the consequent does not, and are void — stake returned — if the antecedent does not occur. The desk must post a price for every contract, and the exchange rule book, which you should write as a one-page handout, requires prices to be coherent in the arbitrage sense: a customer free to buy or sell any contract at the posted price must not be able to assemble a book that yields a sure profit, which comes to the requirement that all posted prices be expectations under a single probability function on the outcome space. In a second phase the desk must re-quote every contract conditional on an announced scenario, and the coherence requirement applies again within that supposition. In a third phase one student, or I, plays a customer with a small budget of chips, may trade at the posted prices, and keeps whatever profit remains; the desk pays out of a shared pool.

Give me rules, roles, materials, the handout, the chip payoffs, a minute-by-minute plan for the ninety minutes, and the debrief. In the debrief, map each moment of the trading onto the corresponding step of the argument — the step at which voiding the contract makes the conditional probability the natural price, the step at which re-quoting under a supposition is forced by the same rule, and the step at which the rule of total probability collapses the price of the conditional into the price of its consequent — and say which student's posted number does the work at each step. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle it, and be frank about which feature of the result the activity cannot reproduce and that I will have to supply myself.

One failure I expect, so handle it explicitly: the desk will try to escape the collapse by pricing the conditional as the material conditional, worth 1 whenever the antecedent is false. That pricing is coherent and it dodges the result entirely. Design a moment where that escape is proposed, priced, and then made to cost the desk something it cares about, so that the escape is not free.

Constraints: no real money; nothing on the board that looks like a proof; the students should arrive at the absurdity by noticing one of their own numbers, not by being told a theorem. I want the debrief to be honest about the conclusion some of them will reach — that the real lesson is that conditionals have no truth value, rather than that the pricing rule is mistaken — and about what the facilitator has to add to make that conclusion, or its denial, an argued position rather than a shrug.
