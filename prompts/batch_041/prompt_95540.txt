I'm moderating the Q&A after a colloquium talk by a historian of nineteenth-century political thought. Her thesis is that J. S. Mill's late sympathy for socialism is continuous with On Liberty rather than a departure from it: on her reading the liberty principle constrains the form of economic arrangements — whether anyone is coerced into them — and not their content, so Mill could look forward to common ownership of the means of production while still denying that the state may compel anyone to join; and his objections to communism were objections about liberty and character, not about efficiency. Acting as an expert on Victorian political economy, and thinking carefully about where Mill's texts and the secondary literature actually press, draft eight to ten questions for the Q&A that make this thesis do hard work rather than merely restate it. Begin by sketching the two or three strongest lines of attack available to a well-informed critic, then write questions keyed to them, with at least one each on: (i) the classical distinction between the laws of production and the laws of distribution — if distribution is a matter of human institution through and through, what keeps property in the means of production from being a convention like any other, and can Mill's own retention of competition be squared with that; (ii) his Malthusian worry about population, and whether it can be answered inside a communal system without exactly the coercive checks her liberty reading forbids; (iii) his proposal to appropriate the future increase in the value of land, and whether that is a substitute for socialism, a step toward it, or a way of postponing the question; and (iv) the stationary state, which he came to welcome on grounds that seem to have little to do with who owns what. For each question include a one-sentence follow-up aimed at the answer she is likeliest to give, and close with a question she cannot wave away by saying that Mill was writing before the modern welfare state.
