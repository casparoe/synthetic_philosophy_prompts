I serve on the medical policy committee of a mid-sized regional health plan. Roughly once a month the committee must decide whether a contested condition counts as a disease for the purposes of our medical-necessity criteria, which determines whether we cover interventions for it, at what tier, and on what grounds we can defend the decision to appeals, regulators, and the press. The conditions that reach us are exactly the ones where the classification is unsettled: something statistically rare that appears to cause no harm; something so common in our population that it is close to normal; something defined almost entirely by distress while the underlying biology looks unremarkable; something whose only apparent cost is social or cosmetic. We have practicing clinicians on the committee but no philosopher, roughly an hour of discussion per condition, and a written rationale that has to survive being read by people who will look for inconsistency across our decisions.

Please give us a decision procedure we can actually run. It should be a small set of questions asked in a fixed order, with a statement of what counts as a sufficient answer at each step, explicit points where the procedure terminates in a negative verdict, and explicit points where it stops and hands the question to a value judgment rather than a fact. Ground it in what the philosophical work on health and disease actually establishes — the naturalist and biostatistical accounts, the harmful-dysfunction analysis, and the normativist and pragmatic alternatives — but write it so that a clinician or a lay committee member can apply it without having read any of that. In particular, the procedure must do the following. It must refuse to treat statistical abnormality as either necessary or sufficient for disease. It must force whoever applies it to state the reference class explicitly — relative to which population, age, sex, and environment the comparison is being made, and whether the comparison is to the local present population or to some ancestral or otherwise idealized one — and it must say what to do when the choice of reference class flips the verdict. It must separate intrinsic harm, meaning suffering or the loss of a capacity the person has reason to value, from extrinsic harm, meaning stigma, disadvantage, or cost, and it must take a position on whether extrinsic harm can ever carry a disease classification on its own. It must include a device for exposing the committee's own normative commitments, for example a counterfactual question of the form: if this condition were equally common and equally harmful but we had no biological account of it at all, would we still call it a disease? And it must be honest about which steps are judgment calls dressed up as procedure.

Then run the procedure on three cases, giving the verdict, the steps that did the work, and the points where it nearly failed. First, myopia in a population where the great majority of young adults are now affected. Second, prolonged grief, which the major psychiatric manuals now recognize as a diagnosis, and its boundary with ordinary bereavement. Third, idiopathic short stature in a child at the low end of the normal range, whose family is requesting growth hormone.

At least one of the three should come out in a way we will not like, and I would rather it be the case we are most tempted to decide on other grounds. Tell us what to do with that discomfort rather than how to make it go away, and say what would have to be true for the verdict to be wrong. Finally, state plainly what the procedure cannot do: which questions about who gets what, and at whose expense, it leaves untouched, and what it would be unreasonable to expect it to settle. Keep the procedure itself to something a committee member can hold on one page, and keep the worked cases short enough to read aloud in a meeting.
