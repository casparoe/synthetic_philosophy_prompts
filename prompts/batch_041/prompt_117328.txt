I'm TAing a seminar on the infinite and I need a one-page decision tree handout for Thomson's lamp — you know, flick the switch, wait half a minute, flick again, wait a quarter, wait an eighth..., is it on or off when you're done? Every handout I can find is just a list of four or five "responses," and it's useless, because the list is sorted by who said what rather than by what actually seperates the answers. I want the tree built from the logical space: internal nodes are questions a student can answer without knowing which position they lead to, leaves are the positions.

The distinctions I think are doing real work — tell me which of these are the same node twice, and add whatever I've missed:

- Does the description plus the laws of physics fix a state at the end at all?
- If not, is the failure in the description (it underdetermines), in the world (the setup is impossible), or in the question (it presupposes something false)?
- Do you accept that infinitely many steps can be completed with no last step — that "the last step" is a confusion rather than a requirement?
- Is the culprit the idealization itself (perfectly sharp switching, infinitely divisible time, a lamp that survives infinitely many toggles), so the puzzle is a reductio of a false model rather than of supertasks as such?
- Does excluded middle force the lamp to be either on or off even when nothing in the setup picks one?
- Is "the state at the end" the limit of the states before it by definition, or is that a substantive assumption about physical continuity?

Leaves I want at minimum: the "no fact of the matter, the question is ill-posed, it's not a paradox" line (Benacerraf's); "supertasks are impossible, you can't finish without a last step"; "the idealization is what's broken, so the lesson is about idealization and not about infinity" (the Earman-Norton move); "the lamp is a perfectly possible object and the answer is settled by the physics of lamps, which we just never specified — and maybe the physics says it breaks or isn't there at the end"; "genuine paradox, our concepts of infinity and of definite states are inconsistent"; and "there's no limit, so there's no answer, and that's not a defect." If the tree forces a leaf on you that nobody actually holds, keep it and mark it unnamed.

Two things I specifically want it to handle, because I think they break the standard answers:

1. A continuous version. Same switching times, but instead of on/off the lamp's brightness after the nth switch is 1/n, or it's on for intervals whose total length converges. Now the sequence converges, so there's a determinate answer and the "underdetermined" leaf looks wrong for this version. Does your tree have a node separating the discrete lamp from the continuous one, or does it lump them? If underdetermination only works when the state sequence is discontinuous, say so at that leaf.

2. Excluded middle plus determinism. A student who thinks on/off is exhaustive and that the setup settles everything about the lamp seems forced to say the description specifies a state it obviously doesn't. Where does that student land? Is it a stable leaf, or does the tree show it collapsing into one of the others?

Also flag any two leaves the tree can't seperate — if "the question is ill-posed" and "the idealization is broken" come out the same way at every node, tell me that instead of pretending they're different.

Format: indented tree, each internal node a one-line question with a short parenthetical on what the two answers do, each leaf the position's name plus one sentence on its most awkward commitment. No citations and no "see so-and-so" — the audience is grad students who've done Zeno and not much else, and I can't have them chasing references. One page, two at the most.
