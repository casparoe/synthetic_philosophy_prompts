I've got a bee in my bonnet about the dollar auction, and I want to use it on a real case. The game, in case the framing matters: an auctioneer sells a £1 coin, bidding starts at 5p, and the twist is that the top two bidders both pay what they bid — the runner-up hands over the money and gets nothing. The lesson everyone draws is "escalation of commitment", and then they explain the escalation with sunk costs and self-justification, as if the bidders were being irrational. But the game doesn't need any of that. The trap is in the payoffs: at some point both players are staring at a certain loss, and the only way to reduce it is to bid again. I want to test that against the twenty years of US and NATO involvement in Afghanistan, from the invasion in October 2001 to the withdrawal in August 2021. Take the standard facts as given — the quick fall of the Taliban, the long occupation, the surge under Obama, the money, including alot of it spent on an Afghan army that dissolved in weeks, the handovers between commanders and between presidents, the deal with the Taliban, the exit — and dont worry about anything finer than that; you can't look it up and the case doesn't need it.

I want the mapping done properly, in both directions, and I want you to be honest when it's only a metaphor. So: state what the dollar auction actually requires of a situation before it applies — a prize, bids that are sunk and visible to both sides, both sides paying, alternating moves, no way to lock in a commitment to stop, the option of quitting at any moment — and tell me if that list is wrong or missing something (someone once told me it's just an all-pay auction with an extra rule, which I've never checked). Then map the case: who the bidders are (the US and the Taliban is the obvious answer, but it sometimes looks like the US bidding against its own previous position, which would be a different game), what the prize is and whether it stayed the same, what counts as a bid, who if anyone is the auctioneer and who was collecting.

Then the part I care about most. The dollar auction predicts escalation with no psychological story at all, which means it should survive the fact that the escalators kept changing: Bush started it, Obama ordered the surge, Trump did the deal, Biden executed the exit, and the generals rotated every couple of years. The self-justification story — you escalate because you can't admit your own earlier decision was a mistake — has trouble with that, and the structural story doesn't. Say which one the episode actually supports, and whether there's a version of the psychological story that survives the turnover, something like an institution owning the commitment even when no individual does. Then the other direction: what does the case show about the game? Confirmation, anomaly, or something the game never anticipated? My suspicion is that the ending is where it breaks — the US didn't simply quit, it negotiated its way out and handed the other side concessions on the way, which isn't a move the game has — but tell me if that's wrong.
