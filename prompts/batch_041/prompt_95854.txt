Draft the joint statement that a committed expressivist and a committed cognitivist about moral judgment could both sign at the end of a good-faith adversarial collaboration on the Frege–Geach problem. Both parties are to be understood as sophisticated rather than as strawmen. The expressivist is a quasi-realist who has already helped himself to a minimalist or deflationary treatment of truth, truth-aptness, proposition, and belief, and who therefore denies that the mere fact that moral sentences embed in truth-functional and other truth-demanding environments settles anything by itself; the cognitivist is a non-naturalist realist who does not concede that the apparent practicality of moral judgment is a datum that only an expressivist can accommodate. Neither party may refuse a proposed formulation merely because it is neutral. Two ground rules the parties have accepted in advance, and which should be respected throughout: neither may treat the notion of a non-cognitive state as primitive or unexplained, and neither may treat truth-aptness as an unexplained primitive either. Every appeal to either notion must be cashed out in terms that do not presuppose the conclusion.

The document should have the following parts.

1. Agreed claims, numbered. These should include: a neutral statement of what the Frege–Geach point actually is — in particular the datum that a moral predicate means the same thing in freestanding and in embedded occurrences, and that an inference such as: lying is wrong; if lying is wrong, then telling your brother to lie is wrong; so telling your brother to lie is wrong, must come out valid; a shared inventory of the embedding environments a compositional account must cover, with an agreed description of what has to be delivered in each — negation, disjunction, conditionals with the moral sentence as antecedent and as consequent, modal and probabilistic operators, tense, interrogative and imperative constructions, propositional-attitude and speech-act ascriptions, anaphoric truth attribution (the case of someone who says of what Sam said, that lying is wrong, that it is true), and factive and explanatory constructions; and an agreed statement of what would count as success for each side.

2. The residual disagreements, each stated in wording that the side in question would accept as a statement of its own position rather than as a caricature of it. At a minimum: what the semantic value of a moral sentence is; whether sameness of meaning across freestanding and embedded occurrences is a constraint any compositional semantics must meet or is itself the thing in dispute; whether the expressivist's treatment of embedding is a semantics or a paraphrase; and whether the cognitivist's truth-aptness claim does any work beyond the minimal work the expressivist is equally willing to grant it.

3. The cruxes, with the negation problem prominent: whether the state expressed by a sentence such as lying is not wrong is the mere absence of the attitude expressed by the corresponding affirmative or a distinct negative attitude, and if the latter, toward what. Include also the crux about whether the expressivist's use of minimalist truth and minimalist belief leaves the two views saying the same thing in different words, so that what remains is a verbal dispute; and the crux about whether there is a practicality constraint on the semantics at all.

4. For each crux, a case, argument, or minimal pair that both sides agree would bear on it, with each side's prediction recorded in advance. Candidate material, to be used or replaced as the drafting requires: an agent who accepts everything the expressivist's analysis assigns to lying is not wrong and yet reports no attitude toward lying; an agent who asserts the premises of the moral modus ponens above and denies its conclusion; the same moral sentence asserted and occurring unasserted as the antecedent of an indicative conditional; and two speakers who utter the same moral sentence with the same attitudes and who, on one view but not the other, thereby disagree.

5. A closing section: what each side would count as evidence or argument against its own view; anything both agree would not move either; and, if some part of the dispute turns out to be merely verbal, which part and why.

Format: a numbered agreed-claims section; then a disagreement table with columns for the issue, the expressivist's formulation, the cognitivist's formulation, the crux it turns on, the case that would decide it, and each side's recorded prediction; then a short prose section on the verbal-dispute question.
