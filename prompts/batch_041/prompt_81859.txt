I'm in a small reading group on philosophy of mind and we keep circling the same thing. One of us is obsessed with a toy picture he calls the careenium: imagine a big box with a huge number of little balls, call them simmballs, bouncing around and colliding; the balls are just physical, but every so often a stable pattern of them forms, and the pattern is supposed to count as a symbol, like a word or a concept. The question he wants to ask is whether those symbols, once they show up, can push the simmballs around -- whether the high-level pattern has any causal oomph over the low-level collisions, or whether it's just a pretty description of what the balls were doing anyway. He then says this is just like the computer analogy everyone uses: the simmballs are the hardware, the symbols are the software, and the software doesn't literally shove the electrons, it's implemented by them. I think he's using the computer analogy to settle the careenium question, and I'm not sure it can.

Can you lay out the analogy as an audit? Give me the correspondence table -- what plays the role of hardware, software, the program, the programmer, the input, the output, the compiler, whatever else -- then tell me where the mapping breaks. In particular: does the software/hardware picture actually deny that symbols push the substrate, or does it just redescribe the pushing at a different level? What does the careenium add that the plain computer analogy leaves out? And does the conclusion he wants -- that symbols are real causes, not epiphenomenal froth -- survive once the disanalogies are on the table? If it doesn't, what would a better analogy be, maybe something like a traffic jam or a hurricane, where the higher-level pattern both depends on and constrains the lower-level stuff? I'd like the answer to separate the disanalogies that matter for his intended use from the ones that are just incidental, and to say which features of the computer picture are quietly being imported into the careenium without argument. Also, if you can, tell me what the careenium is supposed to be a picture of in the first place -- is it a brain, a program, a society of agents, or something else -- because I suspect we're not even agreed on that, and alot of the disagreement might be downstream of it.
