I sit on the acquisitions committee of a small university art museum, and next month we vote on a purchase that our own precedents do not obviously settle. Over three years the committee has ruled as follows, recording a ground in each case.

A seventeenth-century still life, long catalogued to a named master, was reattributed by technical analysis to an unknown follower. We bought it at roughly a tenth of the price the attribution had supported, and hung it with a corrected label; our ground was that the picture's beauty is untouched, and that what had changed was its place in art history.

A painting offered as the work of a famous modernist was shown by our own research to be a forgery so skilled that no connoisseur could distinguish it from the artist's hand. We declined it. Our ground was that a visitor would have had exactly the experience the genuine picture affords, but that we would have been buying and displaying a lie.

A hand-made replica of a famous ancient bronze whose original is lost — cast in period alloys with period tools by a contemporary sculptor — was acquired and given a prominent case, labelled as a modern replica. Our ground was that the replica is a remarkable piece of making in its own right, and that it lets visitors see something they could not otherwise see.

A high-quality photographic facsimile of a famous painting was declined for the galleries and accepted for the study collection. Our ground was that it reproduces an image but is not itself a work of art.

A genuine painting of high quality with an unresolved wartime provenance was declined, on grounds of restitution.

We are now offered a print of a mid-century photographer's best-known negative. The prints he made himself are canonical and command enormous prices. This one was struck in the 1990s by his estate, under the supervision of the printer who had worked alongside him, on the same paper and by the same process he used, and it is indistinguishable from one of his own prints even under a loupe. The estate prints to order, so ours would not belong to a limited edition; it is offered at a small fraction of the price of a lifetime print, and we would label it honestly as a posthumous estate print.

What should we decide if we want to be consistent with ourselves? I want the rules our past decisions could be read as embodying — I expect more than one will fit all five — together with what each of those rules predicts about this print and where they diverge. Say which precedent is the closest analog and why the others are further away. Give me your best recommendation and the reason for preferring the rule it rests on, and tell me plainly what principle we would be adopting — about what aesthetic value attaches to an object, and about what a museum is for — if we acquire the print for the galleries, and what principle we would be adopting if we took it only for the study collection or refused it. If the precedents genuinely underdetermine the case, say so, and name the further acquisition that would come before us and would tell the competing rules apart.
