The three standard responses to the epistemic regress — foundationalism, coherentism, and infinitism — have been traded back and forth for decades, and the objections on each side are by now entirely predictable: basic beliefs are either arbitrary or not really basic; the coherentist's circle never touches the world; the infinitist demands a chain no finite agent could ever hold. Nobody seems to move. I want a diagnosis of the impasse rather than another verdict for one side.

My own hypothesis, which I'd like you to test rather than assume, is that the three are not genuine rivals at all but answers to different questions wearing one word: foundationalism addresses what makes a belief non-inferentially justified (justification as a property of a proposition for a subject); coherentism addresses what makes a subject's total body of beliefs justified (justification as a property of a system); and infinitism addresses what reasons a subject could in principle produce if challenged (justification as availability). On this reading the deadlock is an artifact of equivocation on 'justification.' If that is too neat, tell me what the real axis of disagreement is — a substantive disagreement about how justification is transmitted, a disagreement about what a theory of justification is for, or bedrock intuitions about what it is to have a reason — and defend that diagnosis against the evidence of what each side actually argues. Then say what, if anything, would count as progress here: a distinction, a formal result, or an argument that no progress should be expected.
