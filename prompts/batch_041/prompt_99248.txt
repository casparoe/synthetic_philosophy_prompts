I've been going back and forth with a friend about conditionals and I want to pin down what I actually think, because my gut keeps splitting and I half-suspect there's no single rule behind it. Take these at face value, they're just my verdicts:

"If it's raining out, the match is off" — true. I'm watching the rain and I know the rules. And it seems to say the exact same thing as "the match is off if it's raining," I can't hear any difference between the two orderings.

"If it's raining, it's raining" — true, just useless.

"If the die comes up even, it's not a six" — sure, I'd accept that, probably right. But "if it comes up six, it's odd" — flatly false, no way. Then my friend says the second is just the contrapositive of the first and now I'm annoyed, because I *do* accept contraposition on stuff like "if he's in Paris he's in France" / "if he's not in France he's not in Paris." Both of those seem right to me.

"If 2+2=4 then snow is white" — I reject this. Feels like it's asserting some connection that isn't there, like a bad joke. But both halves are true, and my friend says any conditional with a true consequent comes out true. That can't be right.

"If it rains, the picnic's cancelled" — accept. "If it rains and we move it indoors, the picnic's cancelled" — reject, moving indoors is exactly the fix. So you can't just pile stuff onto the antecedent.

Here's the pair that really gets me. "If it wasn't Mary who leaked the memo, someone in the office did" — I'm near certain of this. "If Mary hadn't leaked the memo, someone in the office would have" — I don't beleive that at all, I think probably nobody would have. Basically the same words, different tense, opposite verdicts.

"If you're hungry there are biscuits on the sideboard" — perfectly fine thing to say, true even, and yet I obviously don't think being hungry makes biscuits appear. Compare with the die case, where I do feel like I'm tracking a real connection.

"If that's a real diamond then I'm a monkey's uncle" — I say this constantly and it never feels false, but corner me on whether it's literally true and I have no idea what to say.

My tentative rule was: a conditional is true just when the consequent is likely given the antecedent. That seems to get the die case, the picnic case, and the Mary-indicative case. But it makes the 2+2 case come out true, which I hate, and it makes the biscuits one come out mostly false, which I also don't like, and it has literally nothing to say about the Mary-counterfactual. So it's probably wrong or at least badly incomplete.

What I want: is there a principle that gets most of these, or do I have to give one up? I'd rather keep the ones I'm surest about — the Mary pair, the 2+2 rejection, contraposition failing on the die — even if that costs me the tidy "likely given" story. So pick your principle, state it precisely enough that I can apply it myself, tell me what it predicts for a case I haven't listed, and be honest about which of my verdicts it can't save. And if the honest answer is "you need two seperate principles here," say so and tell me where the line falls.
