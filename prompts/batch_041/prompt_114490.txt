The draft below is a short essay I have written on the scale argument for insect sentience. I would like you to comment on it.

The Scale Argument and Two Kinds of Uncertainty

Most arguments for caring about insect welfare are, at bottom, arithmetic. Nobody claims to know that a honeybee feels pain. The claim is rather that we should not need to know. Suppose there is some small probability — one in a hundred, one in ten thousand — that an insect's nervous system supports experiences that matter morally. The number of insects alive at any given moment exceeds the number of humans by many orders of magnitude, and the number of insects that live and die in a single season is larger still. Multiply a small probability by an enormous population, and the product can exceed the entire expected welfare of the vertebrate world. Call this the scale argument.

I want to argue that the scale argument equivocates between two kinds of uncertainty, and that once they are separated, the argument survives, but only in a form weaker than its proponents usually advertise.

The first kind is distributional uncertainty. Here we take for granted that some insects are sentient and are uncertain only about which ones, or about what fraction of them. The uncertainty is like a coin flip applied to each individual: for any given ant, there is a chance that it is a subject of experience and a chance that it is not. Uncertainties of this kind aggregate cleanly across a population. If each of a trillion individuals has probability p of being sentient, the expected number of sentient individuals is exactly p times a trillion, and the expected moral stakes scale linearly with the size of the population. Numbers matter, and they matter proportionally.

The second kind is categorical uncertainty. Here the question is not which insects are sentient but whether sentience is even the right category to apply to an insect's internal states. The uncertainty attaches to a general claim about a kind of organism rather than to a distribution of facts across individuals. If the truth is categorical in this way — either insects generally have morally significant experiences or they generally do not — then there are no per-individual coin flips to aggregate. There is one fact, and we are uncertain about it.

This distinction, I claim, is not merely verbal. It corresponds to a real difference in the shape of the underlying facts. If sentience is a matter of some threshold property being present — a certain kind of integrated information processing, say, or a certain functional architecture — then it is plausibly a property that a species either has or lacks, much as having a liver is not a matter of degree spread thinly across a population. Threshold properties are not distributed; they are shared.

The familiar reply is that this makes no difference to the arithmetic, because uncertainty about a categorical fact is still uncertainty, and uncertainty is uncertainty. If we are thirty percent confident that insects are sentient in general, then our expected number of sentient insects is 0.3 times the population, and we are back where we started. The scale argument is restored.

I think this reply fails, though the failure is subtle. The trouble is that the categorical case does not give us a probability of sentience per insect; it gives us a probability that a whole theory of insects is correct. And we should not treat our credences in theories as though they were credences in facts. When we say that we are thirty percent confident in a theory of insect consciousness, we are not reporting a frequency or a disposition to bet at those odds; we are expressing an aggregate of considerations that are themselves incommensurable. Assigning a number there is a bookkeeping convenience, not a measurement.

The consequence is that the force of the scale argument depends on which kind of uncertainty we are in, and its proponents have not shown that we are in the first kind. If the uncertainty is distributional, the argument goes through and the numbers do the work. If the uncertainty is categorical, the argument's apparent strength is an artifact of a probability that we had no right to assign in the first place. And I suspect that the categorical reading is the more likely one, since threshold properties are the norm rather than the exception in biology. So the scale argument should be treated as a conditional: compelling if the relevant properties are distributed across individuals, considerably less so if they are not.

Please give me detailed comments. I am most interested in the following, roughly in this order. First, accuracy: is the distinction between distributional and categorical uncertainty drawn correctly, and does my rebuttal to the restoration reply actually work, or does it fail for a reason I have missed? Second, internal consistency: I have a nagging sense that the essay uses numerical probability in a way that it later disowns, and I would like you to judge whether that is a real problem or only an appearance. Third, assumptions: flag any claim that is doing more work than the essay admits, and suggest how to qualify or repair it — but do not recommend sources, readings, or citations, since I am interested in the argument rather than the literature. Fourth, the conclusion: is it adequately defended, and what is the strongest objection to it? End with two or three concrete suggestions for strengthening the essay, in order of importance.

Write your comments in American English.
