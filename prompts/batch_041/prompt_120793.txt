my seminar keeps reaching for analogies whenever the question of whether 1789 had to happen comes up. the standard one is the pressure cooker: grievances pile up like steam, the ancien régime is the lid, and once the pressure gets past a certain point the thing blows — the bastille is either the valve letting off or the whole pot going up, depending on who's talking. a friend of mine who did a physics degree says that's the wrong picture and the right one is the sandpile: you drop grains onto a pile, it never settles at some safe slope, it works itself up to the point where the next grain either does nothing at all or takes the whole pile down, and you can't tell in advance which grain that'll be. he takes that to mean you can't name any single cause of the revolution, and also that it was bound to happen no matter what.

so how far does each of them actually go? i want the correspondence tables first — grain, slope, avalanche, lid, steam, valve, whatever else is carrying weight — then the places each one breaks as a picture of france from about 1787 to 1794, with a note on which breakages actually matter for the thing we care about (class struggle, ideology, or a run of accidents?) and which are just decoration. then tell me whether the sandpile really gets him what he wants: does it make the revolution inevitable, or only make some revolution inevitable while leaving this one a coincidence of timing, and is that a real distinction or a fudge? does either picture have anything to say about the revolution turning on the people who made it, or is that a different question that the metaphores just can't reach? and if there's a better comparison for what actually happened, i'd rather hear it.
