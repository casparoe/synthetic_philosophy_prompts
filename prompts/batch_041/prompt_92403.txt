I've been chewing on the ant-colony dialogue in Hofstadter's Gödel, Escher, Bach — the one where the Anteater insists that the individual ants are dumb and that it's the colony, Aunt Hillary, who is intelligent and can hold a conversation. What keeps tripping me up isn't the usual "is emergence spooky" worry but something narrower. The Anteater himself cheerfully grants that he can describe the very same cascade of ants either in micro terms that invoke no purpose at all, or in colony-level terms where the movement counts as a meaningful signal, and he treats the choice between them as a matter of which perspective you adopt. Taken at face value, that seems to make Aunt Hillary's mental states depend on the observer's choice of grain rather than on any fact about the colony — which is either a deep point or a cheap one, and I can't tell which. I'd rather not get a rehearsal of the standard emergence talking points; I want the specific issue isolated.

Here's the thought experiment that pushed me on this. Suppose we build a detector that takes a slice of the colony's micro-trajectories and outputs "the colony is in a belief-state about X." Now suppose we find that the detector's verdict flips depending on the width of the time-window we feed it: at fine grains nothing looks like a belief, at coarse grains a belief appears, and no window is privileged over the others by the colony's own dynamics. I genuinely can't tell whether this shows that colony-minds are real-but-grain-relative (in the spirit of the view that some patterns are real precisely because they're robust across many descriptions) or whether it shows that we're projecting minds onto whatever grain flatters our predictive convenience. So: lay out the conceptual issue cleanly. What exactly is at stake in asking whether Aunt Hillary "really" has a mind, once we concede that the mind-talk surfaces only at some levels of description and not others, and what would it take to settle the question in a non-arbitrary way rather than by fiat?
