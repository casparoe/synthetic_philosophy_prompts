Q3. Consider a ring of 400 sites with one particle on each site, 200 labelled A and 200 labelled B, and take as the initial state the sorted configuration in which the 200 A's occupy 200 consecutive sites and the B's occupy the rest. At each tick an adjacent pair of sites is chosen at random and the two particles on them are exchanged. Coarse-grain by describing a microstate through the number k of A/B boundaries around the ring, so the sorted state has k = 2 and the most mixed states have k near 200. (a) The swap rule is its own inverse, so the microdynamics of the model is time-reversal invariant. Explain how the coarse-grained entropy can nevertheless climb away from the sorted state. (b) Suppose we want the model to show a persistent arrow of time, in the sense that entropy reliably increases and the past is different in kind from the future. What, over and above the reversible micro-rule, has to be put into the model, and is that extra ingredient a law of motion or something else? (c) Now add a device that inspects the pair about to be swapped and vetoes the swap whenever carrying it out would increase k; the device logs each decision so the lab can audit it later, the log is of fixed finite size and so must periodically be wiped, and the whole apparatus sits at temperature T. Can the device hold the ring in the sorted state indefinitely? Explain where the entropy goes.

Here's what the student wrote. (a) The swap rule is its own inverse — undoing a swap is just doing it again — so the microdynamics is time-reversal invariant: for every trajectory of the model there is another legal trajectory that runs it backwards. This doesn't stop the coarse-grained entropy rising, because entropy here is a counting notion. There are vastly more arrangements with a large number of boundaries than with two, so the swap dynamics, which is basically a random walk on the space of arrangements, wanders into the big region and stays there. The rise is statistical, not dynamical, and it doesn't require any asymmetry in the rule itself. (b) What's missing is a direction, and the place to put it is the initial condition: the sorted state is a tiny fraction of the state space, and almost any other starting point would already be mixed. So the extra ingredient is the low-entropy starting point, which is a boundary condition rather than a law of motion. I'd argue it isn't really an extra assumption though. We know the ring is out of equilibrium at the start, and things that are out of equilibrium must have got there from somewhere, so the low-entropy end has to sit at the beginning of the trajectory. Once that's in place the second law in the model is exceptionless: after the first few ticks the number of boundaries never decreases. (c) The device can't hold the ring sorted indefinitely. To decide whether to veto a swap it has to find out which particles are where, and measurement isn't free — you pay at least k_B T ln 2 for every bit you record, so the entropy the device saves by keeping the ring tidy is exactly cancelled by the entropy cost of the information it takes in. The device therefore can't reduce the total entropy, which is the modern resolution of Maxwell's demon.
