I'm a novelist, five books in, and the new one moves between Marseille, Lagos and a small town in County Kerry, so I'm writing a lot of characters from cultures that aren't mine. I keep hitting the same wall: a beta reader tells me a character "reads like a national stereotype", I overcorrect, and then he reads like an American with a funny name. What I want from you is the philosophical debate about national character -- whether it's even a coherent notion, and whether there's any legitimate way to generalise about a people -- turned into a procedure I can actually run while drafting. A short ordered list of questions to put to a trait before I let it into a character, what counts as a good enough answer at each step, where the procedure stops with a flat no, and where it says this is a values call and I have to decide it myself. It needs to hold up in the case where the trait is statistically true of the group but I'm using it to explain one individual, and in the case where the generalisation is about my own people and I'd never let an outsider write it. Then run it on two: a Kerry farmer who's taciturn and evasive about his feelings, and a Lagos trader who's relentlessly on the make. One of those should come out of it looking worse than I'd like -- dont spare me. And tell me honestly what the procedure can't do, where a novelist's judgment just has to take over. I'd rather you didn't tell me to avoid the problem altogether; the book doesn't work if everyone's from nowhere. Write it in British English.
