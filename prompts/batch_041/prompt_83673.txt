I've been defending a view I'll call the standing view about dramatizing the real dead. It says the dead can't be harmed — no welfare left to set back, no one home to be deceived — but they can still be wronged. What's wrong with a biopic or a historical novel that bends a real person into the shape the story needs isn't that it misleads the audience or wounds the family; it's that it conscripts an actual life as raw material for someone elses purposes. The wrong is structural, like the wrong of lying: it's about the relation between what you do and the person's standing, not the damage done. So a scrupulously researched prestiege drama can wrong its subject, and a wild fabulation might wrong nobody if the real person was never in it.

Give me the defeaters. Cases the view can't describe, consequences it's stuck with, arguments or results that would sink it — ranked from fatal to merely awkward. I want to know which ones defenders of the standing view would themselves concede, which they'd wave off, and whether the view actually predicts anything its rivals don't, or whether it's just a fancy redescription of the gut feeling that you shouldn't make stuff up about real people. And if it turns out nothing could count against it, say so and say whether that's a bug. 700 words max.
