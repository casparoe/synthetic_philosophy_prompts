I have been reading Plato's Republic slowly, and I have got stuck at the allegory of the cave in Book VII. I am working from the old Jowett translation, and the following is the stretch I keep returning to:

"Behold! human beings living in a underground den, which has a mouth open towards the light and reaching all along the den; here they have been from their childhood, and have their legs and necks chained so that they cannot move, and can only see before them, being prevented by the chains from turning round their heads. Above and behind them a fire is blazing at a distance, and between the fire and the prisoners there is a raised way; and you will see, if you look, a low wall built along the way, like the screen which marionette players have in front of them, over which they show the puppets. [...] And do you see, I said, men passing along the wall carrying all sorts of vessels, and statues and figures of animals made of wood and stone and various materials, which appear over the wall? Some of them are talking, others silent. [...] Like ourselves, I replied; and they see only their own shadows, or the shadows of one another, which the fire throws on the opposite wall of the cave? [...] And if they were able to converse with one another, would they not suppose that they were naming what was actually before them? [...] And suppose further that the prison had an echo which came from the other side, would they not be sure to fancy when one of the passers-by spoke that the voice which they heard came from the passing shadow? [...] To them, I said, the truth would be literally nothing but the shadows of the images."

And then, once the prisoner is released and dragged upward:

"At first, when any of them is liberated and compelled suddenly to stand up and turn his neck round and walk and look towards the light, he will suffer sharp pains; the glare will distress him, and he will be unable to see the realities of which in his former state he had seen the shadows [...] And you may further imagine that his instructor is pointing to the objects as they pass and requiring him to name them, --will he not be perplexed? Will he not fancy that the shadows which he formerly saw are truer than the objects which are now shown to him? [...] He will require to grow accustomed to the sight of the upper world. And first he will see the shadows best, next the reflections of men and other objects in the water, and then the objects themselves; then he will gaze upon the light of the moon and the stars and the spangled heaven; and he will see the sky and the stars by night better than the sun or the light of the sun by day? [...] Last of he will be able to see the sun, and not mere reflections of him in the water, but he will see him in his own proper place, and not in another; and he will contemplate him as he is."

I take the broad picture to be this: the prisoners are like us, the ascent is education, and the sun is the Form of the Good. What I cannot work out is how the details are meant to fit together, and how much of the fit Plato intended rather than later readers have imposed.

The first difficulty is the stages. In Book VI I was taught a scheme of four levels arranged on a divided line: images (shadows and reflections); the ordinary perceptible things those images are images of; the objects the mathematicians reason about; and the Forms themselves, the last two being reached by thought rather than by sense perception. The cave appears to have more stages than that: the shadows on the wall; the fire and the puppets that cast them; then, outside, shadows and reflections; then the objects themselves; then the heavens at night; then the sun. If the cave is meant to display the same scheme, which stage answers to which? The fire looks like a stand-in for the sun, yet there is a genuine sun later on. If the inside of the cave is the perceptible world, the puppets ought to be the ordinary perceptible things and the wall-shadows the images of them -- but then what are the shadows and reflections that the freed prisoner sees outside? Should the lowest level of the divided line be matched with the shadows on the wall, or with the puppets, or with both at once? And is it simply a mistake on my part to expect a one-to-one correspondence anywhere?

The second difficulty concerns the remarks about naming. The prisoners would suppose that they were naming what was actually before them, the echo would convince them that the speaker was the passing shadow, and Socrates concludes that for them the truth is nothing but the shadows of the images. Is this a point about language -- that the prisoners' words have the wrong referents, so that they are not merely mistaken about what they see but systematically talking about something other than what they take themselves to be talking about -- or only the weaker point that their perceptual judgements are false? And how does it connect to the claim that the released prisoner would find the shadows truer than the objects and would have to be dragged up painfully, so that the ascent initially makes things worse?

The third difficulty is what the allegory commits Plato to. Could the ascent be read as a claim only about the difference between opinion and knowledge, with the separate, eternal Forms imported from Book VI but not required by the cave itself? Or do the sun, the shadows and the "images" already presuppose the full theory of Forms?

Please separate what the text itself settles from what is genuinely contested among people who know the Republic well, and do not present a disputed reading as the consensus. I do not need a bibliography or citations. What I want to know is which of these questions have determinate answers in the passage, where the text is silent or ambiguous, and where interpreters divide -- and, where they do divide, which reading you would defend and on what grounds.
