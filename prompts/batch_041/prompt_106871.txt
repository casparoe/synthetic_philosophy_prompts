I am a software engineer with no formal training in philosophy, and I have somehow been volunteered to lead a session of my workplace reading group. The text we have chosen is Kurt Vonnegut's short story 'Harrison Bergeron' — the one set in 2081, in which constitutional amendments require that everyone be made equal and the gifted are loaded with handicaps enforced by a Handicapper General, until the fourteen-year-old Harrison tears his off, proclaims himself Emperor, and is shot for it. I should like to open the discussion by reading the story through the lens of the Law of Jante (Jantelagen) and tall poppy syndrome, and against the ideal of the American Dream, but I want the reading to be genuinely critical rather than a plot summary with a few terms stuck to it. Please err on the side of spelling out the distinctions, since I shall have to defend them to colleagues who are quicker than I am.

To that end: first, draw the distinction the discussion will need — the Law of Jante as a social norm that discourages people from regarding themselves as special or from asserting superiority over their community, as against tall poppy syndrome as the reactive cutting-down of those who have actually succeeded or stood out, as against the American Dream as the ideal that individual excellence should be celebrated and rewarded. Then say which of these, if any, Vonnegut's regime actually dramatises. The handicaps are imposed by the state from above, whereas the Law of Jante, as I understand it, is a bottom-up pressure that people internalise; so I want to know whether the story is best read as a literalisation of Jante, as tall-poppy resentment hardened into law, or as something neither concept quite names — and if the last, what it ought to be called. Press the reading where the story resists it: Harrison declares himself Emperor, which sits awkwardly with any straightforward celebration of the exceptional individual, so is the story a defence of the American Dream, a satire of it, or an attack on both the levellers and the would-be emperor? Point me to the scenes and choices that carry the weight of your reading — the handicaps and what they do to thought and feeling, the handicapped ballerina, the dance, the killing — and be explicit about which of them the Jante/tall-poppy/American-Dream frame illuminates and which it distorts. Finally, tell me what the story contributes to this debate that an essay on equality or envy could not: does fiction let it show something about the experience of being levelled, or of being the one who levels, that argument alone cannot? I do not need quotations or citations, as I have read the story; what I want is a reading I can hold my own in defending.
