I've been in one of those phases where I read a bunch of popular philosophy stuff and then argue about it with my friends at 2am, and the one that's really stuck in my craw is this: are dreams actually experiences? Like, when I dream I'm falling off a cliff, is there really a "me" somewhere having the experience of falling, or is the whole thing more like a story my brain tells me the instant I wake up, and the "experience" is just the memory of a story? Two philosophers I read about disagree about this, and I can't stop thinking about it. The first one (I think his name is Malcolm) says dreams can't be experiences at all, because when you're asleep you're not conscious, so there's nobody home to have the experience, and the only thing we actually have access to is the report you give when you wake up — the dream is whatever the waking report says it was. The other guy (Dennett) says something like dreams are more like tapes that get played when you wake up, that the content gets assembled at the moment of waking rather than lived through during sleep. I think they're both onto something but both miss the obvious point, so I wrote up a short essay trying to say what it is. I'm not a philosopher, I just read a lot and think about this on the bus, so I want you to check me on this. Here's the essay:

"My view is that the whole question is confused, because it smuggles in a picture of experience as something that has to be 'had' at a particular time by a subject who is awake enough to have it. What a dream actually is, is a memory that comes with a false timestamp. When I recall a dream, the memory presents itself as a memory of something that happened (in the dream) over a stretch of time, with me in it as a participant — but there's no fact of the matter about whether that stretch of time was ever lived through. The memory is the only evidence there is, and a memory can be of something that never happened. So dreams aren't experiences and they aren't non-experiences either; they're memory-content that pretends to be a record of experience. The reason this matters is that it means lucid dreaming doesn't rescue the experience view the way people assume. The lucid dreamer's sense of being there and knowing it is itself part of the content remembered afterwards. Even if during the dream you feel like you're deliberately choosing to look at your own hands, that feeling of choosing could have been manufactured at recall just like the rest of it. And this connects to something weirder: the more vivid and coherent a dream is, the less we should trust that its vividness was felt at the time, because coherence is exactly what you'd expect from something assembled by a waking brain trying to make sense of a mess of signals. The 'better' the dream, the more suspicious I am of the timestamp."

What I want from you is comments, not a rewrite. Three things specifically. First, accuracy: am I even getting Malcolm and Dennett roughly right, or am I flattening one or both of them into something they didn't say? Second, is the central move — the memory-with-a-false-timestamp thing — actually interesting, or is it just a fancy restatement of something obvious that I'm dressing up? Be honest, I can take it. Third, the last claim (that more coherent dreams are less trustworthy as evidence of lived experience) — is that a real argument or am I overreaching? And if you had to point at the single weakest joint in the whole thing, where would it be and what would someone say to break it?
