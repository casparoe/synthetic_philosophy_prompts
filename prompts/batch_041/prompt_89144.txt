At a reading group on whether machines could ever do mathematics, someone ended the discussion with a line I have since heard in several forms: Gödel's incompleteness theorem shows that no system can prove everything, that there will always be truths escaping it, and therefore that there will always be mathematical truths no computer and no formal system can ever reach. The point was being made to settle a question about artificial mathematicians: that whatever formal system a machine runs on will be incomplete, so there are theorems it can never prove, so machines cannot match us. I could not say what was wrong with the move, and I suspect the trouble is that several quite different claims are being compressed into one sentence.

Please lay out the distinct precise theses this slogan could be expressing, as a menu rather than a single verdict, and for each say whether it is a trivial restatement, a genuine and defensible claim, or false or an equivocation, and, where it is true, what exactly it is true of. I would like the readings to run from the modest to the ambitious, so that I can see how far the sentence can be pushed before it breaks.

In particular, I want to be able to distinguish: what the theorem actually asserts about a given formal system from what it leaves untouched; claims about provability within a particular system from claims about provability or truth in principle or by any means at all; claims about every system whatsoever from claims about only those systems meeting certain conditions; what the second incompleteness theorem adds to the first, and whether it supports a further reading about a system being unable to justify or ground itself; the reading on which the result bears on minds as opposed to machines; and the loose cultural reading on which the whole thing merely says that reason has limits, or that no framework can be complete, as applied to ideologies, legal codes, or worldviews.

For each reading, tell me whether it would actually deliver the conclusion my group-mate wanted, namely that a machine could never prove certain theorems, or whether it supports only something weaker, or nothing at all in that direction. Close by sorting the readings into the platitudes, the live philosophical contentions, and the ones that are simply mistakes, and say which reading my group-mate most likely meant. I do not need citations or a reading list; what I want is the claims separated and adjudicated.
