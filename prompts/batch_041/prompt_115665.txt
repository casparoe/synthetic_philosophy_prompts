The question was out of 20, four parts weighted equally at 5 points each, roughly a page expected:

"(a) Reconstruct Epicurus's argument that death is nothing to us, separating its two strands: the strand that turns on the subject not existing at the time of death, and the strand that turns on harm requiring experience. Are these the same argument or two different ones? (b) Set out the deprivation account, on which death is bad for the person who dies because it deprives them of goods they would otherwise have had. Which of the two Epicurean strands does the deprivation account have to reject, and can it keep the other? (c) The deprivation account is often said to face a timing problem. State the problem and say what a defender of the account should say about it. (d) Consider the symmetry between the time before our birth and the time after our death: if the first is not bad for us, why should the second be? Is the symmetry a serious objection?"

The answer:

"(a) Epicurus's argument is that death is nothing to us: while we exist death is absent, and once death arrives we are gone. This is really one argument said twice, since being harmed requires having experiences and having experiences requires existing, so the strand about the subject and the strand about experience collapse into each other, and the experience requirement is doing all the work. (b) The deprivation account says death is bad for the person who dies because it takes from them the goods they would otherwise have had. So it must reject the experience requirement: you can be harmed by what you never find out about, like a partner's affair or a friend's contempt. It does not have to reject the other strand, since the person who died is still the subject of the harm; the harm is simply comparative, what they had weighed against what they would have had. (c) The timing problem is that the account seems to need a time at which the harm occurs, but before death the deprivation has not happened and after death there is no subject. The defender should say the harm occurs at the moment of death: the last moment at which the subject exists and the first at which the goods are gone. That no one ever performs the comparison is irrelevant; a harm does not need a spectator. (d) The symmetry argument says that since we do not think it bad that we did not exist before we were born, by parity we should not think it bad that we will not exist after we die. This is not a serious objection, because the pre-natal period is not a loss to us: we have no projects, relationships, or attachments reaching back before our birth, so there are no goods that were taken away. The asymmetry is justified."
