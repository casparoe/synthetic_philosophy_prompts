I have been reconstructing, for a seminar on historical responsibility, an argument that I suspect proves too much, and I would like you to find the fault. The steps are my own; the view they press is a familiar one, and the vocabulary of the debate — the distinction, usually credited to Jaspers, between criminal, political, moral, and metaphysical guilt — is in the background.

(1) A nation is not merely the set of persons living within its borders at a given moment; it persists through time in its institutions, its constitutional order, and the way its members understand themselves.
(2) A wrong belongs to the agent that committed it, and remains that agent's wrong until it is atoned for or forgiven.
(3) So a wrong committed by a nation in the past is still that nation's wrong today.
(4) A nation has no moral agency over and above its members; whatever guilt the nation bears is borne by its members.
(5) So members of the nation today bear the guilt of wrongs it committed before they were born.
(6) One neither chooses one's nation nor can renounce it at will.
(7) So those members bear guilt for acts they did not commit, could not have prevented, and cannot shed.
(8) Guilt that is unchosen, unavoidable, and undischargeable by anything one does is not guilt but a misfortune of birth.
(9) So either a nation's past wrongs are not its wrongs today, or the guilt spoken of at (5) is not the guilt we mean when we say a person is guilty of what he did.

I can live with (9) as a disjunction. What I cannot accept is the reading on which it licenses a clean acquittal — on which the descendants of perpetrators, and the institutions they inherit, owe nothing, because guilt cannot cross a generation. That reading seems to me to prove too much: it would equally acquit the clerk who only filed the deportation papers, the shareholder who only held the stock, and the citizen who only cast a vote. Tell me which step to give up. If the trouble is a flaw in the inference, name it and explain why the step looked sound. If the argument is valid, tell me which premise is least costly to abandon and what abandoning it costs; I would rather keep (2) and (6) than (8), so if the damage falls there, say so plainly. If the fault lies in running distinct senses of guilt together, say which sense belongs at which step; if it lies elsewhere, say where. Finally, state what would change your verdict — which consideration, if it proved correct, would lead you to a different diagnosis — and where your own reasoning is most likely to fail.
