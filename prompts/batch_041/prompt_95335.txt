I'm a PhD student in social ontology, and I want a view I've been defending stress-tested rather than restated back to me. The view: to be a social kind is just to have its membership fixed by collective acceptance, in the constitutive rather than merely causal sense, and a single account of that kind covers money, race, and gender without remainder. I also lean on a distinction of my own between load-bearing representations, which do the constituting, and decorative ones, which merely track or accompany a kind once it exists; only the former are supposed to matter. Tell me what would count against this — which cases, rival accounts, and empirical findings, ranked from fatal to merely awkward — and which of those a defender should concede as genuine costs rather than explain away. I'm especially interested in whether the load-bearing/decorative distinction does any work a plainer causal story couldn't do, and whether my view predicts anything that a view on which social kinds are just entrenched patterns of behavior plus material constraint wouldn't also predict; for instance, whether I'm really committed to the claim that a kind can go out of existence while every pattern associated with it persists, and whether that commitment is a virtue or an embarrassment. If this is a familiar position I've dressed up as my own, or if I've run together a question about what social kinds are with a question about which ones are worth caring about, say so directly instead of working around it. Give your bottom line in the first sentence, then the reasoning; skip the preamble and don't restate the question.
