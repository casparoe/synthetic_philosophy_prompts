The argument about the British Raj is usually conducted as a ledger. On the credit side go railways, telegraphs, the rule of law, famine relief, vaccination, a common language, and the suppression of practices such as widow-burning; on the debit side, the drain of revenue to Britain, the collapse of Indian textile manufacture, punitive massacres, and the mortality of the great famines. Niall Ferguson's Empire: How Britain Made the Modern World (2003) runs the balance one way, Shashi Tharoor's Inglorious Empire (2017) the other. My puzzle is not which side is right but what the genre itself takes for granted, since both are doing the same thing: compiling one balance sheet, in one common unit, over one span of time, for a population treated as a single interested party.

What theory of value has to be in place for a mile of track and a death by starvation to be entered on the same sheet at all — that the goods and harms are commensurable, that they can be summed across persons, that a benefit to one person may be set against a harm to another, and that the question worth asking concerns the total rather than its distribution? Does the practice strictly presuppose those commitments, or would a resourcist, a welfarist, and a capabilities theorist each have reason to compile the same ledger for quite different reasons? Which views does it quietly exclude — that some harms admit of no compensation, that a wrong is not cancelled by later benefits, that the proper unit of assessment is the individual rather than the colony or the century?

Reconstruct the implicit theory of the ledger, separate what it genuinely presupposes from what it merely fits, and say where it is underdetermined. Note also that the same writers who will trade a famine against a railway tend to treat a single massacre as disqualifying rather than deductible — an inconsistency in the arithmetic, or a sign that the ledger is doing rhetorical work it cannot do as valuation? And tell me what a history written on a different theory of value would have to stop doing, and whether anything of the genre survives the change.
