I am a high school student preparing a philosophical self-portrait for a summer seminar, and I would like an audit of my views on discounting the future. I hold the following commitments. (1) It is not irrational for an individual to care somewhat less about a benefit merely because it lies further in the future, even when the benefit and the person are held fixed. (2) In the ethical evaluation of outcomes, however, I think we should not discount a future person's welfare just because it occurs later: if a policy could give an identical benefit to a fixed, guaranteed future person two hundred years from now, with no uncertainty and no difference in wealth, that benefit should count the same as one given now. (3) I accept a person-affecting restriction: an outcome is better only if it is better for some particular person who exists or will exist, and merely possible people who never exist do not count as a loss. (4) I think we have strong obligations to prevent catastrophes that would prevent future generations from existing, even though those future people are not yet determinate and their existence depends on our present choices. (5) In cost-benefit analysis, I think it is appropriate to discount future consumption at a positive rate because future people will likely be richer, but I do not think this justifies discounting future welfare itself. (6) I think existential risks should be evaluated at a near-zero discount rate, even if ordinary future benefits are discounted. Which of these commitments are genuinely in tension, which only appear to be, and what is the least costly revision in each genuine case? Please rank the tensions by severity and tell me which single revision would resolve the most.
