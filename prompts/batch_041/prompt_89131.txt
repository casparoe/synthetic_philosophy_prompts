My buddy and I got into a two-hour arguement last night about whether the world is getting less violent — you know, the whole "everything keeps getting better" thesis — and he ended it by laying out this argument that I honestly can't crack. I need you to tell me which step to give up and what giving it up costs.

He kept using this word he made up on the spot, "count-constituted," for events whose status depends on how we tally them. Here's the argument, cleaned up:

(1) "Is the world becoming less violent?" has a fact of the matter — yes or no, true or false, independent of what anyone thinks.
(2) For there to be a fact of the matter, "violence" has to pick out a determinate class of events — one whose membership doesn't depend on who's doing the counting.
(3) But every candidate class turns out count-constituted: a duel in 1650 gets counted as violence, a judicial execution gets counted as justice, a famine caused by grain requisitioning gets filed under "economic policy," a bombing that kills 100k civilians gets filed under "total war," and whether you file each of these one way or the other has shifted across centuries and between historians.
(4) So there's no determinate class of events that "violence" picks out.
(5) Switching from absolute numbers to a per-capita rate doesn't rescue it, because you still have to fill in the numerator, and the numerator is the same indeterminate class from (4).
(6) If "the amount of violence at time t" has no determinate value, then the comparison "less violent than" has no determinate truth value either.
(7) Therefore (1) is false. There's no fact of the matter here, and everyone arguing about it is just doing wordplay.

His kicker was that this generalizes — he says it quietly kills every "is the world getting X-er" debate, not just this one. I can't accept (7); surely one of "yes" and "no" is right even if it's brutal to establish which. But I can't find the slip. Which step do I reject, and what do I have to pay to reject it?
