I've pretty much come around to integrated information theory as the best account we have of consciousness — the idea that a system's experience just is its maximally irreducible cause-effect structure, and Φ measures how much of it there is. But I want to stress-test that commitment instead of collecting reassurances. So: what would actually count against IIT, ranked by how decisive it'd be? I'm more interested in the structural objections than in the usual panpsychism complaint. Two in particular: the argument that any recurrent causal structure can be unfolded into a feedforward one with the same input-output profile, so IIT ends up making consciousness behaviorally undetectable, which sits awkwardly with its own clinical ambitions; and the charge that boring systems — grids of logic gates, say — come out with absurdly high Φ, or that Φ is computationally intractable enough that nobody is really computing the thing the theory says matters. When defenders reply that only the maximal substrate counts, or that we shouldn't trust intuitions about which systems are conscious, is that a real answer or a rescue that just relocates the problem? Which of these would a committed IIT person themselves concede as counting against them, and which would they wave off as question-begging? And is there any way to state what IIT predicts that a rival like global workspace or predictive processing doesn't, such that an actual experiment could come out against it — or has it been qualified into unfalsifiability, and if so is that fatal or just the price of doing theory of consciousness?
