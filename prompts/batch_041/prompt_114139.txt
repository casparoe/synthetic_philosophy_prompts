A friend and I spent most of last night arguing about suffering, and the argument ended somewhere I did not expect. It began on familiar ground — whether pleasure and pain can be placed on a single scale at all, whether there is any fact of the matter behind the claim that an hour of one is worth so many hours of the other — and it ended with her defending a position she insisted on calling lexical-threshold negative utilitarianism. Her version was not the blunt one. She did not say that a moment of mild discomfort outweighs any amount of happiness, and she did not say that the two are commensurable at some fixed rate. She said the rate itself steepens: at low intensities pleasure can compensate suffering at a finite exchange rate, and as suffering deepens the rate required rises without bound, so that the lexical claim emerges as the limit of a series of ever more unfavourable trades rather than as a separate axiom dropped in from outside. I could not refute it and I could not state it cleanly, and I have come to think the failure to state it cleanly is the interesting part. If you happen to recognise the label from somewhere, please do not substitute whatever version of it you have seen; the view I want examined is the one described above, and if the name is already taken, rename it.

Write me a short research proposal on it — the kind of prospectus a philosophy department would accept for a term paper or a first-year graduate project, six to eight thousand words, one semester. No citations and no reading list; I will add those myself, so refer to positions and arguments rather than to works.

I want the question stated so that it has a determinate answer. Something like: is the steepening-rate view a distinct and stable position, or does every formulation of it either presuppose the common measure it denies or collapse into the discontinuous lexical priority she was trying to avoid? Then say why the familiar alternatives leave this open — the classical hedonism that treats all pleasures and pains as commensurable, the lexical view on which any suffering outweighs any pleasure, and the ordinary threshold views that simply fix a line at some intensity. Then the approach: what I would actually do. I imagine trying to give the steepening rate a precise form and then testing it, against the case of the mildly happy life, against aggregation across persons, against the question whether the steepening is a claim about value or only about how a rational agent should choose, and against the suspicion that an unbounded rate is just lexicality in disguise. Say what a result would look like and which considerations would decide between the possibilities; say plainly what would count as the project failing; and tell me whether this is too big for a semester and where you would cut it.

She has promised to read whatever I write, and I would like it to be good enough to make her either abandon the view or state it properly.
