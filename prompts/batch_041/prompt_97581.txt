You are a philosopher with a deep background in metaphysics and a talent for spotting the general problem hidden inside a mundane quarrel. Before you answer, take a moment to think the situation through and to sketch for yourself what you take the real disagreement to be; then give me your considered reply.

Here is what I am dealing with. My partner and I run a small independent bookshop, and we are redoing the shelves. He wants to arrange everything by genre—mystery, romance, literary fiction, and the rest—and he speaks as though these categories were simply there in the world, waiting to be discovered, with every book belonging to one of them. I keep objecting that the genres are just labels we made up, that there is no fact of the matter about whether a particular novel is really literary fiction, and that plenty of books sit happily in two or three at once. He replies that if the labels were arbitrary, then any arrangement of the shop would be as good as any other, which is obviously false: some groupings let a customer find what she wants and others leave her lost. He has even offered a test—he says a category earns its keep only if it lets you predict something, so that knowing a book is noir lets you guess the customer will like another one, and that this predictive power is precisely what it means for the category to be real. I answered that this only shows the category is useful to us, not that it is really there; and when I pressed him on what makes two books both mysteries, he said they resemble one another, so I asked what makes two resemblances resemble one another, and we went round in circles until the shop opened. I have the nagging feeling that we are not really arguing about shelving, and that whatever we are arguing about is older and stranger than either of us realizes.

I want two things from you. First, name the philosophical problem we have stumbled into and explain its structure: what is genuinely at stake, which positions philosophers have taken on it, why the two obvious positions each seem to capture something, and why the dispute has proved so stubborn. Second, tell me which of us is closer to the truth, or whether the honest verdict is that we are each half-right and the question needs splitting; in either case, give the strongest version of the view you end up rejecting, so that I can see what a thoughtful defender of it would say. Keep the abstract discussion anchored to the bookshop, and do not give me a neutral survey that lists the positions without committing to one.
