I'm a fifth-year PhD student in metaphysics, and I'm assembling the opening handout for a workshop session on origin essentialism — the cluster of questions around whether a person could have come from a different sperm and egg than the ones they actually came from. The audience already knows the Kripkean line and its critics, so I don't need the argument restated; what I need is a poem to open with, because I've come to think the argument's pressure point is nearly impossible to state discursively. The Kripkean says that what we take to be an imagining of ourselves with a different origin is really an imagining of a merely qualitatively similar person, and the objection is that we have no independent grip on that distinction — we can't inspect the imagining to see which one we're doing. I want the poem to make that seam felt rather than described. For what it's worth, I lean toward thinking the necessity claim is probably true and the conceivability argument for it does almost no work, and I'd like the poem to sit exactly there rather than take a side.

Please write it as two numbered versions of the same short poem — call them I and II — no more than sixteen lines each, in unrhymed verse, loosely iambic. Version II should be a near-copy of version I: exactly four words change between them, all four in lines that specify where the speaker came from (a place, a time, a pair of names or cells, a hand that did something), and everything else stays identical, down to the line breaks. The constraint is the point: by the end, a reader should be unable to settle whether I and II are two drafts of one poem or two poems that merely resemble each other, and the poem must not adjudicate this for them — no closing line that decides. I'd like the four substitutions to escalate in effect, so that after the first the change reads as an edit, after the second as a correction, after the third as a different speaker, and after the fourth as a different life, but the words that change should be small and ordinary rather than dramatic.

A few further constraints. Don't use the words 'essence', 'essential', 'possible world', 'counterpart', 'rigid', 'identity', or 'modal' anywhere in either version; I want the structure to carry the idea, not the vocabulary. Somewhere in the material the two versions share, I'd like a second person who arrived from the same beginning as the speaker, since the monozygotic twin case is what I'll use to press the objection that a shared origin fails to individuate — that second person should be present in both versions and unchanged, so the difference between I and II is never a difference in how many people there are. I'd also like the poem to be readable simultaneously as being about a person and about a poem, with the second reading falling out of the same sentences rather than laid on as allegory. After the two versions, add a short prose note of roughly 150 words saying what the form is meant to do and where you think it fails; I would much rather have the failure marked than papered over.
