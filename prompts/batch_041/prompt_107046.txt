In much of the United States a woman may be paid for her eggs and paid to carry a pregnancy to term, and the resulting agreement is treated as an enforceable contract; in many European states the same transactions become unlawful once money changes hands, and only unpaid donation with reimbursement of expenses is permitted. What does that division presuppose about persons, bodies, and the proper bounds of the market? Reconstruct the implicit theory on each side. Does the ban rest on the claim that reproductive labor and gametes are not the kind of thing that can rightly be sold, so that the sale itself wrongs the seller or degrades a good; or on a claim about exploitation and coercion against a background of inequality, so that the sale is not wrong in itself but is wrong here; or on straightforward paternalism toward the woman? Distinguish which of these the practice strictly requires from which it merely fits, and say where the law is philosophically underdetermined. Then probe the internal consistency of the banning regimes: they refuse to let reproductive capacity be bought, yet they allow the parentage of the resulting child to be reassigned by prior agreement, and they police a line between expenses and payment that in practice may be hard to hold. Which commitment does that tension expose, and what would have to change in the law if we replaced the commodification view with the exploitation view, or with the liberal contract view the American market seems to embody? If the question rests on a false premise — if no single commitment is shared across these regimes, or the asymmetry is less principled than it appears — say so plainly rather than assembling a unified theory to satisfy me.
