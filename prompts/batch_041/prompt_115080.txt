I'm in the middle of a graduate seminar on formal epistemology, and we've spent the last three weeks on probability kinematics — Jeffrey's proposal for how to update when the evidence itself is uncertain, rather than the strict Bayesian picture in which learning is always conditionalization on an event you come to know with certainty. I have to write a case study for the seminar, and I want to write it on the OPERA superluminal-neutrino affair of 2011–2012 rather than on a textbook example like the cloth seen by candlelight, because I suspect a real episode will expose things the textbook examples are built to hide. I don't need citations, a reading list, or references to the literature; I know the texts, and I'd rather you spend the words on the analysis.

Here is the case as I understand it, and you should treat this sketch as the shared factual record rather than spending effort correcting me on details. In September 2011 the OPERA collaboration at Gran Sasso announced that muon neutrinos sent from CERN appeared to arrive about 60 nanoseconds earlier than light would have over the same distance — roughly one part in 40,000 — with a headline significance near six sigma once the error budget was treated as statistical; the collaboration reported that it had been unable to find an instrumental effect that accounted for the result, and invited the wider community to scrutinize and repeat the measurement. In November 2011 they published a refined analysis with a slightly smaller discrepancy and a significance of 6.2 sigma, along with a repeat run using a different beam structure that agreed with the main result. In the meantime the result was attacked on theory grounds — superluminal neutrinos should radiate energy away, so the observed neutrino spectrum was itself a problem — and other experiments at the same underground laboratory reported consistency with the speed of light. In February 2012 the collaboration announced two possible instrumental culprits: a fiber-optic link to the master clock that had not been fully seated, which shortened the inferred flight time by about 73 nanoseconds, and an oscillator on an electronic board running outside its specification. By mid-2012 the effect was gone and the measurement agreed with the speed of light.

What I want is a case analysis that runs in both directions. First, the theory applied to the case: who exactly is the updating agent here (the collaboration as a body, each individual physicist, the wider community?) and what partition that agent is supposed to be conditioning on; what the new probabilities over that partition plausibly were, and where they came from; whether the rigidity condition — the requirement that the conditional probabilities of the hypotheses given each cell of the partition be held fixed through the update — was even approximately satisfied, given that the uncertainty about the evidence was itself diagnostic of the hypothesis (the fact that the reading was anomalous tells you something about the apparatus, not only about neutrinos); how the problem of the partition bites, that is, that the same learning experience admits many partitions which give different posteriors, and how the order-dependence of successive Jeffrey updates shows up in the sequence of events above; and what the difference is, formally and practically, between an agent who Jeffrey-updates toward superluminality and one who declines to. Second, the case applied to the theory, where I have two opposing suspicions I want you to adjudicate. On one reading this episode is bad news for Jeffrey conditionalization in a specific way: the standard defense is that you can always conditionalize instead on the certain part of your evidence, and here the certain part — the raw timestamps, the cable, the clock board — turned out to be exactly what mattered, which suggests the uncertain-evidence formulation was a distraction rather than a necessity, and that the physicists' actual reasoning is better reconstructed as ordinary conditionalization on physical facts. On the other reading it is an argument for the framework, since the error model was itself something the physicists were uncertain about, and no amount of credence-shifting over a fixed partition could have located a loose connector — so the case shows the limits of any purely credal account of uncertain evidence. Tell me which reading the episode supports, and be explicit about where the formal apparatus goes silent: whether anything in it tells an agent to go looking for a systematic error rather than to update, and whether that silence is a defect or simply a division of labor between formal epistemology and experimental practice.

Give me a verdict stated with whatever confidence the argument earns, and say plainly which single consideration, if it had gone the other way, would have led you to the opposite verdict, and where in your own reasoning you think you are most likely to be wrong.
