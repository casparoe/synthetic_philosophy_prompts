I am a sixth-form student preparing a short piece for my school's philosophy society, and I should be grateful if you would write a poem for me.

The subject is the problem often called consequentialist cluelessness. Since I cannot assume you hold any particular reading of it, let me set out the version I have in mind, so that the poem can be judged against something definite rather than against a label. The worry is not the ordinary one that we are frequently unsure what our actions will bring about; that kind of uncertainty can in principle be reduced by looking, asking, or waiting. The sharper worry is that the effects of even a trivial act ramify without limit, and that the far future may be either very long or very short and may contain outcomes of enormous magnitude, so that the expected value of an ordinary choice is fixed by branches of possibility to which we cannot assign credible probabilities at all. On this view we are not merely imprecise about the consequences of what we do; we cannot even place an error bar on our imprecision, because we do not know the shape of the distribution we would be sampling from. Two responses are common and both seem to me unsatisfactory. One is to keep the calculation and pretend the tail can be tamed; the other is to set the calculation aside and act on common-sense morality, which looks less like a solution than a decision not to look. I would like the poem to hold both of these in view without settling on either.

Here is what I should like the poem to do. It should be about one small, plainly named, ordinary act — answering or not answering a message, giving up a seat, putting a coin in a hat — and it should stay concrete: no abstractions doing the work that an image could do. It should never use the words consequentialism, utilitarian, expected value, or cluelessness; the thought must be carried by what the poem shows. The horizon should widen as the poem proceeds, so that the early lines stay within a minute or an hour and the middle lines reach a decade and then a span no one could live to see, before the final stanza returns to the small act and the present moment. Formally, I should like four stanzas of four lines and a closing couplet, with the last line of the first three stanzas an identical refrain, and the final line of the poem that same refrain with a single word altered or withheld, so that it no longer closes the tally it had been promising to close. The refrain should feel like an attempt to sum something up; by the end it should feel like the sum refusing. Someone who has thought about the problem should be able to infer from the form alone that the poem is about the impossibility of closing the account, and should not need the commentary to tell them so.

Please write in British English throughout, with British spelling and idiom.

I should like the response in two parts. First the poem, with a title, of roughly eighteen to twenty-two lines. Then a single section headed Commentary on the choices made, of about 180 words, in which you explain why the refrain is worded as it is, why it breaks in the final line, and how the widening and then narrowing of the horizon does the philosophical work.
