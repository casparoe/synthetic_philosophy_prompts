I belong to a small fiction-and-philosophy reading group, and our next session is organised around a theme I keep failing to write about well: the gap between not knowing a number and refusing to pretend that a single number is the right thing to want. I would like you to write me a short story — roughly two thousand words — that dramatises that gap rather than explaining it. The setting I have in mind, though you may bend it: a mid-sized port city that has flooded twice in a decade, and a statutory body, let us call it the Coastal Risk Board, whose rules require that every evacuation be triggered by a single stated probability of inundation within the coming season. Its analysts may not file a range, a set of admissible distributions, or a bundle of expert opinions; the statute says "a number", and the chair has to sign it. My protagonist is one of those analysts — competent, unhurried, not remotely timid — who finds over the course of the story that her honest representation of what the evidence supports is not a point but a family of distributions, and that the family behaves in a way she cannot make anyone else believe.

What I most want the story to get right is a specific and slightly uncanny feature of such a representation. Sometimes, when she adds a genuinely relevant piece of information to her model — a new tide gauge, a consultant's report, a neighbour's account of where the water reached in the previous flood — the interval of values she can defend does not narrow but widens, and widens in a way that is not an arithmetic slip. I would like that widening to be the story's central turn: she notices it, tries to explain it, and finds that the explanation is, to everyone around her, indistinguishable from evasion, from an excuse for inaction, or from the ordinary self-protective hedging of a bureaucrat covering herself. You may recognise this as a known phenomenon in the theory of set-valued or imprecise probability; if you do, use it accurately, and if you do not, you may have her work it out from scratch, provided the mathematics you show is honest. She should have a foil: a colleague who insists that a family of distributions is simply a distribution over distributions, that everything can be collapsed if one is willing to commit to a prior, and that her reluctance is a failure of nerve dressed up as rigour. The Board's chair, who is neither a villain nor a fool, must decide what to do with an analyst who reports that the honest answer is wider now than it was last month. There is a journalist writing about the Board, and there is one more storm coming.

Let the analyst's notebook carry a little formalism if it helps, but no invented theorems and no named literature — if a technical claim would need a citation, have her be unsure of it instead. I do not want a lecture smuggled into the dialogue, and I do not want the ending to resolve into "and so the Board learned to love ambiguity". What I want is the collision: an institution with a legitimate need for a decision, a person with a legitimate reason to refuse the form the decision takes, and no clean way for anyone to tell which of them is being unreasonable.
