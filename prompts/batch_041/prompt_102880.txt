Write an essay on the following controversy: whether a long-run empirical theory can be "right but early," and whether the repairs that keep such a theory alive after its near-term predictions fail are legitimate scientific maintenance or mere rescue. Malthus's account of population and subsistence is the paradigm case, but the essay should treat it as an instance of a general problem rather than as a topic to be summarized.

To keep the discussion concrete, and to prevent it from collapsing into a recitation of familiar philosophy-of-science labels, organize the essay around the three repair strategies below, which you should treat as stipulatively defined:

1. Horizon extension — the mechanism is retained unchanged, and the date on which it is supposed to bite is simply pushed further into the future.
2. Countervailing-factor repair — some force (a preventive check, technological change, institutional reform) is introduced or newly emphasized to absorb the discrepancy between prediction and observation.
3. Domain restriction — the theory's scope is narrowed so that it now applies only to a specified class of cases, and the anomalous cases are declared outside its jurisdiction.

State a criterion for distinguishing a legitimate repair from an ad hoc one, and defend it against the most obvious objections. Candidate criteria you may adopt, modify, or reject: a repair is legitimate only if it was independently motivated before the anomaly appeared; only if it yields new, risky predictions rather than merely re-describing the old failure; only if it does not lower the theory's exposure to evidence overall; only if it specifies in advance what would count as final failure. You may substitute your own criterion, but it must be stated precisely enough that a reader could apply it to a case the essay never mentions. Then apply your criterion to each of the three strategies and say which survive it.

The essay must also confront the scorekeeping problem: when a theory's short-run predictions fail repeatedly but its long-run prediction is eventually borne out, how should the timing affect our retrospective judgment of whether the theory was ever warranted? Consider at least one argument that timing is evidentially irrelevant (a prediction borne out is a prediction borne out, whenever it lands) and at least one that timing is central (a theory that can only be vindicated by outliving its critics has bought its survival at the cost of any real risk). Weigh these against each other.

Finally, reach a verdict. Defend a definite answer to whether a theory can be right but early in a sense that earns it genuine epistemic credit, or whether "right but early" is at bottom a way of laundering a failed prediction. A verdict of the form "right but early is coherent only under conditions X and Y, and Malthus's case does (or does not) meet them" is acceptable and encouraged; an unresolved survey of both sides is not.

The essay should run roughly 1200–1500 words in a formal academic register. Do not include a bibliography, reading list, or citations, and do not attribute specific claims, dates, or figures to named authors — reason from the concepts as defined here, invoking standard positions (for instance falsificationism or research-programme methodology) only by their content, not by name-dropping.
