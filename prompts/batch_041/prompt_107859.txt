The question (10 marks):

We've been calling a view lexical threshold negative utilitarianism (LTNU). On that view, an outcome contains some amount of pleasure and some amount of suffering. There is a threshold T on the suffering scale. Below T, pleasure and suffering are commensurable: they trade off at a determinate rate, so for any two outcomes whose suffering is below T there is a fact of the matter about which is better. Once suffering reaches T, no amount of pleasure, however large, can outweigh even the smallest further increase in suffering above T. (Use the distinction we've kept to all term: two outcomes are comparable if there is a determinate answer to which is better; pleasure and suffering are commensurable if there is a common scale with an exchange rate. These are not the same thing.) (a) Is LTNU a form of negative utilitarianism? (b) What does LTNU imply about whether pleasure and suffering are commensurable, and about whether they are comparable? (c) State the strongest objection to the view, and say whether the threshold helps it.

The student's anwser, transcribed:

LTNU is a form of negative utilitarianism, because it gives priority to reducing suffering over producing pleasure. The difference from the classic version is that the priority only kicks in above the threshold, since below T pleasure and suffering are traded off against each other like ordinary goods. The word lexical is taken from Rawls, who used lexical priority for the relation between his first principle of justice and his second: the first has to be satisfied before the second gets any weight at all, and in the same way suffering has to be below T before pleasure gets any weight.

On (b): above the threshold LTNU says that pleasure and suffering are incommensurable. Once suffering is above T, pleasure doesn't enter into the comparison — there's no common measure on which the two could be weighed, and any amount of pleasure is simply irrelevant. So the view is a hybrid: below T it's a normal Benthamite position where everything sits on one scale, and above T it denies that pleasure and suffering can be compared at all, which is why it isn't the Benthamite calculus applied uniformly.

On (c): the standard objection is the world-exploder objection. Because negative utilitarianism tells us to minimise suffering whatever the cost in pleasure, it looks like it tells us to wipe out all sentient life, since a universe with no sentient life contains no suffering. I think the threshold is what saves LTNU here. The view only gives suffering lexical priority once it's above T, and suffering below T can be outweighed by enough pleasure, so LTNU doesn't have to say that any suffering at all has to be eliminated at any cost. So the objection, which is fatal to the classic version, doesn't get off the ground against LTNU.
