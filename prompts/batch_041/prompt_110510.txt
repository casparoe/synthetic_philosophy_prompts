I chair the grant-review committee of the Marrow Foundation, a small philanthropy that funds work on reducing long-run catastrophic risk, and our next decision cycle closes at the end of the month. One proposal has split the committee badly enough that I need a written opinion I can circulate before we vote, so I'm asking you to draft that memo in my voice. The proposal — the applicants call it the Numerical Dominance application — asks for roughly a fifth of our annual budget to support a decade of work whose central claim is that standard estimates of the future's size are far too small. Its core move is a self-locating one: the applicants ask us to think of ourselves as randomly selected observers, and then argue that on any weighting that treats possibilities as more likely in proportion to how many observers they contain, we are overwhelmingly likely to be living in one of the histories with the largest total number of observers, simply because those histories contribute the most observers to be sampled from. If that is right, the expected number of future people is astronomically larger than our demographic and cosmological estimates suggest, the disvalue of extinction is correspondingly larger, and — the applicants conclude — almost any expenditure that shaves even a tiny fraction off the risk is justified, however speculative the underlying reasoning or unpopular the resulting advocacy. (I should say that I came into this meeting with no strong view; I have a background in applied economics and a lay interest in philosophy, and I've been going back and forth with two colleagues over coffee all week about whether any of this is even the kind of thing a grant committee can adjudicate.)

Two committee members object on opposite grounds, and my memo has to deal with both. The first says the self-locating step is not merely controversial but confused: that a 'randomly selected observer' is a category error unless you have first fixed a reference class of observers, and that fixing it in the way the applicants need — so that it ranges over observers in merely possible histories and not just our own — is exactly the question at issue, not a neutral premise. She adds that the same style of reasoning, run instead on the assumption that you are a random one of the observers in your actual world, delivers the doomsday conclusion that we are probably near the end of human history, which would counsel spending our money on near-term welfare rather than on the applicants' cosmological wager. The second member says the metaphysics is beside the point: even if the reasoning is sound, funding an advocacy program built on it would be reputationally reckless, because the foundation would be seen to have bought a cosmological conclusion in order to justify a large expenditure, and a small funder cannot afford that appearance.

Draft the committee's decision memo — approve, reject, or approve with conditions and a hard cap — with a recommendation the trustees could act on and reasons the applicants could genuinely respond to. I want the memo to engage the self-locating issue on its merits rather than filing it under 'speculative,' to say plainly which of the two objections I should weight more heavily and why, and to be explicit about what would have to be true for the numerical argument to actually reorder our funding priorities rather than merely decorate them.
