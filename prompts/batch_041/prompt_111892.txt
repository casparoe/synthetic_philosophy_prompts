Right — I need a fresh teaching example for the decision theory lecture and I'm sick of wheeling out Allais, so this one's for you to build. Design a single decision problem made up of two pairs of gambles over ordinary money (tens of pounds, not lottery-jackpot sums) in which the majority choice pattern violates the independence axiom, and violates it backwards: the risky gamble gets picked in the pair with the better odds and the safer gamble in the pair with the worse odds. Constraints, and they matter. No option in any of the four gambles may be a sure thing, so nobody can wave the pattern away as the certainty effect. The ratios between the corresponding probabilities in the two pairs should be identical and easy to read off at a glance, so the pattern can't be blamed on people misreading the numbers. And the pattern has to be psychologically motivated — I want a story about why someone would actually choose that way, not just two vectors of probabilities that happen to disagree. Reason I want it: my students are convinced the Allais pattern is all about certainty, and I want a case where that excuse is unavailable. Don't cite anything or send me off to papers, just do the work.

Give it to me as exactly five numbered items, a sentence or two each, no preamble and no summing-up at the end: (1) the two pairs, with the stipulated choices; (2) the demonstration that those choices really do violate independence, showing how the two pairs relate to one another; (3) what expected utility says about each pair; (4) which of the usual repairs — probability weighting, regret, break-even gambling, where you take a long shot to get back to where you started — can generate the reverse pattern and which definately can't; (5) the feature of the case that's doing the work, whether it collapses back into textbook Allais if I drop the reverse-direction requirement, and how a defender of independence would try to talk me out of calling it a counterexample. British English throughout, please.
