Gödel, Escher, Bach introduces the MU-puzzle — given the string MI and a handful of rewriting rules, can MU be derived? — as a contrast between reasoning inside a formal system and reasoning about one: no sequence of rule applications ever reaches MU, while a single observation about the number of I's in a derivable string settles the matter at once. Generalizing from such cases, one can speak of jumping out of the system (JOOTSing): the act by which a reasoner stops operating a system's rules and begins to inspect them, so that a tool becomes an object.

Two accounts of that act are usually run together, and I would like them pulled apart and compared. On the ascent account, the jump is a genuine change of level: the reasoner arrives at a vantage that is not itself governed by the rules in question, and it is precisely because that vantage is external that the system's limits show up as limits. On the lateral account, no such vantage exists. Whatever the reasoner ascends into is itself another system, with its own rules, its own unformulable questions, and its own blind spots; outside therefore means only inside something larger, and the felt difference between using a rule and inspecting it is a fact about the reasoner's position rather than about any escape from systems as such.

Compare the two accounts, and address the following in the course of doing so.

1. Is the invariant argument for MU's unreachability an ascent or a lateral move? More generally, is there a principled difference between establishing a fact about a system S from a stronger system that contains S, and genuinely getting outside S?

2. The lateral account invites a regress: one larger system after another, each with its own blind spots. Does the regress show that ascent never occurs, or only that it is always relative? What would the ascent theorist have to concede, and how much can be conceded before the position stops being distinct?

3. A limit of a system is sometimes formulated, and partly established, within it: a system strong enough to encode its own proof predicate can prove that if it is consistent then it cannot prove its own consistency. What does this do to the tidy picture on which limits become visible only from outside?

4. Consider a third proposal, which I will call the expressibility criterion: a jump is genuine exactly when the new vantage can formulate a question that was not merely unanswerable in the old system but unstatable there, so that the mark of ascent is a gain in expressibility rather than a gain in truth. Does this criterion vindicate the ascent account, or does it collapse into the lateral one, since gaining expressive power just is moving to a stronger system?

5. Finally, is the choice between the accounts substantive, or a terminological dispute about where to draw a line? If it is substantive, say what argument or evidence would decide it; if it is not, say what is lost by treating it as a matter of stipulation.
