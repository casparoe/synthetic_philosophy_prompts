I run a fortnightly seminar for about 18 second- and third-year undergrads, half philosophy half computer science, 75 minutes a session, and I want to do something on tangled hierarchies and the inviolate level — the Escher Drawing Hands situation where each hand is drawing the other and the picture looks perfectly fine untill you ask where the drawing came from. I have the bones of an activity and I want you to turn it into something runnable.

The bones: I split the room into two tables. Each table has to build a working rule-set for a very simple game from scratch, with one twist — no rule has any force until another player authorises it, and the power to authorise has to be granted by a rule, so they have to bootstrap the whole thing themselves. At one table I quietly leave a sealed envelope with a single founding clause in it, unamendable, that just stipulates its own force; the other table gets no envelope, and I dont tell them the tables differ. They play for 35-40 minutes, then we compare. What I want them to come away seeing is that the mutual authorisation can't get off the ground on its own, that something has to sit outside the loop and not be part of it, and that from inside the game you can't see what that something is — the hands can't see the paper. I want the activity to do that work, not me. No real money, no lecturing up front, and dont let the words tangled hierarchy or inviolate level get said out loud untill the debrief.

Give it back to me as exactly five numbered sections, a short paragraph each, no intro and no closing summary: the rules and materials, including the exact wording of the clause in the envelope; a minute-by-minute timeline for the 75 minutes, including when the envelope shows up and when to cut the play; the debrief, tying specific moments from each table to the tangle and to the thing that has to sit outside it; what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle each of those; and an honest note on what the setup can't reproduce — including whether the Gödelian version of this is the same point or just a loose analogy — and what I'll have to say myself.
