I sit on the professional-standards panel of a body that disciplines financial advisers, and as the academic member I get handed the job of writing up the reasoning. Case in front of us next month: an adviser walked a client round their portfolio eleven times in about two years. Roughly A to B to C and back to A again, three times over. Every trade was documented, every trade matched what the client said they wanted at that moment, and nobody's alleging he lied about a product or mis-sold any single step. But the client ended up back in essentially the portfolio they started with, minus around nine grand in fees and spreads, and feels fleeced. The adviser's defence is: at every point I gave them exactly what they asked for, and each individual step left them better off by their own lights at the time. The complainant says you don't need any one bad trade to be cheated, you just need to be walked in a circle. Two of my colleagues think this is a non-case. Either the client's preferences were cyclic, which is the client's problem, or they simply changed over time, which is allowed, and either way there's no wrong here, just a disappointing outcome. I dont think we can wave it away that easily.

Please act as a philosopher of practical rationality and write the short opinion I'll circulate before the hearing. I want a clear bottom line, censure, no case to answer, or censure with a specific remedial condition, plus the reasoning, and be honest about the strongest thing that can be said for the adviser rather than just piling on. I'd like you to say something concrete about the fact that the client, at the very start, would probably have agreed to the whole two-year package if you'd offered it to them up front as a take-it-or-leave-it bundle. Does that hypothetical prior agreement help the adviser or hurt him? And my colleague keeps insisting that a person with genuinely incomplete preferences, someone who simply can't rank two options rather than being torn between them, is a different animal from someone with a cycle, and shouldn't be lumped in with them. Does that distinction actually do any work in a case like this? Think it through before you write, and sketch the rival framings first if that helps. No need for citations, I'm not going to go look them up.

End by telling me where your reasoning is weakest and what would change your recommendation, for instance what the client would have to have known or been told at the outset for you to come out the other way.
