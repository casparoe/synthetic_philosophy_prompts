A friend in my philosophy-of-biology reading group keeps saying species are just like colors: the spectrum's a smooth continuum, we carve out "red" and "orange" for our own convenience, and there's no fact of the matter about exactly where red stops and orange starts -- so, he says, there's likewise no fact of the matter about where one species ends and the next begins, and the whole "species problem" is just us mistaking our labels for joints in nature. I want to push back but I don't want to just stamp my foot and insist species are real. Can you audit the analogy properly? Lay out what maps onto what (the spectrum, the wavelength, the category "red", the perceiver, the boundary itself), then tell me where the mapping actually breaks -- I'm especially intrested in what the color case has that the species case doesn't once you remember that a species is a lineage stretched through time and that the cut between two of them can do real causal and practical work (gene flow stopping, conservation law, phylogeny), not just perceptual work. Which disanalogies matter for the conclusion he's drawing and which are just noise, does his "it's all convention" conclusion survive them, and if there's a better analogy for what a species boundary actually is, what would it be?
