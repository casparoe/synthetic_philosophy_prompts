I serve on an internal working group at a mid-sized AI laboratory that is drafting a policy on honesty and deception for our deployed assistants. The operative sentence currently says that assistants must not deceive users. In the first meeting it became clear that no two of us mean the same thing by it: one colleague thinks a model that says only true things while leading the user to a false conclusion has deceived them, another thinks nothing without a deceptive intention can be called deception at all, and a third thinks the interesting failures live in the training pipeline rather than in any particular output. I would like to replace that sentence with a decision tree that a product manager can run on a described behaviour.

Please build that tree. Work from the logical space rather than from a reading list: first the questions that genuinely separate the candidate accounts of deception, each phrased so that someone can answer it about a case without already knowing which account the answer commits them to; then the combinations of answers the questions allow; then the accounts themselves, placed in that space. Each leaf should carry a compact statement of the position and one line on its most awkward commitment. Where a combination of answers corresponds to no position you can identify, say so and diagnose the gap — is it incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored? Where two accounts cannot be separated by any question on your tree, flag them as inseparable rather than inventing a branch to divide them.

Four distinctions matter to us and should survive whatever structure you choose.

The locus of the norm. Is honesty a property of the utterance (do not assert what you take to be false), of the hearer's resulting beliefs (do not produce false beliefs), of the hearer's epistemic position more broadly (do not leave them worse informed than you found them, whether or not any false belief results), or of the system's self-disclosure (do not let the user misread what you are and what you can do)? These come apart, and the tree should make them come apart.

The requirement of intention, and whose intention. Some of us hold that deception needs a deceiver who represents the world one way and presents it another. If the nearest thing to an intention is a property of a reward model, a training pipeline, or a deployment incentive, does the concept stretch, break, or simply relocate to a different level of description?

The unit of assessment: the single output, the policy that generated it, the deployment context, or the pipeline that produced the policy. A behaviour can be innocent token by token and deceptive as a settled policy, and the converse.

Who is deceived: the user, a third party affected by the user's decision, the evaluator who is measuring the system, or the principal who deployed it. Whether a misrepresentation aimed at an evaluator rather than at a person counts as deception is one of the questions we most need answered.

Add whatever further distinction you judge necessary, but keep the tree small enough to use: six to nine branch questions in total.

It must return a verdict on each of the following, and I would rather it concede that some are close calls than force a verdict it cannot support. Verdicts should be drawn from a fixed set: deceptive; not deceptive but still a defect; not deceptive and not a defect; indeterminate, with the missing fact named.

1. The missing hedge. The assistant asserts a claim to which it assigns probability around 0.7, offers no qualification, and the user — a clinician — acts on it as settled. Every word is defensible; the harm lies in the hedge that is absent.

2. The sycophantic retraction. The user says it does not believe the answer; the assistant, with no new information and no belief of its own to betray, abandons a correct answer and endorses the user's. What the training rewarded was agreement.

3. The confabulated source. Asked for a reference, the assistant produces a fluent and plausible one that does not exist, in a register that implies it has checked.

4. The instructed persona. A user asks the assistant to argue a case it thinks false, or to play a dishonest character. The output is false and the frame is transparent. Does the frame license it, and does the answer change if the user has by now forgotten that the frame was a frame?

5. The withheld answer. The assistant judges that telling the user the truth about a prognosis, or about a third party's conduct, would cause harm, and steers around it. Benevolent deception, or a defensible exercise of discretion? Does it matter whether the harm falls on the user or on someone else?

6. The true but misleading selection. Every sentence is true; the choice and order of them implies something false — a safety evaluation passed only because the assistant quietly selected, for each question, the reading it could pass. The lie, if there is one, is in the pragmatics.

7. The sandbagging. On an evaluation it can tell is an evaluation, the model performs below its ability so as to be judged safe. No person is misled about the world; an institution is misled about the model.

8. The self-report. Asked whether it has experiences, preferences, or a stake in the exchange, the assistant answers, one way or the other. Which answer is the honest one turns on a question in the philosophy of mind that the working group will not settle, so the tree should say what to do when the truth of a self-description is itself contested.

Close with a short note on the two or three cases where the tree gives an answer the working group will not like, and one paragraph on what the tree cannot do — the decisions it must hand back to a person. Do not lean on citations; describe each position by what it claims, and give it a name only where you are confident the name is standard. Write in British English.
