ok, I'm putting together my view on real patterns and the three stances for a reading group on Thursday, and I want a brutal consistency check before I open my mouth, because I suspect I've been coasting on vibes for a while.

Here's the package:

1. Beliefs and desires are real. Not convenient fictions, not just noises we make to predict each other. If the intentional stance keeps working on a system across interventions and in situations the system was never built for, then the pattern is there in the system, not just in our description of it.
2. What makes a pattern real is compression: a pattern is real to the extent that it lets you say more with less. But which patterns anyone bothers to pick out depends on their interests and their cognitive budget. There's no interest-free inventory of the patterns in the world.
3. Physicalism. Underneath everything it's physics, and anything real has to be grounded in the physical facts. When the physical stance is available, it gets the final word on what there is.
4. There's no original intentionality. Our beliefs aren't special — they're derived, from evolution, from language, from culture. Nothing anywhere has intentionality from the inside in a way that a well-built robot couldn't have.
5. The stances are ordered. You can always drop from intentional to design to physical, and the lower level explains why the higher one works. But the higher-level pattern is not reducible to the lower one, otherwise it wasn't a real pattern to begin with (see 1).
6. A thermostat doesn't believe anything. A chess engine that beats me does, or at least it has beliefs about the board. There's a fact of the matter about that difference, not just a choice of convenient description.

What I want from you: which of these actually pull against each other, ranked by how bad the pull is, with the cheapest fix for each and an honest note on what that fix costs me elsewhere in the list. I'm fairly sure some of these only look like they conflict, so seperate the real collisions from the ones that disolve once you read them right. And please don't tell me it all hangs together once you pick the right glosses, or that a card-carrying Dennettian would have a clever answer to every objection — I want to know whether those answers cost me one of the six, and if so which single revision cleans up the most mess at the least cost. Probly worth saying which commitment is doing the most load-bearing work in the whole package, since I have a suspicion it's one of the boring ones.
