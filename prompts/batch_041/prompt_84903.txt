Analyze the collapse of the Soviet Union between 1985 and 1991 through the lens of hindsight bias and the narrative fallacy in historical explanation. Take the salient facts as given rather than argued: through 1990 most specialists inside and outside the USSR expected some version of the union to survive; Gorbachev's stated aim was to renew the union, not to dissolve it; the August 1991 coup attempt failed; by December 1991 the union was gone. I do not want a recitation of the familiar literature on outcome bias. Work instead with this three-part scheme, which I take to be the machinery of retrospective distortion: (1) inevitability inflation, in which the remembered probability of an outcome is raised toward certainty; (2) clue selection, in which only the antecedents that prefigure the outcome are retained as causes; (3) agent re-description, in which actors' contemporaneous uncertainty is rewritten as intention or blindness. Map the episode onto the three, say which does the most explanatory work and which the least, and then tell me where the scheme breaks. Specifically: can it distinguish a process that was overdetermined from one that was inevitable, and does anything about the collapse resist being told backwards -- the ethnic and economic facts that operated regardless of anyone's narrative, say? Then run the analysis the other way: does the episode support the claim that retrospective inevitability is a bias at all, or could it be rational, given that the outcome is itself evidence about which antecedents mattered? Where the scheme is silent, say so rather than forcing it to speak. Lead with your verdict in one sentence; total response under 600 words.
