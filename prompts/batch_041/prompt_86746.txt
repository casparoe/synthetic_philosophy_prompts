My friend Dana and I argued for like three hours at her kitchen table on Sunday night about sortition, term limits, and career politicians, and I'm still not sure I won. Her pitch, roughly: replace our city council with a body of about 45 people picked by lot, one three-year term each, no re-selection ever, staggered so a third of the seats turn over every year; the mayor and everyone else elected gets one term and thats it; and the allotted body gets binding authority over the budget instead of the advisory role everyone gives these things. Her best point, and the one I couldn't shake: the people who actually stick around in city hall are the staff — the policy analysts, the budget office, the consultants — and they're the only ones who never face a vote, so a lottery is the honest way to admit that the permanent government is the staff, and to keep it that way on purpose. No electoral ambitions in the building, no political class forming, just people who write memos for whoever shows up this year.

My counter, which I still think is right: whoever writes the briefing book sets the agenda. A chamber of first-timers with a binding mandate and no institutional memory is the best possible world for the people who decide which three options go on page one of the options paper. Dana says the evidence from state-level term limits backs her up, that the permanent class fills the vacuum either way, and she'd rather that class be the one that can't run for office. Fair. But can't run for office isn't the same as has no power, and I don't think she's priced in how much the agenda-setting is worth.

Anyway. Cut to Thursday: I'm the independent chair of our city's brand-new citizens' assembly — 60 people drawn by lot from the voter rolls, four Saturdays on the budget, mandate is advisory only, council has to respond in writing to whatever we send them but doesnt have to take any of it. I have to give the opening remarks at the first sitting, and I'm dreading it, partly because I half-agree with Dana that an advisory assembly is decoration and the columnist in the back row has basically already written that piece, and partly because the 60 people in the room are nervous, have day jobs, and a few of them are pretty sure they got picked by mistake.

So: write the opening remarks. Five minutes, so maybe 600 words. It has to be honest — I can't sell them on a power we don't have, and I'm not going to tell them they're wiser than the council, that makes my teeth hurt. The stuff Dana and I were fighting about should be doing real work in it: what's actually different about a random group versus a self-selected one, and what an advisory mandate is honestly worth, and why I still want them back next Saturday. It has to land for the 60 members first, and not be embarrassing for the staff or the columnist. No citations, obviously.

Then, after the speech, give me a short JSON summary with these fields, in this order: "speech" (the full text as delivered), "core_claim" (one sentence: what view about sortition, term limits and career politicians the speech actually commits me to), "left_unsaid" (the strongest point on Dana's side that I chose not to make in the room, plus one line on why I left it out), "for_the_nerds" (the one sentence in the speech aimed at people who've read the lottocracy literature, quoted exactly as it appears in the speech), "for_everyone_else" (same, but for the members and their families), "riskiest_line" (the line the columnist is most likely to clip as proof the whole thing is theatre, plus a drop-in replacement), and "if_the_room_turns" (the two sentences I should cut if people start checking their phones at the four-minute mark, and whether cutting them breaks the argument).
