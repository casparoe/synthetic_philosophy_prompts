I am a third-year PhD student in decision sciences, and I run a monthly methods-and-models session for an interdisciplinary group of about eighteen graduate students drawn from economics, psychology, and philosophy. For the next meeting I have 75 minutes and no budget for real payouts, and I would rather run an activity than give a talk. The target idea is information cascades and rational herding. What I want participants to feel on their own pulses is (a) how a sequence of individually rational, Bayesian choices can lock an entire group onto the wrong answer; (b) that this runs through an information externality, where each person's choice reveals something to those downstream while also destroying the information contained in their own private draw; and (c) that the resulting herd is fragile, in the sense that a single later player with a sufficiently strong private signal ought to break it and often will. Please act as an expert in the design of classroom experiments in behavioral and experimental economics, and think carefully before committing to a design: begin by brainstorming two or three candidate mechanisms and saying briefly why you set the others aside, then develop your best one in full.

I would rather you not simply stage the textbook two-colour urn design, since I want a variant that sits far enough in the weeds that participants cannot pattern-match it to an experiment they have already met. Concretely, please build in heterogeneity of private-signal strength, so that some players' draws are nearly informative while others' are close to worthless, and give every player the option — at a small cost charged against their own score — to reveal their private signal publicly before choosing, so that the group has to work out for itself when buying information is worth it and what a cascade does to that calculation. Give me the rules, the materials, the exact points scheme (again, no real money), the order of play, how private information is transmitted while keeping players honest, and how many rounds to run.

Then give me a facilitator's debrief that maps what the group actually did onto the formal apparatus — the Bayesian update, the threshold at which a player rationally sets their own signal aside, the information externality, and the idiosyncratic-shock account of why cascades collapse — and that does this mapping by pointing to specific moments in the activity rather than by lecturing. Finally, tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it, separating the failure modes I should pre-empt in the instructions from the ones I should simply let happen, and be candid about which features of the theory the activity cannot reproduce and that I will have to supply in words myself.
