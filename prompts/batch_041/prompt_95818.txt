I would like help disambiguating a sentence that is currently doing real work in an argument I keep having. A colleague of mine closes down discussions of institutional reform with the claim that this is the best of all possible worlds. He means something specific by it: that if the arrangement we are objecting to were avoidable, it would not obtain; that the costs we want to reduce are therefore not defects to be repaired but necessary components of an optimum; and that anyone proposing a change is claiming to have identified a better world than the one that was, so to speak, selected. I know the phrase is Leibniz's coinage, and I know the Pangloss routine well enough that you need not rehearse it. What I cannot tell is whether the sentence can bear the weight he puts on it. My suspicion is that it compresses several very different claims — some of them near-trivial, some of them theses in modal metaphysics, some of them claims about the psychology of a divine chooser, some of them claims about the structure of value — and that his argument requires one particular reading while the position he is borrowing asserts another.

Please lay the readings out as a menu rather than arguing for one of them. I would like them ordered from the reading that commits us to almost nothing to the reading that commits us to everything, and for each I want four things specified: what 'best' is being measured by; what 'possible' is being contrasted with; whether the claim concerns the whole of reality or of history, or instead particular events, institutions, and individuals within it; and whether the claim is about the actual world, about the act of choosing it, or merely the assertion that some optimum exists somewhere in the space of possibilities. Note as well which readings presuppose a chooser and which can be stated without one. Then give a verdict on each: platitude, substantive and defensible, substantive and probably false, or empty. I would rather be told that a reading is vacuous than be handed a thesis invented to make it look respectable.

Three points in particular I want the menu to settle. First: is there any reading on which the optimality attaches to the parts rather than to the whole — to this arrangement, this loss, this person's situation — since that is what my colleague's argument needs, and I suspect that the doctrine he is borrowing locates the optimum in the whole series while allowing particular events to be bad and particular people to have fared better in some other world. Second: is the claim one that anyone could in principle check or apply, or is it a claim about a computation only a divine intellect could run? If the latter, say what follows for someone who deploys it to settle a concrete dispute about a budget line. Third: does the phrase presuppose that worlds are totally ordered and that a maximum exists? Tell me what becomes of it if the ordering is only partial, or if there is no best but an endless ascent, and whether the modest readings survive that possibility while the ambitious ones do not.

Where you attribute anything to Leibniz, be exact, and mark clearly which items are the popular summary rather than his position. I have heard that he distinguishes between what God wills considered in himself and what he wills given the whole series, and that he takes the best world to be unique; I do not want either of those assumed, so place them on the menu if they are genuine and flag them if they are not.
