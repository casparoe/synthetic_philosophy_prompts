I sit on a small commission -- three of us, plus a lawyer we share with the planning department -- that advises our city council on what to do when somebody asks the city to take responsibility for something it did in the past. A land grab, a forced removal, collaboration in a deportation, a fortune built on a trade we'd rather not name. The council takes our recommendation and usually votes it through, so when we say 'the city should formally acknowledge X and pay Y' or 'the city owes nothing here', that becomes policy. We get maybe six of these a year, each one gets a public hearing, and the descendants on both sides are sitting in the room.

I've been leaning on Jaspers's four kinds of guilt -- criminal, political, moral, metaphysical -- because it at least seperates who did what from who is answerable now. But his book is about Germans who were mostly alive in 1945 and had lived through it, and our cases are almost never like that. The wrongdoers are dead. The people asking are two, four, six generations down. The people being asked to pay didn't do anything, and half of them weren't born here.

So what I need is a procedure we can actually run, in order, on a new case, and that we can hand to a new commissioner when one of us rotates off. Not an essay about guilt -- a set of questions in sequence, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops with a no, and where it hands the decision to the commission as a vote because the question is values and not facts. It has to survive being read aloud at a hearing, and we have about three weeks per case and no historian on staff.

Some things it needs to handle. First, the generation gap: I want the procedure to force us to say, for each of Jaspers's four kinds, whether it survives the death of the perpetrators and if so by what mechanism, and to say plainly when it doesn't survive and we're just importing the moral charge into the political category. Second, remedies: what each kind, if satisfied, actually licenses us to do -- prosecution is usually moot, but what does 'political guilt' license, and is there anything a commission can do with metaphysical guilt, or is that the one we have to say is none of our business. Third, one of our commissioners keeps pushing to add a category for people who did nothing but still benefit -- inherited land, a city that still sits on the confiscated plot, a university still spending the endowment -- and I can't tell whether that's a fifth kind of guilt or just political guilt wearing a new coat. The procedure should make us settle that rather than leave it vague, and if the honest answer is that 'beneficiary' is doing different work than any of Jaspers's four, say so and build it in as its own test.

Then run it on three cases and show me the whole path, not just the verdict:

(a) A city that enclosed common land used by a still-present indigenous community in the 1850s; the community asks for formal acknowledgment, the return of the land, and a payment. The present council says the land is now the site of the main hospital.

(b) A region whose wartime authorities handed a minority group over to an occupying power; descendants of that group, now abroad, want a state apology. The current government's line is that the region was itself occupied and its officials were acting under coercion.

(c) A case where the claimant is sympathetic and the commission will want to say yes: a community harmed by a private company, long since dissolved, whose former owners' descendants are now prominent locally. I want to see whether the procedure lets us say 'no collective guilt here' without it reading as a brush-off, and what it lets us offer instead.

Be honest about the seams. Tell me where the procedure is a judgment call dressed up as a test, where Jaspers's own distinctions stop doing work for a body like ours, what we should do when the categories point in different directions (some of the four satisfied, some not), and what the procedure flatly cannot decide -- the cases where we should stop pretending and just vote.
