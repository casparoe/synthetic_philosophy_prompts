Ok so last night my friend Priya and I got into a stupidly long argument about peacocks. Her line was that the tail is an honest signal of fitness, I said honest is doing no work in that sentence, she said fine then it's just waste, and we went around like that for two hours and neither of us won. What actually bugged me is that we both kept sliding into talking like the bird is trying to communicate something, and neither of us could say out loud what the tail is even supposed to be doing if there's no communication anywhere in the story.

So now I want to build a class session around that exact feeling. Here's my situation: I'm running one 80-minute slot for a philosophy of biology seminar, 18 students, juniors and seniors, mixed bio and philosophy, one seminar room, whiteboard, no laptops (department rule, thank god). No money and no prizes worth anything -- I've got a bag of poker chips and that's it.

What I want them to walk out having felt in their bodies, not just heard me say: first, that a signal which costs everybody the same amount buys you exactly nothing except a bill, and they should live through that and be genuinely annoyed by it. Second, that once the signal is cheaper for the high-quality players than for the low-quality ones, information gets through anyway, with nobody being honest, nobody meaning anything, nobody even trying. Third, that there's a margin -- a line where the low type could still afford to fake it -- and that the whole thing collapses on one side of that line and works on the other, and they should be able to point at roughly where it is. Fourth, that the receiver is doing work here too, that the signal only pays off because someone is set up to reward it, and that this is the part that should make them uncomfortable.

Don't name the principle or the biologist up front, and don't put the moral of it on the board until the debrief. I want the rules and the payoffs to do the teaching, not me. So design the game: rounds, roles, how quality gets hidden, how the signal gets chosen and observed, the payoff table, how many rounds, and how you keep it from turning into a one-shot guessing game where the first person to get lucky wins and everyone else checks out. Materials, a short script for me to read out that gives nothing away, and a timeline for the 80 minutes.

Then the debrief: map what actually happened onto the real idea, moment by moment, and be honest about which part of it the game can't reproduce. I already suspect the big one is that I'm fixing what the receivers want, and in the real story their taste has to get there somehow too, but tell me if I'm wrong about that and what else I'm papering over.

Also tell me what's going to go wrong the first time. I've run alot of these and something always does, and I'd rather know in advance. If it's that someone decides the whole setup is immoral and refuses to play, tell me how to handle that without turning it into a seperate lecture.

American English please, I'm teaching in the States now and the students notice.
