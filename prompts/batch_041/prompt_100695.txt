I am a novelist, and for about a year I have been trying to write an essay around the gender-equality paradox — the finding that in the countries scoring highest on standard measures of gender equality, men and women sort into different fields of study and different occupations more sharply, not less, than they do in poorer and less egalitarian countries. A philosopher friend, impatient with my hedging, helped me turn my half-formed intuitions into a proper argument. Now I cannot decide whether it is a proof or a trap, and I would like you to tell me where it breaks and what breaking it costs. Here it is, set out as cleanly as I can manage:

(1) The more egalitarian a country's norms and institutions, the more men and women diverge in the fields and occupations they choose.
(2) Egalitarian norms and institutions are precisely what remove the external constraints — discrimination, expectations, economic pressure, the sheer cost of deviating — that would otherwise push men and women into the same fields.
(3) If the constraints are removed and the divergence nevertheless grows, then the divergence cannot be caused by the constraints.
(4) So the divergence must be caused by something internal: by average differences in what men and women want, or in the aptitudes that shape what they want.
(5) Therefore the persistence of gendered career sorting in the most egalitarian countries is evidence that such sorting is, at bottom, natural rather than cultural.

My friend says the argument is airtight and that the only resistance to it is wishful thinking. I do not find the conclusion convenient — it cuts against nearly everything I would like to believe — and that is exactly why I distrust my own suspicion that something has slipped. My hunch is that the trouble lies in the move from (3) to (4), or perhaps as early as the move from (1) to (2); I suspect that the countries called egalitarian are egalitarian in a narrower sense than (2) quietly assumes, and that the inference from a country-level pattern to a claim about individuals is doing more work than it can bear. But I can also feel myself wanting to keep (1) — an empirical claim I am reasonably confident of — and to sacrifice (5), which is the one I want to be false, and a person who reasons from what she wants to be true cannot be trusted to police her own argument.

So I would like two things. First, name the step or steps you would reject, and explain why they looked so plausible in the first place — I need to see the trap, not merely be told it exists. Second, and this is the part I cannot do for myself: whichever premise you give up, what does giving it up commit me to elsewhere? If I surrender (2), I seem to owe an account of how the relevant constraints could grow stronger precisely where they are supposed to have been lifted; if I keep everything through (4), I have to explain why the same inferential shape does not prove too much, licensing conclusions about other groups whose measured differences shrink rather than grow as equality increases. I would rather keep (1) than keep (5), but I want to know the price before I pay it.
