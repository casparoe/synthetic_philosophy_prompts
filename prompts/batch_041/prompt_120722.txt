I teach a graduate seminar in decision theory, and there is one session I have never managed to land. We spend the first half of it on the universal prior — Solomonoff's construction, in which an agent's credence in a hypothesis is taken to be proportional to 2 to the power of minus the length of the shortest program that computes it, so that every computable hypothesis receives some weight and shorter ones receive more, after which Bayes' rule does the rest. The students follow the mathematics well enough. What I cannot get across is the consequence for an agent who takes that prior seriously about its own position: because description length rather than plausibility fixes the weights, the pool of hypotheses that fit one's evidence always contains compact ones on which one's choices are duplicated across enormous numbers of similar agents, or on which some process is keeping score and will settle up later, or on which the whole local scene exists for the sake of a single decision. These are not hypotheses of vanishingly small weight — the prior is supposed to be the maximally unbiased one, and nothing one can go on to observe shakes them loose. So an agent who reasons by expected value, and who treats its own case as a draw from that prior, finds its deliberation dominated by whatever the strangest member of the pool has to say. Last term a student told me this was Pascal's wager with more algebra, and then, an hour later, that it was a description of paranoia; I could not persuade her that it was neither, and I have been chewing on that ever since. I want a piece of fiction that does the persuading.

So: write me a short story, roughly two thousand to three thousand words, that dramatizes this. I have in mind an ordinary contemporary setting with no science-fictional furniture — no pods, no visible seams in the world, no reveal, and no machine that turns out to have been running anything. The character should be an adult with an ordinary life and no special training: someone who has absorbed the argument secondhand, from a friend or from reading late at night, and who now cannot get out from under it. Let the story turn on one small, decidable choice with real but modest stakes — a loan to a sibling, a truth told or withheld, something of that order; you choose. The machinery has to be on the page rather than gestured at: at least one passage in which the character actually reasons, with something like an estimate of how much weight the hypothesis deserves and what follows from it, so that a reader can see the shape of the inference and not merely the mood it produces. And I want a second character who pushes back hard — the objection should be a good one, one the protagonist cannot simply dismiss, not a strawman to be knocked over.

A few things I care about more than the plot. First, a reader should finish the story able to say why this is not Pascal's wager, and I would rather this be shown than stated: the difference has to do with where the weight comes from, not with how large the payoff is. Second, do not resolve it. No ending in which the character sees through the thing, and none in which the world quietly confirms it; the story should end with a decision that has been made and a reader who cannot say whether the character is mad, correct, or merely unlucky in the prior it was handed. Third, keep the strange hypotheses inside the character's head — they are things being considered, never things the narration endorses or contradicts. Please avoid the ready-made props: no brains in vats, no Boltzmann brains, no slogans about living in a simulation, no named real people, and no references to particular blogs, books, or authors, since the story should stand without them. I would also rather you not tidy the protagonist's version of the argument into something a logician would sign off on — let it be slightly crooked, as secondhand reasoning is, as long as a careful reader can reconstruct the real thing underneath. Third person, past tense, no framing device, and no closing lecture from the narrator.
