I write about science and ideas for a regional daily, and I sit on the collections and ethics committee at our university art museum. We meet Thursday and I'm on the hook to circulate a short written opinion beforehand, so I need you to draft the thing.

Here's the tangle. The museum owns the Harrow Commission — twelve bronze figures cast in 1873 by the sculptor Ada Rennick (she died in 1890). A local collector has offered us a thirteenth figure that matches the twelve down to the alloy mix and the tool marks. His story: Rennick's 1873 moulds sat in the foundry for decades, and in 1921 the foundry, under new owners and with nobody's permission, pulled one more casting from them for a private client. So the figure is, as he puts it, from the original moulds but not from the original pour. He wants it shown as the thirteenth of the set, and he's floated a new label for it — a "sibling cast" — that he'd like us to be the first museum to adopt.

Our registrar wants it displayed seperate, tagged as a 1921 recast, if we take it at all. The director says the whole distinction is a fetish: if it's visually identical, the public learns nothing from us making a fuss, and we should just show it with the others.

Draft my opinion. I definately need a recommendation — take it or decline, and if we take it, exactly how we display and label it (write the actual label text, keep it to a couple sentences) — plus the reasoning, and the strongest case for the side I'm not taking, stated fairly rather than as a straw man. Spend some time on the thing I can't get straight: does being cast from the artist's own moulds make this an original or a copy, and does it matter that she was dead by 1921 and never authorized it? I also want you to say plainly whether the "sibling cast" idea is a real category or a marketing trick, and what would have to be true for these two figures to count as the same work rather than two works.

Don't give me a menu. Pick a side, commit to it, and own the tradeoffs. One page.
