I'm putting together a session for a grad seminar on the history of ideas about poverty and desert, and I want something that jolts the students out of their usual left/right reflexes. Heres the setup. Take a modern activation-style welfare system, roughly the one we've ended up with in Britain: a single means-tested payment designed to make work pay, so it tops up low wages rather than replacing them; conditionality bolted onto it, so you sign a claimant commitment, you show up to appointments, you take the job you're offered or you eat a sanction; and the whole thing policed by data, where the department pulls your earnings records, averages them across a month, decides you were overpaid, and then it's on you to prove otherwise rather than on them to prove it. None of that is exotic, it's just where these systems have drifted, and I'd like to know what a dead economist makes of it.

Specifically: Malthus. He's the obvious person to ask, since he's the one who argued that poor relief in general, and topping up wages out of the rates in particular, is self-defeating — that it just bids up the price of provisions and leaves the poor no better off, and that the poor laws tend to manufacture the poverty they're meant to relieve. What I want isn't "he'd hate it," which any student can guess in ten seconds. I want the actual shape of the response. Which parts of the modern scheme would he read as the same old error in new clothing? Which parts would he approve of, or at least shrug at, in a way that would embarrass my students' assumptions about him? (I have a hunch he'd be far less bothered by the sanctions than by the in-work top-ups, but give me the argument, not my hunch.) And what would he make of the procedural shift — the fact that the burden of proof now sits with the claimant, which is a seperate question from the moralising about dependence, and which I think is the genuinely novel bit here.

Write it as he'd actually argue it, out of his own premises about population, wages, and self-reliance, and be explicit about where you're extrapolating past anything he actually held versus drawing on positions he really did take. If the honest answer is that his premises commit him to something he'd have found distasteful, say so.
