I am a junior at a secondary school and I run the weekly session of our mathematics club, which about fifteen students attend. They are comfortable writing proofs and have met the pigeonhole principle and induction, but none of them has studied game theory, and most of them believe that a proof which shows something exists ought to be able to say what it is. In three weeks I have a single forty-five-minute meeting, a whiteboard, and no slides, and I want to introduce them to the strategy-stealing argument: the style of reasoning that establishes that one player has a winning strategy in a game without ever producing that strategy. I am not asking you to explain the argument to me; I understand it and can defend it. I am asking you to plan how I should teach it.

Please give me the following, in order.

First, the sequence of ideas, with the dependency structure made explicit. What has to be in place before the argument can even be stated, what each step presupposes, and why the pieces must arrive in that order for the conclusion to feel earned rather than magical. I am especially unsure how much the audience needs about determinacy, about the impossibility of draws, and about the assumption that an extra move can never hurt, and in what order those should be introduced.

Second, the one example that should carry the whole session. Tell me what it must have and what it must lack in order to do the work, and choose between the two games I already have in mind, Hex and Chomp, explaining why one is better for this audience and what the other would cost me. If some third game would serve better, say so and say why.

Third, the misconceptions my students will predictably bring or form, and the precise point in the sequence at which I should head off each. My guesses are: that a non-constructive proof is not a real proof; that the first player's advantage is simply "because they move first"; that the argument tells you which move wins; and that the argument will work for any finite two-player game. Correct my list, add what I have missed, and tell me where each must be confronted.

Fourth, one thing I am tempted to do, on which I want your judgement. To stop the argument from looking like a trick that always works, I had planned to close by presenting a deliberately invalid strategy-stealing argument for a game where it actually fails -- a game that can end in a draw, or one in which being forced to move can hurt you -- and to let the students find the flaw. Is that a good use of the last ten minutes, or will it muddy the lesson? If it is worth doing, which kind of failing case should I use, and how should I set it up so that the flaw is discoverable rather than merely announced?

Fifth, one question I can put to them at the end that would reveal whether they have understood why the argument works, rather than merely memorised that the first player wins.

Finally, what I should deliberately leave out at this level, and how I should tell them I am leaving it out without making the omission feel like a hole.
