Sat through a city council meeting last night where a member shut down residents who've spent nine years trying to get a lead pipe ordinence passed — they finally blockaded the water depot, and he told them "if you don't like the law, work to change it." Thing is, the ordinance is preempted by the state and the neighborhood is mostly renters and non-citizens, so the channel he's pointing them at isn't really theirs. I want to write about this as a claim in political philosophy rather then as a dunk, so: what distinct precise theses could that one-liner actually be making? Consent, fair play, gratitude for benefits received, a natural duty of justice, the idea that you owe obedience just by being a member of a community that made you, plain prudence, or "democracy is the only legitimate channel" — and for each, what does it demand of a person in practice (how much effort, for how long, at what cost, and does it matter whether they have a vote or a voice)? Then tell me which readings are platitudes, which are live and defensible, which are false, and which one he probably meant — and which of them, if any, actually gets him the conclusion that these people are obligated to stay off the street. Menu with verdicts, not a lecture, and skip the reading lists.
