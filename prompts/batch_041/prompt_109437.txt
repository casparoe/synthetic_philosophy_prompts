I'm a 4th-year PhD student (philosophy, but I spend most of my time with the ML crowd) and I've been voluntold to run a 90-minute session at our interdisciplinary grad summer school next month. About 16 people, roughly half philosophers and half CS, they all know what a reward function is, but I'd guess none of them have thought about wireheading for more than five minutes. I definately want the activity itself to do the work here -- no framing lecture up front, and I don't want to say "wireheading" or "reward tampering" out loud until the debrief.

What I'm chasing: I want them to spend the first half happily optimizing some measured number, notice the measure drifting away from the thing it was supposed to track, and then get handed the ability to move the measure directly and find that within about two minutes it stops feeling like cheating. The bit I most want them to feel is that the interesting line isn't "did you touch the reward channel or not" -- the channel is sitting in the world along with everything else, so an agent who can't touch the meter can still arrange a world that the meter can't tell apart from the real thing. And then the last turn of the screw: if you can't edit the rubric, you can edit yourself so that whatever you just did counts as good, and that one doesn't even register as a violation.

Practical constraints: whiteboard, index cards, maybe $20 of props, no laptops, no real money, and it's the 4pm slot so they'll be flat. The rules and the incentives should carry the whole thing.

Give it to me as exactly five numbered items, one or two sentences each, no intro and no conclusion. I need: the setup and what the players get told; the scoring and payoff rules; the part where it bites and how the escalation happens without me announcing it; a debrief that maps specific moments in the game onto the ideas, including the three different things a player can move (the world, the meter, their own preferences) and why the last one is the weird one; and finally what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it, plus an honest note on which part of the idea the game just can't reproduce and I'll have to supply seperate. One of them will say "isn't this just Goodhart" -- the game should get past that, so tell me how.
