Our interpretability group has an internal rule for when we're allowed to say a model contains a given mechanism, and I've been arguing with collegues about what we accidentally signed up for. The rule has two halves. First, a mechanism claim has to replicate: you have to find the thing in at least two models trained separately from scratch (different random seeds, same data), and show that intervening on it -- zeroing it out, or swapping its activations between inputs -- changes behaviour the same way in both. Second, and separately, we run an anomaly pipeline: fine-tune a model on some new task, diff its internals against the base model, and any component that shows up in the fine-tuned model with no counterpart in the base gets flagged as an "anomalous mechanism" and sent for human review, on the working assumption that the base model's components are the normal ones.

What bugs me is that the two halves seem to pull apart. Two things can pass the replication test while sharing almost no weights, features, or wiring -- they're the same mechanism only in the sense that poking them does the same thing. And two things that look like an exact structural match in the diff can behave completely differently when you poke them, and one of those we don't flag. So what does our rule actually presuppose about when two mechanisms are the same mechanism? Functional role, causal profile, structural isomorphism, some mixture? Which rival accounts of mechanism identity does it quietly rule out, and are the two halves committed to the same account or to different ones? I'd also like to know what's strictly presupposed here versus what we just happen to satisfy, and where the rule is underdetermined so that several accounts would license it. Last thing: if we switched to a different account of sameness, what concretely would have to change about how we publish, and about what gets flagged as anomalous?
