I have been trying to settle, case by case, what I actually believe about bringing people into existence, and my verdicts have come out in a pattern I did not expect.

Where a couple knows that any child of theirs would have a high chance of a short life dominated by pain, I think conceiving is wrong, and wrong for the child rather than merely regrettable. Where a couple would have a healthy, happy child and chooses not to, I do not think anyone has been wronged, and I feel no loss; if they said they preferred to travel instead, I would not think worse of them. Where a couple conceives a child whose life will be barely worth living — some pleasure, chronic low-grade pain, nothing that would make the trouble worth it — I think conceiving is wrong, though I would not say the child is harmed in the way the first couple's child is. Where a child is conceived in order to serve as a tissue donor for an existing sibling, I think the child is wronged even if the child's own life goes well. Where a couple could either conceive or adopt a child already in desperate need, I think adopting is better, not merely as good. And then there is the case I cannot place: the painless extinction of all sentient life, with no one left to regret it. I feel this as a loss, as though something had gone out of the world, though I cannot say for whom.

The principle I have been working with is this: bringing someone into existence can harm them but cannot benefit them, since a harm can fall on the person you create whereas a benefit needs someone already in existence to receive it. That seems to me to explain the first two verdicts and to show why the donor case and the adoption case are not, or not only, about anyone's welfare. I want to know whether it survives the rest.

Please give me the principle, or the smallest set of principles, that captures as much of this as possible; for each clause, the case that forces it; an explicit statement of which of my verdicts it cannot keep, and why; and its verdicts on cases I have not put to it — in particular whether there is any number of happy people whose addition to the world would itself be a good thing, and whether a life's being worth living is ever by itself a reason to create one. Please commit to a position: tell me which principle you would actually defend, what you would give up for it, and, if you think one of my verdicts is simply a mistake, which one and why. I would rather have one defended principle with a straight answer about its costs than a survey of the options.
