Right, so — I'm not an academic, I just read a lot of history in my spare time, and I've ended up conviced of something I can't shake. I've started calling it the deep-water view. The idea is that what actually drives history is the slow stuff — climate, geography, where the rivers and the mountain passes are, disease, how many people there are and what they can grow — and that the famous events and famous people are basically froth on top of a wave. Revolutions, the rise and fall of empires, who wins a war: given the deep conditions, most of it was going to happen anyway, and the individuals involved just get the credit or the blame for standing there when it did. I think some French historian has a proper name for this, long door-ay or something, but I've never read the actual stuff.

Please act as a professional historian with a good grounding in the philosophy of history, and think carefully before you answer.

What I want is a proper map of what would count against this view. Which observations, arguements, cases or formal results should actually move me? Sort them by how decisive they'd be — I want to know which are fatal and which are merely awkward. Include the ones that someone defending a view like mine would themselves concede count against it, not just the ones the critics shout about. And tell me honestly whether the deep-water view risks anything at all, or whether it's been quietly qualified until nothing could count against it — and if it has, whether that's actually a problem.

A few things I'd especially like you to get into, though don't let them stop you adding others:

- Cases where the deep conditions look identical but the outcomes diverge. Does that sink the view or can it be absorbed?
- Whether you can actually run the test the view seems to need: rerun the same continent with the same geography and different weather, or the same weather and a different crop, and say what you'd expect. Is that even coherent?
- The single individual who seems to matter — the lucky shot, the chance discovery, the person who was simply in the room. How much of the long run can one person really shift?
- Whether "the deep structures explain it" is doing any real work, or is just a story we tell after the fact about whichever structures happened to matter.

Start by brainstorming the kinds of thing that could count against it before you commit to any verdict. And don't fob me off with "it depends on your level of analysis" — I know it does, I want to know what follows from that.
