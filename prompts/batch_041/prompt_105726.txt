I don't have any training in philosophy — I work as a mechanical engineer and I've been reading about consciousness and personal identity on my own for a couple of years — and I've gotten stuck on the question of whether I would survive having my brain slowly swapped out for artificial parts that do exactly what the original parts did. What I want from you is not a survey of the debate; I've read enough of those to know the usual moves, and they always seem to be decided by which example the author happened to pick. What I want is a single case built to order, so that I can see whether a particular pair of verdicts can be pulled apart by design rather than by rhetoric. For what it's worth, this started when a friend claimed that as long as the swap is gradual enough you obviously survive, because there is never a moment when you stop, and I could not tell whether that was an insight or a trick.

Here is the case I want. Keep it in a plausible near future: ordinary medicine pushed a little further, no teleportation, no copying of a whole brain into a computer, no magic. The replacement should be gradual and piecemeal — a few cells at a time, spread over months — and it should happen in place, in the same living body, which stays alive and healthy the whole way; the person is awake for most of it and their memory, character, and tastes come through unchanged, so that at the end there is exactly one person, not two, and nothing has branched. Now the requirement I actually care about. I want the case to make two verdicts come apart. By a standard that tracks the pattern — the unbroken chain of memories, dispositions, and mental life — the original person is still there at the end. By a standard that tracks the continuity of the experiencing subject itself, the thing that is doing the experiencing, the original is not: the lights went out and a new set of lights came on, even though the two are indistinguishable in what they illuminate. I do not want that divergence simply asserted; I want it generated from the details of the case, and I want it to hinge on one feature that I can then remove. My hunch is that the feature is the timing of the last swap — that if the final piece of original tissue is taken offline in a single stroke, while everything before and after is gradual, then the pattern-standard says survives and the subject-standard says gone, whereas if that last piece had been faded out gradually while the person slept, the subject-standard would flip to survives and the two would agree. Build the case so that this is exactly the hinge, if it can be. If my specifications cannot all hold at once, say which one has to give and why, rather than quietly dropping it.

Then run the checks I would want a careful person to run. State, for the case as built, the belief or fact at issue, the criterion being applied, and why each of the two standards returns the verdict it does, rather than gesturing at the conclusion. Tell me honestly which feature is doing the real work, and whether it is the one I named or something I smuggled in without noticing — I would rather find out that my hinge is a red herring than be flattered. Say what happens if I remove or reverse that feature, and whether the case collapses back into an ordinary disagreement about gradual replacement or stays distinctive. Finally, tell me how someone who holds the standard that comes out worse would attack the case: what they would say to deny that it is a case of survival, or of non-survival, at all, and whether their reply depends on a premise I could independently reject. I care more about where the case is weak than about a tidy answer.
