I'm a novelist (historical fiction, so my whole job is inventing reasons for people who never existed) and I've somehow let a friend talk me into covering one session of her upper-level seminar — philosophy of mind / phil of biology crossover, mostly 3rd and 4th years — while she's away at a conference. The topic she left me with is the free-floating rationale thing: reasons that are really there, doing real work, shaping what happens, with nobody around who has them. A wasp's digging is "for" the larva; a flower's shape is "for" the bee; nobody's mind contains any of it. I get it on the page. I dont believe a single one of those students feels it, and honestly neither do I, and I refuse to stand there and lecture at them about it for eighty minutes. So I want to build something they *do* — 18 students, 80 minutes, a flat room with chairs we can move, no computers, no money, index cards and a whiteboard and that's it.

Here's a seed I want you to take and then fix wherever it's broken. Split the room: half Players, half Watchers. The Players get a rule sheet with a small coordination-ish game on it — say three actions, a payoff table, eight rounds, played silently with coloured cards. They may never speak, explain, or agree on anything out loud, and they're not told what the Watchers are doing. The Watchers sit behind them and see only which cards come out, never the rules, and after every round each Watcher writes one sentence saying *why* the players did what they did — they went left because they wanted to stay with whoever moved first, they switched because they were punishing the defector, and so on. Nobody's stated reasons are ever consulted, because the Players haven't got any: they're just following a table I made up arbitrarily on a Tuesday, and it could have been a different table. Then the reveal, and then a third phase I want you to design, where the Watchers have to sort their own sentences into ones that would still be correct if every mind in the room were switched off and ones that would collapse — and the knife twist being that some of the correct-sounding ones really are correct, and that's the whole uncomfortable point. If you can make the sorting phase turn on something the players actually did in round five, so much the better; I want a moment where the Watchers' story is flatly wrong and a moment where it's uncannily right, and I want the room to notice the difference before I name it.

What I want back: the rule sheet itself with actual numbers, who gets told what and when, the timing minute by minute, how the sorting phase runs (roles, materials, what a Watcher does with a sentence they can't classify), and a debrief that pins each bit of the idea to a specific moment in the room rather than to me talking. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time and what to do when it does — I'm guessing somehwere in round three a Watcher reverse-engineers the payoff table from the outside and announces it like a magician, and that the rest of them start hedging their sentences into mush once they smell a trap. And be straight with me about the one thing this can't do. My suspicion is it can't show them the difference between a rationale that's free-floating and one that's actually grasped by the creature doing the behaving, since none of my Players ever grasp anything, so the contrast never appears in the room and I have to supply it myself at the end. If that's the gap, say so plainly, and tell me exactly what to say to fill it.
