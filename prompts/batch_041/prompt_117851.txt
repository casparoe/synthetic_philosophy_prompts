A finance writer I read summarizes the Kelly criterion roughly like this: 'Kelly's rule says that when you have an edge you should bet the fraction of your bankroll that maximizes your long-run growth rate, which works out to your edge divided by the odds. Kelly proved this is the only strategy that cannot be beaten in the long run: bet more and you will eventually be ruined, bet less and you will grow more slowly, so full Kelly is what rationality requires. The deeper justification is that wealth compounds, and so is non-ergodic -- the average outcome across many gamblers is not the average outcome for one gambler over time -- and since you are one gambler over time, maximizing expected value is a mistake while maximizing the expected logarithm of your wealth is what a rational agent must do. This is why Kelly betting refutes expected-utility maximization as a general theory of choice: you cannot be both a Kelly bettor and an expected-utility maximizer. In practice, any bet that is not full Kelly is a mistake, and anyone who bets less than Kelly is simply being irrational about risk.' I am broadly sympathetic to Kelly betting, but I suspect the last few claims are doing something illegitimate and I cannot see where the mathematics stops and the rhetoric starts. Please audit this sentence by sentence: what a decision theorist or a working quantitative bettor would accept, what they would wince at, and what is simply wrong. Separate the claims that belong to Kelly's own result from those that come from the more recent non-ergodicity argument, say whether that argument actually refutes anything, and tell me whether the practical conclusion about fractional bets survives the correction. I would rather have your definite verdict on each point than a survey of positions, and I do not need citations.
