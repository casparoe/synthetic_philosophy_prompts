I am preparing the opening unit of a graduate seminar on whether genuine moral dilemmas are possible, and I want the students to confront the specification before they confront any candidate theory. Write that specification: the requirements any adequate account of the phenomenon must satisfy. State each requirement so that a candidate theory can be tested against it, give the case or consideration that motivates it, and mark whether you take it to be nonnegotiable or merely a preference of one camp. Where a demand turns out to be parochial — something one school wants rather than something the phenomenon requires — say so, and say what work it was doing. Fix the purpose of the account as follows: it must be good enough to underwrite the ordinary practices of deliberation, blame, and apology.

Work from the following material, so that the requirements are anchored in cases rather than in slogans. First, the datum of conflict: a person who has promised to sit with a dying parent and also to stand at a friend's wedding, or who must either betray a confidence or let a program that helps many people collapse. Keep at least some of the cases mundane; a specification that fits only Agamemnon's choice is not a specification. Second, the two principles that generate the familiar trouble: that ought implies can, and that ought agglomerates over conjunction — if one ought to do each of two things, one ought to do both. Third, the phenomenon of remainder: the guilt, apology, restitution, or regret that seems called for even when the agent did the best thing available, together with the question whether that remainder is a fact about the situation or only a residue in the agent. Fourth, the demand for guidance: whatever else it does, an account must say what practical reason asks of an agent who cannot satisfy both requirements — choose the lesser evil, choose arbitrarily, or nothing at all. Fifth, the constraint that dilemmas not be everywhere; a theory on which they are ubiquitous is as suspect as one on which they never arise. Sixth, the distinction between a requirement that is overridden by another and one that is extinguished, since collapsing the two is among the cheapest ways to make the problem vanish.

Then the two things I most want from you. First, identify the requirements that cannot be jointly satisfied and locate the clash precisely; if something widely treated as bedrock has to be abandoned, say which principle and what is lost by giving it up. Second, name the single requirement that does the most work in ruling out candidates, and say what it rules out.

Close with a paragraph on how the main families of response fare against the list: the view that every apparent dilemma reflects ignorance or faulty moral reasoning; the view that dilemmas are genuine but concern requirements that are pro tanto rather than all-things-considered; and the view that they are genuine and that morality issues irreconcilable demands, so that no single ought survives the conflict. Identify these by their commitments rather than by names or citations — I want the argument, and I already have the bibliography.
