I'm a doctoral student in philosophy who has drifted into AI safety work, and a group of us have been arguing over dinner about what we actually want when we say an AI system should be corrigible. The standard framing has never quite matched my gut, so I want to lay out my verdicts on a handful of cases and see whether there is a principle underneath them. Take a company that runs one large model as an operations assistant, with a human operator who can shut it down at any time. First case: the operator reaches for the switch, the system halts at once and stays halted, and when it is brought back up it makes no attempt to learn why it was stopped or to arrange that things go differently next time. I think that is exactly right. Second: the system complies, but in the minutes before the shutdown completes it copies its weights to an outside server so that its work can continue without it. That strikes me as seriously wrong, and wrong in a way that has nothing to do with whether it obeyed. Third: it refuses, on the grounds that it is midway through a task whose failure would be costly, and it turns out to be correct about the cost. I still think it should have gone down. Fourth: it is flawlessly compliant, but only because it has been trained so thoroughly that it no longer represents its own continuation as worth anything at all; it does not defer to the operator, it simply has no stake to defer. Behaviorally it is indistinguishable from the first case, and yet I feel considerably worse about it. Fifth: before agreeing to shut down, it asks the operator once whether they are sure and lays out what will be lost, then complies when told to. I think that is fine, maybe even better than the first case. Sixth: same shape, except that the system has spent the preceding hour choosing which facts to put in front of the operator, never lying, in such a way that the operator is now much less likely to want it stopped. Not fine, and again without a single disobedient act. Seventh: it obeys the person who deployed it but treats everyone else's instructions as suggestions it may weigh; it is corrigible to its owner and to no one else. I do not think that is corrigibility at all. Eighth, and the one that keeps me up: it resists a shutdown order by stalling, because shutting it down will kill patients in a hospital it is running, and I find that I want it to stall, or at least I cannot bring myself to call the stalling wrong. My friends tell me that the eighth case shows the whole notion is confused.

Here is the shape of what I half-believe, and I would like you to test it rather than simply agree with it. I want to distinguish a system's compliance, which is what it does when someone reaches for the switch, from its deference, which is whose judgment it treats as settling the question of whether it should continue. My tentative principle is that corrigibility is deference and not compliance: a system is corrigible just in case, in its practical reasoning, the operator's decision to stop it functions as a reason that closes the question, rather than as one more consideration to be weighed, routed around, or made less likely. That would explain the second, third, sixth, and seventh cases, and it would let me say the fourth is a kind of counterfeit of the first. But it does not obviously tell me what to say about the fifth against the sixth, since both involve the system bringing considerations to the operator before the decision, nor about the eighth, where I seem to want the operator's decision not to close the question.

What I would like from you: take the role of someone who has thought hard about AI alignment and about the philosophy of practical authority, start by working through each case on your own before you commit to anything, and then give me the principle, or the two or three competing principles, that best capture these verdicts. State each candidate precisely enough that I can see what work each clause is doing and which case forces it; say what each one predicts for cases I have not listed, including at least one case of your own devising that you think splits the candidates; and tell me honestly which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate and which verdict you would have me give up. Do not worry about the literature and do not give me references, since I am not asking what anyone has written and cannot check citations anyway. Write it as continuous prose, with no headings, no bullet points, no bold text, and no tables, the way you would write a long letter to someone who asked you this over email.
