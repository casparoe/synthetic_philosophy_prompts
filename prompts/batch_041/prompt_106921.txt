Ok so I've been running a political theory reading group out of my living room for a couple years now -- 16-20 people typically, grad students, a few rationalist-adjacent engineers, one retired judge, the kind of crowd that will argue about anything. Next session is 90 minutes and I want to do the lottocracy/sortition stuff as an activity instead of just talking about it, because every time we discuss it we end up doing the same two moves (somebody says it's obviously better than elections, somebody says but competence, and we all go home unchanged).

What I want people to actually *feel* is why selection by lot does something elections structurally can't, and why it isn't just a cute thought experiment. The specific thing: nobody can campaign for a slot when nobody knows who'll get picked (I think the lottocracy people have a name for this, the blind break or something) -- and how that changes what people are willing to say out loud before a decision gets made. And I want them to feel the competence objection honestly too, not have it waved away.

So here's the ask. You're a political philosopher who also designs deliberative exercises and classroom games. Before you commit to anything, brainstorm 2-3 candidate mechanics and tell me why you're picking the one you pick. Then give me the full build.

Setting and constraints:
- 18 people, 90 minutes, one room I can split into two halves with a divider. I'm facilitating and happy to be a returning officer, but I want a minute-by-minute of what I'm doing and where I should shut up.
- Stakes should be real but tiny -- I'll put the group's snack fund on the line, or a coffee-run chore, something people actually mildly care about. Give me the payoff scheme.
- Two parallel bodies deciding the same concrete question at the same time, one selected by election and one by lot. I want the mechanics to differ in a way that maps onto the actual structural difference, not just "one group votes and the other group doesn't."
- Somebody in the room will figure out what I'm doing in the first ten minutes and say it out loud. Tell me how to handle that without killing the thing.
- Please make the comparison fair. If the lot body just makes a visibly dumber decision, the activity teaches the opposite of what I want. So give me a scoring scheme or a way to run it so the comparison means something, and be honest if there's no way to make it fair at n=9 per body.
- Nobody hears the words sortition or lottocracy until the debrief.

What I want back:
1. Materials list (hat, cards, timer, printed payoff cards, whatever else).
2. Exact rules for both bodies, including when the selection happens relative to the deliberation -- I have opinions about this and want you to argue for a specific ordering.
3. A timeline with minutes.
4. The debrief, mapped moment by moment onto the actual claims: what the blind break is supposed to buy you, what descriptive representation is supposed to buy you, what the competence objection actually claims and what it would take to settle it.
5. Predictably what goes wrong the first time I run it and how I recover.
6. The frank bit: which part of the lottocracy case this activity cannot reproduce, and that I'll have to supply myself in the debrief.

No citations, no reading list, no bibliography -- I don't need them and neither does anyone in the room. Just the runnable thing. Think carefully about the ordering of selection and deliberation, thats where I think the whole game actually lives.
