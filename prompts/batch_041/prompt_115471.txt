I write about science and ideas for a general readership, and I am at work on an essay about the step archaeologists take from things to thoughts. The claim I want to test is that the two things they are usually asked to recover — what people believed, and what institutions they lived under — are supported by evidence of opposite kinds, which is part of why arguments in the field so rarely settle. A belief tends to show up as excess: elaboration, redundancy, effort spent where no use is obvious, a pattern repeated well past need. An institution — a rule, an office, a prohibition, a claim of descent — tends to show up as regularity, and above all as systematic absence: the object that is never found where one would expect it. Institutions are read out of silences, and silences are precisely what a fragmentary and unevenly preserved record cannot be trusted to contain.

Give me an example — a single site, deposit or assemblage — in which one body of material facts is made to carry both sorts of claim at once, so that the belief reading and the institution reading are not two variants of a single story but make incompatible demands on the evidence. I would rather not have the cases my readers have already met, and if no real case does this cleanly, construct one and tell me what you changed. Set it out as: what was found, and in what context; the belief reading; the institution reading; the third possibility, that the pattern is an artefact of decay, scavenging, reuse or excavation (what archaeologists call equifinality); what each reading assumes that the objects themselves do not show; how much of each is borrowed from an analogy with a living society; and what further find would decide between them — or an argument that nothing could, because the two readings are underdetermined by the record in the same way. Close with the general point: what does the material record license about belief that it does not license about institutions, and the reverse. Plain language throughout; my readers are not archaeologists, and neither am I.
