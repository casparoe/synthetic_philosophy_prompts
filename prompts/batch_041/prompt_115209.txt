I sit on the investment committee of a midsize regional foundation -- we fund things in tranches, and every quarter we have to decide wether to release the next tranche, extend, or kill a grant. It's about six decisions a year, six figures each, and the meeting is 90 minutes. Nobody on the committee is an economist, and the program officer who proposed the original grant is always in the room, which I'm starting to think is part of the problem.

We keep doing the thing where we've put in $400k and the next tranche is $150k and someone says "we can't walk away now, we'd lose everything we've put in." I want a procedure we can actually run in the meeting, not a lecture. It should be grounded in the dollar auction and the escalation-of-commitment literature but usable by people who've never heard of either.

Give me: the questions to ask in order, what counts as a good enough answer to each, where the procedure just stops with a no, and where it has to hand the decision to the committee because it's a values question and not a fact question.

I want it to survive the stuff that makes these decisions hard, not paper over them:
- the difference between "we've already spent it" (irrelevant) and what the past spending has told us (not irrelevant) -- don't let the procedure confuse the two
- the case where the next increment is genuinely cheap relative to what finishing buys us (the half-built bridge, the asset that's worth nothing unless it's completed)
- the dollar-auction bit specifically: in that game the person who's trapped isn't the leader, it's the second-highest bidder, the one who has to pay and gets nothing. So the procedure should make us ask who in our situation is the second-highest bidder, and who's the auctioneer -- who actually benefits from us staying in.
- and the ugly one: sometimes continuing really is correct, and the procedure should be able to say so, otherwise people will just ignore it.

Run it on two real-ish cases: a digital records project that's 70% done and whose vendor has walked, and a cohort-based fellowship where the first two cohorts underperformed but the third looks different. And tell me where the procedure will misfire and what it can't decide. One of the two should come out in a way the committee won't like -- don't sand off the edges.
