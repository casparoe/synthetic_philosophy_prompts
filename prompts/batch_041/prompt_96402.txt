I'm a novelist, about four chapters into a book about Émilie du Châtelet — the Lunéville stretch at the end, 1749, pregnant at 42, finishing the Newton translation and scribbling what turns into her little book on happiness. I've read the Discourse on Happiness in translation twice now and I still cant decide what to do with it.

I'd like you to act as a historian of early-modern philosophy who has actually spent real time with her writing and with the French moralists she's in conversation with, and who is also willing to think like a novelist about what makes a character's inner life legible on the page. Think carefully, and please don't just hand me a survey of her views — I need a decision.

Here's the ask. Start with a messy brainstorm: list every genuinely open or contested question you can think of about her account of happiness — not "what did she say" but "where is it unclear, internally strained, or disputed, and where would the answer change how a reader takes her." Then rank the top three or four for me and defend the ranking.

What the ranking is for: I have maybe six weeks of reading before I draft the Lunévile chapters, and I can realistically hold three or four interpretive commitments in my head while writing. So the criteria I care about are (a) how much the answer would change what she does and says in a scene — her voice, her motive, what she's arguing with herself about; (b) how much of the rest of her thought hangs on it; (c) whether I could even tell from the text that I'd got it wrong.

Some of the tensions I keep tripping over, to show you the kind of thing I mean — take these as my hunches, not as claims about her:

- She's Leibnizian enough that everything happens for a sufficient reason and this is the best of all possible worlds, and yet the happiness book is full of advice about deliberately managing your passions and your illusions, as if you could just choose. Is there a real account of self-fashioning in there, or is she quietly changing the subject?
- Illusions. She seems to think happiness needs them. Which kind, and how does the woman who prizes clarity in physics say that with a straight face?
- It's semi-autobiographical — Saint-Lambert, Voltaire, being 42, an ending she didn't know she was writing. Does knowing that change what the arguments are, or is that a trap for novelists specifically?
- The advice to women. Is it a separate argument, an application of a general one, or an accommodation to a position she doesn't actually hold?

Two ground rules. No citations, no reading lists, no page numbers — I can't use them and I don't want you guessing at them. And work from what you actually know; if you're not sure whether she really says something, say so rather than asserting it. I'd much rather have "I'm unsure, but here's why it matters" than a confident sentence I find out later is wrong.

Finish with which of the candidate questions is most likely to be a red herring for a novelist, and what would move it up or down your ranking.
