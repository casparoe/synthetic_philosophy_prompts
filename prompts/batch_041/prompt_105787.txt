I'm a software engineer with no formal philosophy background, and I keep running into the claim that the meaning of a word or an internal representation isn't fixed by anything inside the speaker's head. Before I read any more arguments for one side or the other, I want the specification: what must any adequate account of what a term means deliver? Give me the desiderata — the cases it has to get right, the distinctions it can't collapse (between what a term means and what the speaker thinks it means, say, or between a term being empty and being meaningless), and the facts about learning, translation, and communication it can't contradict — with the consideration behind each. I don't need a tour of the positions or pointers to further reading; I want the requirements, plus your judgment on which are non-negotiable and which are just the preferences of one camp, and on which pairs look jointly unsatisfiable, since that is the part I care about most. The purpose I need this for is narrow: I want to be able to say when a system's representation is simply wrong about the world, as opposed to being put to a different use. To keep the list honest, run it against three cases: (a) two people with identical internal states whose ancestors handled different substances; (b) two people whose worlds differ only in a fact no one could ever observe; (c) two identical trained models, one trained on data describing one world and one on data describing another. Tell me which of these a theory has to accommodate and which it may treat as negotiable, and say which single requirement eliminates the most otherwise plausible accounts.
