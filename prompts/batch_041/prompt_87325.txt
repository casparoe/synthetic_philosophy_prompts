Movie night with my film club next month is Groundhog Day, and I keep circling why it bugs me philosophically, because the obvious read is eternal recurrence plus self-improvement and I think that's the boring one. What I keep noticing is that the loop is basically a machine for deleting moral luck: Phil gets to run every version of himself — suicide, robbery, seduction, piano — and the slate wipes clean each morning, so there's nothing left but trying. Take Nagel's four kinds (constitutive, circumstantial, causal, resultant) and ask which ones the loop actually removes. My hunch is it kills only resultant luck and leaves circumstantial luck totally intact, which makes the movie an accidental natural experiment on Nagel's taxonomy, and the result comes out badly for anyone who thinks outcome-luck is the deep problem. But steelman the parts I'm skipping: is Phil's decency still his if it took ten thousand retries? Does luck average out over an ensemble of trials the way it does in a long-run frequency, or does it compound instead? Rita gets all the benefit of that cultivation and never knows it — what do we even call that? And where does the film resist the reading, given the loop only breaks after one more lucky break? I want the honest version: which scenes the lens actually illuminates, where it distorts the movie, and whether Groundhog Day gives the moral luck literature anything the usual truck-driver and Gauguin cases don't already have.
