Okay so I'm a novelist — literary fiction, not philosphy — and I'm working on a book set in a world where it's pretty clear humanity is winding down. Not a bang, more the slow version: no kids being born, everyone knows the deal. And I keep coming back to this Scheffler thing, the afterlife conjecture, from his Death and the Afterlife book (the Tanner Lectures, I think — I read it a couple years ago and it's been rattling around in my head since). His claim, as I understand it, is that a lot of what makes our projects and loves feel worth doing depends on the assumption that human life will go on long after we're dead. Not personal immortality — the collective afterlife, the ongoing human story. He's got the asteroid scenario, and he also brings in the infertility case, basically the Children of Men setup. The point being that if we knew the human story was ending soon, we'd be devastated in a way that isn't just about the deaths. The value of the present would drain out of it.

Anyway, I rewatched Children of Men last night (the Cuaron film, not the P.D. James novel, though I've read that too, and I know Scheffler himself nods to the novel, so there's a weird loop here) and now I'm confused, in a good way. Because the film seems like it should be the perfect illustration of Scheffler — twenty years of infertility, the youngest person on earth dies at eighteen in the opening and it's broadcast and mourned by everyone, everything is decaying and brutal and nobody seems to believe in much. But then it also shows people still loving each other, still sacrificing, still fighting for something. Theo's whole arc is him going from numbed-out bureaucrat to someone who'll die for Kee and her baby. And that famous scene where the fighting stops when the baby cries — soldiers and refugees both just freeze. Is that a Scheffler moment or a refutation of him? Is the film saying meaning survives even without a future, or is it saying Kee's baby is precisely the restoration of the future, so the film actually vindicates Scheffler by showing how meaning floods back the moment a future becomes possible again?

What I want from you: read the film through Scheffler's afterlife conjecture. Where does it dramatize his thesis — I'm thinking the opening, the public mourning for the youngest person, the general anomie, the way the state has basically become a machine for managing decline. Where does the film push against him — the tenderness between people, Jasper's house, Theo's sacrifice, the ceasefire. And which is it doing, ultimately? Does the film think Scheffler is right, or does it offer a counterexample? I want a reading that takes both the film and the philosophy seriously — don't just use the film as a PowerPoint for the book, and don't bend Scheffler to fit the movie. If the film gets something right that the argument misses, say so. If the film cheats (the baby is a bit of a deus ex machina, maybe), say that too.

One more thing I'm chewing on: Scheffler says there's this weird asymmetry where our own survival matters less to us than the survival of the human race. Theo dies so the baby can live. Is that the film endorsing the asymmetry? Or is it just that he loves Kee and the kid, which is a different thing entirely? Untangle that if you can.

Also be honest about where the reading breaks down. If there are scenes that don't fit, tell me. I'm writing fiction, not a dissertation, so I'd rather have the messy truth than a tidy thesis.
