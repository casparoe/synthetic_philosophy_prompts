I cover science and ideas for a magazine, and I'm writing about the foundations of computability. Over the past few months I've interviewed two camps who have been arguing past each other about the Church–Turing thesis, and I want to publish the document they should have produced together. The first camp — the conceptualists — treats the thesis as a piece of conceptual analysis: it records what we mean when we call a procedure 'effective,' its warrant is the convergence of every serious attempt to make that notion precise, and it is neither provable nor refutable by experiment, because any candidate counterexample would itself have to be described by an effective procedure before we could accept it as a counterexample. The second camp — the empiricists — treats the thesis as a contingent empirical hypothesis about the physical world, on a par with a law of nature: if physics permits a process that computes a function no Turing machine can compute, the thesis is false, and no amount of analysis of our concepts can rescue it. Both sides agree that the mutual equivalence of the standard formalisms — Turing machines, the lambda calculus, recursive functions, and the rest — is a theorem and not in dispute; the fight is over what the thesis is a thesis about, and over what would count as settling it.

I'd like the joint statement these two could sign: (1) a numbered list of claims both accept, including about the equivalence results, about what the thesis does and does not assert, and about what each side concedes is a genuine strength in the other's position; (2) the precise statement of their disagreement, written so that each side would accept the wording of its own position, with the residual difference isolated as narrowly as possible; (3) the cruxes — the sub-questions such that settling one would move at least one side — and, for each crux, the case, argument, or experiment both sides agree would bear on it, with each side's prediction recorded in advance; and (4) an explicit note on which parts of the dispute, if any, are verbal, turning on whether the name 'the Church–Turing thesis' picks out a claim about human calculability, about all physically realizable processes, or about a stipulated class of formal systems.

Please handle two specific proposals that researchers have put to me, since I want the statement to be concrete: an accelerating machine that performs its first step in half a second, its second in a quarter, and so on, completing infinitely many steps within one second and printing an answer at the end of it; and an analog device whose answer is carried by the exact value of a continuous physical parameter, on the assumption that such a parameter can hold unbounded information. For each, I want the description both camps accept, what would have to be shown for it to count as a counterexample, each camp's verdict, and whether each camp regards the other's verdict as even coherent — in particular, whether the empiricist can ever confirm that such a device computes what it is claimed to compute, given that its answers cannot be independently checked.

Keep it disciplined: no reading list, and separate throughout what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who work on this, rather than presenting a contested view as the consensus.
