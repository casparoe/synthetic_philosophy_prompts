I write fiction, and I have run up against a distinction I cannot name. In the novel I am finishing, a housekeeper is never once prevented from doing anything: she keeps her own hours, her own money, her own friends. Yet she has arranged her whole life around the temper of the man whose house it is — she says nothing when he has been drinking, leaves before he tires, and would not contradict him before guests. A second character, a soldier's wife in the same town, is told by law where she may live, how much of her husband's pay she may keep, and when she may travel; she also has a voice in the assembly that writes those laws. I am sure the housekeeper is the less free of the two, and sure that this has nothing to do with how many of her options are blocked. But I cannot say what the difference is. Freedom as nobody standing in your way does not capture it, and freedom as governing yourself seems a different and far larger demand. Is there an established name for the unfreedom that consists in being at another's mercy rather than in being obstructed, and what does it add to those two ideas? I want its edges, not only its label. If the man is kind and would never interfere, is she still unfree? If the power belongs to no person but to a custom nobody enforces, does it still count? If she does not feel it, does that matter? And is this one thing, or am I conflating several?
