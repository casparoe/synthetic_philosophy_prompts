Medicine appears to run on two different concepts of disease at once, and I want to know what each of them presupposes. On one side, an asymptomatic adult whose blood pressure sits just over the current guideline cutoff, or whose fasting glucose falls into the "prediabetes" band, or whose bone density slips below a statistical threshold, is told they have hypertension, prediabetes, or osteopenia, and is offered treatment. No symptom, no suffering, and no interference with daily life is required; it suffices that a measured value deviates from a population norm or a target range. Thresholds of this sort have repeatedly been revised downward, so that readings once dismissed as merely high-normal now count as disease. On the other side, psychiatry insists that a condition counts as a disorder only when the symptoms cause significant distress or impairment in social, occupational, or other important areas of functioning; an unusual pattern that bothers no one and disrupts nothing is not a disorder, however statistically abnormal it may be.

What concept of disease does each of these practices presuppose? Does the first commit to a broadly naturalist view, on which disease is a deviation from normal functioning or from a species-typical range, discoverable by measurement and independent of anyone's values? Does the second commit to a normativist view, on which calling something a disease is partly a value judgment about harm and the ends of living? Or can a single analysis — say, one that treats a disease as a biological dysfunction that is also harmful to the person — accommodate both, with the psychiatric criterion simply making the harm requirement explicit? Reconstruct the implicit theory that each practice commits to, distinguish what it strictly presupposes from what it merely fits comfortably with, and identify where the overall practice is underdetermined, so that several rival theories of disease would license it. Then tell me what would have to change in clinical practice if we adopted one uniform concept: either a thoroughgoing naturalism, on which the asymptomatic readings are diseases in their own right and the psychiatric distress requirement is a mere practical add-on, or a thoroughgoing normativism, on which the asymptomatic readings are at most risk factors until they actually harm someone.

Finally, say what would change your mind: which argument or piece of evidence would lead you to a different verdict about which concept the practice really presupposes, and where your reasoning is most likely to fail.
