A philosophy department with a strong logic group is creating a small research cluster on the foundations of set theory, and the theme its chair has proposed is the axiom of choice. Over the next several years the department expects to make about three hires in this area and to host a steady stream of visitors, and it wants the cluster's research agenda fixed in advance rather than left to whoever happens to be hired. You have been asked for a memo: which open questions about the axiom of choice should anchor the cluster, in what order, and why?

Treat the following as candidates to keep, drop, or supplement, glossing each in your own words as needed. (a) The distinction between justifying an axiom intrinsically—by its self-evidence or conceptual role—and justifying it extrinsically, by its consequences and unifying power, and the question whether the axiom of choice has either kind of justification. (b) The search for a new axiom that would both settle the continuum hypothesis and be independently compelling, and whether the status of the axiom of choice bears on that search. (c) The competition among full choice, weaker principles such as countable choice (choice for countable families) and dependent choice (the principle behind recursive constructions of sequences), and the axiom of determinacy (the claim that every infinite two-player game of perfect information on the natural numbers is determined), together with the question of what would make one of these the right choice principle. (d) The reverse-mathematical project of determining exactly which choice principles are needed for which theorems of ordinary mathematics. (e) The set-theoretic multiverse view—that there are many equally legitimate set-theoretic universes rather than one intended universe—and whether it deflates the question of the axiom's truth. (f) The objection that the axiom's non-constructive consequences, such as the existence of non-measurable sets and the Banach–Tarski decomposition, count against it.

For each candidate you retain, explain why it is upstream of other work (inside or outside pure set theory), how tractable it is for a philosopher with a set-theory background over a few years, whether it is comparatively neglected, and what argument or result would move it up or down your ranking. Where two candidates compete for the same person's time, say which you would sacrifice. The department also hopes the cluster will eventually matter beyond pure set theory—to analysis, algebra, or the philosophy of mathematics at large—so indicate how that ambition reshapes the ordering. I care less about the final list than about the criteria you use, the trade-offs among them, and the conditions under which the ranking would change.
