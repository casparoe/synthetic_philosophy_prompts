Before I retired I spent thirty-five years in observational astronomy, most of it on the spectroscopy of very distant objects, and since then I have been teaching myself philosophy — epistemology, mostly, and the parts of ethics that concern decision-making under uncertainty. I have been thinking about the Fermi paradox not as an astronomy problem but as a problem in the logic of evidence, and I would like you to write me a short story that dramatizes the point rather than stating it.

Here is the idea I keep circling. Every civilization that looks up will see an empty sky, whatever the galaxy actually contains, because what a civilization is able to detect is a fact about its instruments and not about whether the others exist; silence, on this view, is not a datum about the universe but a datum about the observer. If that is right, then the familiar question of where everyone is has no answer of the kind it is usually asked for, and worse, the two hypotheses that seem most opposed — that we are alone, and that we are surrounded — become evidentially indistinguishable, since both predict precisely the silence we observe. The only thing that could ever break the tie would be a single object or event that is not silence; and precisely because it would be the only one of its kind, no statistics could be brought to bear on it, and its interpretation would rest entirely on what we chose to assume.

I want a story built on that. Give me one such datum: a single thing found, seen, or received, genuinely ambiguous between an artifact and a natural object, between a message and a monument and a mirror, and impossible to replicate or compare against anything else. Put me inside the head of whoever must decide what it is and what to do about it, knowing that the decision cannot be settled by further evidence because there will never again be evidence of this kind. Let the story carry the weight of acting under that underdetermination — what it is like to commit to a reading of a unique thing when the reading will determine whether a civilization broadcasts, hides, expands, or waits. I am not interested in the standard resolutions: no dark-forest predators, no great filter, no first-contact invasion, and no ending in which the object turns out to be one of the familiar things. I am interested in the ambiguity itself, and in the people who have to live inside it.

Length: a proper short story, a few thousand words, with one viewpoint character and a single decisive moment. You may invent whatever physics or biology you need; the story should not depend on any named theory or on anything the reader is expected to have read. Make the idea vivid through the character's situation rather than through exposition.
