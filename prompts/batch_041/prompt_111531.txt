I am a software engineer with no formal background in philosophy or biology, and what I need from you is a specification rather than an argument. A colleague explains a great deal of human behaviour — charitable giving, luxury goods, punishing hobbies, dress codes, the ornamentation of religious ritual — by saying that it is costly signalling, and when I ask what would count against the explanation, he tells me that the cost is what keeps the signal honest. I suspect the story is doing less work than it appears to, but I cannot say what a good version of it would have to do. Please write me the desiderata for an adequate theory of honest signalling and the handicap principle.

Purpose: the theory must be good enough for me to take a proposed signalling explanation of some particular trait and decide whether it is a substantive hypothesis or a redescription of the fact that the trait is expensive. Phrase each desideratum as a test I could put to such an explanation.

I want the full list — the phenomena the theory must explain, the cases it must get right, the distinctions it must not collapse, and the empirical results it must be consistent with. For each item, give the case or consideration that makes it a requirement rather than a preference, and tell me which of the things people commonly demand of a theory of signalling are really commitments of one camp rather than genuine requirements. Among the candidates I have collected, please sort the genuine from the spurious, and add whatever I have missed:

- the difference between a signal and a cue — between a trait shaped by selection for its effect on receivers and a trait from which receivers merely happen to extract information;
- honest signals that are cheap, because the signal is physically or physiologically tied to the quality it advertises and therefore cannot be faked by a low-quality signaller;
- honesty where signaller and receiver have no conflict of interest at all, and honesty where their interests conflict only partly;
- traits whose only apparent function is to make some underlying quality easier for a receiver to assess, if such traits exist;
- signals kept honest not by what they cost the signaller to produce but by what the receiver does to a signaller who is caught cheating;
- deception, mimicry, and the exploitation of receivers' pre-existing preferences — all of which plainly occur;
- the fact that one and the same trait is often displayed to several audiences at once (mates, rivals, predators, parents) whose interests differ;
- the demand that the theory be falsifiable, given the suspicion that almost any trait can be redescribed as a handicap after the fact;
- the fact that the textbook illustrations have been challenged empirically;
- the analogy with conspicuous consumption and the other human cases my colleague cites.

Then flag any pair or larger subset of these requirements that cannot be jointly satisfied, and say plainly which single requirement does most to eliminate candidates — that matters more to me than the scorecard. If the tensions are resolved only by a pluralist theory, with different mechanisms for different signal types, say so and say what that costs.

Finally, and briefly: how do the original handicap formulation, its game-theoretic formalisation, and an index-signal account fare against the list? Keep this short; the list is the deliverable.
