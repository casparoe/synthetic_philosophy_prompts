I'm putting together a short undergrad module on the ethics of eating animals and I've decided to teach E.B. White's Charlotte's Web (1952) as the set text -- you know the one, the runt pig Wilbur, the barn spider Charlotte who spins praise into her web to stop the farmer killing him. Everyone in the room already knows the story so please don't retell the plot. What I want is a proper reading of the book through the lens of meat consumption and wild animal suffering, and I want you to be honest where the lens doesn't fit and you have to push back on me.

Bits I'd like you to actually deal with:

- The farm is basically a machine for turning grain and slops into flesh, and Charlotte's plan doesn't touch the machine at all, it just makes one unit of it too famous to kill. So is the book offering a theory of moral standing, or a theory of publicity? Is Wilbur saved, or just reclassified as an asset? It's surely not an accident that his rescue arrives as a prize at a county fair.

- Charlotte is a predator -- she eats the flies that blunder into her web, and the book is completely untroubled by it. Does White have a consistent rule about who may be killed and who may do the killing, or does he just exempt the characters he likes? Try to state the rule the book would have to be running on, in it's own terms, and then say whether it survives contact with a hard case.

- The barn is a half-wild space: rats scavenging, spiders hunting, and a great deal of death that the novel treats as simply natural -- and in Charlotte's case as good, since she dies on schedule, satisfied, having done what she set out to do. Set that against how the book handles slaughter. Is the novel committed to the idea that natural death is the acceptable death, the one that doesn't need justifying? This is the bit I most want, because the wild-animal-suffering people would say natural deaths are often the worst deaths going, and I want to know whether the book's whole emotional economy depends on not noticing that.

- The animals aren't equal on the page either. The pig and the spider get inner lives; the sheep, geese and cows are mostly chorus. Is there a species hierarchy at work, and does it track anything defensible, or just cuteness and usefulness to the plot?

- And Fern, the girl who saves Wilbur in the first place, grows up and drifts away, and the book seems pretty relaxed about it -- concern for the animals is coded as a stage you pass through. Is the novel quietly telling children that caring about this is for kids?

Then finish by saying what the novel actually adds that the philosophy papers don't -- White can write, and there's something in the book's refusal to argue its case that might be worth more than an argument -- and flag where you think my reading is forcing the text. Definately don't let it turn into a tract, and don't flatten the book into an illustration of a position.

No bibliography or reading list, I know the literature; I want your reading. Essay length, call it 1000-1300 words, written in British English throughout.
