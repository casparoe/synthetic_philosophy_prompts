My reading group has been going in circles for months on whether omissions can be causes, and I'm starting to think we're not even having the same argument. The causalists keep saying of course they can -- the gardener who forgets to water the plant causes its death the same way the frost does, and if you'd say the lifeguard who never swam out caused the drowning then you're already on board. The other side keeps saying no: nothing happened, nothing did anything, the plant died from lack of water, and that's just the absence of a cause, not a cause of its own special kind. And nobody in the room disputes a single fact about any of the cases. So what actually is this? My working theory is that the two camps are quietly running on different defaults -- for the causalists the baseline is what would have happened otherwise, for the skeptics only things that exist can do anything -- and that the omission cases that feel strongest (the ones where someone had a role, a duty, a job) are exactly the ones where some implicit norm is sneaking in and doing the real work, which would mean half the fight is about expectations and blame rather than causation. Is that right, or is there a genuine disagreement about the world underneath that I'm missing? And either way, what would count as progress here -- a distinction, a case both sides would have to accept, a formal result -- or is this the kind of thing that just can't be settled and we should stop trying? It's not idle for me: I keep hitting the same tangle when I ask whether an AI system that could have flagged something but didn't acutally "caused" the harm.
