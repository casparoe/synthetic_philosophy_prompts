I direct a small municipal arts centre in a former mining village, and in three weeks I have to give the address at its reopening after a two-year renovation. The centre exists because of a bequest from Nell Rennie, who trained as a painter, abandoned it in her thirties, and spent the next forty years running the village choir. Her will requires that the building stay free to enter and that the reopening address speak to the whole village rather than only to people who already go to galleries. The flagship commission in the renovated building is a long, slow, abstract video installation by a young artist; the curator is devoted to it, a good part of the village finds it baffling, and the borough arts officer, who is in the audience and whose budget decision matters, will want to hear a case for public support of this sort of thing. Nell's family will be there, the choir will sing at the end, and a visiting professor of aesthetics who works on Tolstoy will notice immediately if I soften him.

I want the speech built on Tolstoy's account of art in *What Is Art?*: art as the transmission of feeling from one person to another rather than the production of beauty or pleasure; the conditions that make a feeling infectious, namely individuality, clarity, and sincerity; the insistence that the best art is accessible to everyone; and his moral test of good art as the feeling of brotherhood and universal love. That account should be the spine of the speech, not its decoration. It should determine what I can praise, what I have to concede, and where the speech turns.

Two difficulties need to be faced rather than smoothed over. First, Tolstoy's formal criterion, the strength of the infection, and his moral criterion, the quality of the feeling transmitted, come apart in this room: the installation genuinely moves the curator and the scholar and leaves much of the village cold, while the choir does the reverse and may be merely sentimental. Second, the infection model seems to presuppose a single maker who has felt something and passes it on, which sits badly with a choir, or with any work whose feeling is generated in the audience rather than transmitted from an author. I do not want the speech to pretend that Tolstoy would have blessed the centre. I want it to honour Nell, who is Tolstoyan in the sincerity sense, and to deal honestly with the installation, which is not, without sliding into the view that whatever anyone happens to feel is art.

Write the address: six to eight minutes, roughly 850 to 1,000 words, in a spoken register that neither condescends to the scholar nor loses the schoolchildren, and with closing lines that hand off to the choir without sentimentality. Paraphrase Tolstoy throughout; do not quote him, and do not name editions, translators, or page numbers. After the address, add a note to me of no more than 120 words naming the one Tolstoyan point the speech deliberately leaves unresolved and saying why leaving it unresolved is the right call for this room. End with a final line beginning "Summary:" that states in one sentence what the address asks the audience to accept about Tolstoy's account of art.
