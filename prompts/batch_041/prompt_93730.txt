I'm in a seminar on Kierkegaard and we just read the review of Two Ages, the 1846 one where he goes after the present age and its levelling (written right around the time the Corsair crowd was drawing him in caricature every week). I keep trying to work out what he'd actually say about a concrete case, and I keep going in circles, because I think I can read several different rules off the things he praises and condemns and I can't tell which one he's really running. Heres the track record I've got, all of it as my reading rather than anything he says outright: (1) the daily paper that reports on everything and everyone — poison, because it makes the reader omniscient and passive, everything becomes interesting and nothing gets done; (2) his own books brought out under fake names, Johannes Climacus and the rest — he defends this as necessary, indirect communication, so the reader isn't simply told what to think and has to make the decision himself; (3) the satirical weekly that mocked him personally — he didn't want it shut down, and he seems to have treated being made a laughing stock as closer to a promotion than an injury; (4) anonymous polemical journalism, where a writer savages named people and never has to answer for it — that he's contemptuous of; (5) a crowd or mob jeering at someone for a decision of conscience — the crowd is untruth, the single individual stands higher; (6) invoking public opinion as the standard for a serious decision, like a pastor who asks himself what people will think — also condemned, since the public is a phantom and nobody in particular; (7) and then the awkward fact that he himself used the press relentlessly, a flood of books, newspaper polemics, and thought it was fine. So at least four rules are available: the vice is unaccountable anonymity; or the vice is addressing the abstract public at all, so a mass audience corrupts regardless of names; or the vice is that the press converts passion into reflection, and the real test is whether anything gets done; or the press is just a tool and everything turns on the author's aim. Rules one and two make (2) and (7) embarrassing, rule three makes (3) embarrassing, so I know none of them is the whole story.

Anyway, the case I actually want a verdict on. A junior doctor at a large public hospital tries the internal reporting route twice and gets stonewalled both times, so she starts an anonymous account under a pseudonym and posts internal documents showing a pattern of negligence in one department. It takes off: 200k followers, the health ministry opens an inquiry, two department managers resign. But by month three she's posting speculation about which individual doctors were responsible, including people she only knows by rumor, and she tells a journalist she's enjoying the attention and is thinking about writing a book. For the sake of argument assume she never puts her name to anything and never hands the account over. What do you think Kierkegaard's verdict would be? I want the closest precedents from the list above and what each one on its own implies, the rule or rules he seems to be running, where those rules come apart on this case, your best guess with reasons, and then the further case that would actually settle it — i.e. what I should change to make the rival rules give different answers. And if the honest answer is that his track record genuinely underdetermines it, say that and tell me which principle he'd be buying with each verdict. One thing I'm especially curious about is whether he gets to have it both ways with the pseudonyms, or whether the anonymous account is just his own move one century later, which would be a bit embarrasing for him.
