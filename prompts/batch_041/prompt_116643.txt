My friend in our game theory reading group has this stock move whenever someone complains that common knowledge of rationality never actually holds — there's always some grain of doubt, some chance the other player hasn't run the reasoning, some payoff nobody's quite sure about. His line is that its the same as frictionless planes. Physics runs on point masses and zero friction, nobody says mechanics has failed because no surface is perfectly smooth, the idealization earns its keep and the departures are noise you add back in later. So he says the fact that common knowledge of rationality is basically never satisfied definately tells us nothing about whether the backward induction and equilibrium machinery is any good at explaining real play, and we shouldn't be spending our time on models that relax it.

Is he right? Start with the correspondence table — what plays the role of the frictionless surface, what plays the role of the point mass, what's the limit you're supposed to take as the neglected quantity goes to zero, and what's the analogue of the measurement that tells you friction is small enough to ignore. Then where the mapping breaks, and which of those breaks actually matter for the argument he's using it for. My suspicion is it turns on how the idealization behaves when you relax it: a bit of friction perturbs the trajectory a bit, whereas a bit of doubt in a finitely repeated prisoner's dilemma or a centipede game can flip the prediction entirely. Is that the real fault line, or am I missing something? Tell me which of his conclusions survive once the disanalogies are on the table, and if the frictionless-plane picture is the wrong one for what these models are actually doing, what we should be comparing them to instead.
