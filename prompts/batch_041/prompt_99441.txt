At a fortnightly reading group on decision theory, two members — I shall call them Nadia and Rafi — fell into an argument that I could not settle between them, and I would like it adjudicated move by move rather than disposed of with a single verdict.

Nadia's position, as she set it out, was this. Evidential decision theory holds that an agent should perform the act that is the best news about how the world turns out, so that a choice can be rational because of what it is evidence for and not only because of what it causes. Now suppose the world is large: either space is infinite, or our best cosmology gives a substantial probability to a multiverse, so that there exist very many agents whose deliberations are correlated with mine — agents who share my evidence and reason as I do, because the same underlying regularities that make me reason as I do make them reason as I do. When I decide whether to give away a tenth of my income, my choice is evidence about what all of those correlated agents do. If there are vastly more of them than there are of me, the evidential payoff of giving is not one donation's worth but a large multiple of it; she called that multiple the multiplicity factor. Her conclusion was that I may have decisive reason to cooperate with agents I can never causally affect, and that ordinary prudence is a poor guide to what I have most reason to do. To make it concrete she offered a scaling illustration: if the multiplicity factor is a thousand, and the act in question costs me one unit while conferring two units of good on each agent who performs it, then the news carried by my performing it is news of roughly two thousand units of good rather than of two.

Rafi's reply came in three stages, and Nadia answered the first two.

(1) He said the correlation is not selective. If my act is evidence of what similarly reasoning agents do, it is evidence about all of them, including those whose ends I deplore; nothing in the argument restricts the reference class to the benevolent. The evidential payoff of my cooperating is therefore a sum over agents of mixed character whose sign is not guaranteed to be positive, and Nadia has quietly assumed that the correlated many are the ones on her side.

(2) She replied that this is an empirical point and not a fatal one. The correlated agents are not a random sample of all agents; they are those who share my evidence and my manner of reasoning, and reasoning as I do is itself correlated with valuing what I value. The sum is accordingly more likely than not to be positive, and even a modest probability of a very large aggregate good can swamp the local payoff. At most, she said, Rafi has shown that the argument needs an extra premise — that the correlated class is predominantly benevolent — and not that it fails.

(3) He then said that the argument is hostage to the reference class problem. There is no fact of the matter about which of my properties delimit the class of agents correlated with me: my evidence, my psychology, my source code, my species and my position in the causal order each give a different class, and different but equally defensible choices yield wildly different multiplicity factors with no principled way of choosing among them. The multiplicity factor is thus a free parameter, and an argument with a free parameter can be tuned to any conclusion one likes.

(4) She answered that the objection, if good, applies to every evidentialist argument, the standard Newcomb cases included, and would refute evidential decision theory outright rather than this application of it. She added that the argument does not need a precise number; it needs the ordering of the acts to be stable across the plausible range of reference classes, and there are cases in which it is, every admissible class making cooperation the better news.

(5) He then said that the argument proves too much. If the evidential consequences of my act include what correlated agents do, consistency requires that the evidential consequences of my holding a belief, or of my adopting a decision theory, be counted in the same way; the argument would then license self-modification for the sake of distant copies — choosing a belief, a theory or a disposition on the ground that many correlated agents will thereby fare better — which is either incoherent or commits me to believing whatever it would be best for the many to believe, including things for which I have no evidence. He added that if space really is infinite the engine stalls altogether: with infinitely many correlated agents there is no finite multiple of anything, comparing acts would require a way of ranking infinite aggregates that nobody possesses, and the multiplicity factor is either infinite, in which case all acts carry the same news and the argument yields no verdict, or undefined.

(6) She conceded that the infinite case is unresolved, but said the argument needs only that the world be large, not that it be infinite; a very large finite multiplicity carries the point, and the fact that our consequentialist arithmetic breaks down at infinity is an argument for doing infinite ethics rather than against her conclusion. She also said that the extension to beliefs mistakes the practical question for the epistemic one: a belief's value as news depends on its being true, so the evidentialist about action is not thereby licensed to believe whatever it would be convenient for distant copies to believe.

That is as far as they got. Please adjudicate their exchange point by point, rating each of the six moves on a scale from clearly mistaken to clearly right and justifying each rating briefly. Then say which premise the whole dispute turns on, and whether it is the sort of premise that could be settled by argument or only by cosmology; who has the better of it overall, and about what specifically; and what, if anything, both of them have missed. I should add that neither was working from a text, and I am not asking you to place their positions in the literature — I want the reasoning assessed as they gave it. I would also like you to be explicit about which standard of rationality you are helping yourself to when you rule on the first move, since I suspect the verdict there depends on it. Please write your answer in British English.
