I teach a 90-minute upper-level seminar in philosophy of mind and artificial intelligence, with sixteen students, and I want to run an activity that makes them feel the force of Turing's objection from extrasensory perception — the one where he worries that if telepathy is real, the imitation game is compromised because the interrogator may be receiving information through a channel the experiment was never designed to monitor. My students have read the imitation game and treat the ESP objection as a quirky footnote; I want them to experience it as a serious methodological problem. The setup I have in mind is this: they run a version of the imitation game in which an interrogator must decide which of two hidden respondents is the human and which is the machine, but I secretly plant a participant who can pass a small amount of information through a non-obvious channel — a pre-arranged cough, a pencil tap, a glance at a clock. The planted telepath is not there to cheat; the telepath is there to make visible an auxiliary assumption the test depends on. I want the group to discover that their judgment of intelligence is contaminated by a channel they did not know to control for, and then to have to design a protocol that would rule it out.

Please give me the full design as a numbered list of exactly five items, each a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion. Item one should specify the materials, roles, and rules, including how the telepath's channel works and what the interrogator is told. Item two should give a minute-by-minute timeline for the 90 minutes. Item three should describe the debrief, mapping what happened onto Turing's argument — the idea that the test's validity rests on an auxiliary assumption, that his response was to add a control rather than dismiss the phenomenon, and that the operationalist criterion is underdetermined if the channel is unknown. Item four should say what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle it. Item five should be frank about which feature of Turing's view the activity cannot reproduce and that I will have to supply in the debrief. Do not quote Turing or any other text; paraphrase the ideas in your own words, and do not ask me to consult sources.
