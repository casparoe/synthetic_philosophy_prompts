I am finishing a seminar on the philosophy of the social sciences, and I have been trying to decide where I stand on the dispute between methodological individualists and their critics. Rather than argue the general thesis, I have been keeping a list of explanatory claims and marking, by intuition, which ones I regard as genuine explanations and which as redescriptions. I would like you to identify the principle behind my marks, if there is one.

The list:

(a) The bank failed because the run on it was self-fulfilling: each depositor's withdrawal was rational given the belief that others would withdraw. Genuine. No one intended the outcome and no collective agent acted, but I take the explanation to be complete once the depositors' reasoning and the way their choices interlocked are given.

(b) The firm kept paying bribes because its competitors did and it expected to lose contracts otherwise. Genuine, even though the grammatical subject is a firm rather than a person.

(c) Inflation accelerated because expectations of inflation became unanchored. Genuine, and I do not think I need to know anything about any particular price-setter's psychology in order to accept it.

(d) The lake's perch have grown smaller over four decades because the largest fish are the ones the nets take. Genuine. This explains a change in the distribution of a trait across a population and says nothing about why any individual fish survived; the survivors were lucky.

(e) The institution of caste persisted because it served the interests of the dominant group. Not an explanation. It tells me who benefited, not why the arrangement continued; the benefit could be entirely incidental to its persistence.

(f) The dueling code declined because the state came to monopolize the use of force, which changed what an unanswered insult cost a man. Genuine.

(g) The commons were kept in good repair because the rules were long-standing and each user expected the others to keep to them. Genuine, I think, though it makes me uneasy; it feels like (e) with more steps.

(h) Capitalism required the crisis in order to restore profitability. Not an explanation, and I would say the same of any claim that an arrangement exists because it is functional for something.

(i) The senator voted for the bill because the donors who funded his campaign wanted it. Genuine.

My difficulty is this. (d) and (h) appear to have the same form, a trait or arrangement explained by its consequences, and I accept one while rejecting the other. (g) and (e) also look alike, and I am less confident there than I would like to be. The principle I keep reaching for is that a genuine explanation must cite a mechanism that produces the outcome, but I suspect this is either circular or trivially satisfied, since I cannot say what counts as a mechanism without already deciding the cases. I would like a principle that gets all nine verdicts. If the honest answer is that none does, tell me which verdicts have to be abandoned and what the simplest principle is that preserves the remainder. Please also say what that principle implies for two claims I have not decided on: that the custom of the open door survived because newcomers who broke it were ostracized, and that the profession became male-dominated because employers preferred to promote men, given that no one intended the aggregate outcome. Finally, if the principle turns on there being a mechanism, state precisely what would have to be shown for a claim like (e) to be repaired into an explanation rather than replaced by one.
