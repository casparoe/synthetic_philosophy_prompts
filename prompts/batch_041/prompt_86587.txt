I'm a PhD candidate in philosophy finishing a chapter on advance directives and the bindingness of promises across radical psychological change, and my reading group has stalled on Parfit's teletransporter. Every session we re-litigate whether the person who steps out at the far end is the person who stepped in, and we never reach the question I actually need answered: what the verdict is supposed to do for us — for the validity of the directive, for the contract, for the debt. I've come to think the productive move is to stop adjudicating between the rival answers for a moment and to specify, first, what any theory of what matters in survival would have to deliver in order to count as adequate. That specification is what I'd like you to write.

Give me the requirements list, each with the case or consideration that motivates it, and be explicit about which entries are genuine desiderata and which look to me like prejudices of the identity-based camp rather than demands any theory must meet. At a minimum I'd expect the list to cover: the branching or division cases, where two successors exist and the theory has to say what matters without simply declaring the scenario incoherent; whether the relation that matters admits of degrees — the gradual-replacement case is the obvious test, but I've also been pushing a variant in which the machine reproduces you with one specific psychological alteration (a value, a cherished memory) and a reversible variant in which the reconstructed person is scanned and transmitted back, so that a round trip is supposed to preserve you, and I want to know what the theory must say about the ninety-nine-percent and the sixty-percent cases; transitivity, since if what matters can hold in degrees between adjacent stages of a spectrum it is not obvious that it survives the whole chain; the requirement that ordinary survival come out as the central case rather than a lucky exception, and that the theory not make our ordinary change over decades either mysterious or trivial; what grounds the special concern we have for our future selves, and whether that concern is a rational requirement or merely a biological disposition, with the past–future asymmetry as the test; and the tie to responsibility and desert — who is bound by the earlier person's promise, who owes the debt, who answers for the crime, and whether an advance directive binds — because I need the theory to be good enough to settle those questions, which is the purpose I'm fixing for it. Add anything you think belongs that I've missed, and cut anything on my list that you judge not to be a real requirement.

Then tell me which pair or triple of these requirements you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied — my own hunch is that degree-talk and transitivity are in tension, and that the branching case puts pressure on whatever is supposed to ground the special concern, but I'd like your independent judgment — and which single requirement does the most work in excluding the most popular theories on offer. A numbered list with a short paragraph under each entry is fine; I care much more about the criteria and their motivating cases than about a scorecard of the contenders, so keep any pass over the existing views brief.
