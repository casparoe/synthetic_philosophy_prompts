I'm a science and ideas journalist at work on a feature about why certain scholarly disputes never seem to close, and I want your help diagnosing one of them rather than summarizing it. The dispute concerns the governance of shared resources. In one corner stands the tradition — Garrett Hardin's parable of the herders, Mancur Olson's logic of collective action — that treats the depletion of a common resource as the near-inevitable result of individually rational choices, so that the remedy has to come from outside the group: privatization or an enforcer. In the other stands the field-and-archive tradition associated with Elinor Ostrom, which keeps documenting communities that manage fisheries, forests, and irrigation systems across generations without either private property or a central authority, through locally made rules and mutual monitoring. Each side knows the other's evidence; the cases are old and widely circulated; and still the exchange shows no sign of resolving. The critics press that the successful cases may be a biased sample of survivors, while the formal theorists reply that the underlying incentive structure is untouched and that local rules are themselves institutions in need of explanation.

I want to know what kind of disagreement this actually is. Is it verbal — the two sides attaching different meanings to "rational," to "commons," or to "governance"? Is it two distinct questions being answered under a single heading — one about whether successful self-governance exists at all, the other about the conditions under which it remains stable — so that the combatants are not, strictly, contradicting each other? Is it a hidden empirical question that better data could settle? Or does it bottom out in a disagreement about human motivation, about whether to model people as self-interested by default, that neither side can argue the other out of? My own hunch, which I'd like you to test rather than accept, is the second: two questions under one name. To stress the diagnosis, apply it to a case I've constructed for the piece — a groundwater basin whose users are anonymous, mobile, and unable to observe one another or make binding commitments, though a cheap third-party monitor is available. If your diagnosis is right, would the two camps even disagree about this case, or would they simply be answering different questions about it?

Given that diagnosis, tell me what would count as progress — a distinction, a formal result, a decisive natural experiment, or a change of question — or argue that no progress should be expected, and why. I'd also value your honest read on whether this pattern is peculiar to collective action or a general feature of disputes in the social sciences. Write in American English, keep the whole thing under 800 words, and put your bottom-line diagnosis in the first two sentences so I can quote it.
