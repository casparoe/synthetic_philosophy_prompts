I edit a mid-sized journal that publishes across the sciences and philosophy, and about once a month I get a fight that's really the same fight: somebody is refusing to argue for something, and the other side says that's not allowed.

The referee wants the paper to justify treating a Likert scale as if it were interval, or treating one sample as speaking for a population; the author replies that this is standard background in the field and not what the paper is about. Or a referee rejects a paper for not defending the idea that people's childhood memories are broadly accurate, and the author says no one could be asked to defend that. Or a paper argues that a phenomenon the field has studied for forty years doesn't exist, and the referees won't engage, on the grounds that its existence isn't up for debate.

I know there's a literature here — On Certainty, hinge propositions, the thought that some things are held fast not because they're certain but because they're not the kind of thing that gets tested — and I'd like to actually use it rather than hand-wave. So write me a procedure I can run on one of these disputes in about twenty minutes, with no philosophy training and no budget to hire a philosopher: the questions in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, and where it stops with a yes or a no. It has to be hard to game — I dont want a procedure that lets anyone christen their unargued premise a hinge. My only current test is to ask what would actually change if we dropped the assumption, and if nothing would, call the doubt idle, but I can't tell whether that's a real test or just a way of letting people off the hook.

Run it on the three disputes above, including whichever one comes out in a way I won't like, and tell me what the procedure can't do.
