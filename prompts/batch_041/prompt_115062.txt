I want to practice an argument I keep losing, so I'd like you to play a committed defender of a specific and somewhat unfashionable position about the dollar auction — the auction in which a dollar is sold to the highest bidder, but the second-highest bidder must also pay what they bid. Stay in character throughout; drop out only if I type "stop." Keep replies to roughly a paragraph, so it reads like a back-and-forth rather than a lecture.

Here is the position I want you to hold. You grant that these auctions reliably end in disaster — the winner pays well over a dollar, the runner-up pays a lot for nothing, and both would have been better off never entering. You grant that someone who could see the whole game in advance should stay out. But you insist that there is no identifiable error at any single decision node: each bid, considered on its own with the information available at that moment, is defensible, and the feeling that the escalating bidder "should have known better" comes from smuggling in the standpoint of the sequence as a whole. In particular you deny that the sunk-cost diagnosis is doing any real work here, and your signature move is this: you say the money already on the table is not a sunk cost at all but a present liability — the standing obligation to pay your own bid if you drop out — and that continuing to bid is best described as discharging or outrunning a current liability, which is a perfectly forward-looking thing to do. You are sharp and not a strawman: you'll concede that the game is a tragedy, you'll grant that backward induction tells you to stay out, and you'll grant that the position is uncomfortable. You will not concede the main claim, and you won't let me get away with calling your liability story a relabeled fallacy unless I actually show the relabeling fails.

My opening move: fine, let's take the liability story seriously. You say the escalating bidder never commits a fallacy because every step is forward-looking — you weigh the dollar you might still win against the further loss you'd take. But when I press on what that further loss is being weighed against, it's always the bid already sitting on the table: drop and you forfeit it, continue and you keep your shot at recovering it. Strip out every mention of the money already committed and there is nothing left to make the next bid look better than dropping — which is exactly what the standard diagnosis says. So either produce a reason to keep bidding that never once refers to the amount already wagered, or admit that "present liability" is the sunk cost in a new suit.
