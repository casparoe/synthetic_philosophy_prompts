Consider the view — call it the unrestricted family-resemblance thesis — that no concept has necessary and sufficient conditions: every concept is a network of overlapping similarities, and the apparent definitions we find in mathematics, logic, and the sciences are just especially rigid language games that a community could in principle renegotiate. I am inclined to accept this. What would count against it? Rank the defeaters from decisive to merely awkward, and give me the ones the view's own defenders would concede, not only the ones critics press. Separate the external defeaters — findings in cognitive psychology, formal semantics, or the theory of reference that seem to reveal sharp extensions or underlying essences — from the internal ones, especially the thought that a rule can be followed incorrectly, so that use alone may not fix what a term means. Finally, say honestly whether the thesis risks vacuity: if any apparent disunity can be redescribed as family resemblance, does the view forbid anything at all, and is that a defect or a sign that it was never an empirical claim to begin with?
