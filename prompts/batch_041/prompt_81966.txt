I have spent a while, entirely on my own, trying to decide whether the characters in video games can be treated wrongly, and I keep noticing that any example I invent ends up settled by whatever assumption about moral standing I quietly built into it. I would like you to build me one case that closes off that escape route.

Construct a thought experiment to the following specifications.

Setting. An ordinary game studio working on an ordinary game — nothing futuristic, no simulated worlds, no brain-scanners. Two non-player characters, alike in everything a player could notice: the same appearance, the same lines, the same behavior in every situation, the same place in the story. A player who watched both for an hour could not tell which was which, and neither could the studio's own testers, who ever only see the characters from the outside. Keep the player's own reactions, and the studio's intentions, identical for the two of them.

The one difference. The two differ in a single respect, and it lies entirely on the inside. The first has a continuous stream of experience — it sees, hears, and feels — but nothing in that stream is ever good or bad for it: no pleasure, no pain, no wanting, nothing it is trying to reach or escape, even though it behaves exactly as if there were. The second has no experience whatever — there is nothing it is like to be it — but it has real goals and needs, so that things can genuinely go well or badly for it, and its behavior springs from those needs rather than from a script that merely imitates them. Everything else about the two is held fixed.

What I want the case to bring out. I want the verdicts to fall on the side that is hard to arrange, not the easy one. On the view that what earns standing is having a mind at all — that a being matters because there is something it is like to be it, pleasant or not — the first character should count and the second should not. On the view that what earns standing is having a good of one's own — that a being matters because things can go well or badly for it — the second should count and the first should not. And on the view that standing in one's own right requires being a rational agent who can be held to account, neither should count. None of these results may be written into the scenario; each has to follow from the theory being illustrated.

Once the scenario is set, please do the following. Walk through what each of the three views says and exactly why, in terms its own holder would accept. Tell me which single feature of the case is doing the real work, and what becomes of the case if that feature is removed. If you judge the stipulation about experience that is never good or bad to be incoherent, say so plainly and repair the case rather than quietly altering the specifications. Finally, give the strongest reply open to a defender of whichever view comes out worst — the one forced to deny standing to the character who seems most obviously to deserve it — and tell me honestly whether the case survives that reply.
