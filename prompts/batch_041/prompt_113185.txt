Okay, here's one that's been bugging me. Every time I run the Nuremberg/Tokyo unit my seminar splits into two camps and they talk past each other for three hours, and I've started to think we're running four or five different arguements under one heading.

Camp A: crimes against peace were invented for the occasion. Nobody had ever been prosecuted for waging aggressive war before 1945, the Kellogg-Briand pact renounced war as a matter of policy but never actually made it criminal, the Charter was drafted by the winners, and the only conduct on the docket was the losers'. Tokyo is the harder case — Pal's lone dissent is right there calling the whole thing a sham use of legal process to satisfy a thirst for revenge, and the Dutch and French judges dissented too. Meanwhile Allied area bombing, the atomic bombings and the Soviet invasion of Poland somehow weren't Class A offences. And the crime they hanged people for at Nuremberg didn't even get an agreed treaty definition until Kampala in 2010, which tells you something about how settled the underlying norm really was.

Camp B: the Charter was declaratory, not creative. It codified customary law that was already hardening, and the judgment called aggression the "supreme international crime" because it contains within itself the accumulated evil of the whole. The alternative to a trial was a firing squad, so this was the least bad option available and arguably the founding act of international criminal law.

Nobody budges. My hypothesis is that the legal-historical question — were these acts already criminal under customary law in 1939? — is seperable and in principle answerable by evidence about state practice and opinio juris, and that once you fix it, the leftover fight is between two different questions: was this valid law, and was it legitimate to do. Each side hears the other's question as a dodge of the real one. I also suspect "victors' justice" is doing at least three jobs — enforced by the winners, selectively enforced, and law-made-after-the-fact — and that the retroactivity objection and the selectivity objection are logically independent, since you can have either one without the other.

I keep trying to wave Radbruch's formula around as a middle path (a statute that is unbearably unjust isn't law), and my colleague says that just relabels the problem. I genuinely can't tell whether that's a deep point about the concept of law or a verbal quibble about the word.

So diagnose it for me. Is my hypothesis right, or am I carving it up wrong? Is the legal-historical part as tractable as I think, or is it itself the disguised normative fight? And what would count as progress here — a distinction, a concession, a formal result, a change of subject? Or is this one of those where it just bottoms out in whether you think the winners ever get to write the rules?
