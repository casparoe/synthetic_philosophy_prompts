I sit in on a small philosophy reading group that has spent the term on a priori arguments for God's existence, and last month we reached the modal argument Gödel sketched in the notes he never published — the one where a primitive notion of a "positive" property, together with axioms saying that positivity is closed under entailment and is itself necessary wherever it holds, yields a being that has every positive property and exists in every possible world. Two members, Iris and Tomas, went at it after the session ended and have been trading emails since; they have asked me to referee because I am the only one who has worked through the proof rather than a summary of it, and I would rather not improvise. Here is the exchange as best I can reconstruct it, moves numbered in order. (1) Iris: the proof is unsound, and you do not even have to inspect the premises one at a time to see it. There is a result — she has it secondhand from a lecture — that Gödel's axioms, read in the modal system he actually uses, the one on which whatever is possible is necessarily possible, have a consequence nobody wants: modal collapse, i.e. no contingent truths at all, so that for any proposition whatever, if it is true then it is necessarily true. A premise set that makes everything necessary proves far too much, and proving too much is a legitimate reason to reject a premise set. (2) Tomas: that is a non sequitur as stated. Grant that the axioms have that consequence; that shows they are strong, not that they are false. "Proves too much" is a heuristic, not an argument, and to turn it into an argument you must show that the surplus conclusion is independently false or absurd. But is it absurd to say that nothing is contingent? Someone who thinks the world is sustained at every moment by a necessary being might accept exactly that, and treat what we call contingency as a feature of our perspective rather than of reality. So the objection presupposes a modal metaphysics it has not defended. Besides, I suspect the collapse is an artifact of one particular axiom — the one making positivity itself necessary — together with the modal system the proof is carried out in; you can set the argument up so that the collapse does not arise, and then the real question becomes what it still proves and what it has to assume in order to prove it. (3) Iris: then the proof fails at the thing it was for. It is supposed to persuade someone who does not already believe; that is what a proof of God's existence is for. If its premises are acceptable only to someone who already accepts that every truth is a necessary truth, or at least that the modal status of ordinary facts is an open question, it is question-begging, and a proof that only goes through for the already convinced does no work. (4) Tomas: "question-begging" cannot mean "its premises entail its conclusion" — that is what validity is — and it cannot mean "its premises are controversial," because every interesting argument for a contested conclusion has controversial premises; that is why there is a dispute. The real question is whether the premises are more credible than the denial of the conclusion, and your standard would rule out any successful argument for a contested conclusion in advance, which is not a standard anyone applies anywhere else. (5) Iris: here is what actually bothers me, and it is not only the collapse. The primitive is "positive" and it is never defined. Then we are handed axioms: positivity is closed under entailment, positivity is necessary, being God-like is positive, necessary existence is positive. Those are precisely the axioms you would need if you wanted to manufacture the conclusion. The proof tells us nothing about God; it tells us what can be extracted from a word we are free to stipulate. (6) Tomas: undefined primitives are not a scandal — "point," "set," and "between" are undefined in the systems that use them. What you have to do is show that some axiom is false, or that the stipulations cannot all be satisfied at once, or that "positive" cannot be given any reading under which they are all true. You have done none of that; you have said you dislike the axioms. I will grant you one thing, though: the closure axiom looks innocent and is not, and it does more work than the word "positive" suggests.

Please adjudicate this move by move: for each of the six, say whether it is clearly mistaken, defensible but overstated, or clearly right, and justify the rating briefly. Then say who has the better grip on what the proof actually does — not by counting moves, but by which of them is closer to the real structure of the argument. Tell me what neither of them has raised, if there is a point that would matter more than anything either of them said. Be explicit about how confident you are in each technical claim you make about what the axioms do and do not entail — I do not want modal logic delivered in a confident voice where the details are actually contested — and single out the one claim you are least sure of, and why.
