I recently read an essay in a general-interest philosophy magazine whose central argument was that the right model for moral compromise is trade. Reconstructing it from memory, it ran roughly as follows:

'Trade benefits both parties even when they want different things, because each side gives up what it values less and receives what it values more. Moral disagreement works the same way. Two camps that disagree about what is right are not disagreeing about a single scale of value; each camp has commitments it cares about more and commitments it cares about less, and a compromise lets each side trade away the lesser for the greater. A compromise, then, is not a defeat for either party but a positive-sum transaction, and the value it creates is a moral surplus. It follows that the midpoint between two opposed positions is morally better than either extreme, and the only real question is where the midpoint lies. Critics complain that this reduces morality to taste; they are right, and the reduction is the price of the gains: once moral convictions are things to be traded, one has given up on any of them being true.'

I have a strong sense that something has gone wrong with the analogy, but I cannot say precisely where, and I would like to know which of these sentences I can keep. Please treat the passage as an object to be audited rather than an argument to be refuted. Specifically:

- Go through it sentence by sentence and sort the claims into three piles: those a careful philosopher would accept, those that are legitimate simplifications a specialist would tolerate with a shrug, and those that misrepresent the position they are meant to state.
- Say what the trade analogy actually establishes about moral compromise, and what it must assume in order to establish it. If it requires the parties' commitments to be commensurable, say so plainly, and explain whether that is a fatal defect or merely a constraint on how the analogy should be stated.
- Assess the notion of a 'moral surplus': is it coherent, and if so, in what currency is it denominated?
- Tell me whether the closing charge of relativism follows from any part of the argument that survives scrutiny, and whether the underlying objection — that compromise treats convictions as preferences — is an objection to compromise itself or only to one particular account of it.
- Finish by reconstructing the strongest version of the view, the one a defender would actually hold, and say what is lost when the summary is weakened to that version. If it helps to make the audit concrete, test it on a case where one party regards the other's practice as unjust rather than merely distasteful.

I am not asking whether the essayist has read the right things; I want to know what survives.
