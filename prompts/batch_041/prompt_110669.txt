I'm writing a piece on the idealizations game theorists use to model reasoning, and I'd like to test the argument by putting it into two mouths. Please draft a dialogue between two game theorists, set during a break at a seminar on the centipede game. The first argues that any solution resting on common knowledge of rationality is a knife-edge idealization: allow the tiniest chance that a signal fails to arrive — as in the electronic-mail scenario, where each player would need arbitrarily many rounds of mutual confirmation before the relevant fact becomes common knowledge — and the predicted outcome flips entirely, so near-common knowledge is no approximation of common knowledge at all, and the backward-induction story has no grip on real play. The second counters that this mistakes a fixed point for a limit: common knowledge is not the vanishing tail of ever higher orders of belief, and its discontinuity is precisely what makes it the right concept, a genuine threshold rather than a fragile assumption. Let them press each other on whether the centipede game's backward-induction outcome, and the experimental deviations from it, tell for or against either view. Neither should be a strawman, and each should answer the other's actual points rather than talking past them.
