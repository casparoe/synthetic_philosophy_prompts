I spent thirty-five years in a laboratory, and for most of that time I took expected utility to be simply the correct account of rational choice, since its axioms looked self-evident. I have spent my retirement reading the decision theorists who replace the linear weighting of probabilities with something else, and I now think the objections to the linear version are stronger than I once assumed. I would like you to act as a decision theorist with a good instinct for what makes a case prove something rather than merely illustrate it, and to build a case for me. Think carefully before you commit to a design; cases that look decisive at a glance usually turn out to assume what they were meant to show.

The theory I have in mind works as follows. Order a gamble's outcomes from worst to best as x1 ≤ … ≤ xn, with probabilities p1, …, pn. Fix a utility function u and an increasing risk function r with r(0) = 0 and r(1) = 1, and set

REU = u(x1) + Σ from i = 2 to n of r(p_i + p_{i+1} + … + p_n) · [u(x_i) − u(x_{i−1})].

Each increment is weighted by the risk function applied to the chance of doing at least that well. If r is the identity this is ordinary expected utility. If r is convex the agent is risk averse, since the chance of the best outcome is weighted by r(p_n) ≤ p_n while the worst increment carries weight r(1) = 1.

I want a case built to these specifications, not an exposition of the theory.

1. Realistic. A decision that a person or a small institution could actually face, described in the concrete terms of that situation. No urns, no roulette, no options labelled Gamble A and Gamble B; I should be able to read the case without being told which theory it is for. Money is acceptable, but the stakes must be ones a real household or department could face.

2. The case must contain two choices made by the same person, with this property: taken together they cannot be the choices of any expected-utility maximizer at all, for any utility function over money and any risk attitude. I recognise that the Allais pair has this shape; do not reuse its numbers, its outcomes, or its framing. Invent your own. In addition, in the first of the two choices neither option may be a sure thing — I know that is the easy way to make the case work and I want it to be harder than that. If you become convinced the two requirements cannot be met together, stop and say so rather than quietly dropping one of them.

3. Both choices must be the choices of one and the same risk-averse risk-weighted agent. Give me an explicit u — a simple form such as linear or square-root will do — and an explicit convex r such as a power function, and show the arithmetic for each choice. Nothing should turn on exotic numbers.

4. Then the checks. (a) Show symbolically why no expected-utility maximizer can make both choices: derive the condition on u that each choice imposes, and show that the two conditions cannot hold at once. This is the load-bearing part. (b) Name the single feature of the case that does the work, and say exactly what happens to the reversal when that feature is removed — for instance if the shared element is moved so that it no longer occupies the same position in the ranking, or if r is replaced by the identity. (c) Say whether the reversal depends on the agent being risk averse; if a risk-seeking agent with a concave r would produce the mirror-image pattern, give that variant in two sentences.

5. Finally, the adversarial part. State the strongest reply open to someone who insists that expected utility is the correct theory, including the reply that the agent's choices merely reveal a different utility function, and then say precisely what in your case blocks that reply.

Open with two or three sentences naming the structures you considered and why you discarded all but one. Cap the whole answer at 900 words, and keep the case itself to a single paragraph of no more than 150 words. Do not cite literature, name authors, or offer me a reading list; I want the case and the argument.
