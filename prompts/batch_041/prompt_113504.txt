I teach a seminar that's half metaphysics of death and half clinical ethics, and every year I make the same mistake: I put the cases up on the board first — the vegetative patients, the brain-dead bodies on ventilators, the organ procurement protocols — and the students immedietly start arguing about verdicts before anyone has asked what a criterion of death is even supposed to do. So this year I want the specification before any cases go up.

You're a philosopher of medicine who's spent a long time on this. Think carefully, and brainstorm first — I'd rather watch you work out what the demands on a criterion actually are than get a tidy list straight off. Then write me the desiderata: what any adequate theory or criterion of death has to deliver. Concretely, I want the phenomena it has to explain, the cases it has to get right, the distinctions it can't collapse, and the empirical and institutional facts it can't contradict — with the case or consideration behind each requirement, and your view on which requirements are non-negotiable and which are just one camp's prejudices.

Fix the purpose rather than leaving it vague: I want a criterion good enough to (a) go into hospital policy and state statute, (b) be applied at the bedside by a clinician who is not a neurologist, at 3am, without a confirmatory scan or an apnea test kit, and (c) certify that organ procurement doesn't kill anyone. If those three purposes can't all be served by one criterion, don't paper over it — say which one I should give up and why.

Cases and pressures I want the list to have taken seriously (not solved, just not ignored):

- the patient in a permanent vegetative state — breathing unassisted, no consciousness, and on most statutes alive
- the brain-dead body kept on a ventilator and feeding tubes for months, long enough to gestate and deliver a baby
- the pregnant patient in a jurisdiction whose statute requires continued support, against her family's wishes and probably against what she would have wanted
- infants born with almost no cortex
- the donor whose heart stops and who is declared dead on circulatory grounds, then perfused again for organ retrieval — and the rare cases where circulation comes back by itself after everyone has stopped
- the hypothermia and overdose patients whose lost brain function was reversed by warming or naloxone, which makes you wonder what irreversibility was ever tracking
- patients on ECMO: no pulse, no heartbeat, sometimes awake and talking
- the locked-in and minimally conscious patients — the criterion must not quietly classify them as dead, and I want the requirement that blocks that spelled out
- the fact that jurisdictions disagree (whole brain, brainstem only, circulatory), that patients and families cross those borders, and that some religious communities reject the neurological criterion outright
- a case that isn't in the literature: a patient whose neurons are replaced one at a time by silicon prosthetics that reproduce their function, so there is never a moment when the brain stops working — tell me whether a criterion of death is even supposed to return a verdict here, and if so, what it has to be a verdict about
- and the general shape of the thing: death looks like an event that happens once and can't be graded, while everything biology hands us is a graded process. Say whether that's a genuine requirement on the theory or just a habit of speech.

Format the answer with these section headings, in this order:

1. **What's actually at stake** (about 150 words). Why a criterion of death can't just be a definitional preference — what turns on getting it right.
2. **The requirements** (the bulk of it, 800–1000 words). One requirement per paragraph or table row, each with the requirement stated in a sentence, the case or consideration that motivates it, and whether you'd call it non-negotiable or negotiable. Number them so I can refer to them in class.
3. **Which requirement does the most work** (about 150 words). The one that knocks out the most otherwise-attractive candidates, and why.
4. **The joint-satisfiability problem** (about 250 words). The subset of requirements I can't have all at once, the argument that they conflict, and which I should sacrifice for which purpose. This is the bit I care about most, so don't hedge.
5. **Where the three familiar families land** (about 150 words total). Whole-brain, higher-brain, circulatory — a sentence or two each against your list, no more. I don't need a scorecard.

Don't give me citations, a reading list, or a summary of what the major reports and clinical guidelines already say — I know them, and I'm not asking you to reconstruct the literature. I'm asking you to think about what the thing has to do. And if a requirement on your list is really just a seperate camp's preference dressed up as a constraint, mark it as such rather than dropping it. Be decisive where you can be and explicit where you can't.
