I teach an upper-level seminar on rational choice and game theory and am drafting the take-home portion of the final. I need a single essay question on superrationality that a student cannot answer by reciting the familiar line that identical reasoners cooperate in a one-shot Prisoner's Dilemma. It should force them to derive when cooperation is actually recommended, and to say whether the superrational style of reasoning is a distinct principle or a special case of ordinary expected-utility maximization.

Give the students the following construction in full. Two strangers play a one-shot Prisoner's Dilemma with these payoffs (first entry for you, second for the other):

|  | Other cooperates | Other defects |
|---|---|---|
| You cooperate | 3, 3 | 0, 5 |
| You defect | 5, 0 | 1, 1 |

The two are not duplicates, but it is common knowledge that they were shaped by the same process, so their choices are correlated; let r be the probability that the other player makes the same choice you do. The question should ask: (i) on the evidential, superrational style of reasoning, for which values of r does cooperating maximize expected utility, with the computation shown; (ii) what the answer gives at r = 1 and at r = 1/2, and what that implies about whether superrationality is a separate decision rule or the limiting case of expected-utility maximization with correlated acts; and (iii) whether the Kantian, universalizability reading of superrationality — act only on the maxim you could will that everyone follow — yields the same threshold in r, and if not, where the two readings come apart and why it matters.

Then provide, in this order and under these headings:

The exam question — the question as the students will see it, including the payoff matrix; a few sentences.

Model answer — about 600 words, deriving the threshold, identifying the limiting cases, and defending a position on (ii) and (iii).

Marking scheme — the must-hit points a top answer contains, the marks each is worth (total 20), and for each a one-line note on the mistake that typically costs students those marks.
