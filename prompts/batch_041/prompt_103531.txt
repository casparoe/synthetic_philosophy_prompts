I am assembling a specification — not a definition, but the list of requirements any adequate definition of life would have to satisfy — and I want you to write that list. Treat the deliverable as the criteria themselves: the phenomena the definition must explain, the cases it must classify correctly, the distinctions it must not collapse, and the results it must not contradict, each with the case or consideration that motivates it. Assume the target is a general-purpose notion of life rather than one tailored to a single application, but expect that different applications (detecting life on other worlds, deciding whether a virus is alive, demarcating the origin of life) may pull the requirements in different directions — where they do, say so rather than smoothing it over.

The cases. Say which verdict each demands and why that verdict is the one to get right: viruses, including the giant ones with genomes larger than some bacteria and the ones that parasitize other viruses; viroids; prions; plasmids and transposons; mitochondria and chloroplasts, once free-living and now organelles; sterile hybrids such as the mule; a dormant spore, a frozen tardigrade, a seed; a corpse as against a statue; a flame; a self-replicating crystal; a computer virus; a synthetic minimal cell; and the isolated parts of a living cell considered on their own.

The distinctions. The list should say which of these a definition may not run together, and why: currently alive versus was-alive versus never-alive; being an organism versus being part of one versus being a product of one; properties of an individual versus properties of a lineage (reproduction and evolution look like the latter, which threatens any definition that requires them of a token); life as a natural kind versus life as a cluster or family-resemblance concept; and what is alive versus what we could detect as alive, which is a separate question from the first.

The constraints from biology and chemistry. Which empirical results a definition cannot contradict — for instance that metabolism, reproduction, and Darwinian evolution come apart in real cases, so no criterion may quietly assume they travel together; that life as we know it is cellular; and any others you judge load-bearing.

Then the analysis I actually want. Sort the requirements into genuine desiderata and mere prejudices of a camp — for example, the demand that a definition supply necessary and sufficient conditions at all, or the demand that it be operational and testable in the field. Mark each as non-negotiable or as a preference of one approach. Identify any subset of the desiderata that looks jointly unsatisfiable, and say which subset and why; that result is the part I care about most. Then say which single requirement does the most work in excluding popular candidates. Finally, briefly, how the main families of contenders fare against the list — metabolic, evolutionary, replicator-first, thermodynamic, and cluster or functional views — but keep the weight on the criteria rather than the scorecard.

One further requirement I want you to consider and either adopt or reject on the merits: that a definition should license the inference from something being alive to its being able, in principle, to stand as an ancestor in a lineage of Darwinian reproduction. Call it the lineage-eligibility condition. Decide whether it belongs on the list, and if it does, what it forces and what it costs.

End your answer with a final line of the form \answer{...} containing a short label for the single requirement that eliminates the most candidate definitions.
