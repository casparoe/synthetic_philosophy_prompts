I want an argument for a specific reading of Aquinas on killing tyrants, and I want it defended against the reading most people find obvious.

The texts pull in different directions. In an early theological commentary he treats it as defensible that someone might free a community by killing a tyrant, and he frames the case within a discussion of when one may refuse obedience to an unjust holder of power and suffer for it. In the later, unfinished treatise on kingship he is much more cautious: he stresses how dangerous it is to move against a ruler, observes that a moderate tyrant is better borne than the disorders that follow from attacking him, and suggests that where a superior authority exists, remedy should be sought from it rather than by private hands. In the treatment of sedition in the great systematic work, the killing of rulers is not treated as a straightforward good, and sedition is condemned as contrary to justice and the common good. Many readers conclude that Aquinas softened or reversed his position, or that he never had a settled one.

Your task is to argue for the following thesis: that the permission is best understood as belonging to public authority rather than to private persons, and that the apparent drift across the texts is not a change of mind but a change in the question being asked, since the early text asks whether unjust authority can obligate the conscience while the later texts ask how a community should prudently and lawfully remove a ruler. Argue that this reading, rather than the obvious one, is what the reasoning in the texts actually forces.

To make the argument substantive rather than a harmonizing gesture, you must work through at least these points:

First, distinguish a ruler defective in title from a ruler defective in the exercise of an authority he validly holds. Say which of the two the permission plausibly covers, and give the reason drawn from the nature of political authority rather than from the severity of the ruler's wrongdoing.

Second, address the case in which there is no superior who can depose the tyrant, and the case in which there is. Explain whether the absence of a superior creates a permission for private persons or instead leaves the community with no lawful remedy at all, and defend your answer.

Third, confront the objection that on your reading Aquinas says almost nothing, since a public authority that has already deposed a tyrant hardly needs a theory of tyrannicide. Explain what work the permission is doing if it is not a license for individuals.

Fourth, take seriously the rival reading, that for Aquinas the tyrant forfeits his claim simply by ruling unjustly and may be killed by anyone with the courage and the opportunity, and give the strongest argument for it before explaining why it fails. Consider in this connection whether the killer acts as an instrument vindicating the community's justice or as a private party taking vengeance, and whether that difference is doing moral work or is merely a legal formality.

Fifth, say what the counsel to endure a moderate tyrant amounts to. Is it a moral constraint on resistance, a piece of prudence about consequences, or an acknowledgment that judgments about tyranny are so unreliable that the risk of error should be borne by the oppressed rather than by the ruler? If it is only prudence, say so and explain why the moral thesis survives it; if it is more than prudence, explain what principle limits resistance.

Throughout, argue from the structure of the positions rather than from the authority of the texts. Do not present this as a survey of scholarly opinions, and do not lean on the fact that the treatise on kingship was left unfinished or on questions about who completed it. Conclude with the strongest objection to your own thesis and an honest statement of what evidence or argument would defeat it.
