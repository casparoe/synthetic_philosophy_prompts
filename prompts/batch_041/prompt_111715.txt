After a career in experimental physics, I have spent the last several years reading philosophy, and I have become interested in the epistemology of political disagreement. I keep encountering a pattern for which I have no term. In a dispute over a politically charged empirical matter—the reliability of a scientific institution, say, or the likely effects of a policy—each party accuses the other of arguing in bad faith. The accusation is not merely that the other side is mistaken. It is that the other side would not revise its view in the face of evidence. But the evidence that each side treats as decisive is precisely what the other side disputes. One party says that if the other were honest, it would accept the consensus of the relevant experts; the other says that if the first were honest, it would accept that the experts are captured by political interests. Each then treats the other's refusal to concede the contested premise as proof of bad faith. The test for good faith is thus internal to each side's position, and each side can apply its own test and fail the other without ever asking whether its own test is the right one.

To be clear, I am not asking which side is correct, nor whether bad faith is common. I am asking whether there is an established concept for this structure: a dispute in which the criterion for arguing in good faith is itself one of the things in dispute, so that the charge of bad faith becomes question-begging. The pattern is related to echo chambers, but an echo chamber can exist without this particular circularity; it is related to deep disagreement, but deep disagreement concerns the absence of shared premises for rational resolution, whereas here the parties do share a vocabulary of good faith and simply disagree about its application; it is related to motive attribution asymmetry, but that concerns the distribution of motives rather than the criteria by which good faith is judged. If there is a name, I would like it identified and its boundaries marked. If there is no name, I would like a term coined and defined, with attention to what the phenomenon is not—how it differs from an ordinary charge of hypocrisy, from a self-sealing belief, and from the mere fact that people are biased. A bare label would not satisfy me; I am after the carving.
