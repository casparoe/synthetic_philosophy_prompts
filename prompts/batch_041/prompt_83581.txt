Write a dialogue between two philosophers who disagree about whether there can be reasons without reasoners.

The first holds that natural selection can build a structure — an organ, a reflex, a whole behavioral repertoire — whose parts stand in genuine means-end relations to an end, so that it is true of the organism that it does one thing in order to bring about another, and that it ought to do the first if it is to achieve the second. This is so, he insists, even when nothing in the organism represents that end, wants it, or is moved by any consideration in its favor. The rationale, on this view, is real but free-floating: it belongs to the design rather than to any mind, and it is what licenses and grounds teleological language in biology.

The second holds that this is a category mistake dressed up as a discovery. Reasons, she says, are essentially the kind of thing that can figure in a subject's deliberation — or at minimum in the explanation of an action as something the agent was responsive to — and where there is no subject of that kind there is only a causal history of selection, describable in wholly non-normative terms. To call the upshot a reason, she argues, either adds nothing to the causal story or smuggles in a normativity that nothing in the world answers to.

Anchor the dispute to a concrete case: a parasitic flatworm that must reach a grazing mammal to complete its life cycle, and does so by altering the behavior of an ant it infects, so that the ant climbs to the tip of a blade of grass and clings there until it is eaten. Neither worm nor ant has any grasp of what is going on; the worm does not pursue the mammal, and the ant does not serve the worm on purpose.

Write the dialogue in which the two work through this case and the general issue. Requirements: both interlocutors are fully competent — neither is a strawman, and each states the strongest version of their position; they respond to each other's actual points rather than talking past one another, with at least one exchange in which one of them concedes something and the other presses the concession. At least one of them should also test the two views against a case in which the design is out of date — the behavior that was adaptive in the environment of selection has become harmful because the environment has changed — asking whether the reason still exists, and if so, for whom. Let them reach the deepest available disagreement: whether the first can state a principled criterion for when a rationale is present that does not collapse into "whatever selection has favored"; whether the second's insistence on a subject is a substantive constraint on reasons or merely a stipulation about how to use the word; and whether their dispute is ultimately about the facts or about terminology. Do not resolve it by fiat. End with the sharpest question still open between them.
