I am preparing a position paper for a workshop on Aristotle's economic thought, and before I commit to it I want an audit of whether the reading I have assembled actually hangs together. The reading is this.

(1) In the exchange discussion of Nicomachean Ethics V, Aristotle's norm is proportional reciprocity: the parties bring goods that differ in kind, and a just exchange restores a proportionate equality between them rather than an arithmetical one.
(2) The measure of that proportion is need (chreia); need, rather than labor, cost, or utility, is the single standard that makes heterogeneous goods comparable.
(3) Aristotle nonetheless denies that goods of different kinds are commensurable in themselves, and he treats money as a conventional device introduced to represent need and to serve as a practical measure, so that exchange can go forward even where strict commensurability is absent. On my reading, money's authority is therefore a matter of convention (nomos), and its value is positional and social rather than natural.
(4) Yet Aristotle also holds that usury is unnatural because money's nature is to serve exchange rather than to generate more of itself, and he treats unlimited acquisition (chrematistics) as a corruption of the natural art of household management.
(5) I take him to offer no predictive theory of price formation in the modern sense: proportional reciprocity is a normative criterion of just exchange, not an account of how prices are actually set.
(6) I am also drawn to the view that chreia is best understood as socially recognized demand, which would make his measure of exchange conventional in a deeper sense than most readings allow and would place him nearer to a unit-of-account or credit conception of money than to a commodity conception.

I suspect the package does not fully cohere, but I cannot tell which part is doing the damage, and I do not yet know which commitment I would be willing to surrender. Please audit it. Which of these commitments are genuinely in tension, which pairs only appear to be in tension, and how severe is each genuine tension? Rank the genuine tensions by severity rather than treating them as equally costly. For each one, say what would have to give, what the least costly revision would be, and what that revision costs elsewhere in the package. I am aware that Aristotle distinguishes natural from conventional justice in general; I have never been sure how far that distinction reaches into his account of exchange, so please address it rather than assuming it settles the matter.

Finally, tell me which single commitment you would abandon if you had to, what argument or consideration would change that verdict, and where your reasoning is most likely to fail.
