Hey, I host a small philosophy podcast and next month's guest is a philosopher of perception who's spent years arguing that the Molyneux problem has been misstated by basically everyone. Quick refresher in case it helps: a man blind from birth learns to tell a cube from a sphere by touch alone, and can name them; then he gains his sight. Could he, before touching them again, tell by looking which one is the cube? The classic fight is over whether he'd *know*. She says that fight is on the wrong axis. Her actual view: what carries over from touch to sight isn't shape-as-geometry, it's shape-as-something-you-can-do-with — the blind man's knowledge is really a grip on how the thing behaves under his hands (it rolls or it doesn't, corners catch, it slides, it tips). That grip transfers to vision only when vision is allowed to be active: he turns his head, the thing moves, he watches it roll, he reaches for it. Freeze it all into a still image and he's lost. So for her the answer is yes or no depending on a parameter nobody in the debate bothered to pin down, and the real question underneath is what kind of thing perceiving is.

She's got two side claims I want to poke at. One: the real-world cases of people who had their sight restored settle nothing, because they never isolate the variable. Two: she predicts that a properly designed study would find cross-modal transfer on dynamic tasks and none on static ones.

I need like 9-11 interview questions that dont just get her to restate all this. Please make sure there's at least one question each on: (a) whether "it depends on the parameter" is a real answer or just a way of never being wrong, and whether she can name a case where her view predicts a clean no; (b) the static/active distinction itself — is it a line inside vision, or a line between seeing and doing, and does the second reading make her unfalsifiable; (c) her prediction, and what evidence would actually count against her; (d) the restored-sight cases — is her dismissal principled or ad hoc, and does her whole argument quietly need those cases to stay silent; (e) the "you've changed the subject" objection, i.e. Molyneux asked about shape and she answers about what you can do with the thing; (f) whether the view implies animals do this cross-modally, and whether that helps or hurts her. For each question also give me a follow-up keyed to the answer she's most likely to give — she's slippery, she tends to reply "thats exactly the distinction I'm denying," so the follow-ups need to be ones she can't wriggle out of that way.
