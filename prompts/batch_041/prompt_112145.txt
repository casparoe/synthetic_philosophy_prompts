I teach an undergraduate seminar on Wittgenstein and Kripke, and this term I want to pair the rule-following problem with Shirley Jackson's "The Lottery" — not as a cute illustration, but because I think the story is doing philosophical work that the literature on the skeptical solution has not fully absorbed. Give me a reading of the story through that lens.

The obvious points of contact are the ritual's decayed condition. The black box is said to be made partly from the box that preceded it; the chips of wood have been replaced by slips of paper; the chant has been forgotten, though villagers still disagree about whether the official used to stand in one place or walk among the people; some neighboring villages have given the lottery up, and Old Man Warner treats that as a betrayal of something. Jackson never tells us what the lottery originally was, what rule it encodes, or what would count as getting it wrong. That is precisely Kripke's situation: you have never added numbers above 56 before, and nothing in your past use of plus determines that 68 + 57 is 125 rather than 5 under the deviant function the skeptic calls quus. So: is there a fact of the matter about whether the villagers are still performing the same practice, or are they, without knowing it, performing a quus-like variant? Does the story stage the regress of interpretation — that any interpretation of a rule still hangs in the air — or does it stage something else?

Then there is the skeptical solution. Kripke's Wittgenstein does not answer the skeptic by finding the fact that fixes meaning; he replaces the question of truth conditions with the question of when an assertion is licensed, and the answer runs through communal agreement and the convergent dispositions of the community. Take that solution seriously and then press it with the story. The villagers agree; their dispositions converge; they go on in the same way. Does that make their practice meaningful? Does it make it correct? Old Man Warner is a dispositionalist and a traditionalist at once — the lottery is what has always been done — while Tessie Hutchinson, when her household is drawn, becomes a straight-solution theorist in a panic: she appeals to the lottery's actual rules, its fairness, the fact of what is really being done, and is stoned for it. Which of them is closer to the truth about rule-following, and what does the ending do to our confidence in the answer?

I want the reading to be honest in both directions. Do not reduce Jackson to an allegory of Kripke: the story is also about scapegoating, conformity, the small talk that surrounds a killing, and the initiation of a child into a practice, and it is not a treatise on semantics. Nor should you bend Kripke to fit the story. The skeptical solution is not a licence for any practice; Kripke is explicit that it leaves our practices as they are rather than justifying them, and the community-agreement account is meant to explain the assertibility of meaning claims, not to certify the community as a source of reasons. So say with some care whether the story exposes a limit of the skeptical solution — that it can tell us what we mean but not what we ought to do, and so leaves the villagers' practice untouched and horrifying — or whether the story is better read as a reductio of the whole community-agreement move, of the sort that McDowell presses when he objects that agreement in use is just a regularity and cannot itself be the norm. Consider also at least one straight solution, dispositionalism or the view that meaning is primitive and unanalysable, and say what it would say about the villagers, and whether that is better or worse than what Kripkenstein says.

Finally, since I will use this in class: point me to the scenes that do the most work — the box, the slips, the lost chant, the dispute over whether Tessie's married daughter should draw with the Hutchinsons, the children's pile of stones, the moment someone puts pebbles in the youngest Hutchinson child's hand, the talk of villages that have quit. You need not quote the story; describe the scenes. End by telling me what, if anything, the story adds to the philosophical debate that the argument alone does not — whether it gives the skeptical problem an emotional and social dimension Kripke's arithmetic example cannot, or whether it shows that rule-following, when the rule is a rite, is really a question about who has the power to say that we are going on the same way.
