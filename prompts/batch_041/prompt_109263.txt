I'm in a little reading group on Lakatos and philosophy of science, and we keep having the same arguement after every session. Someone always says that modern deep learning is the perfect example of a research programme: it has a hard core (roughly, that general-purpose learning algorithms trained on lots of data with lots of compute will keep producing better and broader capabilities), a protective belt of architectural tricks, optimizers, prompting methods, RLHF, chain-of-thought, etc., and a positive heuristic that says scale first and hand-engineer later. The negative heuristic is basically: don't go back to symbol manipulation. The critics in our group say it's degenerating: every failure gets explained away by saying we just need more scale or more data, every success is announced after the fact, and the programme never predicts in advance which capabilities will emerge. The defenders say no, it's progressive because it keeps predicting new capabilities and keeps surviving attempted refutations.

I know Lakatos died long before any of this, but I'd love a serious reconstruction of what he would have said if he'd been around to read the scaling laws literature and the argument that general methods which scale with compute keep beating hand-crafted knowledge. Would he classify deep learning as a progressive or degenerating research programme, and on what exact grounds? What would he say the hard core actually is -- is it the scaling hypothesis, or something thinner like the claim that gradient descent on differentiable architectures is worth trying? How would he treat the scale-first heuristic: as a positive heuristic, a metaphysical commitment, or just an empirical generalization that's doing too much work? And what about the AI safety and alignment work that's grown up around it -- would he see that as a seperate progressive programme, a protective belt for the same one, or a degenerating splinter? I'm less interested in a generic summary of Lakatos's criteria than in watching him apply them to this specific case, including where you think his framework would strain or give a verdict he might not have liked.
