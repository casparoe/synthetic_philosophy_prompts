My sister has been on a tear ever since she found out a sleep-tracking app she'd used for years sold her data to a broker — and honestly she's got a point, but her fix is where I get stuck. She keeps saying it's basically theft: her data is her property, somebody took it without paying, and the obvious solution is to give people ownership of their data and let them sell it if they want, same as you'd sell a car or a plot of land. I've heard this data-is-property line from everyone lately — a guy at a meetup who's building a data co-op, a couple of op-eds, my cousin who works in VC — and I mostly nod along because it sounds clean. But it nags at me, and I can't tell if that's because it's actually wrong or because I'm being precious about it.

Can you audit the analogy for me properly? I want the mapping spelled out — who plays the owner, what's the asset, what corresponds to trespass, to sale, to the deed, to the right to exclude — and then the places where personal data just stops behaving like a car or a lot. I have a feeling some of the breaks matter a lot for what she wants and some are just trivia, so please separate those. The features I keep half-noticing: a car is rival (if I sell it I don't still have it, but my data can be copied and sold a thousand times over), a car is only about me (but my step-count is also a fact about my neighborhood, my friends who synced with me, the pattern of everyone else on the app), and owning something usually means I can permanently give it away forever, whereas a friend who does privacy law says the real problem is that info that's totally fine in one setting is creepy in another, so the same fact can be innocent in my doctor's office and a violation in an ad auction. Which of those actually break the argument and which don't?

And then the part I care most about: does her conclusion — own it, sell it, and that's what protects you — actually ride on the analogy, or does it survive even if the analogy is a mess? If the property frame is doing the heavy lifting and can't carry it, tell me what a better frame would be, and be honest about what the property framing buys you that a contextual one doesn't (there must be something, or smart people wouldn't keep reaching for it).
