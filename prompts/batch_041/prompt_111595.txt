You are a philosopher of mathematics and a working set theorist, and you have spent years on the independence phenomenon and on the question of what, if anything, settles the continuum hypothesis. Before you write anything, think carefully and brainstorm: draw up an inventory of every correspondence the analogy below might be taken to license, and every respect in which the two situations might come apart, before deciding which of them matter.

The analogy I want audited is this. A colleague of mine, a set theorist, argues as follows. CH — the claim that there is no infinite set of reals strictly between the countable sets and the reals themselves, equivalently that the continuum has size ℵ₁ — is independent of ZFC: Gödel showed that CH cannot be refuted from the standard axioms if they are consistent, and Cohen showed that it cannot be proved from them either. My colleague says this is exactly the situation of Euclid's fifth postulate. The parallel postulate is likewise not decided by the rest of the axioms, and the nineteenth-century response was to stop asking which geometry is the true one and to accept a plurality of geometries, Euclidean and non-Euclidean, each internally consistent and each legitimate for its own purposes. He draws the conclusion that CH is a matter of choice: that there is no fact of the matter whether the continuum is ℵ₁, that we may adopt CH or its negation as convenience dictates, and that the continuing search for new axioms that would settle it is a hangover from a picture we should have outgrown — the picture of a single intended universe of sets that our axioms are trying, and failing, to describe.

I want an audit of that analogy rather than a survey of the literature, and I want it technical wherever the technicalities are what carry the weight.

1. Correspondence. Give me the mapping explicitly — a table is fine — saying what on the set-theoretic side plays the role of each of the following: the Euclidean axioms; the parallel postulate; the non-Euclidean geometries; the consistency proofs for those geometries; the pre-theoretic subject matter that geometry was supposed to be about; and the eventual acceptance of plurality.

2. Where it breaks, with the technical asymmetries first. In particular: how the independence of the parallel postulate was actually established, by constructing models and so interpreting the disputed axiom in a theory already trusted, versus how the independence of CH is established, by forcing — and in each case, what the result is relative to, and whether the two are even results of the same logical kind. Whether anything on the set-theoretic side answers to the pre-theoretic anchor that physical space provided in the geometry case. And what difference it makes that in the geometry case the background theory was never itself in question, whereas in the set-theoretic case the background theory is the very thing under investigation.

3. A rival reading I want adjudicated. A third colleague insists the analogy cuts against the conclusion rather than for it. Her point is that the independence of the parallel postulate is a first-order, proof-theoretic phenomenon: give a second-order axiomatization of plane geometry and the theory becomes categorical, so the parallel postulate has a determinate status after all, and the plurality of geometries is a plurality of structures rather than a plurality of truths. She says something similar can be said of set theory, since a second-order axiomatization of ZF pins the universe down up to the height of the cumulative hierarchy, and that if we fix the height and read the second-order quantifiers at full strength, CH is either true or false and not merely a postulate to be adopted. Tell me how far this parallel holds on each side, whether the two cases are symmetric here, and what it does to my first colleague's conclusion. If there is a position in the vicinity that says CH lacks a truth value not because the axioms are silent but because there are many equally legitimate universes of sets, say whether the geometry analogy does any work for that position that the independence theorem alone does not already do.

4. Verdict. Separate the claim that ZFC does not decide CH from the claim that CH has no truth value, and tell me which, if either, the analogy actually earns. Say which disanalogies are load-bearing for the conclusion and which are decoration. Then tell me whether there is a better analogy for the situation — the axiom of choice, the law of excluded middle, something else entirely — and what the better analogy captures that the geometry one distorts.
