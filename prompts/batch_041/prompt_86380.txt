Bit of an odd request, so bear with me. I spent 35 years as a physical chemist and retired a couple of years ago, and since then I've been sitting in on, and now helping out with, a second-year undergraduate course on practical reason at the university near me. The prof is an old friend and she's asked me to write one question for the final, so I'm out of my depth and outsourcing.

The topic is Buridan's ass, choice without preference, that whole area. I need one essay question. Students get 50 minutes and it's worth 20 marks out of 100. The thing I want to avoid is a question they can answer by reciting the standard moves — the starving donkey, the line that rationality only demands that you take some permissible option rather than a uniquely best one, a nod to satisficing. I want it to force them to seperate three things I think get muddled: two options being equally good; two options being incomparable, or on a par, so that neither is better and yet it isn't a tie either; and the agent simply having no preference.

Here's my angle, and it's why she asked me rather than one of her own students. In my old line of work you get systems that are perfectly symmetric — a ball balanced on the crest of a symmetric hill, a ferromagnet cooling through its Curie point — and they end up going one way or the other. Nobody thinks the magnet needed a reason to point north rather than south; a fluctuation broke the symmetry and that was that. I want the question to make them say whether that analogy does any real work for the donkey or whether it's a red herring, and in particular whether no reason to prefer A over B, plus having to do something, leaves the agent under any rational requirement at all. And I want them to have to say what a plausible principle of rational choice would have to look like if it's going to do both jobs: let the agent act when there's a genuine tie, and still not leave the donkey standing there until it drops. I'll put the physics in the question stem so they don't need to know any of it going in.

So: the question, a model answer of around 700 words, and a marking scheme. I'd like the scheme built as five or six must-hit points with marks attached totalling 20, and for each point a one-line note on how students typically miss it. Use the marking scheme to say what an A answer has that a B answer doesn't, not just what's correct.

One last thing. I'm conscious I'm a tourist in this subject, so please have the model answer finish with a short paragraph saying how confident it is in what it's claiming and singling out the one point it's least sure about. And don't pad it with references or reading lists — I can't check them and neither can the students.
