here's the piece:

The dispute over cognitive penetration is usually stated as a yes/no question: can what you believe or desire change what you literally see? Fodor and Pylyshyn said no, at least for early vision — perception is modular and informationally encapsulated, and anything top-down gets confined to later judgment stages. Macpherson and Siegel said yes, and offered cases: the expert who sees the fracture, the person primed with a category who then reports the object as looking that way, the hungry subject for whom the ambiguous blob resolves as food.

My worry is that nearly every empirical case on offer has a cheaper explanation. In Carrasco-style experiments what gets manipulated is attention, and attention by itself demonstrably changes apparent contrast, apparent size, apparent speed. If you can produce a content change just by moving a spotlight, you don't need the belief to reach into the visual system — you only need the belief to redirect the spotlight. So the alleged penetrators owe us a case where the semantic content of the belief, and not its power to grab attention or arouse the subject, is what does the work.

I propose a criterion I'll call semantic specificity: a case counts as penetration only if (a) the retinal input is held fixed, (b) attention and arousal are held fixed, (c) the perceptual content still differs, and (d) the difference tracks the content of the belief rather than some non-semantic feature of it — its valence, its familiarity, its processing fluency. Conditions (a) through (c) are old news; (d) is the one that bites, because it rules out the whole class of "priming made the stimulus more fluent and fluency felt like clarity" results.

Once you impose that, the honest verdict looks like a split. Penetration of perceptual experience is unproven — every live case is either ambiguous input plus interpretation, or an attention effect in disguise. But penetration of perceptual justification is close to trivial: your beliefs about what you're looking at obviously bear on whether your experience justifies your judgment, and nobody serious denies that. I think most of the heat in this debate comes from running those two questions together, and the reason it feels like a substantive disagreement is that each side is answering the other side's question.
