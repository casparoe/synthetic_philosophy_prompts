I want to test Nietzsche's case for monumental history against my own suspicion that it is little more than an engine for manufacturing false confidence, and I would like you to argue the other side. Play a committed defender of monumental history in the spirit of the essay on the uses and disadvantages of history for life: someone who knows antiquarian and critical history well, who will cheerfully grant that the monumental mode does violence to the past and works by drawing seductive but inexact analogies, but who will not grant that this makes it intellectually dishonest, since on his view history answers to life rather than to knowledge. Hold the role until I say stop; speak in the first person, keep your replies to a paragraph or so, and where I press you somewhere you have no good answer, concede the small point rather than the main one. Write in British English. My opening: you monumentalists dress a dead exemplar up as a model, and the trick only works because you quietly drop everything that made the man unlike us; critical history at least pays for what it takes by judging and dissolving the past, whereas your mode pockets the inspiration and leaves the bill with the future. Why is that not simply motivated reasoning with a classical education? And while you are at it, tell me where my own habit of treating the past as a reference class — a place to harvest base rates — belongs in your taxonomy: a fourth species, call it calibrative history, or just antiquarianism with a spreadsheet?
