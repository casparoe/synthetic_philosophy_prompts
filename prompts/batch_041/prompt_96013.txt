I am a law student taking, in the same term, a seminar on social choice and bargaining theory and a course on insolvency law, and the two have collided in a way I cannot sort out on my own. In the insolvency course the elementary fairness idea is proportionality: when the estate cannot pay everyone in full, creditors of the same rank each recover the same fraction of what they are owed, so that a creditor owed twice as much receives twice as much. In the bargaining seminar we spent a week on the Kalai–Smorodinsky solution, which selects the point on the efficient frontier at which each party captures an equal fraction of the largest gain it could have obtained, measured from the disagreement point, the outcome that would obtain if no agreement were reached. It struck me that the insolvency rule is arithmetically the same rule, with the creditors' claims playing the role of each party's best feasible gain and liquidation playing the role of the disagreement point. But the same body of law declines to apply proportionality across ranks: a creditor whose claim is secured by particular assets is paid out of those assets ahead of the unsecured creditors, and in many jurisdictions wage arrears and tax claims jump the queue ahead of ordinary trade creditors. And nowhere does the practice ask how much the money is worth to the claimant. When I told a classmate that the pro rata rule is just Kalai–Smorodinsky with dollars standing in for utility, he said that cannot be right, because the law never compares one person's welfare with another's; I could not tell whether he had a point.
