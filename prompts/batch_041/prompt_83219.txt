I have been betting seriously for a little over a year — I left my job in the spring to do it full-time — and next month I am giving the evening talk at the annual weekend of the group I play with: about thirty people, most of them good recreational players, six or seven who bet for a living, and two economists who come for the arguments and who between them hold most of the decision theory in the room. My thesis is the case for full Kelly, and I mean it as a claim about rationality rather than about anyone's taste for risk. The short version: the quantity worth maximizing is not expected wealth but the growth rate of the bankroll over the sequence of bets you will actually make — the average over time for one person, not the average over many copies of you at a single instant — and once you accept that, betting below the full Kelly fraction is simply selling growth in exchange for less variance, which is a good trade only if you have a reason to care about the variance: a consumption preference, an edge estimate you do not trust, or a bankroll you cannot afford to lose. Nearly all the advice I hear to bet half Kelly, I think, is one of those three things smuggled in without saying which. I am attached to the position and I know it has soft spots.

Prepare my question-and-answer sheet. What will this room actually throw at me, in its own words — the practitioners will not object the way the economists will — which objections have real force, what I should concede on the spot, and what I should hold. Order them by how likely they are and how much damage they do, give me a short reply to each with an honest note on where the reply is thinnest, and include the one I am hoping nobody asks. Write it as continuous prose — no headings, no bullet points, no bold, no tables — the way you would explain it to me in a letter; I will be reading it on the train.
