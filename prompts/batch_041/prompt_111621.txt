I retired from a career in experimental physics three years ago and have spent much of the time since trying to teach myself philosophy, chiefly by sitting in on a reading group at the university near me. Last month the group read a paper on whether ecology can generate its own management prescriptions, and when I suggested that a sufficiently detailed account of an ecosystem might itself tell us what to do with it, a philosopher in the room disposed of the suggestion with the old maxim that one cannot derive an ought from an is. I did not have a reply ready, but I went home and tried to reconstruct the reasoning behind the maxim, and in doing so I built an argument that I now find both compelling and alarming, because it appears to establish something rather larger than either side of that dispute wanted. I would be grateful if you would tell me which step to reject and what rejecting it costs.

Here is the argument as I have it. (1) An argument is valid only if its conclusion is contained in its premises; whatever concept the conclusion deploys must already be deployed somewhere in the premises, on pain of the conclusion asserting something the premises never asserted. (2) Purely descriptive premises deploy no normative concepts. (3) Therefore no valid argument runs from purely descriptive premises to a normative conclusion, and any argument that appears to do so must carry a suppressed normative premise. (4) The business of the natural sciences is to extract conclusions from descriptive premises, namely observations, measurements, and recorded regularities. (5) It follows that the natural sciences can never by themselves deliver a normative conclusion, and whenever a scientist appears to deliver one a normative premise has been smuggled in. That, as I understand it, is the maxim in its proper form, and I have now derived it rather than merely repeated it. (6) But observe that the same reasoning applies to every concept whatever. Cause, law, evidence, probability, and explanation are no more deliverances of observation than ought is; each is a concept that appears in the scientist's conclusions and not in the raw observations, so each must, on this showing, be smuggled in as a suppressed premise. (7) So by the standard of step (5) the natural sciences cannot deliver their own conclusions either, and every inference from data to theory rests on premises the data cannot supply. (8) But the natural sciences plainly do deliver conclusions; that is the whole of what they do. (9) Therefore the standard in steps (1) and (5) must be wrong, and the maxim that one cannot derive an ought from an is is not the local truth about ethics that it is advertised as being but one instance of a general falsehood about inference. And if it is not that local truth, it cannot be used to quarantine ethics from the sciences.

I would most like to keep the practical upshot of step (5), since I do not in fact believe that a description of an ecosystem, however complete, dictates what we should do with it, and I am not eager to hand that point to anyone who wants to say that the sciences are simply one more ideology. What I am not willing to pay for it is steps (7) and (9); I spent forty years doing what step (8) describes and I will not be told that it is impossible. So I want to know where the argument goes wrong, and if it does not go wrong, which of the claims I would like to keep is the one I have to give up and what that costs me. I suspect the trouble is somewhere in the neighbourhood of step (1) or step (6), but I cannot see how to reject either without either trivialising the maxim or letting the ought back in through the same door as cause and evidence, and I would like to be shown the price of each option rather than merely told which premise is false.

Please answer as a letter in continuous prose, without headings, bullet points, bold text, tables, or numbered lists of your own; the numbered steps above are mine, and I would rather the reply simply talk me through the options in ordinary sentences.
