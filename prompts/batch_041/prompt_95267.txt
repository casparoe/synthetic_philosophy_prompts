I have a set of verdicts about which ancient stories I am willing to call history, and I want the principle, if there is one, that explains why I draw the line where I do.

The Trojan War: history in outline — a coalition sacked a city at Hisarlik — even though I take the poem to be fiction from beginning to end. My reason is not the poem but the outside material: Hittite tablets that appear to name a place called Wilusa and a ruler whose name resembles Alexandros, together with a destruction layer at the right site at roughly the right time.

The Exodus: not history. No Egyptian source, no trace of a mass departure or of decades in the desert, and the story's shape follows the interests of the kingdom that later told it. I know an Egyptian inscription from around 1200 lists Israel among the peoples it claims to have dealt with, but I read that as evidence that Israelites were in the land, not that they had left Egypt.

The settlement in the highlands: history — and I notice I am calling a slow process historical while rejecting the conquest narrative attached to it.

David: history, on the strength of a ninth-century Aramaic inscription referring to the house of David; one inscription, from a rival kingdom, naming his dynasty but not the man.

Arthur: not history, not even a kernel. The earliest mentions come centuries later and are already legendary.

Jesus: history. Letters written within a couple of decades of his death, plus a Roman historian and a Jewish one, neither of them sympathetic.

Moses: here I keep changing my mind. There is no outside evidence at all, yet I resist treating the question as closed, and I notice the name itself sounds Egyptian.

My tentative principle is this: a claim is history rather than legend to the extent that something outside the tradition telling the story attests to it, and the more of the claim that outside evidence touches, the more of the claim I count as history. Test it against my list and tell me whether it can be made to fit without collapsing into a list of special cases, or whether I must give up a verdict — and if I must, which one, and what the simpler principle costing me that verdict would be. For each clause you add, name the case that forces it. I suspect the Trojan War and the Exodus are the pair that breaks the principle, since in both cases the outside evidence touches the setting rather than the event, and yet I treat them differently; if there is a principled difference, I want to see it stated rather than assumed. Then give the principle's verdict on a case I have not listed, say which of my verdicts it fits worst, and say what argument or discovery would make you abandon the principle you propose. I would rather have the weak joint identified than a tidy summary.
