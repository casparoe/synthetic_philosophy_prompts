I write a ideas column and I'm working on a peice about the "you can't say anything, you're a hypocrite" move — the way people try to shut down criticism by pointing out that the critic has done the same thing. I need a case built to order and I'd rather you build it than me.

Here's the shape of what I want. A thought experiment where sombody is a genuine, no-excuses hypocrite — they've done the exact thing they're now calling out — and yet most people, reading it, still feel the blame lands. The interesting version isn't the hypocrite who gets off on a technicality. It's a case where the hypocrisy condition on standing (the rule that says: you did it too, so you don't get to complain about it) is plainly triggered, and the blame still seems to go through anyway.

Constraints, because I want this clean:

- Realistic. A neighbourhood, a workplace, a family, a school gate. No thought-experiment machinery, no desert islands, no "imagine everybody is blind."
- One paragraph for the scene itself. Keep the theory out of it.
- The hypocrisy has to be the real deal: the same wrong, roughly the same size, done knowingly, and not repented in a way that washes it out. She only stopped because she got caught.
- Don't cheat on the other side either. The blamer isn't the victim of the target's behaviour in any special way, the target's wrong isn't trivial, and the blamer isn't secretly justified in having done it. The only thing special about the case should be the feature I describe below.
- The feature I want doing the work: the blamer is completely transparent about her own record. She names it, out loud, in front of the person she's blaming, as part of the same act of blame, and she doesn't claim to be better than the person she's blaming. She talks about "us", not "you".

Then do the analysis. Use these headings, in this order, with roughly these lengths:

The scene — one paragraph, just the story.

What the three views say — three short paragraphs. Take (1) the strict view: hypocrisy kills standing, full stop. (2) The permissive view: hypocrisy never kills standing, it just hands the target a comeback. (3) The middle view: hypocrisy kills standing only when the blamer is putting on airs — claiming superior standing or hiding their own record — so a self-inclusive, transparent reproach keeps standing. For each, give the verdict it returns on my case and why.

What's doing the work — one paragraph. Name the exact feature pulling the intuition, and say which of the three views it makes uncomfortable.

Take it away — one paragraph. Now remove or invert that feature (she hides her record, or she claims to be better than him) and tell me what happens to the intuition and to each view's verdict.

How they fight back — one paragraph. If one of these views comes out looking bad, how would its defender attack my case? Is there a reading on which the case doesn't really trigger the hypocrisy condition, or on which it's smuggling in something else? Be honest about whether the case is doing the work or just stipulating the answer.

Where I'd come out — two or three sentences. Which view I should lean on in the column, and what the case does and doesn't show.

One more thing: don't invent quotes and don't cite anybody. Just the argument.
