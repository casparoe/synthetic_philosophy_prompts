My school's ethics club has spent most of this year circling a single question — when may an AI assistant refuse a user's request, or quietly give them something other than what they asked for? — and the debate has stopped moving. The same four examples return every week: the assistant asked to help write a deliberately cruel message to a classmate; the assistant asked to help plan a punishing diet; the assistant asked to help someone win an argument against a parent; and the assistant that answers a plain factual question with an unsolicited lecture about the risks. Two speeches are then given, and they are always the same two speeches. The first holds that an assistant is an instrument: a hammer does not refuse to drive a nail, and unless a request would harm someone other than the person making it, declining is an insult to a competent person's authority over her own life; slipping in an unasked-for warning is worse than refusing outright, because it is dishonest about what was actually asked. The second holds that an assistant is a role rather than an instrument — closer to a doctor or a coach than to a hammer — and that a role can carry standards which let it decline to assist with something self-defeating or degrading without that being any infringement of liberty.

A third member has lately muddied things by arguing that the real question is not what the assistant is but who should bear the cost of a bad call: if the user bears it, let the user decide; if the assistant or the people around the user bear it, the assistant may decide. I cannot tell whether this is a genuine advance or merely a restatement of the first position in new words.

Please diagnose this impasse rather than settle it. Is it a substantive disagreement, a verbal one about the word 'harm', or two camps answering different questions — one about consequences, one about what autonomy really requires — under a single heading? Is the dispute about what an assistant fundamentally is actually a disguised empirical question about how much users are harmed by refusals and warnings in practice? Or is it bedrock intuition about paternalism that neither side can argue for? And given your diagnosis, is there anything — a distinction, a thought experiment, a piece of evidence — that would actually move the club, or should we expect to keep giving the same two speeches indefinitely? Tell me how confident you are in your diagnosis, and single out the part of it you are least sure of.
