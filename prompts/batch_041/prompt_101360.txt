I hold a view about markets that I will state without hedging: asset prices are epistemically authoritative. It is not merely that prices reflect available information, but that the price at which an asset trades is the best evidence we have about its fundamental value, and that the bare occurrence of a trade is a reliable signal that its parties possess relevant private information. I take the no-trade theorem — the result that rational agents sharing a common prior, already at a Pareto-efficient allocation, will not trade on private information alone — as confirmation rather than embarrassment: in the idealized limit the information is already in the price, so there is nothing left to trade on, and the trade we observe is simply the mechanism working. And I think the right response to the theorem's apparent absurdity, that trade is everywhere, is not to abandon the epistemic story but to relax the common-prior assumption: let agents read the same public signal differently, and trade reappears without anyone being irrational.

Tell me what would count against this. I want the defeaters — observations, arguments, formal results, empirical regularities, and possible future developments — ranked from decisive to merely awkward, not a survey of the literature. In particular: which defeaters threaten only the strong claim, that prices are the best evidence, and which threaten the weaker claim, that prices aggregate information; whether the no-trade theorem is in fact a reductio that undercuts the epistemic story rather than supporting it, given how ubiquitous trade actually is; whether the view can be tested at all, or whether it has been insulated by qualification — I am aware that testing it seems to require an independent model of what returns ought to be, and I want an honest verdict on whether that makes the position unfalsifiable and whether that is fatal; and which of the defeaters its own defenders would concede as counting against them rather than dismiss. Finally, tell me what would have to be true for the strong version to survive — what it predicts that its rivals do not, and whether any of those predictions have already failed.
