The gist as I understand it: the only thing that makes a credence good or bad is how close it is to the truth, you measure that closeness with a scoring rule, and the good ones are "strictly proper," which means your actual credences are the ones that maximize their own expected score — so no other set of credences has a higher expected accuracy than yours does. Then theres the dominance argument: if your credences don't obey the probability axioms, there's some rival credence function that scores better than yours in every possible world, so an incoherent person isn't just wrong, she gets beaten by an alternative no matter how things turn out. And apparently the argument for conditionalizing when you learn something is that conditionalizing maximizes expected accuracy. My favorite part is the immodesty thing: if you're coherent, it's basically a theorem that you should think nobody has higher expected accuracy than you.
