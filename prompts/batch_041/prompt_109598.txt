I am a retired physiologist — thirty years on the pharmacology of pain — who has spent the last few years reading moral philosophy without a teacher. I have just finished an essay in a general-audience philosophy magazine on the ethics of suffering, and its treatment of what it calls lexical threshold negative utilitarianism strikes me as suspiciously tidy. I would like you to audit it. The passage that matters runs as follows:

Negative utilitarianism is the doctrine that suffering alone is intrinsically bad and that pleasure is at best instrumentally good. Its lexical threshold variant concedes that mild pleasures and mild pains may be traded off against one another, but holds that there is a level of suffering — the threshold — above which no quantity of pleasure can compensate. Because such a view still ranks every outcome against every other, it is a form of the commensurability thesis: it assumes that pleasure and pain lie on a single scale, with pain at the top. The practical consequence is severe. Any practice that inflicts threshold-level suffering must be abolished, however much happiness it produces; and since the world contains such suffering, the view implies that the world had better be brought to an end. Its defenders generally accept this implication and dispute only the details — whether ending the world would itself involve suffering.

Go through this sentence by sentence and tell me, for each, what someone working on the ethics of suffering would accept as written, what they would accept only with a qualification, and what they would reject outright. I am particularly interested in the places where a term of art is being used in its ordinary sense: lexical, I take it, comes from the dictionary's ordering of entries, and I want to know exactly what it commits one to; commensurability and compensate seem to be doing the same work in the passage, and I doubt they can be equated. I would also like to know what in fact motivates a threshold of this sort. The passage presents it as a concession that leaves the doctrine intact; I suspect a threshold is more often proposed in order to tame a doctrine than to sharpen it, and I would like to know which reading the literature bears out.

Two further requests. First, the bottom line: does the practical conclusion follow from those parts of the summary that survive scrutiny, or does it require premises the summary never states? If the latter, name them and say how plausible they are on their own.

Second, a distinction of my own, offered with an amateur's diffidence. A threshold might be of two kinds: axiological, meaning that above it there is no fact of the matter and no common measure to be had; or epistemic, meaning that above it our comparisons become unreliable, though a fact of the matter presumably remains. Which kind does the passage need in order to reach its conclusion, and is either kind independently defensible? This matters to me because my old field bears on it in a way I suspect is a red herring, and I would like you to say whether it is. After a career spent measuring pain I can report that we have no single scale: intensity, duration, affective disturbance and the meaning of an episode resist reduction to one another, and the instruments are notoriously sensitive to how the question is put. It is tempting to conclude that pleasure and pain are therefore incommensurable. Tell me whether anything in the physiology can bear on a question about the structure of value, or whether I am confusing the difficulty of measuring something with the absence of anything to measure.
