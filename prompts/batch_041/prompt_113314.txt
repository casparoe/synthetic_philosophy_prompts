I cover science and ideas for a magazine, and I've somehow talked my editor into letting me spend the next year or so reporting on care ethics — the whole ethics-of-care tradition, not just the care-vs-justice stuff everybody already knows. I'd rather build the year around a handful of live open questions than do a tour of the usual suspects, so what I want from you is a defended shortlist: which open questions in care ethics actually matter most right now, and why those over the others.

A few things I keep bumping into that may or may not be the real action — tell me if I'm off: (a) the power problem, i.e. caring relationships are frequently sites of domination, and it's unclear the theory has a good account of when care slides into control or paternalism, which is roughly the disability-rights complaint about caregiving institutions; (b) the scope problem, whether care can ground obligations to strangers far away or to people who don't exist yet without just turning into a justice theory with extra steps; (c) the definitional mess, whether "care" is a virtue, a practice, a relation, or a political value, and whether that ambiguity is a bug or the whole point; (d) the machines, companion bots and eldercare robots, where I genuinely can't tell if care ethics has the tools to say whether that counts as care at all or whether it's a category error; (e) care as labor and the political economy of it, given the care-labor squeeze everyone keeps writing about.

So prioritize for me. I've got roughy a year of reporting and maybe four to six features. Rank the questions, tell me which are upstream of which, which are actually tractable for a journalist — meaning the philosophers have gotten somewhere I can report on rather than it still being vibes — and which ones sound big but definately wouldn't change anything downstream. I want the reasoning more than the list: what makes a question worth a year of somebody's attention, what would move it up or down your ranking, and what I'd be dumb to spend the year on. And be honest about your confidence — say how sure you are of each claim, and single out the one you're least sure about, because I don't want to bet a year of my life on a list you're bluffing your way through.
