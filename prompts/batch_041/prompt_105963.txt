A friend lent me a pop-science book on consciousness (I forget the author, some cognitive scientist) and the chapter on Dennett's "real patterns" and the three stances goes roughly like this:

"Dennett's whole philosophy of mind boils down to the idea that beliefs and desires are useful fictions. Nothing in your brain answers to 'the belief that it'll rain tomorrow' -- believing is just something we say about a system when it's handy to predict it that way, like talking about the average family having 2.4 children, or about an object's center of gravity. That's what he means when he calls patterns 'real': a pattern is real when it pays to describe the system in those terms, and the question of whether the pattern matches anything in the world never comes up. He tells us we can take three stances toward a system -- the physical stance, where you use the laws of physics; the design stance, where you assume the thing is working the way it's built to work and predict from its function; and the intentional stance, where you treat it as a rational agent with beliefs and desires. The stances are just different levels of description, so which one you adopt is a matter of taste or convenience -- no stance is more objective than another, and there's no fact of the matter about whether a thermostat or a chess program really has beliefs. It's like picking a map scale: a 1:250,000 map isn't less true than a 1:50,000 one, you just grab whichever is handy. And this is how he dissolves the mind-body problem: since the mental is only a description we find useful, there's nothing extra that has to fit into the physical world. So for Dennett, whether something like a chatbot has beliefs is entirely up to us and how we choose to describe it."
