I'm a novelist, about two thirds of the way through a book, and I've written myself into a hole that I think is philosophical rather than dramatic.

The set-up: a woman is alone on an island for years and keeps a diary in a notation she invents herself — symbols for tides, stores, moods, that sort of thing. Later a second survivor washes up, and crucially he turns out to be right about what her symbols meant on a particular page, and she turns out to have been wrong about her own past meaning. That's the climax I want.

Years ago I read Kripke's book on Wittgenstein and rules — the plus/quus thing, where no fact about your past behaviour or your inner life settles that you meant plus rather than quus — and I came away with two beliefs I now want to check, because I can't look anything up out here and I'd rather know now than after another year of drafting.

First: I've been assuming the upshot is that one person alone can't mean anything by anything, because meaning needs a community to hold you to it. Hence the second survivor — meaning only begins when there are two of you. Is that what the argument actually gives you, or have I built a novel on a misreading?

Second, and this is the bit I suspect is a muddle: if meaning is fixed by the community agreeing, doesn't it follow that a community can't be wrong about its own words? If everyone on the island ends up using quus consistently, then that's just what it means, and there's no fact of the matter left over for my character to have got wrong. Which would kill the whole ending. So does the community get to be the final court of appeal, or is that a cruder claim than anything the argument makes?

Two smaller things. Is any of this specific to maths? Her notation is for tides and stores, not numbers, and I've been assuming the argument bites just as hard on ordinary words — and on her own private ones. And is this Kripke's view, or Wittgenstein's? I keep seeing the portmanteau and I can't tell whether it's a joke, a warning, or both. Because if the argument definately does make solitary meaning impossible, is there a respectable line that says her own past use, her own reactions, and the world pushing back are enough on their own? I'd rather not invent a second character purely to keep a philosopher happy — though if that's what it takes, fine, he's already in chapter nine.

Don't send me off to read anyone, I can't get hold of books out here, and I don't need citations — I want to know what the argument actually commits you to. Please answer in British English. And don't soften it: if the premise is wrong, say so plainly.
