Bit of an odd request, I think. I'm a novelist — historical fiction, mostly about wars people lost — and I've been asked to give a talk at a writers' festival on "writing the lost cause." Before I commit to it I want to pressure-test the position I've been drifting into, because I half-suspect it doesn't hang together. Take the role of a philosopher of history who's thought seriously about national myth and collective memory, and please actually think it through before you answer — maybe start by spelling out what each of my claims commits me to, then audit them.

Here's where I've landed:

(1) National myths aren't innocent. A story that recasts a defeat as a moral victory licenses the next round, and the Lost Cause is the proof — a narrative built to make Jim Crow feel like fidelity. So the myth needs debunking, and the historian doing the debunking is doing moral work, not just fixing the record.

(2) But I'm a novelist, not a historian, and I think fiction answers to a different court. What I owe a scene isn't the documented fact but the felt truth of the moment, and if an invented detail gets the reader closer to what it was like to be there, the invention is more truthful, not less.

(3) I think I can write from inside a myth's point of view — the Confederate boy's dignity, the Serb's grief over Kosovo, the Zealot's resolve at Masada — without endorsing any of it. Moral clarity in a novel comes from inhabiting, not from standing outside and pointing.

(4) Honestly, I don't think the line between myth and history is as clean as the debunkers want it. History is also selection, emplottment, a story told from somewhere, so "myth" can't just mean "history I don't like."

(5) And here's a thing I've started saying in interviews: a lost cause is only narratable in the future perfect — you can only really tell it once the loss has already been redeemed by something else, a martyrdom, a later nation, a grievance that outlived the battle.

(6) I'll also admit that the reason Masada and Kosovo move me while the Lost Cause repels me is that the first two are myths of endurance and the last is a myth of ongoing domination.

So: which of these actually pull against each other, and how badly? Rank the real tensions by severity, tell me which pairs only look like tensions because I've phrased them sloppily, and for the genuine ones tell me the cheapest thing I could give up and what giving it up would cost me. I'd rather find out now than halfway through the lecture.
