I spent thirty-five years as a microbiologist and have spent the last four reading philosophy of biology, which leaves me reasonably confident about the biology and appropriately humble about the philosophy. I would like your credence in one specific proposition, with the reasoning decomposed, because I suspect my own number is contaminated by professional habit. The proposition is this: there exists at least one class of actual, non-hypothetical biological traits on which the selected-effects account of function and the causal-role (systemic) account of function return determinate but conflicting verdicts about what the trait's function is, so that the choice between the two accounts is a substantive disagreement about real organisms rather than a verbal dispute between two different questions that happen to share a name.

Here is why the proposition has bite for me. My own corner of biology is full of cases in which the effect for which something was selected and the effect it currently contributes are not the same event, and in some of them I cannot see how the etiological account even locates the relevant lineage. A symbiont acquired afresh from the environment each generation, beneficial to its host but never vertically transmitted, is under no heritable selection for that benefit, yet plainly contributes to the host's capacities. An organelle with a long independent ancestry was selected for its chemistry in a lineage that is not the lineage of the cell it now inhabits. A plasmid-borne gene may be maintained by selection acting at the level of the plasmid rather than at the level of the bacterium that carries it. In each case I can describe what the trait does and, with more difficulty, what explains its presence, and I cannot tell whether the difficulty marks a defect in the etiological account or a sign that I am asking it the wrong question.

Please give me your credence in the proposition above, decomposed into at least these terms, each with a number and one line of justification: (i) the probability that the two accounts are answering one question rather than two, so that their verdicts can conflict at all; (ii) the probability that real organisms contain traits in which the effect selected for and the effect currently contributing come apart, judged on the biology rather than on the accounts; (iii) the probability that the causal-role account can fix the relevant system or level non-arbitrarily in such cases, so that its verdict is determinate; (iv) the probability that the selected-effects account can state its condition in such cases without collapsing into whatever-explains-presence or else failing to apply; and (v) the probability that, where both accounts are determinate, their verdicts actually conflict. Tell me how you combine the terms, whether you regard them as independent, which one you are least confident in, and what argument or empirical finding would move that term most. Finally, I have been toying with a formulation of my own, that a trait's function is the effect whose past contribution to fitness explains the trait's presence and that present contribution is admissible only as fallible evidence about that explanation, and I would like to know whether adopting it genuinely resolves the dispute or merely stipulates a winner.
