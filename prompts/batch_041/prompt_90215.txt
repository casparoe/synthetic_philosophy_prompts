Ok, I've been roped into giving a one-off session in a colleague's second-year Gospels course. Officially I'm there as the philosophy department's epistemology person, and the topic is the criteria of authenticity in historical Jesus research (dissimilarity, embarrassment, multiple attestation, coherence, the Aramaic and Palestinian-environment ones, and the rest). Don't explain the criteria to me, I know them. What I want from you is a teaching plan.

Seventy-five minutes, about twenty-five undergrads, they've read chunks of the gospels and basically no secondary literature. What I care about most is the order of the pieces. What has to be in place before the first criterion even makes sense to them? My own guess is that it's the distinction between an authentic saying and a picture of the historical Jesus, plus the idea that a criterion is a defeasible tie-breaker rather than a test with a pass mark, but tell me if something else has to come first and exactly what.

Then: which single example should carry the whole thing? I was going to run it off the criterion of embarrassment, using something the early church would have had obvious reason to drop, but is that the right carrier, and why not dissimilarity, since that's the one they'll have half-heard of already? What does the carrying example have to have, and what would make it backfire?

Misconceptions I expect, and want mapped onto points in the sequence: that the criteria are additive and can be tallied and voted on; that multiple attestation means counting Matthew, Mark and Luke as three independent witnesses; that dissimilarity delivers a neutral Jesus rather than one manufactured to be unlike both his Jewish setting and the church that remembered him; and that the whole enterprise is either objective science or hopelessly subjective with nothing in between. Where in the hour does each one get headed off, and which of them should I deliberately leave as a live question rather than close?

And this is the part I actually care about philosophically: I want them to leave seeing the criteria as presumptions that move a burden of proof rather than instruments that find facts, i.e. as a local instance of the old problem of the criterion, where the tools seem to presuppose the very picture of Jesus they're supposed to license. Is that too much for this audience at this stage, and if it isn't, where does it go so that it lands as a payoff rather than a gotcha?

Last two things. Give me one question I can put to them in the final five minutes that would show whether the idea landed, as opposed to whether they can just recite a list of criteria. And tell me what I should openly say I'm not going to cover at this level.
