I have drafted the essay below for a general-audience volume on the Anthropocene and would like substantive comments before I revise. It is meant to be argumentative rather than expository, so I care less about polish than about whether the central distinction survives scrutiny, and whether the factual scaffolding is sound.

Three Questions Wearing One Word

For fifteen years a small committee of stratigraphers tried to answer a question the rest of us had been asking loosely: when did the Anthropocene begin? The Anthropocene Working Group, a body of the International Commission on Stratigraphy's Subcommission on Quaternary Stratigraphy, proposed in 2023 that the answer be the mid-twentieth century, and that the boundary be pinned to the sediments of Crawford Lake in Ontario, whose annual layers record the sudden appearance of plutonium fallout from atmospheric nuclear testing. The proposal set the base of the new unit at 1950 and named the interval the Crawfordian. In 2024 the subcommission rejected the proposal by a wide margin, and the commission and the International Union of Geological Sciences confirmed the rejection. Much commentary treated this as the death of the concept. I want to argue that it was nothing of the kind, because the controversy has been conducted under a confusion of three separate questions.

The first is stratigraphic: at what point does human activity leave a signal that is globally correlated and effectively instantaneous in geological time, so that a single boundary can be drawn and recognized in sediments worldwide? This is a question about markers, and it has a determinate answer of exactly the kind the working group gave. A worldwide radionuclide horizon is nearly ideal for the purpose.

The second is dynamic: at what point did human activity become the dominant forcing term in the Earth system, the point after which the planet's trajectory is better predicted from the human economy than from orbital or solar variation? This is a question about the magnitude of a cause, not the legibility of a trace, and the two come apart in both directions. A dominant forcing can leave a poor marker, if it is gradual or spatially patchy; an excellent marker can be left by a trivial forcing. The mid-century radionuclide horizon is a good marker partly because nuclear testing was punctuated and globally distributed, not because 1950 is when human forcing became dominant. If the forcing question has an answer, it is probably much earlier, and probably a matter of degree rather than a date.

The third is political, and it is the one the phrase usually gestures at. When did humans acquire the collective capacity to choose their geological trajectory, to be not merely a force but an agent? Call this the agency horizon. The agency horizon is not a stratum. It is a change in the kind of cause at work, from aggregate unintended consequence to deliberate self-determination. And it is not obviously locatable in time at all: it is a property of a system's capacity, which may be acquired gradually, may be possessed without being exercised, and is certainly distributed unevenly across the species and its institutions.

Once the three are separated, the institutional drama looks different. The 2024 rejection settles, at most, the first question, and it settles it only by saying that an excellent marker is too recent and too shallow to be useful as a formal epoch. It does not touch the second or the third. And the question of who has the authority to name the age is a question about the sociology of a discipline, not about the Earth. The mistake, I want to say, is to keep trying to read a change in the mode of causation off a change in the sedimentary record. If what motivates the concept is the agency horizon, then the Anthropocene-as-epoch is a category error, and the geologists were right to refuse it, though for the wrong reasons.

I would be grateful for comments on four points in particular.

1. Does the threefold distinction hold? I can imagine the objection that the third collapses into the second, or that the first is merely the operational form of the second. If you think the distinction is real, what would tell the questions apart in practice?

2. Is the agency horizon doing any work, or is it a relabeling of the familiar distinction between an epoch and an event, or of existing talk about a good Anthropocene? If it is genuine, how should it be defined so as not to smuggle a normative claim in under a descriptive label?

3. Are the factual claims accurate as stated, especially about the working group's proposal, the marker, and the 2024 votes? I would rather correct them now than in print.

4. Is the conclusion too quick? Specifically, is it fair to say the rejection settles nothing beyond the marker question, given that the bodies involved also judged the signal too recent and too shallow to be stratigraphically useful?
