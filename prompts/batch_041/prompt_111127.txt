I'm slowly working through Spinoza's Theologico-Political Treatise (the old Elwes translation, the free one online) and I've got abit stuck in the preface. Here's the bit that's bothering me:

'Men would never be superstitious, if they could govern all their circumstances by set rules, or if they were always favoured by fortune: but being frequently driven into straits where rules are useless, and being often kept fluctuating pitiably between hope and fear by the uncertainty of fortune's greedily coveted favours, they are consequently, for the most part, very prone to credulity.' [...] 'Superstition, then, is engendered, preserved, and fostered by fear.' [...] 'Anything which excites their astonishment they believe to be a portent signifying the anger of the gods or of the Supreme Being, and, mistaking superstition for religion, account it impious not to avert the evil with prayer and sacrifice.'

What I can't work out is what kind of claim this is meant to be. Is it a general thesis about human psychology -- fear breeds superstition, full stop -- or is it a piece of local diagnosis aimed at the particular anxieties of his own readers, so that the general-sounding claim is really a rhetorical set-up for the argument about freedom of thought that follows? The reason I care is that the treatise came out anonymously, and in the preface he also says who he's writing for and that he'll defer to the authorities; and elsewhere (chapter 7, as I understand it) he says the way to interpret a text is to reconstruct what its author meant from the text and the ordinary usage of the language, not from whether the doctrines are true. So by his own method, do I need to know Spinoza's intentions to know what 'superstition' means here -- and if so, which intentions? The ones he announces, or the ones the anonymous publication and the rhetorical shape of the preface reveal? Or am I just overreading a fairly straightforward passage? I'd like help seeing what work the fear-claim is actually doing, and whether reading it as an authorial move changes what the passage means.
