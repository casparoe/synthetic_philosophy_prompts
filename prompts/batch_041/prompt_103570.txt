I'm a novelist — three books, the last one fairly confessional, which is probably why this has been rattling around my head. Every spring I run a masterclass: twelve writers, one room, one three-hour session, mostly people working on a first or second book. I want to build the whole session around a thing I can't seem to get across by talking about it, and I'd rather they run into it than hear it.

The thing: power isn't only the thing that stops you. It's the thing that makes you. It doesn't just forbid — it produces the person who then does the forbidding to herself, quietly, long after whoever was watching has gone home. It's the Foucault stuff, obviously, but I don't want the name said in the room until the very end, if at all.

My hunch, tell me if its a bad one: the workshop is already that apparatus. So instead of fighting it, I rig it. I want them to walk out having been made into cases — compared, ranked, filed, diagnosed — and having done most of it to themselves, and having started writing for the file instead of for the page. Then the turn: the "voice" they found in the second round is something the room made for them, and the more honest they got, the more precisely they wrote what the room's rules rewarded. I want them to feel that, not be told it.

A few things I've been reading around in alot of that I think belong in the mechanics — use them, or tell me they don't fit. One is the difference between two machines for getting truth out of people. The exam: you get measured against everyone else and turned into a case with a number attached. The confession: you're supposed to go inward and produce your real self for someone. I want the session to run both, in that order, and I want the writers to notice that the confession doesn't uncover a self that was already there — it manufactures one, and the room decides which one counts as real. The other is the shepherd kind of power: the leader who cares about each of you individually, knows your name and your habits and your worst sentence, and where the caring itself is the leash. I usually play that part without noticing. I'd like the activity to make that visible without me announcing it.

Mechanics I'm imagining, take or leave: each writer brings one page of something they'd rather not read out loud. I keep a dossier on each of them — I'm the only one who writes in it, but everyone knows it exists and roughly what goes in it. Reading is done by someone else, never the author. There's a ranking at some point, anonymous, or so they believe. And a stretch where I leave the room and they're told they're free to stop — and I want to see whether they keep the protocol running without me. That last part is definately the one I most want to work, because it's the thing I can't explain.

What I need from you: the actual runnable session. Setting as above — twelve writers, three hours, one room, no budget, paper and pens, and I won't make anyone cry on purpose. Give me the rules, the sequence with rough timings, the materials, the roles (do I need plants in the group? a co-facilitator who isn't me?), and the absolute minimum I have to say out loud, because I over-explain and it kills the whole thing. Then the debrief — I want it mapped moment by moment onto what actually happened in that room, not a lecture on the theory, and I want it to end with them arguing about whether any of it was avoidable. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it. My guesses: somebody refuses to play, somebody starts performing for the dossier, and the confessional material gets too real for a Thursday night. You'll know better. And be straight with me about what the activity can't do — I assume there's a whole dimension of this that only exists at the scale of institutions, prisons, schools, armies, and that twelve writers in a nice room can't reproduce it no matter how I rig the rules. Tell me what I have to say myself, out loud, because the room can't show it.
