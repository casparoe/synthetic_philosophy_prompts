Next month I'm presenting to a consciousness science group that meets jointly between a psychology department and a philosophy department: most of the members build predictive processing or global workspace models, two of them work on introspection and metacognition, and one is an illusionist who thinks the entire debate about phenomenal consciousness is a confusion. My paper argues that higher-order theories have been mis-sold as theories of phenomenal consciousness when they are really theories of introspective access, and that this is a virtue rather than a retreat: the higher-order state is what explains why you can be wrong about your own mental states in ways nobody else can be, and once we stop demanding that it also explain qualia, the familiar objections -- that a higher-order state can misrepresent a first-order state that isn't there, that it generates a regress, that it leaves the first-order state's own status unexplained -- either lose their force or transfer to first-order theories, which face them too. Prepare my Q&A sheet: the objections this audience will actually raise, phrased in their vocabulary, ranked by how likely and how damaging each one is, each with a short reply and a note on where that reply is weakest; say which objections I should concede ground on and which I should hold; and include the question I'm hoping nobody asks.
