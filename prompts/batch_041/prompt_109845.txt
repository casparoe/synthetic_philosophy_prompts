I am a third-year PhD student in philosophy, working on moral standing and the minds of non-human animals, and since last term I have held the seat my department is given on the university's animal care and use committee. This is the first protocol I have had to vote on where I genuinely do not know which way I should go, and I have to circulate a written opinion before Thursday's meeting. The protocol comes from a systems neuroscience lab that studies motivated behaviour. They want to implant electrodes in the lateral hypothalamus and medial forebrain bundle of rats and let the animals earn brief trains of stimulation by pressing a lever, in a design with a second lever that raises the current available, so that within a fixed session the only real constraint on how much stimulation an animal takes is its own behaviour. The investigators' case is that this paradigm has been in continuous use since the middle of the last century, that the animals are in no pain, that our guidelines charge us with preventing pain and distress rather than with promoting welfare, and that an animal which works for something is by that very fact being given something it wants, so any ceiling we impose is our preference and not the animal's. Two members want approval only with a hard daily limit on total stimulation and with sessions run so that staff can watch for animals that stop eating and drinking, and they are willing to say plainly that the animal is being harmed. Please draft the memo I will circulate: a recommendation, approve or approve with stated conditions or reject, with the reasoning behind it and an honest statement of the strongest case for whatever I end up rejecting. Say explicitly whether the committee should be asking the lab to change the protocol at all, or whether the right answer is that our guidelines cannot address this kind of harm and ought to be amended.

Two things are shaping my thinking, and I would rather you engage with them than summarise them. The first is that this looks to me like a biological instance of something I have been reading about in reinforcement learning, where the worry is not that an agent pursues the wrong goal but that it learns to intervene on the channel through which reward is delivered, treating the reward signal as the thing to be obtained rather than as a stand-in for states of the world. Here the experimenter has quite literally placed the reward channel inside the animal's own head, so that what the animal works for is not food or a mate or a nest but the signal itself; the same animals, I gather, will often work to turn the stimulation off as well as on, which makes me doubt that their pressing can be read as straightforward evidence of benefit. The second is the distinction I keep meeting in the affective literature between the motivational component of a reward and its hedonic component, between wanting and liking, with brain stimulation reward usually treated as a paradigm of the former and sometimes as operating in the near-absence of the latter. If that is right, the lab's inference from work to welfare is broken, and it is broken in a way that the dissenters' language of harm also cannot quite capture, because the animal may be neither suffering nor enjoying anything; it may simply be pulled. But I am not confident this cuts only one way. A rat that wants and does not like may still be better off getting what it wants, if welfare consists in preference satisfaction, and I have no settled view about whether this committee should be a preference-satisfaction body or a pain-and-distress body. Nor have I been able to dismiss the argument that a rat's life is mostly made of pursuit, so an animal whose pursuit is unusually intense and self-directed has not obviously been made worse off in the way that a person who wireheads himself would be.

I would like the memo to take a position rather than catalogue considerations, and I would like the conditions, if there are any, to be specific enough that the lab could comply and we could check them, with numbers, monitoring arrangements, and a review point. Write it as continuous prose, with no headings, bullet points, bold, or tables, in the register of a letter to colleagues rather than a slide deck, and put the recommendation up front instead of building to it.
