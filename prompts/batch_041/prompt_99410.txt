Ok so I'm a novelist, midway through a book about a literary prize jury — three judges, one afternoon, a lot of bad coffee — and I've gotten myself tangled up in what the jury is even allowed to have decided. Before I write the scene where they tally up I want to check that my own views about group decisions aren't a mess, because right now I keep flip-flopping every time I think about it.

Here's roughly where I've landed:

(i) Majority rule is the right way for a group to settle anything. Whatever the majority of members think about a given question, that's the group's answer to that question. No exceptions, no fancy weighting.

(ii) A group's answers have to hang together logically. If the jury says yes to "the prose is good" and yes to "the plot is original" but no to "the book should win" — where winning just means good prose plus original plot — they've made a mistake. That's not a quirky outcome, that's the group contradicting itself.

(iii) The group's verdict has to be a function of what the members individually think, and nothing else. No chair's prerogative, no standard smuggled in from outside the room.

(iv) There's definately a fact of the matter about which book is best, and the jury's job is to land on it. The best book is best whether or not they see it.

(v) Members vote on each question on its own. They don't horse-trade — no "I'll give you originality if you give me prose."

(vi) Each individual judge is perfectly rational and their own judgements are internally consistent.

Now the scene. Three judges, and the questions are P (prose good), Q (plot original), R (should win = P and Q). Votes land like this:

Judge 1: P yes, Q no, R no.
Judge 2: P no, Q yes, R no.
Judge 3: P yes, Q yes, R yes.

Can you go through (i)–(vi) and tell me which ones actually pull against each other given this vote, which ones only look like they do, and which has to give if I want to keep the rest? Order the real tensions by how bad they are, and for each one tell me the cheapest fix and what I'd be giving up by taking it. I'd rather you be blunt than polite — if one of these is just confused, or if the whole puzzle is a non-problem that only exists because I set the scene up weird, say that straight out instead of smoothing it over.
