Next month I'm hosting the first ever joint meeting between our local wildlife trust and a animal-welfare charity — 80-odd people in a church hall, half of them volunteer rangers and birdwatchers who've spent four years fighting a housing development on the marsh, the other half campaigners who think wildlife is mostly a word for a lot of animals dying badly. I have to give the opening remarks, ten minutes, then sit through the Q&A. I've been putting of writing them because I can't work out how to be honest without losing half the room.

Here's what's stuck in my head. A bloke at the pub told me the marsh is a horror show. His argument: look at what's actually in there. Frogs, insects, warblers — animals that lay hundreds or thousands of eggs, and nearly all of them get eaten, starve, or die of parasites inside a fortnight. Count the lives and the overwhelming majority are short and end badly; the ones that make it to breeding are a rounding error. So by his count the total welfare in that marsh is negative, hugely negative, and when the council drains half of it we should expect the suffering to go down, not up. And he wasn't trolling. His line was that the ones killed by the diggers would mostly have died horribly anyway, and younger, and the ones never born are the lucky ones. He also said that when we talk about restoring the marsh to how it was in 1900 we're not describing some good baseline — 1900 was the same meat grinder, we'd just be making more of it.

I don't think he's obviously right and I don't think he's obviously wrong. What I do know is that if I stand up and say we're all on the same side, we all want to protect life, the welfare people will hear it as either dishonest or clueless, because on their view protecting life is precisely the machinery that produces the suffering. And if I say the marsh's net welfare is up for grabs, the rangers will think I've handed the developers a talking point. Also the first talk after mine is from the welfare side, and it's called something like a factory farm with better PR, so whatever I say has to set that up without me endorsing it.

Write the ten minutes. Warm, plain, funny if you can manage it, not a philosophy seminar, no bullet points — something I can actually read out loud. It has to name the real disagreement in the room rather than paper over it, without landing on a side that half of them can't sit through. And don't tell me to say we all love nature unless you can make that do actual work.
