I'm assembling a five-year research agenda for a small programme in long-run ethics — eight philosophers and decision theorists, no empirical data collection, and a mandate to produce work that actually changes how longtermist funders allocate money. My own starting conviction is that consequentialist cluelessness is the load-bearing problem for that whole enterprise: if we cannot assign probabilities to most of the downstream consequences of an intervention, the expected-value machinery that longtermism leans on is either unusable or fanatical. I want your help deciding which open questions here most deserve the programme's years, and I want the answer defended rather than surveyed.

To keep this concrete, here is a taxonomy I have been working with, which I would like you to adopt, correct, or replace. I distinguish three loci of cluelessness. First, act cluelessness: the ordinary inability to put probabilities on the consequences of a particular action, standardly blamed on chaotic causal chains and on the sheer number of downstream effects. Second, measure cluelessness: uncertainty not about what will happen but about how to aggregate and compare goods across persons, times, and perhaps kinds of value, so that even a complete probability distribution over outcomes leaves the ranking underdetermined. Third, frame cluelessness: uncertainty about which option set and which decision rule are even in play, so that the agent cannot say whether she is choosing among acts, policies, or dispositions.

I also want you to consider two ideas I have not seen properly worked out, and to tell me whether either is a real research question or merely a confusion. The first is what I call cluelessness symmetry: if the very same argument that makes doing X unassessable also makes refraining from X unassessable, in what sense, if any, does the uncertainty cancel rather than paralyse — and can that symmetry be turned into a principled decision rule rather than a rhetorical escape? The second is the cluelessness budget: if reducing cluelessness is itself an activity with costs, delays, and diminishing returns, when is it worth buying information about consequences rather than simply acting under the uncertainty — and does that question belong to decision theory, to value theory, or to neither?

Given all of that, produce a prioritised agenda of the open questions in this area. I care far more about the reasoning than the list. For each question, say why it is upstream of other work, how tractable it is for philosophers and decision theorists rather than empirical researchers, how neglected it currently is, and what would move it up or down the ranking. Be explicit about the criteria you are using, where they conflict, and which trade-offs you resolve how. End by naming the two or three questions you would actually fund first, and why. No reading lists or citations — argue from the ideas themselves.

Return the whole thing as a single JSON object with exactly these fields:

- agenda_target: a string, one sentence restating whose effort, how much of it, and toward what end, so that the ranking is relative to something.
- ranked_questions: an array of six to nine objects, ordered best-first, each with the fields rank (integer), question (the open question stated sharply, in one or two sentences), locus (one of "act", "measure", "frame", or "other"), why_upstream (what other questions or practical decisions depend on settling it), tractability (why someone with no data and no lab can make progress), neglectedness (who, if anyone, is already on it, and why it remains underworked), and what_would_change_the_rank (the evidence or argument that would move it up or down).
