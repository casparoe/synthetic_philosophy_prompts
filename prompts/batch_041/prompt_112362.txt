I'm a software engineer with no formal training in linguistics or philosophy, and in three weeks I'm giving a colloquium talk to the linguistics department at the university where I did my undergraduate degree. My thesis is that the rise of large language models has pulled the rug out from under the poverty-of-the-stimulus argument for an innate, domain-specific universal grammar. The argument I'll sketch runs roughly like this: generativists have long held that children acquire hierarchical grammatical rules from input that is too sparse and too noisy to determine those rules, so the missing structure must be supplied by the mind; but models trained by generic next-token prediction on raw text, with no grammatical priors and no hand-coded syntax, now reproduce a wide range of the generalizations that were supposed to require innate structure, which suggests the input carries more signal than the argument assumed and the inference is easier than it was made out to be. I expect that last step — from "a model can do it" to "so the poverty-of-the-stimulus argument fails" — to be the whole fight.

The audience is the hard part, and I need you to take it seriously. It is mostly generativists: syntacticians working in a minimalist framework, several of whom have taught the poverty-of-the-stimulus argument for decades and regard it as settled. There are one or two usage-based and corpus linguists who are quietly sympathetic to my conclusion but distrust machine learning for reasons of their own. There is a single computational linguist who trains neural models and will know exactly where my technical claims are sloppy or overstated. Assume the generativists have read the child-language-acquisition literature very closely and the machine-learning literature hardly at all, and that almost nobody there has a working sense of what a transformer actually does.

Prepare me for the question period. Give me the eight or so objections I will actually get, ordered by how much damage they would do to the argument rather than by the order they will arrive in; phrase each one the way that particular person would phrase it, in their own vocabulary; and for each give me a two-minute spoken reply plus an honest note on where that reply is soft. Tell me what I should concede up front in order to keep the room with me, and which single objection I should not try to win but should visibly sidestep. Finally, include the question I am privately hoping nobody asks — the one that, if it comes, means the argument does not survive the hour. I want a prep sheet, not a defense of the thesis: I have already decided what I believe, and what I need is to know what is coming and what to say back.
