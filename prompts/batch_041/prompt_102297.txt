I've been in a consciousness reading group that keeps returning to the color phi phenomenon, and I've assembled an argument from it that I can no longer tell whether to trust. The setup: two dots are flashed in different positions in quick succession, with different colors. Subjects report seeing a single dot travel from the first position to the second and change color partway along the path — that is, they report the change as occurring before the second color was ever displayed. Here is the argument I built.

(1) Subjects report experiencing the color change before the second color was displayed.
(2) A subject's report is a report of what she experienced.
(3) So subjects experienced the color change before the second color was displayed.
(4) No experience can occur before its physical cause.
(5) Therefore the experience ostensibly belonging to the first flash must have been altered after the second flash occurred.
(6) So the content of an experience is not fixed at the time it occurs; it can be revised retrospectively.
(7) That revision is either a revision of memory after the fact (the Orwellian reading) or a revision of the experience itself before it is delivered to the subject (the Stalinesque reading).
(8) If content can be revised at all, there is no principled point at which revision must stop, so any given report may reflect a revised experience.
(9) Therefore no report can tell us what was actually experienced at any time, and there is no fact of the matter about the timing or content of experience.

Each premise strikes me as individually plausible, and (9) strikes me as absurd — I don't believe there's no fact of the matter about what someone experienced and when. Tell me which step to reject, why it looked fine enough to write down, and what rejecting it costs. I'd most like to keep (4) and the thought that reports are at least evidence about experience; I'm least attached to (8). If the argument is in fact valid and the real question is which plausible premise is cheapest to abandon, say so and price the alternatives rather than inventing a flaw.

After the diagnosis, give me a brief structured summary as a JSON object with these fields: step_rejected (the number or label of the step you reject, or "none" if you think the argument is valid); why_it_looked_fine (one or two sentences); flaw_type (one of "equivocation", "scope_or_quantifier_shift", "modal_slide", "valid_but_costly", "other", with a short gloss if "other"); cost_of_rejecting (what is given up); alternatives (an array of objects, each with premise and price, for the other premises you considered giving up); preferred_repair (the smallest revision that restores a sane conclusion); and residual_question (what remains genuinely unsettled after your repair).
