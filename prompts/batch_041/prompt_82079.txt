I'm trying to work out whether evolutionary psychology is a science or a pile of just-so stories, and I'd rather do it through one real episode from the field than argue in the abstract.

The episode is the Wason selection task business. You know the setup — four cards, a rule along the lines of: if there's a vowel on one side, there's an even number on the other, and you have to say which cards to turn over to check it, and most people fluff it. Then in the late eighties some evolutionary psychologists noticed that if you dress the identical task up as a social contract (borrow my car, you must bring it back with a full tank; drinking beer, you must be over eighteen) people suddenly get it right, and they said this reveals a specialised bit of the mind for catching cheats, built for the kind of reciprocal deals our ancestors would have struck. Cue a long argument. Others said the effect is nothing special: people are just good at permission- and obligation-type reasoning in general, or the social version is simply easier to understand, or it depends on how relevant the case feels. And over the years people kept tinkering with the scenario — making the rule about a benefit instead of a cost, having you police someone else's deal rather than your own, swapping cheating for looking out for an altruist — and the results move about in ways each side reads its own way.

What I want from you. Take the adaptationist lens — a reliable, content-specific effect like this is a decent sign of a dedicated adaptation — and run the episode through it properly: what exactly is claimed to be the adaptation, what's the phenotype, what would count as evidence for and against? Then flip it and run the episode through the just-so-story charge: is the cheater-detection hypothesis falsifiable in principle, and did the episode actually falsify it or leave it standing? I'm especially interested in a wrinkle I've been chewing on. Suppose the real story isn't a cheat detector but a system tuned to the asymmetric costs of getting it wrong, where missing a cheat is worse than wrongly suspecting one — the same logic people use for why we're jumpy about snakes and strangers. Does that rescue the adaptation, or does it just relocate the unfalsifiable bit, so that whatever the data come out as, you can absorb it by fiddling with the assumed costs?

And then the bit I care about most: what does this episode actually show about the science-versus-just-so-stories line? Does it cut for the field — here's a content effect, here's a story, and the story made a risky prediction that survived — or against it, because the story was never really at risk? I want a disciplined mapping (who's claiming what, what the evidence is, where the theory goes quiet) and a verdict I can defend, not a both-sides shrug. British English please, and skip the reading list — I just want the arguement.
