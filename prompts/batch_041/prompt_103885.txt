I'm doing an MA module on nudging and autonomy and I've managed to tie myself in knots. I built the argument below for a seminar and I honestly can't tell now whether it's clever or just broken: the conclusion seems far too strong, but every step looked fine when I wrote it down, and I'm supposed to turn it into a 4,000-word essay in a fortnight so I'd like to know before I build on sand.

(1) A nudge is an intervention that changes what people do without forbidding any option or making any option much more costly. (2) Any influence that does that must work either through the target's deliberate reasoning or by recruiting non-deliberative processes — salience, default bias, affect, that sort of thing. (3) If it works through deliberate reasoning, then it's really persuasion or information: it changes what the person believes about their options, so it isn't a nudge at all. (4) If it works by recruiting non-deliberative processes, then it only works to the extent that the person doesn't see through it — the more transparent and easily resisted the influence, the weaker it gets. (5) To influence someone's choice by design, in a way that works only in so far as it fails to engage their rational agency, is to treat them as a means rather than an end, and that's manipulation. (6) So every nudge is either manipulation or not really a nudge. (7) So there is no such thing as a permissible nudge, and 'libertarian paternalism' is a contradiction in terms.

That can't be right, because the same shape of argument seems to sink half of ordinary life — a GP texting you a reminder, a pharmacist stocking the common painkiller at eye level, road markings, a mate asking whether you really want a fourth pint — and I don't think anyone wants to call all of that manipulation. Someone in my group keeps insisting that means-paternalism isn't paternalism at all, and I suspect that's relevant but I can't see how to wire it in. I'd rather keep (1) to (4) than give up (5) or (6), but I don't know which of those I'm actually entitled to, or whether the real flaw is somewhere subtler that I've walked straight past. So: which step goes, why did it look so plausible, and what does rejecting it cost me — specifically, what can I still say about the line between a nudge and manipulation, and does anything of the usual means-versus-ends distinction survive once I've paid up?
