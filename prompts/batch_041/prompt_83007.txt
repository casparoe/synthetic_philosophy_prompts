I'm giving a work-in-progress next Thursday and I'm defending something the room won't like: I think the Frankfurt-style cases everyone uses to bury the principle of alternative possibilities don't actually work, and I want to walk in ready for the question period. The audience is our moral-responsibility crowd — two people who've built a good chunk of their reputation on those cases and will hear my line as special pleading, one Kantian who thinks the whole free-will industry is downstream of a bad theory of the will and will attack the question rather than my answer, a criminologist from the law school who cares only about what any of this does for mens rea, and a couple of grad students who'll ask the obvious thing I havent thought of. Not looking for a defense of my view, I want the objections: what these specific people will actually say, in the words they'd actually use, and what I should say back in the two minutes I'll have.

My arguement, briefly, so you can aim at it. Every Frankfurt case needs an intervener who is inert with respect to the agent's deliberation — a backstop whose presence changes nothing about how she thinks. I call that the inert-presence assumption, and I don't think it's ever met, because a standing guarantee about the outcome is scaffolding: it shapes what the agent treats as live, what she bothers to weigh, whether her reasoning is load-bearing at all. So the case can't isolate the agent's own contribution, and the intuition that she's responsible is being carried by the parts of the story where she isn't under the guarantee. My positive claim is that responsibility needs the agent's reasoning to be the thing that settled the matter — call it the settling condition — and that PAP, read as being about whether anything outside her settled it rather than about whether alternatives existed, comes through untouched.
