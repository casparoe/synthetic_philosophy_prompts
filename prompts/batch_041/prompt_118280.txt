I spent most of last night in a long and increasingly circular argument with an old friend — a philosopher by training — about a pattern I have noticed for years and have never had a name for, and we parted still unable to say whether I was describing something familiar under a bad label or something genuinely unnamed. Here is the pattern. There are things I do reliably and fluently, and for which I can give a perfectly accurate account of what I am going by, and yet the account, followed as instructions, does not reproduce the performance — not for someone else, and not for me. Cooking is the clearest case. I can tell when a piece of meat is ready to leave the pan, and if you ask me how I know I will tell you honestly: the pitch of the sizzle drops, the smell goes from raw to nutty, the juices at the surface run clear. Every clause of that is true; it describes exactly what I am responding to. But hand those clauses to a friend and he cooks with a stopwatch, checks the smell at the wrong instant, and produces something edible and dead. And if I deliberately follow my own written account, one instruction at a time, I cook worse than when I simply cook. The description is faithful but not generative: it is correct about the performance and useless for producing it. It is the same with knowing when an argument has turned and it is time to stop talking — I can do it, I can afterwards name precisely the signs I was reading, and I have never once taught it to anyone by listing the signs.

My friend kept insisting that I was just rediscovering Ryle on knowing how, or Polanyi on tacit knowledge, and neither quite fits. Polanyi's point, as I understand it, is that the knowledge cannot be put into words; mine can, and accurately. Ryle's point is that knowing how is not a species of knowing that; I am not asking whether it is. What I want is a name — established if one exists, coined if not — for this specific gap: the account that is descriptively faithful and yet not reproductive, where following the rule is not the same as exercising the skill it correctly describes. And I care far more about the carving than the label, so please do the work of distinguishing it from the nearest things it is not: tacit knowledge, the Dreyfus-style claim that experts outgrow rules altogether, ordinary 'paralysis by analysis,' and the difference between a rule that merely fits what someone does and a rule that could generate it. Tell me what exactly the phenomenon is, what it is not, and where its edges lie — for instance, is a written proof the same kind of thing or a different one? A proof is a fully accurate account of why a result holds, yet following it does not reproduce the act of finding it; is that the same gap or only a cousin?
