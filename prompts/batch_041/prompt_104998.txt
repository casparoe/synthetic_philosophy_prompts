Two philosophers are arguing about why one should be just, and I want the argument conducted under a modification of the ring of Gyges — the story Glaucon tells in Book II of the Republic — that I will specify below.

The first holds the instrumentalist view: whatever reason an agent has to be just is exhausted by justice's effects — reputation, the trust and cooperation of others, the sanctions of law and custom, and the psychological costs of guilt and self-reproach. Strip those away and nothing remains; justice is a second-best good, a device for getting on with others, and a rational agent who could secure the benefits of injustice without any of its costs would have no reason to refrain. The second holds the intrinsic-value view: justice is a constituent of the agent's own good, so that the unjust agent is harmed in a way no amount of concealment can repair. Make the second position as strong as it can be — not a bare assertion that injustice damages the soul, but a developed account of why the first-personal standpoint of practical reason cannot be a pure maximizer of external goods, or whichever version of the view you think is most defensible.

Now the modification. The ring guarantees, first, perfect concealment in every possible world, including any afterlife in which one's deeds might be judged; second, that the agent's character and dispositions are frozen exactly as they now are, so that acting unjustly under the ring cannot corrupt, habituate, or reshape the agent; and third, that the agent suffers no memory, no guilt, and no alteration of self-conception afterward. The instrumentalist will say these clauses remove every self-regarding cost by construction, so that if the intrinsic-value view still forbids the unjust act, it must be grounding the reason in something outside the agent's good — and it is then unclear why a rational agent must care. The intrinsic-value proponent must say what, if anything, survives, and why the modified ring does not simply change the subject.

Write the dialogue between them. Each should answer the other's actual last point rather than restate a position, and each should be the strongest version of its side. Then step out of the dialogue and commit: say which of the two you take to have the better of the exchange, and in particular whether the modified ring is a genuine counterexample to the intrinsic-value view or a rigged case that leaves it untouched. Give your reasons, and do not leave the verdict to me.
