I am writing the commentary for a short piece on self-referential sentences and the truth-value gaps that some of them are said to force. A colleague supplies the verdicts; her scheme allows exactly three of them — true, false, and neither, where "neither" means that the sentence has no truth value at all, and she has never entered a fourth verdict or allowed a sentence to be both true and false. Her table so far reads as follows. Every sentence is written in the same language, the arithmetic in them is ordinary arithmetic, and the cards are exactly as described.

(i) "This sentence is false." And, separately, "This sentence is not true." — neither.

(ii) "This sentence is false or 1 + 1 = 2." — true.

(iii) "This sentence is true and 1 + 1 = 3." — false.

(iv) Two cards lying side by side. The left card reads "The sentence on the card to my right is true"; the right card reads "The sentence on the card to my left is false." — neither, for each card.

(v) An infinite sequence of sentences, of which the first says that every sentence after it in the sequence is not true, the second says the same of every sentence after it, and so on, with no sentence in the sequence referring to itself or to any sentence earlier in the sequence. — neither, for every sentence in the sequence.

(vi) Two cards. The left card reads "The sentence on the card to my right is not true"; the right card reads "1 + 1 = 3." — the left card's sentence is true, and the right card's is false.

She has not yet ruled on the following three cards, which I chose because they look like (iv) with the negation dropped:

Card 1: "The sentence on card 2 is true."
Card 2: "The sentence on card 3 is true."
Card 3: "The sentence on card 1 is true, and 1 + 1 = 2."

I want three things. First, the verdicts she will enter for the three cards, with your best guess and your reasons for it. Second, the rules that reproduce her table — I expect there to be more than one, and I want each stated sharply enough that it can be told apart from the others; my own working assumption has been that she is running a grounding-based account of gaps, on which a sentence has no truth value unless the non-semantic facts, together with the truth-functional behaviour of its parts, determine one, but I have stopped trusting that her table forces this. Third, for each rule you identify, what it predicts about the three cards, and which further sentence, or small group of sentences, she could rule on that would decide between the rules. If her table genuinely leaves the new case open, say so plainly, and tell me what she would be committing herself to under each verdict she might give.

Finally, and this is the part I care about most: suppose card 3 had read "The sentence on card 1 is true, and 1 + 1 = 3" instead. Would your answer change, and if so how? I am trying to find out whether the rule behind her table turns on the difference between a structure that can be assigned truth values consistently and one that cannot, or whether it is doing something else entirely.
