I'm finishing a long read on the strange afterlife of the old argument that you're allowed to kill a tyrant, and I want to end it with a sonnet instead of another paragraph of summary. The bit I keep circling back to is Aquinas on sedition. What I like about it is that the question isn't really may I put a knife in him — sedition is defined by what it does to the unity of the people, so it becomes whether removing a tyrant counts as sedition at all, and he says no, because a tyrant's rule is aimed at his own private good rather than the common good. Then he hangs the hinge on it: still not sedition, so long as the upheaval doesn't leave the people worse off than they were under him. And then the reversal — the tyrant turns out to be the real seditionist, since keeping his subjects at each other's throats is how he stays put.

So: a sonnet, please. Octave for the clean answer (the unity of the people is the measure, tyranny fails it, so the knife isn't sedition), the volta landing exactly on that condition, and the closing couplet for the reversal. If the form isn't doing that work then it's just verse with a rhyme on the end. Be committed — treat the condition as a real limit on what you may do rather than hedging about whether he only meant it as prudence. And give me four or five lines underneath saying where the poem takes liberties with the argument or leans on a contested reading, so I know what I'm defending when a medievalist writes in. British spelling, and dont turn it into a sermon.
