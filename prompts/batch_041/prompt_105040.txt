I built the following argument for a seminar on Hacking's looping effects and now suspect it proves too much. (1) A classification is scientifically legitimate only if it tracks a kind whose members would have shared the same causal structure even if the classification had never been devised. (2) Psychiatric categories are interactive kinds: once a person is classified, the classification can reshape how they interpret and report their experience, how clinicians attend to them, and which symptoms are treated as central. (3) In such cases the extension of the category and its diagnostic criteria are partly caused by the category itself. (4) So the members of a psychiatric category do not share a causal structure that would have been there absent the category. (5) So psychiatric classifications are not scientifically legitimate. (6) The same reasoning applies to any human kind that can be internalized, including intelligence, race, sexual orientation, and even chronic illness once patient advocacy changes how it presents. (7) But we should not conclude that every human classification is scientifically illegitimate. Therefore one of (1) through (4) is false. I would rather keep (2) and (3) than (5), but I want to know the price. Which step do you reject, why did it look plausible, and what does rejecting it cost a Hacking-style account of psychiatric classification?
