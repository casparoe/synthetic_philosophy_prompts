So I write a weekly advice column for a travel magazine — Dear Wanderer, basically — and I keep getting letters that are all the same shape: a reader was somewhere, everyone around them was doing X, they didn't do X, and now they want to know whether they did something wrong. Tipping at a sit-down place in the US when you're from a country that doesn't. Standing at a red light on an empty street at 2am while a river of locals crosses around you. Joining a queue from what turns out to be the wrong end. Half of these I can answer in a line, but I've noticed I'm just winging it, and my answers don't agree with each other from one week to the next, which readers have definately noticed and emailed me about, which is embarrassing.

What I want from you is a decision procedure I can actually run on a letter in about fifteen minutes: an ordered set of questions that tells me whether the local convention my reader skipped was something they were genuinely obliged to follow, or just the local way of doing things that they're allowed to opt out of at some social cost. Obliged in the real sense, I mean, not just rude — I can already tell them they were rude, that part's easy.

Ground it in whatever philosophy actually knows about this: the Lewis-style story where conventions are just solutions to coordination problems that happened to stick, Hart's point that a social rule is different from a mere habit because people take a critical attitude toward deviations from it, the fair-play argument that you owe compliance to a practice you're knowingly taking the benefits of, and the limits of when-in-Rome. Don't send me a reading list though, I won't read it and my readers definitely won't.

One thing I've been half-thinking about and can't get straight — use it, fix it, or tell me it's rubbish: some conventions seem load-bearing, in the sense that something would actually come apart if enough people stopped, and others are pure shibboleth, a way of telling locals from tourists, where skipping them costs you nothing but a look. I suspect the obligation question turns on which one you're dealing with, but I can't tell them apart in the cases that actually matter, which is the whole problem.

Then run the procedure on three letters and show your work: (1) a reader who didn't tip at a sit-down restaurant in the US because they think the tipping system is exploitative and don't want to participate in it; (2) a reader who waited at an empty crossing at 3am in a city where everyone jaywalks and got jeered at for it; (3) a reader who was told at a land border that there's a small "processing fee" you pay the officer directly, which everyone apparently does. At least one of those should come out in a way I won't like, and I want you to say which parts of the procedure are handing the call back to me because it's a values question dressed up as a factual one. My editor gets final say on anything that reads like legal advice, and the whole column is 600 words, so whatever the procedure concludes has to survive being said in two sentences.
