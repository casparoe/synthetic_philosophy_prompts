After a career in experimental physics — my own specialty was optics — I have spent the past few years giving myself the philosophical education I skipped as a student, and I have been reading Locke's Essay Concerning Human Understanding slowly, pencil in hand. I have reached a passage that I can parse sentence by sentence but cannot get to hang together as a single argument, and I would value a careful reading of it.

Locke sets out the case as follows: "Suppose a Man born blind, and now adult, and taught by his touch to distinguish between a Cube, and a Sphere of the same metal, and nighly of the same bigness, so as to tell, when he felt one and t'other; which is the Cube, which the Sphere. Suppose then the Cube and Sphere placed on a Table, and the Blind Man to be made to see. Quaere, Whether by his sight, before he touch'd them, he could now distinguish, and tell, which is the Globe, which the Cube." He then gives the answer he had received: "Not. For though he has obtain'd the experience of, how a Globe, how a Cube affects his touch; yet he has not yet attained the Experience, that what affects his touch so or so, must affect his sight so or so; Or that a protuberant angle in the Cube, that pressed his hand unequally, shall appear to his eye, as it does in the Cube." And he endorses it: "I agree with this thinking Gent. whom I am proud to call my Friend, in his answer to this his problem; and am of opinion, that the Blind Man, at first sight, would not be able with certainty to say, which was the Globe, which the Cube, whilst he only saw them: though he could unerringly name them by his touch, and certainly distinguish them by the difference of their Figures felt."

Several things defeat me, and I would rather have your best reasoning about them than a digest of what commentators have said.

First, what is actually being asked? The phrase "distinguish, and tell" seems to run together two different achievements: telling the two objects apart from one another, and identifying each one as the shape he already knows by touch. Would the answer come out differently on the two readings, and is the passage indifferent between them?

Second, the wording shifts underfoot. The supposition gives us a cube and a sphere; the question asks which is the globe and which the cube. Is this a mere slip of the pen, or does something turn on the substitution?

Third, why the stipulations that the two objects be of the same metal and of nearly the same bigness? What possibilities is Locke closing off, and would the case lose its point if they were relaxed?

Fourth, Molyneux's stated reason: is this an argument that the ideas given by sight and by touch do not resemble one another at all, or only that the man has had no opportunity to learn the correlation between them? And is Locke's endorsement the same claim, given that he weakens it to the man's inability to speak "with certainty," which seems to leave room for a lucky guess?

Fifth, a variant of my own. Suppose that while still blind the man had been given raised-line drawings — reliefs he could feel with his fingertips — of a square and a circle, and had been told that these are the shapes that a cube and a sphere respectively will present to the eye. Would that supply the missing bridge, or would it merely relocate the puzzle? And what of the simpler variant in which he is permitted to look at the objects while he handles them: does that not settle everything, and if so, does it show that the difficulty was never about the two senses' ideas but only about the absence of any experienced correlation between them?

Finally, I have read that the original letter to Locke contained a second question as well — whether the man could tell by sight alone, before stretching out his hand, whether the objects lay within reach, even if they had been set twenty or a thousand feet away — and that the version Locke printed contains only the first of the two. Does dropping the second question change what the case is meant to establish?

Please stay close to the text and reason the case through rather than surveying the literature; I am not after citations.
