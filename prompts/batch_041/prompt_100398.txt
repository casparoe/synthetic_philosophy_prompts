I am a philosopher whose work concerns welfare and its measurement, and I have been asked to comment on a methodological preface that a colleague in our health-policy school has drafted for clinicians, explaining why the agency's evaluations are framed in terms of QALYs. He is a competent economist, but the preface is doing philosophy without advertising the fact, and I would like a rigorous audit before I write my comments. His précis runs as follows:

"Since the ordinalist revolution, economists have understood that interpersonal comparisons of utility are meaningless. Utility is a representation of a person's preferences, defined only up to any increasing transformation of the numbers assigned; there is therefore no fact of the matter as to whether my satisfaction exceeds yours, or whether a given gain to you outweighs an equal loss to me. Robbins's argument settled the question: scientific comparison requires a common unit, and no common unit of satisfaction exists. Modern welfare economics therefore confines itself to Pareto improvements, while the practical business of comparing policies has passed to cost–benefit analysis in money and, in health, to the QALY. A QALY is not a utility but a unit of health, and health states can be described and compared without appealing to anyone's satisfaction; a year lived in full health is a year lived in full health, whoever lives it. The remaining difficulty is aggregation. To move from a set of individual QALY gains to a policy recommendation one must add them up, and that addition is a value judgment rather than a scientific finding — which is why we report totals alongside distributional breakdowns and leave the weighting to the commissioner."

My own inclination is that this is broadly fair: I take the ordinalist point seriously, and I have always assumed that the live questions lie in aggregation rather than in measurement. But something in the argument moves faster than I can follow, and I would rather be corrected now than discover the trouble while defending his preface in public. What I need:

First, a sentence-by-sentence audit: for each claim, whether a competent economist or philosopher of economics would accept it, wince at it, or reject it, and on what ground. Where a sentence is a standard simplification rather than an error, say so; where he is right for the wrong reason, say that too.

Second, an inventory of the distinctions that have been folded into one another, with an account of what turns on each collapse. I have the impression that at least one technical distinction is being suppressed, but I cannot name it.

Third, an assessment of the practical conclusion. Does the claim that a QALY-based analysis, unlike the older hedonistic calculus, escapes the ordinalist objection survive the repair of the argument? If it can be reached by a better route, give me that route; if it cannot, tell me what the agency must concede instead.

Fourth, and not least, tell me where my own stated inclination is wrong.

I do not need citations, a reading list, or attributions; I can place your points without them, and I would rather have the argument than the bibliography.
