I am presenting a paper next month at a departmental colloquium and need a Q&A preparation sheet. The paper defends a view I call neural-magnitude weighting. The claim is that a creature's moral weight -- how much its interests should count in our practical deliberations -- should be indexed to a species-neutral measure of neural complexity, which I take to be the absolute number of neurons in the forebrain (the cortex in mammals, the pallium in birds and reptiles), rather than to species membership or to any capacity we can only detect behaviorally. I hold that moral weight increases monotonically with that number, and specifically that it rises with the logarithm of the neuron count, so that the moral gap between a creature with a million neurons and one with a hundred million is large, while the gap between a hundred billion and a trillion is comparatively small. I use absolute counts rather than brain volume or an encephalization quotient because I think those measures are confounded by body size. My argument is that sentience and self-awareness are the wrong currency for moral status: they are unobservable, and our tests for them are gerrymandered by whatever the animals in question happen to do, whereas neuron number is at least measurable and is the physical quantity that realizes experience.

The audience is a philosophy department colloquium, and I know roughly who will be there. About a third work in philosophy of mind and cognitive science and are functionalists; they will want to know why the realizing medium matters at all. Several work on animal cognition and animal welfare, including one person who studies cephalopods, and they will be quick to raise taxa whose nervous systems are organized very differently from ours. Two or three come from bioethics and disability studies and will be attentive to what my view implies about human beings whose neuroanatomy is atypical. One decision theorist will ask how moral weights aggregate across individuals. Nobody in the room works directly on my topic.

Draft the prep sheet I will actually use. I want eight to ten objections, ordered by how likely each is to come up and how much damage it would do if I fumble it, each phrased the way that particular audience member would phrase it -- not the way a sympathetic reader of my paper would. For each, give me a reply I can deliver in about two minutes and a candid note on where that reply is weakest or is likely to invite a follow-up. Include, separately, the points I should concede up front rather than try to defend. Finish with the one question I am hoping nobody asks, and say plainly why it is the dangerous one.
