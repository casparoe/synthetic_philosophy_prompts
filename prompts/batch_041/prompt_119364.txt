I am writing up a test case for whether superrational reasoning generalises beyond games with identical payoffs, and I want a second opinion on the following claim. Consider a one-shot, simultaneous game between two players, each of whom knows both payoff matrices and knows that the two of them are disposed to reason in exactly the same way from the same information, though each knows which of the two he or she is. The payoffs, written as (your payoff, the other player's), are: mutual cooperation, (10, 1); you cooperate and the other defects, (1, 10); you defect and the other cooperates, (11, 0); mutual defection, (2, 9). My colleague claims that the superrational analysis carries over intact: since the two players cannot arrive at different actions, only the two diagonal outcomes are live, and each player therefore has a determinate superrational reason to cooperate. I think that claim is false. Please give me your credence that it is true, decomposed into rough probabilities for the following sub-questions: how likely it is that the premise that identical reasoning yields identical choices can be stated precisely for agents who differ in their payoffs and their private information, rather than coming out either trivial or false; how likely it is that, given that premise, the reasoning delivers a determinate action rather than an outcome that only a symmetry-breaking convention could settle; how likely it is that such a convention is the right completion of the theory and that it recommends cooperation; and how likely it is that any such recommendation should override the dominance argument in a one-shot, anonymous interaction. Then say how those terms combine, which of them your final number is most sensitive to, what argument or result would shift it most, how confident you are in the decomposition as a whole, and which single step in it you are least sure of.
