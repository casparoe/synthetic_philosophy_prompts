I'm the knowledge-representation lead on a small team building a collaborative planning assistant, and I've hit a wall that the epistemology people on our advisory call "logical omniscience" and have been no help with. The assistant keeps a model of what each user knows so it can decide what to say, what to leave implicit, and when to flag a gap — but the standard machinery for representing knowledge is closed under logical consequence, so our model ends up asserting that a user who knows the office closes at five, and knows the meeting is at four-thirty, "knows" every entailment of those facts, including tautologies no one has ever entertained and consequences buried behind a hundred inferential steps. That is useless in the product: it makes the assistant either patronizing or silent. I've proposed we stop asking whether a proposition is a consequence of what the user knows and instead classify each proposition into one of four buckets the team has been arguing about — holds (the user has explicitly endorsed it), would grant (the user would assent if asked, and the inference is short enough that we'd expect them to make it on request), could reach (the user has the concepts and the tools to get there, but only with real effort or help), and merely follows (a consequence of what they hold, with no claim that they would ever get to it). What I need from you is a decision procedure that a non-philosopher on the team can run, thousands of times at build time, on a single proposition together with a snapshot of a user's beliefs, that assigns the proposition to one of those buckets, keeps the four from collapsing into each other, and is honest about where it is guessing.

The procedure should be a set of tests in order, with what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where it stops and returns a bucket, and where it must hand the question to a person — the user's own confirmation, a domain expert, or the product owner — because the call is about norms or product policy rather than facts. It has to say what to do in the cases that break naive consequence-closure: a user who holds two beliefs that cannot both be true (so that, under the classical rules, they would "hold" everything), a proposition the user has never encountered but that follows in one obvious step, and a tautology. It should also mark the steps that are judgment calls dressed up as procedure.

Then run it on four cases and tell me which bucket you land on and how confident you would be: (a) a user told the office closes at five and that the meeting is at four-thirty in the office, and the proposition that they should arrive before five; (b) a user who has asserted both that all their meetings are on Tuesdays and that this meeting is on a Wednesday, and the proposition that the office is on fire; (c) a user who holds that all quarterly reports are reviewed by legal and that this document is a quarterly report, and the proposition that this document is reviewed by legal, where the user is a new hire who may not know what "legal" refers to; and (d) the proposition that if it is raining then it is raining. I expect at least one of these to come out in a way I don't like, and I want you to tell me which, and to say plainly what the procedure cannot do — where it will misfire and where the whole exercise is pretending to a precision it does not have.
