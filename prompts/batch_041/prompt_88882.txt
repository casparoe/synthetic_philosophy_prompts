For several years I have taught a graduate seminar on the knowing-how/knowing-that distinction, and I have come to dread the moment when a student reaches for the bicycle example: it is so well rehearsed that the seminar learns nothing from it, and the discussion collapses into the same exchange about whether knowing how to ride is just a compressed proposition about a way of riding. I would like to replace that ritual with an exercise that forces the students to ask which cases actually carry weight. Please rank the following candidate items by how much evidential weight each can bear in the argument that knowing how is not a species of knowing that — that is, how strong a case each makes for the irreducibility of practical knowledge — and give me the result as a set of tiers, three or four of them, with the items honestly distributed; I would rather have tiers I can argue with than a total ordering I will not believe. For each item, say briefly what its defender would have to claim, what the intellectualist must say in order to absorb it, and whether that absorption looks principled or merely ad hoc.

The items, in no particular order: (1) knowing how to ride a bicycle, including the fact that a competent rider cannot say what he does with the handlebars; (2) a jazz improviser's capacity to respond to the phrase the bassist has just played in a way that fits; (3) a taster's capacity to identify a wine, together with the fact that one can be better or worse at this by degrees; (4) knowing what it is like to see red for the first time, on the part of someone who has known all the relevant facts about colour vision all along; (5) knowing how to speak one's native language, including the grammar one cannot state; (6) knowing the way to the station from here — the route, not the map; (7) the capacity to tell when a rule one has been following no longer applies, that is, the know-how of judgment in bringing a rule to bear on a case one has not met before (this is the item I care most about, and the one I expect the students to mishandle); and (8) a colleague's proposal, which I find both attractive and obscure, that what unifies the practical items is their gradability — that one can know how to do something more or less well, whereas propositional knowledge is not like that. I would like you to treat (8) not as a further item on the list but as a proposed criterion for sorting it: does gradability cut where the distinction needs cutting, or does it cut across the list in the wrong place, and does it survive the cases in which someone has all-or-nothing practical competence?

Finally, tell me which items are doing no work at all — the ones a well-constructed intellectualism absorbs at no cost — and which single item you would keep if you could keep only one, with your reason. I am asking for your considered weighing of these puzzles, not a survey of what has been written about them, and I have no use for references; what I want is the ranking, the reasons, and an honest account of the items on which you are least confident.
