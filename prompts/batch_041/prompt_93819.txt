I am working through chapter 22 of Nāgārjuna's Mūlamadhyamakakārikā, the chapter on the Tathāgata, and I have gotten stuck on how its closing verses relate to its opening ones. Here is the passage, in an English rendering with the Tibetan lines that accompany it in the edition I am using:

Verse 1: Not the aggregates, not other than the aggregates; the aggregates are not in him; he is not in them: the Tathagata does not possess the aggregates. What is the Tathagata?

Verse 10: In that way, what is grasped / depended on and what grasps / depends are empty in every aspect. How can an empty tathagata be [conventionally] understood by what is empty?

Verse 11: Do not say "empty," or "not empty," or "both," or "neither:" these are mentioned for the sake of [conventional] understanding.

Tibetan: stong ngo zhes kyang mi brjod de / mi stong zhes kyang mi bya zhing / gnyis dang gnyis min mi bya ste / gdags pa'i don du brjod par bya.

What I want is not a general account of emptiness but a reading of this specific movement, from an interrogation that exhausts the candidate relations between the Tathāgata and the aggregates, through an affirmation that the Tathāgata is empty and conventionally designated, to a prohibition on saying any of the four things. Please produce the following, arguing from the passage itself rather than from citations:

1. A literal paraphrase of verse 11 that separates three things: what exactly is prohibited (uttering a word, asserting a proposition, or holding a view); the scope of the prohibition (all four terms without exception, or the four terms only when used in a particular way); and what work the final clause does.

2. A reconstruction of the argument running from verses 1 through 10 to the prohibition in verse 11, set out as a numbered chain of premises and inferences, with the weakest link identified and the kind of opponent who would reject that link named by position rather than by person.

3. The self-application problem stated as sharply as you can: verse 11 appears to enumerate four options and negate them, which is the very form it forbids. Give the strongest reply available to a defender of the verse, then the strongest counter-reply to that reply, and say which you find stronger and on what criterion.

4. Two readings developed side by side. On the first, verse 11 forbids treating any of the four as a description of how things ultimately stand, while allowing them in conventional discourse. On the second, verse 11 is a therapeutic refusal that asserts nothing at all, not even the thesis that nothing can be asserted. For each reading, state precisely what the phrase rendered as "for the sake of conventional understanding" must mean, and what observation about the chapter would count against that reading.

5. A treatment of whether verse 11 can be read as a fifth position — the view that none of the four holds — and whether that is genuinely distinguishable from the view that each of the four is false. If the distinction collapses, explain exactly where it collapses.

6. A brief assessment of whether the shift in the Tibetan from a verb of uttering in the first line to a verb of doing or making in the next two lines carries any philosophical weight, or is merely metrical.

Close with a single sentence stating which of the following you take verse 11 to be: a prohibition on speech, a prohibition on assertion, a claim about the limits of predication, or a retraction of verses 1 through 10.
