I've got a 90-minute session coming up with our political theory reading group — mostly law and sociology grad students, a couple of philosophers, everyone's read a bit of Berlin at some point but nobody's a specialist. Topic is liberty: negative, positive, republican. I dont want you to explain it to me, I want a plan for how I explain it — the order the ideas have to go in, the one example that carries the whole thing, the misconceptions, all of it.

Here's my problem. Every time I've seen this taught, the carrying example is the slave with the kind master, and the legal status does all the work — students just say "well he's a slave, of course he's unfree" and the concept never actually gets tested. Same with the heroin addict for positive liberty; everyone's heard it and they coast. So I want to try something else, and I want you to tell me honestly whether it can carry the load. Nadia lives with her older brother. He has never once stopped her doing anything — no threats, no violence, nothing you could point to and call an obstacle. But he holds the family money, he's the one the community listens to, and everyone including Nadia knows that if she announced she was leaving, or marrying someone he didn't approve of, he could make her life impossible. He never does. Meanwhile her coworker is under a boss who interferes constantly — shifts, requests, all of it — but through a contract with published rules, an appeal, and the option to walk. So one of them is interfered with and doesn't read as unfree, and the other is untouched and does. Then the third wrinkle: Nadia says she's happy, she'd choose it again, she doesn't experience herself as constrained.

Tell me the sequence: what has to be in place before the question can even be posed to them (I assume they need some grip on what a liberty-claim is at all — an agent, an obstacle, an end — but you tell me what actually depends on what), where the positive stuff has to come in and why it can't come first, and where the republican move lands. Then: what the example has to have and, just as important, what it mustnt have — I'm worried that as soon as there's a contract or a right in the picture the students stop thinking and start lawyering. The misconceptions I expect and where in the sequence to head each one off — my guesses are "domination requires a dominator who intends to dominate," "if she says she's happy then it isn't a constraint," and "republican liberty is just negative liberty with a probability bolted on, freedom depending on what might happen." Also, I'm aware that swapping a legal dependence for a family/care-type dependence opens a can of worms — is dependence as such a loss of freedom, or only arbitrary dependence? If that's a real question and not just me overthinking, put it in the sequence somewhere rather than hiding it. One question I can ask at the end that would show me they got the distinction rather than memorized three labels. And what I should openly say I'm not covering in 90 minutes.
