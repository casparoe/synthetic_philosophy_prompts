I am preparing a seminar on the problem of dirty hands, and I want to use it to test how far a philosopher's system can be pushed beyond the cases he actually confronted. Kant died in 1804, so nothing he wrote addresses the modern shape of the problem: an elected politician who must authorise a wrong in order to avert a catastrophe, who knows the act is wrong, and who is expected to carry the guilt afterwards. I want a prediction of his position, argued from his own commitments rather than from the general impression that he was a rigorist.

Begin with the one passage that bears on the question directly. In the introduction to the Doctrine of Right (1797), Kant examines what he calls the right of necessity, taking the case of shipwrecked people who need the same plank to stay afloat. His answer is that necessity cannot generate a right: what is unjust does not become just by being unavoidable. He then adds that the deed, though wrongful, is not to be punished by a human court. Please say exactly how much that concession concedes, and whether it is a piece of indulgence or a structural feature of his account of right.

Then work through four cases, giving for each his likely verdict and the argument he would give for it: (a) a minister authorises the torture of a captured suspect in order to find a bomb that will otherwise kill hundreds; (b) a government grants amnesty to a warlord whose prosecution would restart a civil war; (c) a prime minister lies to parliament about a military failure in order to hold a coalition together and prevent a run on the currency, bearing in mind Kant's notorious refusal to allow a lie even to a murderer at the door; (d) a leader orders the killing of a foreign politician who is preparing an attack, where no lawful process is available.

I am especially interested in whether he would distinguish a state that legislates an emergency licence in advance from one that indemnifies an illegal act after the fact. Kant has a use for permissive laws elsewhere in the Doctrine of Right; say whether that idea can be stretched to cover political wrongdoing, and whether the temporal order matters morally or only politically.

Next, the guilt question. If the deed is wrong yet unpunishable, has the agent done wrong in the fullest sense, and what does Kant think should happen to him: punishment, pardon, or a private burden of conscience that no one else may lift? Finally, assess the position. Contemporary defenders of dirty hands say that a politician who refuses to dirty his hands is either a fanatic or a coward. What would Kant answer, and is the answer good enough? End with what his framework sees about political wrongdoing that the contemporary debate misses, and what it flattens.

I do not need references; I want the reasoning. Please write in British English.
