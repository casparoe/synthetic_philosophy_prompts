I'm a journalist who covers psychiatry, neuroscience, and the human sciences, and for several years I've co-taught a graduate seminar for students in science writing and philosophy of psychiatry. The final is a closed-book, 90-minute essay exam, and I want a single question that cannot be passed by reciting the standard Hacking vocabulary—interactive kinds, making up people, the contrast with indifferent kinds—which every student in the room already possesses. What I want to test is whether they can use that vocabulary on a case that has been deliberately complicated.

The case I've drafted is a fictional diagnosis, "adolescent affective dysregulation disorder" (AADD), introduced by a professional body after lobbying by parent groups. In the first year, clinicians apply it to a broad range of teenagers, including many who would previously have been diagnosed with depression, trauma, or ordinary adolescent turmoil. In the second year, a patient-advocacy network adopts the label as an identity and publishes first-person accounts that emphasise a distinctive inner life and a shared political history. In the third year, schools and insurers begin using the code to allocate accommodations and deny coverage, and clinicians complain that the category is now capturing people who do not resemble the original cohort. In my own reporting I have started to distinguish what I call "identity looping"—the way patients take up a label and reshape their self-understanding—from "administrative looping"—the way institutions harden the label into eligibility rules, metrics, and gatekeeping. I do not want to give students that distinction for free; I want the question to force them to decide whether the two are separable, whether either is an epistemic defect, and what would count as evidence that the category is tracking a real kind rather than creating one.

Please write the exam question, a model answer of about 800 words, and a marking scheme worth 30 marks. The question should ask students to (i) explain what Hacking's looping effects are supposed to show about human kinds; (ii) apply the apparatus to the AADD case, distinguishing at least three different mechanisms by which the category could change the people it classifies; (iii) assess whether the case gives us reason to think psychiatric classification is fundamentally different from classification in the natural sciences; and (iv) say what a defender of AADD could concede and what they would have to deny. In the model answer, make the crux explicit: the difference between a category that is merely reactive to the people it classifies and one whose validity is constituted by the reactions it produces. In the marking scheme, give a breakdown by marks, name the specific moves that separate a first-class answer from a merely competent one, and list the three most common errors you would expect—for example, treating any social response to a diagnosis as a looping effect, or assuming that looping effects automatically make a category invalid. The students will not have web access, so the question must not depend on citations, reading lists, or any particular paper.
