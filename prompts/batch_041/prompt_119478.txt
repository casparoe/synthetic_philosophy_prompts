I'm supposed to hand in a position statement for a conditionals workshop next week and I keep noticing my views might not actually fit together. I'd rather find out from you then from the discussant. Here's the honest inventory of what I think about indicative conditionals:

1. Indicatives have truth conditions. 'If A then B' is true or false; it isn't merely acceptable or unacceptable, and it isn't just an expression of a conditional credence. I'm not an expressivist and I don't want an error theory about them.
2. Those truth conditions are nearest-world ones: 'if A then B' is true iff B holds at the closest A-world(s). What I like about this is that it lets 'if A then B' be true when A is false — but it is never true *just* because A is false, which is exactly what I dislike about the material conditional.
3. My credence in 'if A then B' should always equal my credence in B given A. Always, not just when I happen to care about A.
4. They embed. 'It's not the case that if A then B' and 'I doubt that if A then B' are things I can think and mean; conditionals nest, and they can be objects of belief, doubt, and probability talk. Whatever a conditional expresses, it's the sort of thing that can show up inside other sentences.
5. Accepting 'if A then B' is just this: you're in a state such that the minimal revision of your beliefs that adds A also contains B. Minimal = you don't throw out anything that's independent of A. I use this in class all the time and students like it.
6. I've been toying with a restrictor story for the 'if' clause: the antecedent isn't a proposition being asserted at all, it just narrows the domain of a modal or quantifier. I like it because it explains why 'if' clauses feel like they're *doing* something rather than *saying* something.
7. I'm sure 'if Maya missed the train, she took the bus' is true if she missed it and took the bus, and nearly as sure it's just false if she flew. But I have no idea whether it's true if she never missed anything, and I dont think that's a defect on my part. Relatedly: 'if Maya missed the train, she took the bus' versus 'if Maya had missed the train, she would have taken the bus' — I'm confident in the first and much less confident in the second, but I don't think there's a deep semantic difference between them; the difference is just which world counts as nearest.
