I am a novelist, midway through a book whose central character is a field archaeologist, and I have hit a problem that is conceptual before it is literary. I need my character to stand over a set of excavated remains and say what they show, and I want to know which of the sentences I put in her mouth the evidence could actually license and which are mine alone. Rather than read up on method, I have been going case by case on instinct, and I would like to know whether my instincts answer to a single principle or whether I am being quietly inconsistent in a way a real excavator would catch. Every detail below is invented — these are the sorts of finds my book contains — so I am after the principle rather than a ruling on any one case.

My verdicts are these. A cemetery of several hundred graves in which adult males are consistently buried with blade-like stone tools and adult females with pierced shells: I am happy to say the community sorted its dead by sex and equipped the two groups differently, but not to say the men held authority. Cut marks and marrow fracturing on human bones in a midden, indistinguishable from the butchery of game animals: I am happy to say the bodies were dismembered and processed, but not to say the people ate them as a matter of custom. A hilltop enclosure with enormous ditches, no dwellings, and a single deposit of cattle bones: I am happy to say crowds gathered there and slaughtered cattle, but not to say it was a temple. Thousands of near-identical figurines of local clay, kept in the floors of houses across a whole region: happy to say a shared image circulated and mattered enough to be kept at home, not happy to say the people held a common belief about a goddess. A skeleton with a badly healed fracture that would have left the person unable to feed themselves for a year: here I am happy to say someone kept them alive, and I feel this more strongly than anything above, even though it is a claim about concern, which is exactly the kind of thing I have been refusing. And a body buried with its grave goods deliberately smashed: happy to say the living expected the dead to have some use for them, which I also feel strongly, and which is again a claim about what someone thought.

What I cannot square is this: I keep refusing to infer beliefs and refusing to infer institutions on the grounds that the objects underdetermine them, and then in the two cases where the inference is most plainly about a mind — concern for the injured, expectation about the dead — I allow it at once and feel more confident than I do about the cases where I am inferring only what people did. I suspect the real difference is not belief versus behaviour but something about whether the pattern could plausibly have come about otherwise, and if that is right I want the principle stated exactly, with the clause that does the work identified. I do not want reassurance that archaeologists argue about this. I want the principle, or a small set of rival principles, that best fits the verdicts above, the verdict I have to give up if I keep it, and what it predicts for a case I have not listed.

Please answer as exactly five numbered items, each one or two sentences, with no introduction and no conclusion: the principle first, then its verdicts on two or three of my cases, then the verdict it forces me to abandon, then the further case it predicts and what it says about that case.
