Here is the draft.

"Fitch's paradox, stated as I would state it: suppose every truth is knowable — for any true proposition, it is possible that someone knows it. Take any truth that in fact no one knows, and call it p. The proposition 'p and no one knows that p' is itself true. By the knowability principle it is possible that someone knows it. But knowledge is factive and transparent to its own conjuncts: whoever knew 'p and no one knows that p' would know p and would also know that no one knows p. From that second piece of knowledge it follows, by factivity, that no one knows p — in particular that the knower does not know p — contradicting the first conjunct. So 'p and no one knows that p' cannot be known, hence is a truth that is not knowable, hence the knowability principle is false; and since the reasoning is general, the principle appears to collapse into the far stronger claim that every truth is in fact known.

Everyone agrees the derivation is valid. The dispute is over what to give up, and the standard menu is short: restrict the knowability principle (to basic statements, or to propositions whose being known does not entail a contradiction), or reject the modal machinery that reads 'could be known' as a possibility operator. I want to propose a third option, which I think has been overlooked and is better motivated than either.

The derivation needs more than factivity and the closure of knowledge over conjunction. It needs the knower to be what I will call a surveyor: someone whose knowledge of a proposition can include knowledge of the totality of facts about who knows what. The second conjunct — 'no one knows that p' — is a negative existential that quantifies over all knowers, the knower included. To know it, the knower would have to be positioned outside the very field their knowledge is about. I claim this is an idealization, and not one the knowability principle is committed to.

Once we drop the surveyor assumption, the step from 'someone knows the conjunction' to 'someone knows the second conjunct' is no longer free. A bounded knower can know a conjunction without having verified each conjunct, when verifying one of the conjuncts would require a survey they cannot carry out. Knowing 'p and no one knows p' would then not deliver knowledge that no one knows p, and the contradiction never arises.

The obvious objection is that this is no substantive move at all: knowing a conjunction just is knowing its conjuncts, so the surveyor assumption is not an extra premise but a trivial consequence of what knowing a conjunction means. I have two replies. The first is concessive: if conjunction-knowledge really does entail conjunct-knowledge, I will abandon the claim about closure and locate the mistake elsewhere — in the assumption that 'knowable' means 'possibly known by a being whose knowledge is closed in this way'. On that reading the paradox refutes an idealized gloss on the knowability principle rather than the principle itself, which is all the anti-realist needs. The second reply is more aggressive: closure fails for belief — the preface paradox is the standard exhibit — and if we cannot assume that a rational agent's beliefs are closed under conjunction, we should not simply assume it of knowledge either. I am not certain the aggressive reply works, and I suspect it may prove too much.

What makes this worth writing up, I think, is that unlike the standard restrictions it does not restrict which propositions are knowable. It restricts what knowing a conjunction delivers, and it is motivated by a general fact about bounded agents rather than by the desire to save the principle. Whether that is enough to make it non-ad-hoc is exactly what I want to know."
