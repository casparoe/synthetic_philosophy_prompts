Exam question. Adams held that the probability of an indicative conditional equals the conditional probability of its consequent given its antecedent, and drew the conclusion that indicative conditionals do not have truth conditions. Lewis, by contrast, assigned counterfactuals truth conditions using a similarity ordering over possible worlds. (a) State Lewis's truth condition for a counterfactual and explain the work done by the similarity ordering. (b) Explain the argument, based on the triviality results, that a truth-conditional treatment of the kind Adams rejects is unavailable for indicatives. (c) A classmate argues that because an indicative conditional and its corresponding counterfactual can differ in truth value, the word "if" must be ambiguous, in the way that "bank" is ambiguous between a river's edge and a financial institution. Assess this argument and defend a verdict.

Student's answer.

(a) Lewis says "If A had been the case, C would have been the case" is true at a world w just in case either there are no A-worlds, or some A-world where C holds is closer to w than any A-world where C fails. The similarity ordering does the work because we do not count every A-world: a world very unlike w where A holds but C fails does not make the counterfactual false, so long as some nearer A-world has C. That is what lets "If I had struck the match, it would have lit" be true even though there are distant worlds in which I strike the match and it is soaking wet.

(b) The triviality results show that the indicative conditional cannot be the material conditional. If we assume that P(if A then C) = P(C|A) and also that the probability of a conditional is the probability that it is true, we can derive contradictions with the probability calculus. So the conditional cannot have truth conditions of the ordinary sort, and Adams is right to treat it as lacking them.

(c) The classmate is right. Take a barn that burned down last night, in fact because of a lightning strike. Compare (1) "If lightning did not start the fire, something else did" with (2) "If lightning had not started the fire, something else would have." (1) is true: the barn burned down, so if it was not lightning that started the fire, then something else must have. (2) is false: had lightning not struck, nothing else would have ignited the barn and it would still be standing. Same antecedent and consequent, different truth values, so "if" must mean one thing in (1) and another in (2), just as "bank" means one thing in "river bank" and another in "bank account."
