I am a software engineer with no formal philosophy background, applying for a one-year part-time fellowship at a university center for AI and society. The application asks for a research proposal of about 1,200 words, and I have only a half-formed idea, so I need you to turn it into the document. Begin the proposal with a single sentence that states the question and what turns on the answer; let everything after it be support. No preamble, no restating of my instructions, no opening paragraph about how quickly AI is advancing.

The domain is the responsibility gap for autonomous systems. The familiar shape of the problem: when an autonomous system harms someone, the ordinary conditions for blame — that some person knew what they were doing, could have acted otherwise, and failed a duty they had — may be met by no one, because the harm runs through a design choice, a training distribution, a deployment decision and an in-the-moment behavior, none of which any single person controlled or foresaw. My half-formed idea is that this framing takes for granted something that deserves an argument: that whether a factor counts as a cause of a harm is a fact about the harm rather than a fact about our description of it. In the counterfactual and structural-model treatments of causation I use in my day job, whether something counts as an actual cause of an outcome depends on how the situation is carved into variables — how coarse or fine the actions are, whether upstream steps like training or deployment are represented at all, and which values are treated as the default or normal case. Recarve the situation and the same harm acquires a different set of causes, or loses them. If that is right, the responsibility gap may be an artifact of one description among several, and the real question becomes which descriptions are admissible for the purpose of blaming anyone.

So the proposal should pose the question precisely, in roughly this form: is the responsibility gap a fact about the world, in the sense that no human agent satisfies the conditions for blame under any defensible description, or a fact about our descriptions, in the sense that for any harm there is a defensible description under which some human is blameworthy — and is there a principled, non-question-begging criterion for which descriptions count when we assign blame? Make the question answerable rather than merely discussable: say what would count as an answer and which considerations would decide between the two options.

Then explain why the existing answers leave it open. Lay out in plain terms, without naming papers or authors, the standard responses — that the gap is real and calls for new accountability devices such as strict liability, insurance, or institutional duties; that it is illusory because some human is always in the loop in a way that suffices for blame; that it is a temporary lag in our concepts and will close as responsibility is extended to new cases; and that the question is malformed because responsibility is a social practice rather than a fact to be discovered. Show that none of them says what constrains the description of a harm, and why the obvious alternatives — more empirical work on whom people actually blame, or a straightforward analysis of legal liability — would not settle it either.

Set out the method as a phase-by-phase plan over the fellowship year. I would settle on two or three small, fully specified scenarios that I can model myself, such as a warehouse robot that injures a worker and an automated braking system that fails to brake; build two or three causal models of each harm at different granularities; and show where the standard counterfactual account of actual causation flips on the question of whether any human is a cause. Then the philosophical work: assemble and test candidate criteria for admissible descriptions — the default or normal course of events, the evidence available to the agent at the time, the purpose of blaming (backward-looking desert versus forward-looking deterrence and repair), and the constraint that a description may not be chosen after the fact to manufacture a culprit — against the scenarios. Finally, bring in the engineering material: incident postmortems, version-control blame, audit logs and model documentation are existing machinery that already embodies a theory of attribution; check whether the criterion I land on matches what competent practitioners do and where it diverges, and be candid that this part is suggestive rather than decisive. Say briefly why I am the right person for this rather than a philosopher — I can build and stress-test the models and I know the engineering artifacts from the inside — without overselling it.

State what a result would look like and what would count as failure. A result might be: the gap is description-relative, and here is a criterion that narrows admissible descriptions enough to confine the gap to a specific class of cases, spelled out; or: description-relativity is real but every criterion we can defend is pragmatic, so the gap is a policy choice, and here is the shape of that choice; or: there is a canonical description fixed by causal structure together with the agent's evidence, so the gap is real after all. Failure would be finding that the flip occurs only under gerrymandered variable sets, which would empty my thesis, or producing a criterion that turns out to restate the conclusion I wanted. Be realistic about scope for a part-time year: flag that original formal work on causation, generalization beyond the scenarios, and claims about legal doctrine I cannot check are the parts that would sink the project, and say what I would cut first.

No citations, no bibliography, no reading list; describe positions in plain terms and I will attach the references myself. Do not hedge with phrases like "many scholars argue" that presume I know who they are. Write it as continuous prose, with headings only if they earn their place.
