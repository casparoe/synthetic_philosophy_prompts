I'm a backend engineer with no formal training in philosophy, and for the past few weeks I've been playing referee in a lunchtime argument between two colleagues that keeps going in circles, so I'd like you to draft the document the three of us should have written at the outset. The subject is the transitivity of "better than." I've been reading about spectrum arguments — the ones that line up a series of options differing by a hair and then point out that the two ends are ordered the other way — and I've invented a version of my own that I find harder to wave away than the standard ones. Picture a dial with a billion settings, wired to a single person for one continuous stretch of time. Setting n makes the experience last 3600 × (1 + 10⁻⁸)ⁿ seconds, with pain held at 100 × (1 − 10⁻⁹)ⁿ on a zero-to-hundred scale. So the bottom setting is one hour of maximal pain, and the top setting is roughly two and a half years of pain at about a third of maximal intensity. Two features of the dial I want treated as stipulated rather than argued about: first, any two adjacent settings differ by at most a fraction of a second of duration (a few microseconds at the bottom, under a second at the top) and by at most a billionth of a point of intensity; second, no human judge, including the person on the dial, can register any difference in value between adjacent settings in either direction — the settings are evaluatively indistinguishable to us. The colleague I'll call the keeper says transitivity is non-negotiable: some adjacent step must be a worsening, and our inability to locate it is a fact about our judgment rather than about the values, so the boundary is real but invisible. The colleague I'll call the breaker says the dial shows that "at least as good as" is not transitive: if every setting is at least as good as its predecessor, and "at least as good as" is transitive, then two and a half years at intensity thirty-seven is at least as good as one hour at intensity one hundred, which is absurd.

What I want is not a verdict on who is right, and not a survey of the literature — please don't cite papers or suggest readings, since I have no way to chase them down and wouldn't know what to do with them. I want the joint statement these two could both put their names to after a genuinely good-faith collaboration, in three parts. First, a numbered list of claims both of them accept: about the dial itself, about what the two stipulations do and don't establish, about what "better than," "at least as good as," and "indistinguishable" are being used to mean at each stage of the argument, and about what would have to be true for either position to become untenable. Second, a table of the disagreements that remain, each one stated in wording its own side would accept as fair to it, with a note on which of them look like genuine disagreements about value or about the logic of value and which might be disagreements about words. Third, the cruxes — the sub-questions such that settling one would move at least one side — and for each crux a concrete test, case, or question that both sides agree bears on it, together with each side's prediction about how it will come out, written down in advance so neither can reinterpret it afterward.

I have some hunches about where the real fault lines are, and I'd like you to address them directly rather than only the ones you find more tractable. I suspect the two sides may be talking past each other about what "indistinguishable to us" entails: whether it means the settings are equally good, or merely that we are not entitled to call one worse, or nothing at all about the values. I suspect the appeal to a sharp but unlocatable boundary is doing less work than it appears to, and I'd like you to say whether it survives the stipulation that the difference between adjacent settings is below the resolution of any human judgment. I'm also unsure whether any version of the money-pump or exploitation argument against intransitive preferences actually bears here, or whether it quietly assumes the completeness that the argument is supposed to establish. And I wonder how much of the fight dissolves once you separate "better in respect of intensity" from "better all things considered," and whether the breaker's per-step judgments are really about the former while his endpoint judgment is about the latter. If you conclude that some part of the dispute is verbal, say which part and why; if you conclude that a specific empirical measurement or formal result would settle a crux, describe the measurement or result in enough detail that we could run or derive it ourselves, rather than gesturing at it by name.
