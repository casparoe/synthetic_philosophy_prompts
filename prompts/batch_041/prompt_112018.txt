The question was:

"Ulysses has himself tied to the mast so he can hear the sirens without steering into the rocks. Political theorists sometimes use this as a model for constitutional precommitment: a people binds itself now so it can't do later what it will badly want to do. Explain the analogy. Then assess this objection: 'A constitution that binds future majorities is just the present majority tyrannizing the future. The Ulysses story doesn't help, because Odysseus was one person and a majority is not.' In your answer, distinguish precommitment from paternalism and say what would make a precommitment democratically legitimate, if anything. You might also say whether it matters that a constitutional rule makes an option unavailable rather than merely costly."

The student wrote:

"Ulysses contracts are cases where an agent, knowing that in the heat of the moment they will choose badly, deliberately removes their own future options. Odysseus knows the sirens' song will make him want to steer toward them, so he arranges to be bound and tells the crew not to untie him. The key is time inconsistency: the same person has different preferences at different times, and the earlier self tries to constrain the later self. Constitutional precommitment is the same structure at the collective level. A constitutional right, like freedom of speech or a ban on torture, makes certain policies legally unavailable even if a future majority wants them. The present majority is like Odysseus, and the future majority is like Odysseus tied to the mast. This is not paternalism, because paternalism is when someone else restricts you for your own good, whereas here the restriction is self-imposed by the same people over time. So the Ulysses analogy does answer the tyranny objection: the future majority is not being ruled by a foreign power, it is being held to its own earlier, more considered choice. The only remaining question is whether the earlier choice was really more considered. If the constitutional rule was adopted democratically and protects basic interests, then it is legitimate. If it just entrenches a temporary partisan advantage, then it is not. Elster's point is that precommitment is rational whenever it corrects for weakness of will, so the real test is whether the future majority would thank the past one. I think that is basically right, though I'd add that courts are needed to enforce the bind, which makes it less like Odysseus and more like the crew."
