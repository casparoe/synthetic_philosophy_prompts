I am a retired experimental physicist who has spent the past three years reading philosophy in earnest, and there is a question about the Church–Turing thesis that I have not been able to resolve on my own. I would like you to answer as a philosopher of mathematics and computation would — someone genuinely at home in computability theory and its foundations — rather than as a popular expositor. Please think the matter through carefully, and begin by working out for yourself which distinct claims might be getting conflated here, before you write to me.

Here is where I have got to. Turing proved, in the 1930s, that every function computable by a finite step-by-step procedure is computable by a Turing machine; that, as I understand it, is the Church–Turing thesis. The result seems to me confirmed from an unexpected direction by the fact that the various independent formalisms for computability — Turing machines, recursive functions, the lambda calculus, and the rest — all turned out to coincide. When several unrelated attempts to capture the same notion converge, surely the notion has been captured. So I take the thesis to be a theorem of mathematics, in something like the way the incompleteness theorems are. It would follow, I should have thought, that no physical system can compute a function that no Turing machine can compute, and that talk of "hypercomputation" is confused in roughly the way a perpetual-motion machine is confused.

And yet I keep meeting the opposite view. A relativist colleague tells me that general relativity permits a device that completes infinitely many steps along one worldline while a second observer, stationed at a point in the future of that entire infinite run, receives the answer after a finite interval of her own time — so that the halting problem could be decided after all. He treats this as a live challenge to the thesis, not as a reductio of the physics.

So: if the thesis is a proved theorem, why is hypercomputation discussed as an open possibility rather than a settled impossibility? And if it is not a theorem, what sort of claim is it — what is its epistemic status, and what could count as evidence for or against it?

Please lead with a direct statement of where my reasoning goes wrong, then give me the correct picture. Answer in no more than 450 words.
