I'm putting together a grad seminar on teleology in history — Kant, Hegel, the whole providence-to-progress pipeline — and I keep getting stuck on one paragraph in Kant's 1784 "Idea for a Universal History from a Cosmopolitan Point of View." Its the pivot where he goes from being disgusted with history to positing a plan in it. Here it is (Beck translation):

"One cannot suppress a certain indignation when one sees men’s actions on the great world-stage and finds, beside the wisdom that appears here and there among individuals, everything in the large woven together from folly, childish vanity, even from childish malice and destructiveness. In the end, one does not know what to think of the human race, so conceited in its gifts. Since the philosopher cannot presuppose any [conscious] individual purpose among men in their great drama, there is no other expedient for him except to try to see if he can discover a natural purpose in this idiotic course of things human. In keeping with this purpose, it might be possible to have a history with a definite natural plan for creatures who have no plan of their own."

Don't summarize the essay at me and don't give me the unsocial sociability bit — I've taught that to death. What I want is help with the inference. Take the role of a Kant scholar who also knows the third Critique well, think carefully, and brainstorm a couple of rival readings of the move before you commit to one.

(a) What kind of move is "there is no other expedient"? It reads like an inference from the absence of a conscious purpose to the legitimacy of positing a natural one. Is that a transcendental argument, a regulative maxim, a confession of a need, or just a bad inference dressed up? Say which and point to what in the surrounding text supports your call.

(b) What is the indignation doing? Is it evidence — the failure of history to make sense at the level of individuals being a symptom that the plan must be located elsewhere — or is it rhetoric meant to soften the reader for the plan, or a problem the plan is supposed to solve and quietly doesn't? And why "idiotic": what does Kant need the course of things to look like before the plan can be supplied, and would a less chaotic-looking history make the teleology easier or harder to motivate?

(c) Is the natural purpose doing explanatory work or evaluative work — a pattern in events, a standard we judge history by, or a claim about capacities (reason developing in the race rather than in the individual, which he asserts earlier)? And if the earlier principle that all natural capacities are destined to develop completely is the thing carrying the weight, is that principle meant as empirical or regulative?

One thing I want to be careful about: I don't want the answer to collapse the difference between Kant's natural plan and a theodicy or a providence story, and I definately don't want it treating "nature" as a disguised agent without argument.

Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then the reasoning. No preamble, no restating my question.
