The two-envelope problem: one envelope holds twice as much as the other; you choose one, may open it, and are then offered the chance to switch. The familiar argument says that the other envelope is equally likely to hold double or half of yours, so its expected value is 1.25 times yours, and you should always switch — which cannot be right, since the same reasoning, run from the other side, tells you to switch back. Below are eight candidate diagnoses of where that argument goes wrong. Rate each on the fraction of the paradox it genuinely resolves, on a scale from 0 to 1, and say which of the others it collapses into if it is not really a distinct diagnosis.

A. The argument uses "X" inconsistently: in the "double" branch X is the smaller of the two amounts, in the "half" branch X is the larger, so the two expectations are taken over different random variables and cannot be averaged.

B. No proper probability distribution over the amounts is invariant under scaling, so the 50/50 split between double and half cannot hold for every observed X; the argument silently relies on an improper prior, and expectations computed with it are undefined.

C. One should partition by the unordered pair {S, 2S} rather than by the amount in your envelope; conditional on the pair, switching wins S half the time and loses S half the time, for zero expected gain. The error is the choice of partition.

D. With any bounded utility function the expected gain from switching is zero; the paradox needs linear utility over unbounded support, which is independently implausible.

E. Before opening, the envelopes are exchangeable, so any argument that privileges one of them breaks a symmetry it is not entitled to break.

F. The correct facts are that the expected *ratio* of the other envelope to yours exceeds one while the expected *difference* is zero; since utility is linear in money, the favorable ratio is irrelevant, and the argument confuses a favorable ratio with a favorable bet.

G. Under the standard improper prior the expected value of the game is infinite, and expected-utility theory gives no guidance when expectations diverge; the paradox exposes a limitation of expected utility with unbounded payoffs rather than an error in the switching argument.

H. The switching conclusion is not an invariant of the problem but depends on the prior; the only priors under which the posterior is 50/50 at every observed amount are improper ones, so the honest answer is "it depends on your prior," and the naive argument illicitly assumes a particular one.
