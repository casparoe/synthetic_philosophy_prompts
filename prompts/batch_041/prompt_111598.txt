I retired three years ago from a career in the biological sciences; most of my working life was spent designing and analyzing controlled trials, and I have since spent a great deal of my time reading moral and political philosophy. The debate I keep returning to is the one over reparations for historical injustice, chiefly the American case, and I find that I cannot read it except through the conceptual apparatus of my old trade: the potential-outcomes, or counterfactual, framework of causal inference, with its contrast between a unit's outcome under treatment and under control, its insistence that a causal claim be tied to a well-defined intervention, and its caution about interference between units and about treatments whose version is left unspecified.

Please set out the most important implications of that framework for the reparations debate. I am not asking for a survey of the literature, nor for a verdict on whether reparations are owed; what I want is what follows for the debate once one takes the framework seriously as a way of stating its questions. Specifically:

- Which of the claims actually made in the debate come out as well-defined counterfactual contrasts and which do not, what the framework has to say about the ones that do not, and whether a distinction I find natural — between reparations as the correction of a present wrong and reparations as compensation for a counterfactual loss — falls on one side of that line or the other.

- What becomes of the non-identity problem when it is restated in the framework's own vocabulary: is it dissolved, relocated, or merely rephrased?

- Whether the requirement of a well-defined intervention is a genuine constraint that the historical case simply fails, or a feature of the framework that ought to make us suspicious of the framework itself, and how those two readings differ in their practical upshot.

- What happens when one tries to specify, in the manner of a target trial — the trial one would have run had randomization been possible — the study that would estimate the effect of a reparations policy, and which requirement of that specification breaks first.

- How the distinction between the effect of a cause and the cause of an effect sorts the empirical questions from the conceptual ones, and whether the disputes that look most empirical are in fact the ones the framework can settle.

- What all of this leaves untouched in the moral argument, since I suspect the framework tells us what can be measured rather than what is owed.

Order the implications by how much they alter the shape of the debate rather than by how often they are rehearsed, and say plainly where the framework distorts the question instead of clarifying it.
