I have no formal training in philosophy, but there is a puzzle about consent that I keep circling and cannot settle on my own, so I would like you to reason through it with me rather than hand me a summary of the debate. Please take the role of a philosopher who works on consent, autonomy, and moral responsibility, think carefully before answering, and begin by setting out in your own words the conditions that are usually treated as necessary for consent to be valid — competence, voluntariness, being adequately informed, and the consent matching what actually happens — before you commit to any verdict.

Here is my case. A person agrees to something — a medical procedure, a loan, a contract — while holding a false belief about it, but the false belief is one they assembled themselves and never asked anyone to check. The other party, who happens to be a professional with a duty of care toward them, notices the mistake and says nothing. That party did not create the error, did not encourage it, and was never asked about it. My question is whether the person's agreement counts as valid consent.

Give me your genuine credence that it does, as a number between 0 and 1, and then decompose the number. I want the sub-questions it rests on, each with a rough probability and a sentence of justification: whether adequate information is really a condition of valid consent or only a means to some other good such as autonomy or welfare; whether a person's own failure to ask can be charged to them as a choice, so that the ignorance is theirs to own; whether the professional's silence is itself a form of deception or merely a permissible non-interference; and whether validity should be judged at the moment of the act or by what the person would have chosen with full information. Show how those terms combine into your final figure, tell me which one your number is most sensitive to, and tell me what argument or fact would move it the most. Do not tell me that it simply depends — give me the number.

Write in American English.
