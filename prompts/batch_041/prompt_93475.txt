I have a maths background and have been reading rationalist blogs and forums for the better part of fifteen years, which means the reference class problem is more or less native to me: you want to reason about some particular case, you have to say what population it is a random draw from, and every answer is either so broad that it tells you nothing or so narrow that there are no statistics left and the choice starts to look reverse-engineered from the conclusion you wanted. I recently let myself be recruited into a reading group on democratic theory, where the recurring topic is what people there call the boundary problem: any account of democracy has to say who the demos is, but the demos apparently cannot be identified democratically without presupposing itself, and the two most popular ways out -- include everyone whose interests are affected by the decision, or include only those actually subject to its coercive authority -- each look either arbitrary or absurd at the edges. The standard hard cases came up (children, the severely cognitively disabled, non-citizen residents, people in other countries downstream of a climate policy, future generations), and someone raised AI systems, at which point I said out loud that I felt like I had read this exact argument before in a completely different key and everyone looked at me. I would like to find out whether that reaction was insight or pattern-matching. My fluency is lopsided: I know the probability and self-locating-belief material cold, and I know the political theory only from this reading group and a few essays.
