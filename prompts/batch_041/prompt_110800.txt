I am the director of a small municipal archive in a river town that was badly flooded two years ago, and the archive is reopening next month. I have to give the address at the reopening: eight minutes, outdoors, maybe two hundred people. The audience is mostly townspeople whose houses were under water, plus the volunteers who salvaged the collections, the mayor, and — because the university's philosophy department helped coordinate the salvage and one of its graduate students ran the drying tables — perhaps a dozen people who have read Wittgenstein's On Certainty closely and who will hear every sentence as a philosophical claim about certainty and its limits.

I want a speech that is honest in a way that this genre usually is not. The standard inspirational address in a situation like ours does two things I have come to distrust. It reassures people that the ground under them is guaranteed — that what we took for granted will hold, that the river will stay where it belongs, that records persist, that the town is simply there and will go on being there. And when the guarantee has visibly failed, it treats the failure as a lapse of justification, as though with better evidence, higher levees, or more foresight we could have been entitled to our confidence. I want neither move. The ground we stand on is not guaranteed by anything underneath it, and the fact that we could not doubt it in advance is not a defect we can be argued out of.

Here is what I would like the philosophy to do, and it needs to do it structurally rather than decoratively. In Wittgenstein's late notes, the things we never doubt are not conclusions we reached but the frame within which reaching conclusions happens at all; doubting everything at once is not a more rigorous version of doubting something, it is the collapse of the practice of doubting. These certainties are held not because we have seen them to be true but because we act on them — they sit at the bottom of what we do, not at the top of what we have proved. He also pictures them as a river-bed: some propositions have hardened into channels that carry the current, others are still fluid and may harden later, and the hardened ones can in time liquefy and shift. I want both halves of that picture in play, and this is the hard part. The first half tells me I cannot argue the audience back into security, because any reason I could offer them already presupposes the thing I would be trying to support; so the speech has to enact the certainty rather than defend it, has to be a piece of the practice rather than a report about it. The second half forbids the cheap resolution: I cannot promise that the channels are permanent, and I cannot pretend that the groundlessness of our believing is something we might one day remedy. The speech has to hold both without tipping into either false consolation or mere bleakness.

Constraints. Do not use the technical vocabulary of the debate — hinge, proposition, language-game, foundational, epistemology — because it would turn a reopening into a seminar and lose most of the room; the philosophy should be doing its work invisibly, in what the speech permits itself to say and refuse. Do not quote Wittgenstein, do not cite section numbers, and do not attribute anything to him by name in the speech; if any of his ideas appear, they should appear as the speech's own thinking, and the philosophers in the audience can recognize them or not. The river is available to you as an image because the town is literally on one, but it must carry the hardening-and-liquefying point rather than serving as local color. My own house was in the floodplain, so I can speak from inside this rather than above it, and I would like to, briefly, once. The speech should end in a way that is not consolation-by-guarantee: something closer to a community resuming an activity it never had grounds for in the first place, and doing so anyway.
