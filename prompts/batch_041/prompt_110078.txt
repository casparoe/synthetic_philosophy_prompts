I'm in a seminar on free will and criminal law and we just finished the Dennett/Caruso exchange on just deserts — Caruso as the hard incompatibilist who says nobody ever deserves basic desert in the sense that would justify blaming or punishing someone for its own sake, Dennett pushing back that a world without desert either can't be lived in or would cost us something real in how we hold each other to account. Now I have to write a paper reading a novel or film through one of the positions, and I've picked A Clockwork Orange (Burgess and Kubrick both) because something about it has been bugging me for a week.

Here's the thing I can't shake. The Ludovico Technique isn't retributive at all. Nobody's saying Alex deserves to be made sick by Beethoven, or that the nausea is a fitting response to what he did. It's a straight-up forward-looking intervention: it's cheap, it works, it reduces future victims, and the Minister basically says as much when he brags about the results and about freeing up prison space. The chaplain is the only one who objects, and his objection is about free will, not about desert. So my question is: if Caruso is right that desert is a fiction we should stop invoking, does the Ludovico Technique stop being wrong? Because it feels like the main popular argument for dropping desert is that it will make our institutions more humane, and here's a case where dropping desert seems to license something horrifying. Is that a real problem for Caruso, or does he have a non-desert-based way of condemning it that doesn't smuggle desert back in? I'd like you to actually try to construct his best response and then say honestly how much it costs him.

Second thing, and this is where I want you to be careful about the two endings. I know the American edition dropped the twenty-first chapter, and I'm pretty sure Kubrick's film doesn't have it either, but I'm honestly not certain about the publishing history so correct me if I've got it backwards. In the film, Alex comes out of the hospital with his aversions gone and the Minister offering him a job, and it reads as the state getting a useful monster back. In the full novel, Alex runs into Pete, who's grown up and married, and Alex finds the appetite for violence just draining out of him on its own — he starts thinking about a son. So the novel suggests the cure was time, and the state's real crime was impatience. Which version does the philosophical work better? I think the chapter-21 ending is a gift to Dennett, since it shows people do change and that treating someone as beyond change is the thing that forecloses it, but I want you to push back on me if that's too neat.

Third, a thought experiment I want you to run. Suppose the technique were painless, fully reversible, and didn't wreck Alex's music. Same conditioning, same removal of his ability to act on his desires, just no suffering and no Beethoven. Does the wrongness disappear? If it does, then the whole objection is about side effects and not about the technique, and I think that's a much weaker position than people assume. If it doesn't disappear, then name the residual wrong precisely enough that a desert-skeptic could sign onto it without cheating. I suspect the interesting thing the book dramatizes, that the debate papers over, is a difference between changing what someone wants and leaving them wanting it while making sure they can't do it — Alex still wants to smash the guy's face in during the demonstration, he just can't get up. Is he less free, or differently unfree, and does either of them think that distinction matters for desert?
