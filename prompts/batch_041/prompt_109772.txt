I am assembling materials for a graduate seminar on historical injustice, and for one session I want a diagnostic instrument rather than another lecture: a decision tree that carries a reader from a stipulated set of facts to a named position on the question of what a present-day state owes, by way of apology, to the descendants of those it wronged, and what distinguishes an apt apology from an empty one. The tree should be usable as a handout, and a reader should be able to answer every node by consulting only the case and their own judgment. Please write it.

Begin from a stipulated case, deliberately composite rather than any real episode. A state, call it R, was governed for two centuries by a monarchy overthrown ninety years ago; the present republic has a different constitution, different borders, and no institutional continuity with the old regime beyond the civil service it inherited. Between about 120 and 100 years ago the monarchical state carried out a sustained campaign of dispossession and massacre against a minority population, using its army and encouraging settlers. The monarchy is gone. Perhaps half of the descendants of both perpetrators and victims have since emigrated or intermarried with one another; a substantial minority of the victims' descendants remain in R, are on average poorer than other citizens, and are the ones demanding an apology; a vocal minority of R's citizens oppose any apology, treating it as an insult to their own ancestors. Over the past forty years R's parliament has three times passed resolutions expressing regret, none of which used the word apology, and has funded a museum and two scholarships. Several of the disadvantages the victims' descendants now suffer have current causes as well as historical ones — land title law, policing practice, the distribution of school funding — so the wrong and its aftermath are not cleanly separable.

Requirements for the instrument:

1. Every branch node must be phrased as a question about the case, or as a claim about it, that a reader can answer without already knowing which position the answer leads to. No node may ask, in effect, which label the reader prefers. Each node should have two or three answer options, and where an option corresponds to no defensible position, say so at that node rather than pruning it silently.

2. Build the tree from the logical space first — the questions and the combinations of answers they permit — and locate the positions in that space second. Where a combination of answers is left unoccupied, say whether that is because the combination is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored, and diagnose it in a sentence.

3. The following positions must appear as leaves, though you should name each leaf descriptively rather than by author or school: that only individuals can bear responsibility or apologize, so a state apology is either shorthand for facts about persons or a fiction; that responsibility travels with institutional continuity, so R is the same agent as the monarchy; that responsibility instead travels with benefit or with complicity, so it falls on those who hold what the wrong produced; that the dead cannot be harmed and their descendants cannot be wronged by acts that predate their existence, so nothing is owed to anyone now living; that the point of the apology is recognition, since it constitutes the wronged as parties who were wronged, and that material repair without recognition is not repair; that words unaccompanied by transfer of resources are not apologies at all; that apology is a standing practice with no completion point rather than a discrete act; that apology should be refused or deferred because a successful apology would be too cheap, releasing the wrongdoer and conscripting the victim's forgiveness; and that the primary beneficiary of the apology is the apologizer, whether because integrity demands it or because the successor community needs to be a certain kind of community.

4. The following distinctions matter for my purposes and should each be forced by at least one node. First, keep apart three questions my students constantly collapse: whether an apology is owed, whether it is apt or felicitous as an act, and whether it is efficacious. Second, the addressee problem: whether the act is directed at the dead, at living descendants, at a designated body, or at an indefinite public. Third, uptake: whether an apology can be complete as a one-sided act or requires acceptance, and what follows if the descendants reject it as insufficient. Fourth, persistence: whether one can apologize for a wrong whose effects are still unfolding, or apologize for a wrong while continuing to commit it. Fifth, standing: whether an official can apologize for citizens who reject the apology, and what that does to the act. Sixth, the level of description: whether apologizing for a type of act (a massacre, a genocide) is the same kind of act as apologizing for a token of it (this village, these months), and whether anyone can coherently apologize for a type. Seventh, the counterfactual wrinkle: whether it matters that some present citizens of R would not exist had the wrong not occurred, and whether that makes an apology self-undermining for them. Eighth, seriality: whether a fourth resolution after three failed ones is a fresh act or evidence that the earlier ones were never apologies, and where a regret resolution belongs if it is neither a failed apology nor a success.

5. Aim for roughly twelve to fifteen nodes and seven to nine leaves. If two leaves cannot be separated by the tree, flag them as a pair and explain why, rather than adding a contrived question to prise them apart. If a position seems to require a node that would be circular, note the circularity.

6. Do not cite or name any authors, books, articles, or real historical episodes, and do not supply further reading. If you find yourself wanting to report what someone argued, describe the position instead.

Close with a short note: which nodes you expect readers to answer strategically, having already settled on the leaf they want, and what that reveals about the instrument's limits.
