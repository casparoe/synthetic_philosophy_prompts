Quick background so it's self-contained: two agents are in a one-shot prisoner's dilemma and can't affect each other at all, no communication, no common cause, nothing causal running between them. But they're both running the same decision procedure, so whatever one does is evidence about what the other does. The classic line is that they should both cooperate, because my cooperating is evidence that you cooperate. Acausal trade generalizes the trick: you can "trade" with an agent you can never causally influence, by each of you doing the thing that's evidence the other does the thing.

Okay, here's the argument:

(1) A rational agent should maximize expected value, and expected value includes every way the world might be that its choice is evidence for, not just what its choice causes.
(2) If another agent's behavior is correlated with my choice, because we share a decision procedure or are both running "the same reasoning," then my choosing X raises the probability that they choose X.
(3) There are, or at least could be, astronomically many agents whose reasoning is correlated with mine: copies, simulations, agents in other branches, agents in other possible worlds who just happen to reason like me.
(4) So when I pay a dollar into the acausal fund, my act is evidence that all of those agents pay a dollar too, and if even a small fraction of them pay me back acausally, the expected value is astronomical.
(5) Therefore I should give basically everything away, since any finite cost to me is swamped by the astronomically many correlated agents.
(6) But that's crazy. A theory of rationality that bankrupts me on behalf of agents who may not even exist, or can never touch me, is a bad theory.
(7) So (2) is false: mere evidential correlation, with no causal connection, doesn't make another agent's payoff count in my deliberation.

I feel like the move from (2) and (3) to (4) sneaks something in, but my study partner says no, the argument is valid and the real price is giving up (2), which would also kill ordinary acausal trade.
