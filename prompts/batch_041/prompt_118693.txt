I'm writing a novel about an actuary who's slowly losing her nerve about the numbers, and to get her head straight I've been reading around the philosophy of probabilty. What puzzles me is that the scrap between the frequentists, the Bayesians and the propensity crowd has been going on for the best part of a century and nobody has budged an inch. It can't be about the maths: everyone agrees on the calculus, on how to run a trial, on what makes a bet fair. The frequentist says a probability just is a limiting relative frequency (or, if he's being careful, the disposition of a setup to produce one); the Bayesian says it's a degree of belief, and that objective chance is either a special sort of credence or a useful fiction; the propensity theorist says it's a disposition of the single case, this coin, now. Then the objections come round again: the reference class problem, the fact that we do seem to talk sense about the chance of a one-off event, and the nagging suspicion that the Bayesian can always redescribe whatever the others point to as evidence about her own credences, which makes her look unfalsifiable and them look like they're doing metaphysics by the back door. My hunch is that it's a fight about the job description -- what an interpretation of probability is even meant to deliver -- and that each side is answering a question the others didn't ask. But I'd rather you diagnosed it than agreed with me. If it's substantive, say what the hidden factual or metaphysical question is; if it's verbal, say what's really at stake in the word. Then tell me what would count as progress: a distinction, a formal result, a change of question. Or tell me straight that none should be expected. I'll be putting this in a character's mouth, so say how confident you are in the diagnosis and which bit of it you're least sure about.
