For most of my career I was a physiologist, and since retiring I have been working my way into philosophy of mind — slowly, and with the lingering habit of a scientist who wants to know what the evidence actually licenses. A recent popular book on consciousness has left me uneasy, and I would like a careful audit of the following passage, which I have paraphrased from memory.

The book's summary runs roughly as follows. The two cerebral hemispheres are joined by a thick bundle of fibers, the corpus callosum; in a last-resort treatment for intractable epilepsy, surgeons sometimes sever this bundle, producing so-called split-brain patients who appear, in ordinary life, entirely normal. Careful testing, however, reveals something remarkable. Because in most people the left hemisphere is dominant for language and controls the right side of the body, while the right hemisphere is essentially mute and controls the left side, an experimenter can direct a stimulus to one hemisphere alone. Words or objects presented to the left hemisphere are named at once; the same stimuli presented to the right hemisphere elicit no verbal report whatever, though the patient's left hand may reach out and select the correct object. From these facts the book draws three conclusions: that each hemisphere is a separate conscious mind with its own perceptions, intentions, and even its own will — two persons sharing one skull; that because the mute right hemisphere plainly perceives and pursues goals that the patient cannot report, conscious experience cannot be the single unified thing we ordinarily take it to be; and that the familiar feeling of being one unified self is therefore an illusion manufactured by the talking half of the brain, which fabricates a narrative to paper over the discontinuities.

My own reaction is that the experimental observations are probably sound while the gloss on them is doing considerably more work than the evidence supports, but I lack the background to say precisely where the seams are. I would therefore like you to go through the passage almost sentence by sentence and tell me: which statements a careful researcher in this area would endorse as accurate; which are legitimate but oversimplified, and what the simplification leaves out; which are outright misrepresentations of what the experiments show or of what the investigators took themselves to have established; and, in particular, whether the three conclusions actually follow from the observations that precede them, or whether the book has quietly imported further premises of its own. I am especially curious about the step from a patient's inability to report what the right hemisphere has seen to a claim about what the patient does or does not consciously experience, since that strikes me as exactly the kind of inference a scientist ought to flag rather than glide past. Please also tell me what the more defensible version of the interesting claim would be, if there is one, and what sort of evidence would genuinely count in its favor.
