I am giving a one-off forty-five-minute session on the surprise exam paradox to a group of upper-sixth (Year 13) students on an access day. They have met a little propositional logic and basic conditional probability; none of them has seen the puzzle before. I want a plan for teaching it, not an explanation of it, and no readings.

Write me the plan, organised around the following six things.

First, the spine. I want the session to end with the students holding a diagnosis they can state and defend, rather than with the comfortable shrug that paradoxes are paradoxical. The diagnosis I have in mind is this: the backward induction never gets going, because its first premise — that the class knows the teacher's announcement — is exactly what the announcement forbids; so the announcement is not false but unknowable in advance, and the exam can perfectly well fall on the Wednesday and surprise everyone. Tell me whether that is the right diagnosis to build towards, and if it is right but not the right thing to lead with, say so and give me the one to lead with instead.

Second, the carrying device. I want to try a device of my own, which I have not seen used on this puzzle. The teacher fixes the day by a rule the class knows: each evening a coin is tossed, and if it comes up heads the exam is held the next morning; tails means no exam tomorrow, and the toss is repeated the following evening, with tails on the Thursday evening meaning no exam at all. The week runs Monday to Friday. The class can then never be certain the night before, so even a Friday exam is a surprise; but the price is a one-in-thirty-two chance that no exam ever happens. If instead the exam is guaranteed to occur by Friday, the Friday exam is predictable, and the induction's base case is restored. Guaranteed occurrence and guaranteed surprise therefore cannot be bought together; certainty of surprise is paid for with a chance of no exam. I think this is correct, but check it. Tell me whether it should carry the session, what it has to do and what it must not do — my worry is that it collapses into a probability exercise, and that the students conclude the paradox has been solved by randomisation when all that has happened is that a premise has been swapped — and what it leaves out that the ordinary version supplies.

Third, the order. What has to be in place before the puzzle has any grip on them? In particular, how do I get across, early and cheaply, that unpredictability is a fact about the class's information rather than about the world, without which the whole thing sounds like a claim about teachers' powers of secrecy? Give me the dependency order of the components, saying what depends on what.

Fourth, the misconceptions. I expect at least the teacher-was-lying reading, the logic-is-broken reading, the it-happened-so-there-was-no-paradox reading, and a version of the induction that treats near-certainty as certainty. Give me the full list in the order it will surface in this group, with the exact point in the sequence where each should be headed off, and which of them I should let run for a while because it is doing useful work.

Fifth, the check. One question to put at the end that separates the diagnosis I am after from its two nearest misses.

Sixth, the omissions. What should I openly refuse to explain at this level, and how do I refuse it without making the session feel truncated? I am assuming modal logic and common-knowledge machinery are out, and that the legal cousin of the puzzle (sentences promising an unexpected execution) deserves one sentence as a hook and no more.

Finally, give me rough timings for each stage, so that I can see where the session will overrun.
