My friend and I have just spent a long evening arguing about whether a Newtonian and an Einsteinian are even talking about the same thing when they both say "mass," and we ended it unable to agree on what would settle the matter. So before I read another account of incommensurability, I want the specification: what must any adequate theory of incommensurability deliver? Set out the requirements, each with the case or consideration that motivates it — the episodes it has to handle (the chemical revolution, the Newtonian-to-relativistic shift in the concept of mass, the move to Copernican astronomy), the distinctions it must not collapse (meaning versus standards of theory choice versus perceptual and practical training; local versus global holism; a graded loss of translatability versus an all-or-nothing gap between paradigms), and the constraints it must respect (that scientists on either side of a revolution do communicate, argue, and are sometimes persuaded; that we can still say the earlier theory got something wrong). Then add the requirements I would not think to ask for: what incommensurability is supposed to be a relation between — paradigms, languages, models, practices, communities — whether it can be diagnosed from inside a paradigm or only in retrospect, and what observations about actual scientific practice would count as evidence against the theory. Finish by telling me which of these requirements cannot be jointly satisfied; my suspicion is that the demand that the theory explain the difficulty of theory choice and the demand that it leave rational persuasion intact pull against each other, but I want to know where the real conflict lies. Don't bother ranking the familiar accounts against the list — the list is the deliverable.
