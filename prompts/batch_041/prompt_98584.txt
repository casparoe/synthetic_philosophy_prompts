I sit on — well, I staff — the commemoration review panel for a mid-sized city, and I've been asked to turn our vague collective sense of "we'll know it when we see it" into something the five of us can actually run inside a two-hour monthly meeting. We get about a dozen petitions a year to remove or rename something, the council votes on our recommendation, and every one of them lands on the local news, so the stakes are real, but the panel is volunteers: a retired archivist, a judge, two neighborhood reps, and me, and none of us can do original historical research beyond what the petitoners and the city records hand us. What I keep running into is that the same object gets defended and attacked under two different descriptions — as a record of what a past era chose to honor, and as a claim the city is making right now in the present tense, with the monument's own rhetoric doing the talking before any added plaque gets a word in — and whichever description we settle on seems to decide the case before we've argued about anything. I read somewhere about counter-monuments that are built to erase themselves, and about cities that relocate rather than remove, and I want the procedure to have room for more than keep-or-scrap.

So: give me a decision procedure — the questions in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where it stops with a no, and where it hands the decision to a vote because the question is one of values rather than facts. It has to force us to pick and defend a description of the object early on, and it needs a real test for standing — who is actually wronged by a monument: the descendants, the people who walk past it every day, the honoree's own community, the whole city — plus a place for the middle options (reinterpret in place, relocate, add context, replace with something new, empty the plinth and let that be the statement), and some honest guidance on the plaque problem, where the added sentence competes with the monument's original rhetoric instead of answering it. Then run it on three cases: an 1908 equestrian statue of a general, sitting on the city's busiest roundabout, who also ordered troops to fire on striking workers, petitioned by a high school history class; a street named for a botanist who was a loud eugenicist, where the actual complaint is that four hundred households and a hardware store would have to change their addresses; and a memorial to the victims of a factory fire that a neighborhood group wants moved because it's become a spot for dealing drugs, which has nothing to do with who's being honored. Include at least one case where the procedure tells us to leave the thing standing and I won't like it, and tell me straight where the procedure will misfire, or just launder a political fight as a checklist. End with a single last line beginning "Verdict:" that names the case the procedure can't settle on its own.
