A colleague in a theory reading group has been pressing the following argument on me, and I have reconstructed it in numbered form because I cannot decide whether it is sound or merely clever. The setting is standard: fix a universal machine; hypotheses are computable measures; K(μ) is the length of the shortest program that computes μ; Solomonoff's prior weights a hypothesis μ by 2^(−K(μ)).

(1) A hypothesis is simpler than another just in case its shortest description is shorter.
(2) Shortest-description length is relative to a universal machine. The invariance theorem tells us that any two universal machines differ by at most an additive constant, and that this constant is independent of the object being described.
(3) That constant is unbounded over machines, and it can be aimed. For any hypothesis μ one can build a universal machine that returns μ on a short input and otherwise simulates the original machine — in effect hard-wiring μ into the interpreter. On that machine K(μ) collapses to a few bits while every other hypothesis pays only a small fixed surcharge. So for any two hypotheses whatever, there exists a universal machine on which the more complex of the two is the simpler.
(4) Therefore "μ is simpler than ν" is not a fact about μ and ν. It is a fact about μ, ν, and a machine. Every ranking of hypotheses can be reversed by a suitable machine.
(5) Occam's razor instructs us to prefer the simpler hypothesis. But if no ranking is machine-independent, the instruction is empty: for any hypothesis there is a machine on which it is the simplest, so the razor never singles anything out.
(6) The same construction governs the prior. A machine can be built on which any chosen hypothesis carries prior probability near the maximum the framework allows, and more generally on which any finitely specifiable set of favourites is favoured at the cost of a few bits to everything else. So Solomonoff induction can be rigged to encode any prejudice you like.
(7) Hence the simplicity that Occam's razor and Solomonoff induction trade in is not a feature of the world or of the hypotheses but of a free choice of description language. The razor is empty and the induction is arbitrary.

I cannot accept (7). I would most like to keep (3), which reads to me as a construction rather than a contentious premise, and I would like to keep the invariance theorem as a genuine limit on how much the choice of machine can matter. I am aware that there are convergence results for the mixture predictor which are supposed to show that machine-dependence washes out, but if (3) is right then it does not wash out, and I take those results to be either vacuous or misstated. My own irritation is practical as well as philosophical: I work with samples of a few thousand observations, where a thirty-bit complexity difference is nothing beside a constant that can run to millions of bits, so a theorem whose reassurance is "at most a constant" reads to me as "at most everything that matters."

Tell me which step to reject, why it looked persuasive, and what rejecting it costs. I want to know what survives of the claim that simplicity-based inference is not arbitrary, and whether the residue is enough to make Occam's razor more than a slogan.
