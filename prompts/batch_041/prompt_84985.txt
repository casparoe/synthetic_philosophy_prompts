You are an expert in formal epistemology — specifically in the logic of confirmation — and in the practice of third-party assurance and audit. Think this through carefully before you write, and begin by brainstorming, in a few lines, the ways a dossier of findings can fail to be evidence for a universal claim; then commit to the procedure.

I am the assurance lead at a mid-sized water utility. Four times a year I sign a statement to the board's safety committee covering thirty to forty claims of the form 'every X on site has a current Y': every pressure vessel has a current examination certificate, every lifting accessory has an in-date inspection, and so on. Each claim arrives with a dossier, and I have about fifteen minutes per dossier. My auditors have no philosophy and no statistics beyond percentages, and my working papers go to an external auditor who will not follow up references, so do not cite literature.

The dossiers that worry me are the negative ones. The last read, in substance: 'we examined 214 items from the asset register; 19 had no current certificate; none of the 19 was a pressure vessel.' Everyone in the room treated that as strong support, and I could not articulate why it might be worth almost nothing.

What I want is a written procedure I can hand to an auditor: steps to run on a dossier, in order, converting the findings into a defensible verdict. It must keep faith with the underlying problem, which is Hempel's raven paradox. 'All ravens are black' is logically equivalent to 'all non-black things are non-ravens', so a white shoe is an instance of the claim; yet examining shoes seems to tell us nothing about ravens. Three consequences must survive your translation into audit language:

1. The procedure must take a stand on whether a finding of the form 'an item that is not an X also lacks a Y' is evidence at all, and if it is, must say what makes it more or less so. Do not pretend this is obvious, and do not hand my auditors a formula they cannot run.
2. The selection procedure is part of the evidence. An item that could not have come out the other way, given how it was chosen, is not evidence. Give the auditors a short sequence of questions that exposes a dossier whose sampling method could never have surfaced a counterexample, and say explicitly what such a dossier is worth.
3. Confirmation and refutation are not symmetric. These are universal claims: one counterexample refutes, and no quantity of clean findings repairs it. The procedure must say what I am to do at that point, given that 'collect more positive instances' is not available to me.

Say also which distinctions from the philosophy you had to drop to make the procedure usable, where it will misfire, and where it must stop and hand the judgement back to me because the remaining question is about the boundaries of the reference class rather than about logic.

Then run the finished procedure on all four dossiers below, showing the steps that do work and not the ones that do not:
A. the 214-item sample above, drawn at random from the full asset register of roughly 9,000 items, of which 130 are pressure vessels;
B. a census of all 130 vessels, every one certificated;
C. an inspection that deliberately sought out uncertificated items using the finance system's list of lapsed contracts; it found 40, none of them a vessel;
D. one vessel found in service with a certificate that expired four months ago.

Finish with a single final line, exactly in this form and using only the labels listed: \answer{A: <label>; B: <label>; C: <label>; D: <label>} where each label is one of CONFIRMS, CONFIRMS NEGLIGIBLY, REFUTES, NOT COUNTABLE.
