I want a comparison of two pictures of creativity, and a verdict about their relation.

The first is Hofstadter's parametric picture: a theme is a conceptual skeleton with knobs, variation consists in twiddling them, and the genuinely creative act is frequently the prior one of deciding which features of the theme are knobs at all, what ranges they range over, and which features are constitutive. That decision is itself revisable, since the representation of the theme is fluid and can slip; so on this picture the space of variations is real but not given, being a function of how the theme is currently conceived.

The second picture, which I will call space-expansion, denies that this is the interesting level of description. Novelty, on this view, consists in adding a dimension or rewriting the grammar that generated the space, so that a genuinely new work is not a point within the old space but a re-constitution of what the theme was. The paradigm case is a move within a tradition that becomes legible as a departure only retrospectively, once the tradition has been re-read through it.

Compare them on the following points.

(i) Once knob-selection is conceded to be revisable and the representation of a theme fluid, does the parametric picture quietly become the space-expansion picture? If so, is that a collapse of the distinction or the explanation of why the distinction felt real? If not, what residual work does the difference do?

(ii) I suspect two cases pull the accounts apart. Entangled knobs: features that a description treats as independently variable but that no practitioner can vary independently, because each is bound to something constitutive, so that the knob turns only in lockstep with a feature that is not a knob. Phantom knobs: a dimension that appears in the theorist's space but that nothing has ever occupied and that cannot be reached by any sequence of intelligible variations, not because it is ugly or forbidden but because the space contains a region with no path into it. Which picture handles these better, and what does each predict about what it is like to work in a form where they occur?

(iii) Which picture better explains the felt difference between a clever variation and a breakthrough, the retrospective legibility of radical departures, and the common case of a work that looks like a variation until one notices that it has altered what counts as the theme?

I am thinking of formal domains — a musical form, a verse form, a proof technique, a recipe — but the question is meant generally, and you need not adjudicate what Hofstadter actually meant; I care about the pictures. Give me a verdict: which is the better account of creativity, or, if the honest answer is that they are two descriptions of one phenomenon, say so and say what each description is good for. Then state what would change your verdict, and where your reasoning is most likely to be wrong. No citations or reading lists; argue from the ideas.
