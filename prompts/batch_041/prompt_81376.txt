I am preparing to lead a session of a small reading group on existentialism, and I have been reading Sartre on my own. Before I commit to a picture of the doctrine, I want to be sure the picture I have is not a caricature. The account below comes from an explainer essay I found while reading about bad faith; it is written by someone who plainly knows the texts, which is why I want a diagnosis rather than a shrug. Here it is, as faithfully as I can reproduce it:

"Sartre's central claim is that human beings are radically free. Nothing determines us: not character, not upbringing, not circumstance. This is why he says that we are 'condemned to be free' — we cannot escape the necessity of choosing. Bad faith is the failure to live up to this. It is self-deception, a matter of lying to ourselves about our own freedom: we pretend we had no choice, that our job, our temperament, or our love for someone compelled us. The café waiter is the showcase example — the man whose movements are a little too precise, a little too eager, who has talked himself into being a waiter the way a glass is a glass. He has fled his freedom into a role. Sartre's prescription is the reverse: sincerity, the honest recognition of what one is, is the remedy, and bad faith is essentially a mistake about the facts, which the correct belief cures. Two consequences follow. First, since no situation can limit what we do, the difference between the prisoner and his jailer is a difference in power and not in freedom; Sartre holds that the prisoner is as free as the guard outside his cell. Second, since we choose everything, we are responsible for everything — our birth, our body, the way other people treat us. Sartre is also unusual in refusing even the Freudian unconscious, since the notion of a mental agency that conceals things from us makes no sense; the censor, he observes, would itself have to be in bad faith. The doctrine is extreme, and it is telling that in his later Marxist work he gives up the pure article and speaks instead of a freedom conditioned by material scarcity."

Please audit this claim by claim. For each one I would like a verdict — fair statement of the doctrine, common simplification, distortion, or flat error — together with a short explanation of what goes wrong and why it matters. Where the summary is a distortion, I want to know what Sartre actually maintains, what a careful defender would say on his behalf, and whether the defender would be right. Three things make me uneasy, though I cannot tell whether my unease is well placed: whether the sincerity prescription belongs anywhere in Sartre's account at all; whether the prisoner claim is a fair rendering of his view of freedom and constraint or something he explicitly denies; and whether the "mistake about the facts" framing is merely a popularizer's slip or instead points at a genuine difficulty in the account itself, since a lie seems to require someone who is both deceived and deceiving. Finish with a paragraph on whether the summary's overall verdict — that the doctrine is extreme and was later softened — survives once the errors are corrected, and one on what a reader who took the summary at face value would most badly misunderstand. Please explain in your own words; I do not need a reading list, citations, or page references, and I would rather have the diagnosis than the bibliography.
