I'm working through Descartes' Discourse on Method (the 1637 one, Veitch-style English) and I've stalled out in Part V, where he's contrasting humans with machines and animals. The gist as I understand it: he says if there were automata that looked like us and imitated our actions as far as morally possible, we'd still have two ways to tell they weren't real humans. First, they could never use words or signs the way we do, putting them together to respond appropriately to whatever is said in their presence — even the dullest person can do that, whereas a machine would only manage it for particular things you'd programmed in. Second, even if they did some things as well as or better than us, they'd fail at others, which would reveal that they act not from knowledge but from the arrangement of their organs. And then he says the same two marks distinguish us from beasts.

Here's what I can't get straight. He presents these as ways to tell, but they read to me less like observations and more like conclusions he's already derived from the fact that machines have no rational soul — so is the test doing any work, or is it just a restatement of the metaphysics in behavioral clothing? If it's the latter, in what sense could a machine ever fail a test whose outcome was fixed in advance? And if it's the former, I don't see how he gets from "no machine will ever arrange words appropriately" to a necessary truth rather than a claim about what 17th-century clockwork can do.

I'd also like help with what "knowledge" is doing in the second test. Why would failing at some tasks reveal the absence of knowledge rather than just the absence of a certain kind of mechanism? And there's a tension I keep tripping on: he says beasts are machines and lack souls, and he uses the same two marks for them, but beasts clearly do respond to things said to them in some sense and do some things well and others badly. So either the marks are looser than they look or the beast case is meant to show something different.

Can you walk me through the passage and untangle (a) what exactly the two criteria are criteria *of*, (b) whether the argument is a priori or empirical and whether Descartes can have it both ways, and (c) how this stands relative to later behavioral criteria for mind — I know the Turing test is often called a descendant, but I want to know precisely where the family resemblance holds and where Descartes is doing something Turing explicitly refuses to do. Also tell me if I'm wrong that the second criterion is the one that's actually load-bearing, since the first one alone would be satisfied by any sufficiently flexible mechanism and he seems to know that.
