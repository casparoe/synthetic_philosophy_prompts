For most of my career I have priced personal lines, where the reference class problem — though I would never have called it that — is simply the daily business of choosing rating factors. A recent project has pushed it from a technicality into something closer to a crisis of conscience. We filed a new risk-classification scheme for a book of business, and the filing was challenged on the ground that one of the factors we use to sort risks into classes is, as the objector put it, a proxy for a protected characteristic. I discovered that I could defend any given classification and dismantle any other with equal fluency, and a colleague with a philosophy background told me I had reinvented a problem that has a name. What I want from you is this: take the philosophical debate over the reference class problem and restate it, as faithfully as you can, in the vocabulary I actually work in — risk classes and rating factors, the tension between homogeneity and credibility, Bühlmann-style credibility weighting that blends a class mean with an individual's own experience, cohort versus period measures in a life table, hazard and survival models, GLMs, in-sample versus out-of-sample performance, and the whole apparatus of choosing a level of classification granularity. I am fluent in that vocabulary, having been through the exams, and I have essentially no philosophy, so please do not presuppose the philosophical idiom; translate into mine.

Then give me the residue report. Which parts of the philosophers' dispute map cleanly onto the actuarial trade-off between a finer class whose members resemble one another and a coarser class that carries more data? Which parts survive only under distortion — when the philosophers speak of a degree of belief rather than a long-run frequency, or of a propensity attaching to a single case, is there anything in my apparatus that answers to that, or am I obliged to pretend the distinction does not exist? And what has no counterpart at all: the suspicion that there is one correct class for a given individual, the thought that which class is right depends on the decision the probability will feed into, the difference between a class that is arbitrary and one that is merely convenient. I would particularly like your honest view on that last contrast, because in my world a class is right when it prices and reserves well and a regulator will let me use it, and I genuinely cannot tell whether that is a debunking of the philosophers' question or merely an admission that my vocabulary was never built to ask it.
