My friend and I spent most of Saturday arguing about how much we owe strangers in danger, and by the end I could no longer tell what my own view was, so I want to see what principle my verdicts actually commit me to. On a walk I come across a child drowning in a pond, and wading in costs me a good coat and my phone: I must go in. Same pond, but thirty other adults are on the bank, all able to swim, so my going in changes nothing: still mine to do. A child I will never meet, whose name and photograph a charity has sent me, will die unless I send $200: I must send it, and I resent it. The same $200 to the same charity's general fund, which saves a life with high probability but names no one: admirable, not required. Saving the drowning child means accepting a one-in-a-thousand chance of drowning myself: required. A one-in-five chance: not required. It means breaking a promise to sit with my mother while she dies: not required, I get to stay. It means spending the money I had put aside for my own child's operation: not required. The person drowning turns out to be an adult who ignored the warning signs: required all the same.

Any one of these I can defend on its own; together they look inconsistent. If I am obliged to accept a one-in-a-thousand chance of dying, I cannot see how $200 is a cost I am permitted to refuse, and if a promise to my dying mother is too much to ask, I cannot see how the chance of dying is not. Is there a principle, or a small set of them, that yields all nine verdicts — each clause stated precisely, with the case that forces it — and if not, which verdict should I give up? And tell me what the surviving principle says about the case I keep going back and forth on: a sponsorship scheme that hands me the name and photograph of one child at a time, out of a queue of thousands, each of whom $200 would save. My friend says I am rationalizing; I would like to know whether he is right.
