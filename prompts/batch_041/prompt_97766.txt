Here's the half-formed idea. I keep arguing with a friend about the units-of-selection thing and we never get anywhere, and I've started to suspect it's because everyone in the debate is answering a different question and calling it the same one. There's the question of what bears adaptations — what the design in nature is *for*, so to speak — and there's the question of what causes evolutionary change, and there's a third thing, what entity's fitness you can actually predict from, and I think these get run together constantly. My hunch is that the debate only looks interminable because "unit of selection" is doing all three jobs at once and nobody notices when they swap.

What I want to try is to import an interventionist account of causation — the kind of thing where X causes Y just in case intervening on X changes Y — and use it as a criterion. Roughly: a level counts as the unit of selection just in case the fitness covariances at that level are invariant under interventions on entities at lower levels. Then the real work is seeing whether that criterion (a) gives a determinate verdict in the cases everyone fights about — meiotic drive, sterile castes in the social insects, the transition to multicellularity — and (b) doesn't just collapse into "well then it's always the gene" or "it depends what you're asking." I also want to steal the notion of a module from evo-devo and ask whether "unit of selection" should just be "the level at which the fitness structure is modular," because I think that might be the same idea, and if it is that's interesting, and if it isn't that's even more interesting.
