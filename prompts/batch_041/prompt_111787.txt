I recently read a book on divine providence — a serious work of popular theology rather than a polemic — and its chapter on how God can know in advance what free creatures would do has left me unsure how far to trust it. Here is its argument, as fairly as I can reconstruct it.

The author presents what he calls the middle-knowledge solution to the problem of providence. On this view God's knowledge falls into three layers. First, God knows all necessary truths and every possibility; the author calls this His natural knowledge. Second, God knows, for every possible free creature and every set of circumstances it might be placed in, exactly what that creature would freely choose — and here he gives a worked example: God knows that a certain merchant, were she placed in a particular city in a particular year, would freely refuse a bribe, and on that basis arranges for her to be there. He stresses that this second layer is middle because it concerns neither what must be the case nor what God has decided to bring about, but the free choices of creatures who do not yet exist. He sometimes glosses it, though, as God's foreknowledge of creaturely choices, and treats the two as interchangeable. Crucially, he says, this middle knowledge is logically after God's decree of which world to actualise: God first settles what He will create, and then, given that decision, knows infallibly which free choices will occur in it, which is what lets Him be certain that His purposes will be achieved without ever coercing a single will. The author notes that the position was developed in the sixteenth century against the rival view that God determines the will from within by a special physical impulse, and that the dispute turned on whether predestination rests on God's decree alone or on His foreknowledge of how creatures would freely respond. He closes by conceding that critics have asked what makes these counterfactual claims true in the first place — a question he dismisses as an unfortunate technicality that the position has never satisfactorily answered, and which he takes to show that the whole scheme is a clever device for holding divine sovereignty and human freedom together rather than a genuine account of how they are compatible.

I am inclined to think the position is more defensible than he allows, and I have a nagging sense that he has quietly swapped it for a caricature somewhere, but I cannot see where. Could you go through the summary paragraph by paragraph and tell me what a Molinist would accept as a fair statement of his view, what he would wince at, and what is simply wrong? Where the author is offering a standard objection rather than a neutral description, please say so; where he has saddled the position with a claim its opponents make, flag it; and where he is simply using a technical term loosely, point out what the term actually marks. Where he goes wrong, give me a corrected version of the offending claim in a sentence or two. Finally, tell me whether his dismissive conclusion survives once the description has been corrected. Please answer in British English.
