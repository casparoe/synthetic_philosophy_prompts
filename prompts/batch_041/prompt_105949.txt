Okay, weird ask, bear with me.

I write novels — two out, both about people who take an idea further than any sane person would — and I'm starting a third, but before I commit I want to try the material as a short story first. So: write me one.

What I'm circling is Nietzsche's late writing about his own body. Not the Übermensch stuff everyone already knows. The other stuff: the book where he goes through his own diet, the climate he lives in, how far he walks, how he sleeps, and treats all of it as more decisive for his thinking than any argument he's ever read. He thinks a persons stomach shows up in their philosphy. He treats alchohol as a catastrophe and files it away with religion as one of the great European drugs. He says he's a decadent and the opposite of a decadent at the same time, and that what cured him wasn't an idea but cold air, plain food, and long walks. And he has this notion of a health that you don't just have — you have to be ill first, and then earn it. I find the whole thing funny and also genuinely frightening. If every thought has a metabolism, what's left of truth?

So: a short story, 1200–1600 words, about a man who takes that as a method rather than a mood. He keeps a ledger. Three columns: ate / slept / believed. Every conviction he holds gets logged against what he ate that day, how he slept, the weather, how far he walked — and he starts striking out beliefs that only ever show up on bad weeks. At first it's funny, and it works. Then it works on something he can't afford to lose.

A few things I want:

- He is not a professor, and nobody in the story explains Nietzsche. No lectures, no name-dropping. The philosophy lives in the details — the ledger, the grocery list, the route he walks.
- Present day, ordinary life. No sanatoriums, no mountains, no mystics.
- Take a side. Either the ledger is real self-knowledge, or it's a machine for never having to be wrong. Don't hedge — but don't let the character settle it by announcing the verdict either.
- One scene where he runs the method on a person who matters to him — a marriage, a friend, a job — and the reader sees the bill.
- Optional foil if you want one: somebody running the same experiment with the opposite sign, fasting and silence and cold, and getting somewhere too.
- Last image: his body doing something the ledger has no column for.

That's the shape. Go.
