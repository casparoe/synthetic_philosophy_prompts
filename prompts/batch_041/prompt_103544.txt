I chair our county's historical marker commission — we're the volunteer board that decides what goes on the bronze plaques at courthouse squares and whether old markers come down. (I'm a retired middle-school history teacher, four years on the board, and we meet in the old courthouse annex, which has the worst parking of any building in the county, but that's neither here nor there.) We've recieved a petition, signed by about 600 people, to remove the 1987 plaque honoring Mayor Wendell Ashcombe, who gave the land for Ashcombe Park and the library wing, but who also, in the 1920s, was the public face of the county's eugenics board and wrote pamphlets urging sterilization of "defectives." The plaque mentions only the land gift. Two commissioners want to leave it up; their line is that you can't judge a man of 1925 by the standards of 2025, and anyway his charity is real and the plaque is about the charity. A third wants it gone entirely. I have to circulate a written opinion before next Thursday's vote.

I'd like you to draft that opinion for me — recommend one concrete course of action (keep, remove, or replace/contextualize, with a time frame and who does the work), give the reasoning the other commissioners could actually respond to, and engage the strongest version of the "standards of the time" argument rather than brushing past it. Here's the wrinkle that's been nagging me: that argument seems to assume a time has one standard, but I keep reading that plenty of people in Ashcombe's own day — church groups, the Black press, a few scientists — were already saying eugenics was monstrous, so it's not clear he was "of his time" at all. Which makes me wonder whether the real question is about judging the past, or about who gets to decide what a community commemorates now, and whether those are even the same question.

If I've got this tangled up — if the "can we escape the standards of our own time" angle is a red herring for a decision that's really about public space and whose history counts, or if the memo format can't really hold the philosophical part — tell me straight instead of producing something tidy. I'd rather you push back on my framing than flatter it.
