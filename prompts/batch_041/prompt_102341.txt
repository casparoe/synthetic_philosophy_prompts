Okay so I had a four-hour fight with my friend Devin last night and now I need to get my head straight, because — great timing — I'm giving a talk on exactly this on Thursday.

Devin's the kind of Kantian who thinks moral autonomy is the whole ballgame: never do a thing just because you were told to, always ask whether you could will it as a universal law, conscience over code. And somewhere around midnight I realized I'd been arguing the opposite, and that I actually believe it. My claim isn't that Germans are better people. It's that the German way of doing morality — the rules-and-order way — has the better of it than the American conscience way, because a morality built on shared, public, followable rules is more honest and more just than one built on each person consulting their inner voice: the rules bind the powerful too, and they don't pretend we all have equal moral insight. The part that really set him off was when I said the American conscience-morality is actually the more conformist and more dangerous of the two, it just hides its conformity behind the word 'authenticity' and dumps collective problems onto individuals and calls that freedom. What kicked the whole thing off was him saying a good person sometimes has to break the rules, and me saying that's the American disease right there — the assumption that the rules are for other people and that the virtuous individual is the one who rises above them.

So Thursday I'm presenting this, and I need a prep sheet, not a defense. The room is mostly American moral psychologists — the moral-foundations, run-a-big-cross-cultural-survey kind — plus a couple of Kant scholars, and one emeritus historian who spent his whole career on ordinary Germans under the Nazis. So I've got empiricists who'll say my entire thing is vibes and anecdotes, Kantians who'll say I've traded autonomy for heteronomy, and a historian who is going to bring up the obvious thing and I know it's coming.

Give me the objections I'll actually get from THIS room — not the generic ones I'd get from a Reddit thread — ordered by how likely each is and how much damage it does, each phrased the way that particular person would actually put it, with a two-minute reply for each and an honest note on where my reply is soft. Tell me what to concede up front and what to hold, and finish with the one question I'm praying nobody asks.
