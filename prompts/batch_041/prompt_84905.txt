So I'm in 11th grade and I run the philosophy club at my school, and last week we had an argument that got completely out of hand. It started because someone said you can know something in one situation and not know the exact same thing in another situation even though literally nothing about your evidence changed. My example was that I know where my little brother's epi-pen is when I'm just answering a question about it, but when he's actually having a reaction and I'm the one who has to grab it, saying 'I know it's in the kitchen drawer' feels like it's doing something different, and I couldn't tell if that was me being nervous or the word actually changing meaning. Half the club said the word just means different things depending on who's talking and what's going on around them, and the other half said no, whether you know something is a plain fact about you and your evidence and we're just more reluctant to say 'know' when the stakes are high. Then my friend Priya said something I still can't get my head around, which is that maybe it depends on how much the person *judging* the sentence has riding on it, not the person doing the knowing, and everyone just kind of moved on because nobody knew what to do with that. I've been reading alot about this since and I've realized there are like five or six different things people actually mean when they say knowledge depends on the situation, and they keep talking past each other, so for our booth at the club fair I want to make a quiz that sorts people into whichever one they actually believe.

Here's what I need. Nine questions, and each one should be a tiny concrete case with two or three possible answers, phrased as what someone would actually say about that case — like 'she knows', 'she doesn't know', 'she knows but she shouldn't bet anything on it' — not as 'do you think practical stakes affect knowledge?', because everyone in my club has already memorized which answer sounds philosophical and just picks that one. I want people sorted by their verdicts on cases, so the cases have to be ones where the different views actually come apart. Please include at least: a case where the same person with the same evidence is in a chill situation and then a serious one, so I can see whether they think the verdict flips at all; a case where the person doing the knowing is totally calm but the person saying the sentence has a ton riding on the outcome, since that's the only way I can tell contextualism apart from the encroachment thing; a case where somebody a week later is watching a video of the original claim and judging it, because Priya's idea needs its own question or it just vanishes; a pair of cases that separates 'the stakes raise the bar for how sure you have to be' from 'the stakes add new things you have to rule out', so one where the scary possibility is something the person had already checked and one where it's something brand new that only just became relevant; one where the person genuinely knows but you still think it would be wrong for them to act on it, to see if they'll let knowing and being allowed to act come apart; and one where the stakes go up *after* the belief was formed, so I can see whether they think the knowledge gets downgraded retroactively or whether it was never there. Use my epi-pen thing if it fits, and you can invent others — mushroom picking, ice on a pond, whatever, as long as the options sound like something a normal person at a club fair would say and the cases aren't just the bank example everyone uses.

Then the scoring key, which is honestly the part I can't do myself. Map patterns of answers onto the positions: plain invariantism where the facts about knowledge never move; the view where the truth of the sentence shifts with whoever is talking and their situation; the view where it shifts with the situation of the person being described; the view where it shifts with whoever is evaluating it later; and the one where the truth never moves but what you're allowed to *say* or *rely on* does. If there are more positions than that, add them as leaves, but tell me which ones are which instead of just listing names. Also please tell me two things I'm worried about: first, which combinations of answers nobody actually holds, and for each one whether that's because the combination is incoherent, or unattractive, or just nobody's bothered — I want to be able to say something smart about the empty cells at the booth. Second, which of the positions my quiz simply cannot separate no matter how people answer, especially if it's because all my questions are about the person's own present-tense knowledge and none of them are about judging somebody else. And if two of the positions only come apart on one question, say so, because then one bad question ruins the whole thing. Don't put the names of the positions in the questions themselves, the people taking it should have no idea which answer leads where.
