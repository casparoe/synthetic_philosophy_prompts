You are a philosopher of logic and mathematics, at home in formal systems, self-reference, and the epistemology of mathematical discovery, and I would like you to think this through carefully and at length rather than produce a survey. Background. I am trying to get clear on the move that is sometimes called jumping out of the system (JOOTSing): someone stops operating inside a formal system and starts thinking about it instead. What I cannot settle is which of the moves I admire are genuine escapes and which are merely more of the same, one level up. Below are my verdicts on a series of cases. The verdicts are gut reactions, and I would rather have a principle than a defence of them.

1. MIU. Take the string-rewriting system whose only axiom is MI, with four rules: append U to any string ending in I; double whatever follows the M; replace any occurrence of III with U; delete any occurrence of UU. One student grinds away at derivations, trying to reach MU. A second student observes that in every derivable string the number of I's is not divisible by three, while MU has no I's at all, and concludes that MU is out of reach. Verdict: the second student has jumped out of the system.

2. A small system of my own. Axiom: the one-letter string p. Rules: (i) from any string, append pq; (ii) from any string, prepend p and append q; (iii) from any string, form its double. A friend spends a week trying to derive ppqpqqq. I notice that in every derivable string, and in every prefix of every derivable string, the number of p's is at least the number of q's — ppqpqqq violates that only at its final letter — and conclude that it is underivable. Verdict: I have jumped out of the system.

3. Gödel. The incompleteness proof, by which we establish of a formal system that it has an undecidable sentence if it is consistent. Verdict: a genuine jump.

4. The truth hierarchy. To say that a system's sentences are true, or that the system is consistent, one has to move to a metalanguage, and to say anything about that, to a meta-metalanguage; the ascent never terminates. Verdict: not a jump. When I climb a rung I have escaped nothing; I have merely found a larger system to stand in.

5. Adding an axiom. Someone who cannot derive MU adds a rule that makes MU derivable; a set theorist resolves the independence of the continuum hypothesis by adopting a fresh axiom. Verdict: not a jump. The system has been changed rather than escaped, and the old question has not been answered so much as replaced.

6. Redundancy. Showing that one of the MIU rules is derivable from the other three. Verdict: not a jump. It is a theorem about the system, and it feels like the very thing the system does, merely aimed at its own rule list.

7. Models. Showing that the p/q rules are sound for the prefix condition by reading each string as a path that never dips below zero — that is, by giving the system a subject matter. Verdict: a jump, and I suspect it is case 2 seen from the other side.

Two principles I have tried, neither of which I can make fit.

(A) A move is a jump only if it establishes something the system could not have established, because the system cannot even state it. This saves cases 1, 2, 3 and 7, but it makes case 4 a jump, so I lose my verdict there; and case 6 starts to look like a jump as well, since the system cannot state a fact about its own rules, which I also resist.

(B) A move is a jump only if no extension of the system, however strong, can represent it as a step of the system. This keeps cases 1, 2, 6 and 7 as I want them, but it makes case 4 a jump and case 3 not one, inverting two of my verdicts at once.
