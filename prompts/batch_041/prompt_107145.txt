I have no background in law or philosophy, and I am not asking for a reading list or a survey of the literature. I have simply been thinking on my own about a measure my town may vote on, and I keep running into an analogy that I suspect is carrying more weight than it can bear.

The measure would write a greenbelt rule into the town charter, so that a future council could repeal or weaken it only by a supermajority vote followed by a public referendum. Its supporters put the case in a familiar shape: the town is Ulysses, the sirens are the developers and the money they bring, and the charter is the mast. On this telling, the town, knowing how future councils will be tempted, ties its own hands in advance, and doing so is not a surrender of self-government but an exercise of it, since a person who binds himself against a foreseen weakness is governing himself rather than being governed.

I want you to audit that analogy rather than rule on the measure. Please give me the following, in this order.

First, a correspondence table. For the story as it is usually told, set out what plays the part of Ulysses, of the crew, of the ropes and the mast, of the wax the crew puts in its own ears, of the sirens, and of the song; and map each onto something in the charter measure. Where there is no counterpart, say so plainly rather than inventing one.

Second, the places the mapping breaks. I have noticed two wrinkles and want your judgment on whether they are fatal or merely cosmetic. In the story, Ulysses gives the order and is then bound by it, but the crew who actually hold him to the mast are third parties who were never asked whether they agreed to the arrangement, and who are instructed to ignore him if he begs; in the town, the people who vote the rule in are not the people it will bind, and the future council is somehow at once the Ulysses and the crew. And Ulysses is warned of one specific, irresistible temptation that he can hear and recognize when it comes, whereas the town is binding itself against temptations it cannot yet name, some of which may in hindsight look less like seduction and more like good sense. Tell me which disanalogies actually threaten the argument and which are decoration.

Third, what the story quietly imports. I suspect the picture smuggles in assumptions the town does not share: that the agent binding itself is a single thing with one settled will across time, that the temptation is a known quantity, that there is a clean line between the self that binds and the self that is bound. Lay out what the source story assumes and the target lacks.

Fourth, the verdict on the conclusion. Once the breaks are on the table, does the claim survive that entrenchment is an exercise of self-government rather than a limit on it? Say clearly what the analogy can still support and what it cannot.

Fifth, a better analogy, if there is one. I have also heard the measure defended as a promise the town makes to its future self, and compared to a prenuptial agreement between the town and its future officials. Tell me whether either of those does the work better, or whether some other comparison fits the case more honestly.

I would rather have your reasoning than a recital of what others have said about precommitment in general.
