Two members of a research group I sit on spent most of a meeting arguing about the "comparability module" in a wellbeing model the group is building. The module takes two people's self-reported states and outputs a single number expressing how much better or worse off one is than the other on a common scale, calibrated to be stable across contexts and over time; the group intends to use its outputs to divide a fixed budget among programs that benefit different people. One of them works on measurement theory and thinks the module cannot be coherent even in principle; the other works on distributive justice and thinks it is fine and that the objections are misplaced. Here is roughly how it went.

(1) He said that "better off than" is a comparative relation that at most orders people, and that the ordering is all the concept carries: even granting that each person's wellbeing is well defined and that any two people can be compared, there is no further fact about how much better off one is, so any single number the module emits is a convention, and a policy that turns on those numbers is turning on a convention. (2) She said the convention charge proves too much: we say with full confidence that a person in agony is worse off than someone with a mild headache, and we can say such a gap has narrowed or widened, and that is a determinate claim, not a stipulation. (3) He replied that everything she just described is ordinal — worse off, gap widened — and can be captured by an ordering; the module is making a cardinal claim, and that is the step he is rejecting. (4) She said the cardinal step is not a measurement claim at all but a moral one: once you know the size of a gap, weighting it is a distributive choice, and adopting a principle (say, giving priority to the worse off) is not the same as misreading the facts. (5) He said that concedes his point — if the number becomes usable only once you have chosen the weighting, then the number is a decision procedure wearing the costume of a measurement, and its apparent content depends on the ethics. (6) She said no: the module supplies the facts and the principle supplies the trade-off, and keeping them separate is exactly what lets us argue about the trade-off honestly. (7) He then pressed that "how much worse off" has no common unit: length comparisons work because both objects have the same dimension, but two people's wellbeing does not share a dimension, so there is nothing for the number to be about. (8) She answered that we accept exactly this kind of comparison within a single life across time, where there is also no physical common unit, and that nobody thinks "she is worse off now than ten years ago by this much" is gibberish; if the intrapersonal case is legitimate, the interpersonal case cannot be ruled out merely by the absence of a shared dimension. (9) He said the intrapersonal case works only because there is a subject whose preferences fix the scale, and that between persons there is no such subject, so the module must be importing some chooser's preferences and presenting them as the facts. (10) She replied that this shows comparisons may be standpoint-relative, not that they are empty, and that the agony-versus-headache case is one every standpoint worth taking seriously will rank the same way.

Neither would budge, and I would rather not referee badly. Please adjudicate move by move: for each of the ten moves, say whether it is clearly right, clearly mistaken, or defensible-but-overstated, and briefly justify each rating. Then say what each of them is missing, whether their disagreement is ultimately about measurement, about ethics, or about what "wellbeing" means, and who, if anyone, has the better of the exchange overall.
