I spent thirty-five years as an experimental physicist and have spent the four since my retirement auditing graduate seminars in philosophy, mostly in decision theory. My reading group has asked me to circulate a written statement of my own position on rationality over time so that the others can attack it, and before I do that I would like an audit of it. I should say at the outset that I cannot tell which parts of it are load-bearing and which are decoration, and that I have been wrong in print before, so I would rather be told plainly that something is broken than be reassured.

The episode that started all this is worth setting out, because my commitments were assembled around it. I collect old scientific instruments, and a dealer once proposed a series of swaps to me: my marine chronometer for his barometer with some cash to me, then the barometer for a theodolite with more cash to me, then the theodolite back for a chronometer with cash to him, and around again if I liked. Each swap, evaluated on its own, was one I would have accepted. Taken as a loop, I would finish holding exactly what I began with and less money. I declined, because I could see the loop coming, and a friend tells me that this was incoherent of me: if each step was an improvement, then the whole sequence was an improvement; and if the whole was a loss, then at least one step must have been a loss, and I ought to be able to name it. I could not name it. My commitments, as best I can state them, are these. (1) Rationality attaches to choices, not to lives: there is no further requirement that a life, or a sequence of choices, be coherent when taken as a whole, and each choice is to be assessed against the preferences and the information available at the moment it is made. (2) Nevertheless I take it as a genuine constraint that a rational agent cannot be made a certain loser — that no chain of choices, each defensible when it is made, should be able to strip her of resources with certainty. (3) For choices under risk, expected-utility maximization is correct, the sure-thing principle included. (4) I am comfortable with precommitment in the cases where it plainly works: I have a standing arrangement that stops me from buying instruments online after midnight, and I would sign a binding arrangement of the kind Ulysses made with his crew if the stakes were high enough. (5) I have no patience for sunk-cost reasoning: money spent, time invested, and harm already suffered are not reasons, and I would rather write off an entire investment than be moved by it. (6) I think the largest choices in a life — a marriage, a child, abandoning one field for another — are ones in which I cannot know what I will come to value afterwards, that this ignorance is not a defect of the choice but part of what the choice is, and that it is rational to make them anyway.

What I want is an audit rather than a survey. Tell me which of these six are actually in tension with which, and which pairs only look tense and are not, and for each real tension how severe it is and what the cheapest repair would be. Rank the real tensions by severity rather than handing me a flat list, and tell me which single revision would clear up the most. I would also like to know which of the six, if any, is not merely in tension with the others but confused on its own terms, and how much of your answer reflects settled results as against your own taste. If the way I have posed the whole exercise is confused, or if some of my six are not the kind of thing that can conflict at all, I would much rather you say so directly than work around it.
