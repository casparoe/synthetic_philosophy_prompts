The idea I'm bringing you is half-formed, and I want it either sharpened into something with a determinate answer or shown to be a dead end. Dutch book arguments are supposed to show that a certain kind of incoherence among an agent's degrees of belief is a rational defect, and in every version I've seen, the force of the argument seems to be carried by the thought that the agent loses something. But "loses something" is doing a great deal of work, and I suspect the standard presentations slide between at least three conditions that are not the same:

(a) a set of bets exists that leaves the agent worse off in every state of the world, and which can simply be imposed on her — nothing is assumed about what she would accept, only that she prefers more money to less;

(b) a set of bets exists such that she would accept each one taken on its own, at odds she herself regards as fair, and holding them all together guarantees her a loss;

(c) no single such set exists, but there is a sequence of trades she would accept one at a time, ending somewhere she herself regards as worse than where she began.

I suspect these come apart in two ways. First, they may need different preference assumptions to get from "exploitable" to "irrational" — my guess is that (b) needs something like linearity in the stakes, or the absence of wealth and risk effects, that (a) and (c) do not. Second, the loss in (b) and (c) is denominated in whatever she happens to care about, while in (a) it is usually denominated in money, and I am not sure the argument means the same thing in the two cases. If the conditions really do come apart, then the strength of the conclusion and the cost in assumptions trade off against each other, which would explain why the debate over these arguments feels as stuck as it does.
