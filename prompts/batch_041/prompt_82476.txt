I am a doctoral student in philosophy writing a chapter on the mechanisms of moral change, and I want to build the argument around a thesis I find both attractive and troubling: on Appiah's account of dueling, footbinding and abolition, the decisive engine in each of those revolutions was not argument but honor — the practice came to be a source of shame in the eyes of precisely the people whose esteem its practitioners needed, and it died once that shift had occurred. Please work out the most important implications of that thesis, taking care to distinguish two readings of it: one on which honor is the cause of the change, and one on which honor is merely the idiom in which deeper shifts of interest and power became legible. I am especially interested in what follows for (i) whether a norm adopted chiefly out of status anxiety can count as a moral advance, and in what sense; (ii) how durable such victories should be expected to be, given that the very machinery that toppled a practice might later be turned against it; and (iii) what the thesis implies for practices whose participants are dispersed and anonymous — consumers in a supply chain, say — where there may be no community of esteem whose contempt could do the work. Where an implication depends on a contestable assumption, identify the assumption.
