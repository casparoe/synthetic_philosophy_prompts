Last month I was in a minor collision at a crosswalk, and the dashcam footage shows the events in an order that contradicts what I would have sworn I saw: I remember the other car starting to move before my light changed, but the footage says the light changed first. Since then I've been unable to stop thinking about a perceptual puzzle I once came across, the color phi effect — two dots flash in different locations, the second in a different color, and observers report seeing the first dot travel and change color halfway across, even though the second dot's color cannot be known until it appears. That seems to force a choice between two readings of what the brain is doing. On one reading, the brain rewrites the stream of experience itself, inserting the color change into an interval that has already elapsed before consciousness ever receives it — call it the Stalinesque reading: the film is re-edited before it is screened. On the other, you genuinely experienced the first dot as it was, and only the memory or the report was revised afterward — the Orwellian reading: the film screened honestly and was re-cut for the archive.

Write a dialogue between two equally competent proponents of these readings, neither a strawman, each answering the other's actual points rather than talking past them. Have them confront a wrinkle I cannot resolve on my own: imagine an online, non-verbal measure — a blink or a fast motor response keyed to the first flash — that appears to track the unedited color, while the subject's verbal report tracks the edited one. Does that vindicate the Orwellian, or can the Stalinesque theorist absorb it by claiming the motor system keeps an earlier draft that never becomes conscious? Let them press each other on whether the two readings are empirically distinguishable at all, and on what could count as evidence either way. They should argue from the structure of the case, not from anyone's authority.
