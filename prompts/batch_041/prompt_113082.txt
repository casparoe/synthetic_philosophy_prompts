Two months ago I sat in on my mother's care assessment, and I have not been able to leave it alone since. The facility scores residents on an instrument — orientation, mobility, continence, medication management, a few others — and the level of care she is entitled to changes at a cutoff. On the Tuesday she was two points under; by the following Friday she was two points over; in between, as far as any of us could see, she lost one more small competence and nothing else. Everyone in the room, including me, thought the second score was the correct one. Nobody could say when she had crossed, and I noticed that I badly wanted there to be a fact of the matter — some particular afternoon — while suspecting that there isn't one, and that the instrument is less a measurement of her than a decision about where to stop drawing lines. I have since read my way into the old sorites literature, and what I want from you is a short story, under 2,500 words, that makes this problem live for a reader who has never heard of it.

Please give it a concrete, worldly apparatus of the kind that actually generates these cases: a boundary board, a licensing office, a zoning commission, a triage protocol, an emissions permit, an admissions rubric — some body obliged to attach numbers to a predicate that admits of degree (tall, bald, polluted, crowded, adult, fluent, disabled, in pain, still alive, one of us), with a tolerance clause in its rules to the effect that no single unit of difference may ever be treated as decisive. The story should leave real room for each of the competing diagnoses: that the boundary is perfectly real but unknowable, so the cutoff is a lucky guess at a hidden line; that there is no one line but many admissible ways of drawing it, and a score is true only relative to one of them; that the predicate is a matter of degree all the way down and the verdicts should have been graded too; that the term shifts with context and comparison class, so the same person is tall in one room and not in the next; and that the tolerance premise is simply false and the whole argument unsound. I would also like the second-order problem in there somewhere — the line between the clearly-over and the merely borderline, and whether that line is itself sharp. Please don't stage it as the heap of sand or the balding man; I have read those, and I would rather the machinery be one that people actually operate. And please resist the reveal that the cutoff was arbitrary all along: I want to feel the pull of every diagnosis, not be told which one wins.

Begin with the story itself, with no preface and no commentary. After it, add a brief note on where you took liberties with the ideas, which reading of vagueness you leaned on, and where someone who holds a different one would say you cheated. If the premise is confused, or if you think a story cannot carry this without collapsing into a lecture, say so plainly in a sentence or two instead of writing something that papers over it.
