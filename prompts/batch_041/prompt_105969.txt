I teach a grad seminar on higher-order theories of consciousness -- the family of views on which a mental state only counts as conscious if some higher-order representation, or some form of availability to the subject, rides on top of it. Every year I get the same fight in week three: one student says the whole thing is unfalsifiable, another says it's just the reportability criterion dressed up in a lab coat. I want to make that fight concrete by turning to clinical practice, because I'm fairly convinced the clinic is quietly running on a theory of consciousness and it would be worth dragging that theory into the open.

Here's the practice I want you to work with. In anesthesiology, "awareness" during surgery is basically defined by explicit recall -- the patient later reporting that they heard or felt something -- and the depth-of-anesthesia monitors are calibrated against those reports, not against anything the patient couldn't tell us about. The cleverer studies use the isolated forearm technique: they keep one arm cut off from the muscle relaxant with a tourniquet, so an apparently unresponsive, paralyzed patient can still squeeze the experimenter's hand on command. Meanwhile, over in the disorders-of-consciousness ward, a patient gets called vegetative or unconscious when they can't follow commands or produce any voluntary response on the standardized bedside scales; a patient who only shows covert command-following inside a brain scanner is written up as a discovery, a hidden consciousness we'd been missing. And the whole field is haunted by the thought that some patients with no reportable experience whatsoever might still be conscious -- that worry is the entire motivation for the covert-consciousness research in the first place.

So: what theory of consciousness does this cluster of practices presuppose? Is it a genuine higher-order theory, a global-workspace/access theory, or just a reportability criterion that stays neutral between them? Seperate what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits comfortably with. Then tell me where it's actually inconsistent -- I think there's a real tension between defining consciousness by reportability and then hunting for a consciousness that by hypothesis can't be reported -- and finally, what would have to change at the bedside and in the OR if we took a first-order theory seriously, one on which a state can be conscious without any higher-order representation of it and without any route to report. American English, please, and keep it under 800 words -- one page, no headings.
