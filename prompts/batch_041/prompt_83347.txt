I'm doing an MSc in economics and philosophy and we've been reading Harsanyi on social choice. I'm writing an essay that asks what John Stuart Mill would have made of Harsanyi's aggregation theorem. I know it's a bit of a weird question because Mill died long before Harsanyi wrote, but that's the point—I want a serious prediction, not a pastiche. The theorem, as I understand it, says that if each person's preferences and the social preference all satisfy the expected utility axioms, and the social preference is Pareto-inclusive, then social utility has to be a weighted sum of individual utilities. So it looks like a proof of utilitarianism from rational choice axioms. But Mill's utilitarianism was qualitative—higher and lower pleasures, competent judges, and so on—and I'm not sure he would have accepted that all goods can be represented by a single cardinal utility function. Would he have seen Harsanyi's result as a vindication, or as a reductio of the idea that society can be treated as a single rational agent? And what would he have said about the ex ante perspective Harsanyi uses, where you choose social rules behind a veil of ignorance or under a assumption of equiprobability? Mill always seemed more interested in actual consequences and actual character than in hypothetical choice.

I'm also curious about a more technical wrinkle. Harsanyi's theorem relies on the sure-thing principle and on the idea that social preferences are defined over lotteries in the same way as individual preferences. If Mill had been confronted with that, do you think he would have accepted it as a reasonable idealisation of the greatest happiness principle, or would he have said that it ignores the qualitative differences between pleasures and the fact that people's happiness cannot be summed without a common metric? A friend in my seminar said Mill would have loved it because it gives utilitarianism a rigorous foundation, but I think he would have been more ambivalent—maybe even hostile to the idea that rational choice theory can capture the whole of morality. I'd like you to predict Mill's position as carefully as you can, drawing on his actual views on pleasure, character, and the greatest happiness principle, and say where the prediction is uncertain. No need for a bibliography—just a clear argument about what Mill would likely have thought and why.
