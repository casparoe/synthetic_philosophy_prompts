I chair the benefits advisory comittee at a mid-sized regional health plan, and I have to circulate a written recommendation before Thursday's vote on how we cover a drug that slows a degenerative nerve condition. Right now we don't really have a policy -- a nurse reviewer and I decide case by case, which in practice means asking, for each new patient, whether this case is worse than the last one we approved or denied. The result is a mess. Our appeal file reads like a ladder of cases, from bedbound and losing speech at the top down to occasional numbness at the bottom, and every rung looks like a genuine step from the one right above it.

Now we're trying to set an actual threshold. The options run from cover only the most severe cases up to cover everybody, with about eight stops in between, and each stop differs from its neighbors by a hair. Here's my problem. Take any stop and compare it with the one above it, and the lower one looks like the better policy: you help more people, the per-patient cost is modest, the extra spending is a rounding error next to the rest of our budget. But I'm also dead sure the bottom stop -- cover everyone, no severity cutoff at all -- is worse than the top stop, because it would eat the money we use for things that save lives outright. So I've got a chain where every neighboring pair points one way and the two ends point the other, and I can't tell which link to give up. One of our board members teaches an intro philosophy course at the community college and says this is all about whether "better than" is transitive, and keeps calling it a spectrum argument, which is no help to me on Thursday. Another one says that if each little step is an improvement, then eight little steps have to leave us better off, so covering everyone is the right call and I'm just being sentimental about the very sick.

Draft the memo I'll hand the board. Give me a specific threshold, say plainly what happens to the cases right at the edge, and tell me what to do with the three appeals actually sitting on my desk (they're at the two stops on either side of the line I'd be drawing). And deal with the philosophy for real: is my chain actually a contradiction, or does something in it have to go -- and if so, which link, and what's the argument for dropping that one rather than another? I don't want a tour of the literature; I want to know what to do and why. Take the other side seriously, including the claim that any cutoff is arbitary and that if I accept the logic I should just cover everyone.

At the end, tell me how confident you are in each part of this and flag the one point you're least sure about.
