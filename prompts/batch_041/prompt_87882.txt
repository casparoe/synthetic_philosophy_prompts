Ok so I lurk on a bunch of rationalist-adjacent blogs and Discords, and I keep watching the same fight about Ubuntu happen, and last weekend I finally got a front-row seat. Context: a friend of ours, Kwame, got into a great PhD program abroad and told his family he's probably not coming back — not to run the family business, not to be around for his mom as she gets older. Two other friends, Ngozi and Tunde, went at each other about whether that makes him a worse person, and I got asked to referee and totally failed.

Ngozi: 'You know the whole thing — a person is a person by way of other people. Your self isn't private property, it's something you build in and with your community. And it's not a switch, it's gradable: you're more or less of a person depending on whether you actually tend those relations. What Kwame is doing isn't neutral. It's letting part of himself atrophy.'

Tunde: 'That's just "the group owns you" with a nice proverb taped over it. The real claim is metaphysical, not moral: language, reason, selfhood — you only get those by growing up among people. Fine. That says nothing about whether Kwame owes his family his career. Descriptive relationality, not a debt.'

Ngozi: 'You can't wall the descriptive off from the normative that cleanly. If my self is literally constituted by these particular relations, then walking away from them isn't like declining a favor — it's closer to cutting off a limb. The obligation is internal to who I am, not handed to me from outside.'

Tunde: 'Then the view can never criticize a community, because whatever the community does is by definition the thing that makes you a person. And it turns the exile, the dissident, the kid who leaves an abusive family, into a defective human being. That's the conclusion authoritarians want, and you've argued them straight into it.'

Then Ngozi threw in a distinction neither of them could sort out: maybe there are two separate claims — thin relationality (you need *some* people to become a self at all) and thick relationality (you're constituted by *these* people, and those specific ties are what you have to maintain). She thinks the thick version is the real one and that it survives Tunde's objection, because a community can be wrong about which relations actually constitute you. Tunde said that's just smuggling in a liberal individual who gets to audit his own obligations and call the audit "personhood."

Neither would budge and honestly I couldn't either.

You're a philosopher who actually knows this literature (personhood debates in African philosophy, social ontology, the metaphysics of the self) — don't give me the Wikipedia gloss. Before any verdict, brainstorm the strongest version of each position and say where the real crux is. Then adjudicate move by move: rate each of the moves from clearly mistaken to clearly right and briefly justify each rating, say who's right about which specific point, and what both of them are missing. And answer the question they couldn't: is there a version of the thick/gradable view that keeps the metaphysics without the authoritarian upshot, or is Tunde right that you can't have one without the other? Think carefully and don't just split the difference. (sorry for the typos, typing this on my phone)
