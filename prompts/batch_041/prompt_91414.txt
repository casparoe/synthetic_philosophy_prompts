I spent forty years as a physicist and have passed most of the decade since my retirement reading history and philosophy of science, largely in the hope of understanding a pattern I saw at close range: a body of work whose conceptual content outlives, and seems largely independent of, the mathematics its author believed was holding it up. I have lately been reading Hobbes, and I have arrived at a position I should like you to attack. Hobbes presented his civil philosophy as a demonstrative science on the model of geometry, and he also insisted for the rest of his life that he had solved the ancient problem of squaring the circle, a claim his contemporaries, most persistently the Oxford geometer John Wallis, showed to be false. My position is that this mathematical failure is irrelevant to the philosophical worth of his political theory: the geometry served as scaffolding, as a claim about method and as a bid for intellectual prestige, and the state of nature, the covenant and the sovereign can be assessed on their own terms.

What would count against that? I do not want the truism that a valid deduction survives its author's incompetence elsewhere; I want to know where, if anywhere, the failure is load-bearing. Give me the considerations that should move me, ordered from those that would force me to abandon the position to those that would merely embarrass it, and say which of them Hobbes's sympathetic interpreters would themselves concede rather than merely which ones his critics would press. It would also be worth knowing whether the position ends up so hedged that it risks nothing, and whether that is a defect. The case I would find most damaging is one in which a substantive political conclusion turns out to rest on a premise that only his faulty geometry supports. Reply with a numbered list of exactly five items, each one or two sentences, with no introduction and no conclusion.
