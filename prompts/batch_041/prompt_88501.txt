You are an expert in philosophical semantics and aesthetics, with enough of a foothold in legal theory to move among all three, and you are far more at home in the technical apparatus of these fields than I am. Please think this through slowly and at length before committing to anything. Begin by assembling a short list of the strongest candidate concepts or traditions that might cover the pattern I describe below; test each against the details I give; say plainly which fail and where; and only then give me your answer. I would rather have a careful comparison of near misses than one confident label, and if nothing established covers the pattern, I would rather you said so and proposed a term of your own, defined and supplied with edge cases, than that you stretched an existing one to fit.

I am a novelist, and for some years I have been troubled by a shape that turns up in my own drafts and, more vividly, in the work of writers I admire. Three instances.

The first is revision. Over a long revision I make, on any given morning, only changes I can defend. Each is a small improvement and I can usually say why. There is no day on which I make a mistake. Yet the manuscript that results is worse than the one I began with — not worse in some respect I could point to, but worse as a whole; it has stopped being the kind of thing I was writing. When I go looking for the sentence that did the damage, there is none. Every sentence I could name is, taken by itself, better than what it replaced.

The second is the workshop. A story opens with a man who is decent and closes with a man who is monstrous, and no reader can point to the paragraph where the decency left him. Worse: in the earlier draft, where the turn was blunt, the readers who said it was too abrupt were right, and in the revised draft the readers who say he was always monstrous are right too. There is no version of the story that is not wrong somewhere, and no place in any of them where the wrongness lives.

The third is the editor's number. My publisher's house rule is that a book of this kind must not run past a certain length. Everyone concerned agrees the number is arbitrary; nobody thinks the rule is meaningless, or that the word 'novel' stops doing any work when we apply it. The arbitrariness of the number and the reality of the distinction seem to coexist, and I have never understood how.

What I want to know is whether there is a name for the structure common to these cases. The features I would want named: a term or verdict that holds, or fails to hold, across a series of cases in which no two neighbours differ enough that anyone asked about that pair alone would call one good and the other bad; where the verdicts at the two ends are confident and well grounded; where there is no fact I can find that fixes the changeover; and where — this is the part I care about — the term is not therefore empty, or merely conventional. Each of my three cases has that shape, though the third seems to me to sit differently from the first two, and I would like to know whether that difference is real.

I have bumped into three things that are close and are not it, and I want you to tell me exactly where each falls short. First, the argumentative move in which someone warns that if you permit this you will end up permitting that; that is a prediction about consequences, and my cases are not predictions. Second, the moral case in which a great many people each do something too small to notice and the sum of what they do is appalling; there the trouble is spread across many hands, whereas mine is one hand making one series of edits. Third, the regulatory case in which a legislature fixes a number — a speed limit, a blood-alcohol threshold — that nobody believes corresponds to anything in nature; there the arbitrariness is openly conceded, and I do not think that is quite my position, though I may be wrong about my third instance.

Finally, and this is what I am really after: tell me what follows for judgment. If there is no place where the change happens, is my verdict at each step mistaken, or my verdict at the end, or neither? Is there a way of describing what I am doing when I judge such a case that leaves the verdict answerable to the work rather than to my taste, without pretending to a line I cannot find? And when I must decide — to stop revising, to pass or fail the story, to accept or reject the manuscript — what is the honest description of what I am doing when I pick a point, given that I do not believe there is a point to pick?
