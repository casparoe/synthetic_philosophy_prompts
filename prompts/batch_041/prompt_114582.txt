ok so this is a work thing but two people now have told me it's basically a philosophy question and I dont know where else to ask it.

I'm a backend engineer at a mid-size company, about 40 people on the eng side. Design reviews happen in a shared doc: someone posts a proposal, reviewers add comments as they get to it over the course of a week, all of it visible to everyone as it lands. The first person to comment gets a big colored banner at the top that says APPROVE, DISCUSS, or BLOCK, and everybody else's comments sort underneath it. By convention the most senior person on the project reviews first, because they're usually right and we want the review to be fast.

Here's what's been bugging me. Last three proposals went the same way: senior person approves with a light comment, then every subsequent reviewer approves, mostly within a day, a lot of them with "agreed with [name]" and nothing else. On one of them I had a real concern — I'd found the migration would lock our main table for ~20 minutes in prod — and I sat on it, because I figured if the sharpest person in the room hadn't flagged it, I was probably missing something. I wasnt missing something. We rolled it back.

So now I'm writing a proposal to change our review process and I want to get the diagnosis right instead of bolting on a "please speak up" line. My manager's fix: everyone writes their review before seeing anyone else's, then we unblind them all at once. My worry is that this doesn't obviously fix anything — by the time I write mine I've already read the proposal, I know who wrote it, I know it's the kind of thing the lead tends to like, and I know my review is going to be read by that person. Also half our reviewers are contractors whose renewals depend on the lead's opinion of them, which seems relevant.

Whats more, a week after the rollback, the same lead approved the next two proposals faster than ever and nobody's behavior changed at all. That seems like it shouldnt happen if the process was actually self-correcting.

Stuff I want sorted out:

1. What's actually going on, in general terms? I keep describing it with bad metaphors. What's the difference between "everyone independently looked at the same evidence and agreed" and "everyone agreed because everyone else agreed," and how would I tell which one happened in a specific review after the fact?
2. Is my manager's fix a real fix? If not, what's the smallest change that actually puts the missing information back? Randomize the order? Put the senior person last? Make reviews anonymous? Have people write reviews before the doc is even shared? I want to know which of these do work and which are theater.
3. One teammate says keep it as is, because the lead is right ~80% of the time anyway and the process is fast. I dont have a good answer to this and I'd like one. What exactly are we giving up when it goes right?
4. Related: someone suggested we just have everyone vote independently and go with the majority. But our reviewers aren't all answering the same question — one person is checking prod safety, another is checking API design, another is checking whether it fits the roadmap. Does majority voting even mean anything here, and if not, what replaces it?
5. If I wanted to push a bad proposal through on purpose, how would I structure the process to maximize my odds? I figure thinking about it adversarially tells me what to fix.
6. Is there a name for this pattern? Just the term, not a reading list.

And if all this is standard econ/psych stuff I'm reinventing, fine, tell me — but then tell me what the standard story gets wrong about my specific setup, because the contractor-renewal part and the fact that the doc is visible to management feel like they change the answer.

Answer in American English, plain language, no jargon unless you define it, and skip the reassurance — if the fix is "your process is fine and you just need to actually say something," say that.
