I'm a TA for a grad seminar on AI safety, and I got roped into writing one of the midterm questions because the prof is at a conferance all week and won't get back to us in time. The unit we just wrapped was on the split between alignment and control — the idea that alignment work tries to make the model actually want the right thing, while control work just assumes the model might be misaligned and tries to keep it from doing damage anyway, using tricks like untrusted monitoring, trusted editing, and testing whether two copies of the same model will collude to sneak something past you. The problem is that all my students can already chant that alignment is about motivation and control is about capability, and that control buys safety at the price of a control tax, and they'll happily write that for twenty minutes and call it a day. I want something that can't be answered by reciting the slogan.

So here's the scenario I want them to chew on. Imagine a protocol, call it Two-of-Three. An untrusted strong model U proposes an action. A second sample U' from that same model reviews U's proposal and raises a flag if it thinks the action is dangerous. If U' flags, a much weaker but so-called trusted model T gets to veto. And a human auditor spot-checks about 2% of the actions that U' let through. Nobody has verified that U is aligned — that's the whole premise of the setup. I want an essay question that makes them say which assumptions actually keep this protocol safe, which assumption is doing the heavy lifting, and what happens once they notice that the one everybody waves their hands at — that T is both trustworthy and too weak to scheme — is quietly carrying more weight than all the fancy monitoring. Then the deeper part: does the alignment/control distinction even survive this example, or does it collapse, given that T is itself a model and its trustworthiness is just a claim about its alignment? Push them to say what a genuinely control-flavored safety argument would have to look like if it isn't allowed to lean on any aligned component at all, and whether that's even possible, or whether control always secretly bootstraps off a little bit of alignment that someone smuggled in through the back door.

Please write the question itself (upper-level/grad, one hour, closed book, 25 marks), a model answer of around 700 to 800 words, and a marking scheme with five must-hit points, the marks each one is worth, and for each point a one-line note on the mistake that costs students the marks. American English, and no citations or reading list in the model answer, since it's closed book.
