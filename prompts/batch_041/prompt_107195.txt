I'm a PhD candidate in metaphysics, and I've argued myself into a position I would like stress-tested. I accept the principle of identity of indiscernibles, and I think Max Black's case of two perfectly similar spheres in an otherwise empty universe does not refute it. My reason is that the spheres are discernible after all, because they differ in impure, identity-involving properties: each sphere has the property of being five feet from the other, but only one of them has the property of being five feet from a while the other has the property of being five feet from b — and, more bluntly, each is identical with a different sphere. Once such properties are allowed to count, no two distinct things are ever indiscernible, and the alleged counterexample dissolves.

I want a map of what would count against this, sorted from decisive to merely awkward, separating the defeaters I should concede from the ones I can dismiss. Specifically: does the appeal to impure properties trivialize the principle rather than rescue it — if any two things differ in being identical with themselves, is the principle now vacuous, and is that fatal or just a change of subject? What would a principled restriction to purely qualitative or intrinsic properties have to look like, and does the principle survive that restriction? Do any independent cases bear on it once my move is on the table — for instance the indistinguishability of particles in quantum mechanics, or a world whose complete qualitative description is a symmetric structure with no privileged node? And what would a defender of the principle concede counts against it, as opposed to what critics merely assert? If the position turns out to be unfalsifiable or trivially true, say so plainly and say whether that is a problem for a principle of this kind. I would find it most persuasive if you could exhibit a case the position cannot describe without circularity. Please write in American English.
