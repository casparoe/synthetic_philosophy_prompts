I retired out of a geochemistry department a few years ago and, having nothing better to do with my mornings, I've been sitting in on a philosophy reading group. Mostly I ask questions and annoy people. Last month the topic was self-reference, and the picture everyone kept waving around was Escher's Drawing Hands, the one where two hands come up out of the sheet of paper and each one is drawing the other. Two of the regulars used it to argue for opposite conclusions and both came away feeling they'd won, which is usually my cue that the picture is doing the work instead of the argument.

The first one says the drawing shows a loop closing on itself, so nothing outside is needed: each hand is drawn by the other, done, self-supporting. He uses that to say a program that rewrites its own code could in principle pull itself up by its own bootstraps, and, more to my interest, that the origin-of-life chicken-and-egg problem just solves itself. You know the one: the nucleic acids need proteins to get made, and the proteins need nucleic acids to get made, so neither can come first. He says that's exactly the picture, and the picture is fine.

The second one says no, the picture only works because of the paper and the artist. The hands are drawn; the drawing can't draw the paper. He says there's a name for that, something about an inviolate level, a level the tangle can't reach up and touch, and I think he got it from Hofstadter but he wasn't sure. His conclusion is that any tangle of this kind needs something above it, outside the loop, so the origin of life does need a genuine outside, and he's happy to let that be the laws of chemistry or a designer, he says it makes no difference to his point.

My problem is that I spent thirty years on mineral surfaces and early-earth chemistry, and in my experience what broke the chicken and egg was a surface, a piece of rock doing chemistry that wasn't itself alive and also wasn't above anything, it was just the next thing over. That doesn't look like an artist to me. So I don't trust either of them, and I can't tell whether the picture is carrying either argument or whether they're both just pointing at a lithograph.

Can you lay out the correspondence for me, what maps onto what if we take the picture seriously (hands, paper, the act of drawing, the artist, the levels), and then tell me where it breaks down? I have a suspicion that the fact the hands are a picture of hands and not hands is what kills both readings, and I'd like to know if that's right or if I'm being too clever. I'd also like to know what the picture quietly smuggles in that neither of these cases actually has, and which of the mismatches matter for the conclusions the two of them are drawing versus which are just alot of noise about art. Then say whether either conclusion survives once the disanalogies are on the table, and if there's a better picture for what actually goes on in these cases, tell me what it is.
