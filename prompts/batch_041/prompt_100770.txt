I've spent the last few months arguing with friends from a seminar on the origins of the First World War, and I've ended up defending a fairly strong version of the thesis associated with Fritz Fischer: that the July Crisis was not a shared, roughly symmetric slide into war among the great powers, but that Germany bears a distinctive and primary responsibility for the outbreak — that its leadership used the Sarajevo crisis as an occasion for a bid at continental dominance, pressed Austria-Hungary toward a punitive war on Serbia, and chose escalation at nearly every fork where de-escalation remained available. I don't want a bibliography, a reading list, or citations; a model without a library can only guess at those, and I'd rather have your reasoning than your footnotes. What I want is a straight assessment of how exposed this position actually is. Tell me what would count against it: which findings, which re-readings of the July Crisis, which comparative cases, and which conceptual objections should move someone who holds it, and how decisive each one really is. I care especially about where the thesis's own defenders would concede ground, not merely where its critics fire — and about whether the position, as it is usually defended, has quietly been qualified into something that risks nothing at all, and if so, whether that is a defect or simply what a claim about a single historical event has to look like.

I've also been trying to split the claim into three parts, and I'd like you to keep them apart throughout, because I suspect the thesis is far stronger under one reading than the others. Call them trigger responsibility (who chose war at the moment of decision), structural responsibility (who built the alliance commitments and the arms dynamic that turned a local crisis into a continental catastrophe), and escalation responsibility (who, once war had become imaginable, refused every off-ramp). Which of these does the Fischer-style thesis actually need in order to say what it wants to say, and what would count against each one separately? I also have a half-formed test I'd like you to pressure-test rather than adopt: what I've been calling the swap test, in which you replace Germany in the summer of 1914 with another great power — or with a counterfactually more restrained Germany — and ask whether the war still happens. If it does, that seems to drain the specifically German responsibility; if it doesn't, that seems to confirm it. I want to know whether that test is doing real work or is a rigged counterfactual, and whether there is a version of it that isn't rigged. As for what would personally move me most: I think I'd abandon the strong thesis if someone showed me a case in which the same escalation dynamics operated with no dominant aggressor at all, since that would suggest I'm pattern-matching a general feature of 1914 Europe onto one country.

Please structure the answer as follows, in American English: (1) "The thesis, sharpened" — restate the position I'm defending in the strongest, most attackable form, roughly a paragraph; (2) "What would count against it" — the defeaters, ranked from fatal through serious to merely awkward, each with a short note on how decisive it truly is, this being the longest section; (3) "What the defenders concede" — which objections a committed Fischer-style historian would themselves grant rather than deflect; (4) "The three responsibilities" — a compact treatment of trigger, structural, and escalation responsibility, saying which the thesis needs and which it can safely drop; (5) "On the swap test" — your verdict on whether my counterfactual test is doing real work; (6) "Verdict on insulation" — a closing judgment on whether the thesis risks anything at all, and whether that is a problem.
