I am a journalist who covers the behavioural and social sciences, and I am fact-checking a passage from a colleague's forthcoming book on what she calls the politics of persuasion. The passage is meant to introduce the nudge idea to a general readership. I have my suspicions about it, but I am not a specialist, and I would rather not publish a correction to a correction. It runs as follows:

"Behavioural economists have a name for the art of steering people toward better choices without banning anything or changing the price of anything: a nudge. The insight, due to Thaler and Sunstein, is that we think with two systems, a fast intuitive one and a slow deliberative one, and that in most decisions it is the fast system that decides. A nudge therefore works by arranging the options so that our biases do the work for us: the fruit moved to eye level, organ donation as the default, a text message reminding you to vote. Because nothing is forbidden and no one is made worse off, a nudge leaves the chooser's freedom entirely intact; that is precisely what separates it from a tax, a fine or a ban, and it is why the approach is called libertarian paternalism — paternalist because it aims at your own good, libertarian because the cake is still there for anyone who wants it. Nor is there any real conflict with autonomy: the nudge only influences those whose fast system is getting the better of them, while the person who is already choosing well is unaffected. That is what makes the whole programme asymmetric. The familiar objection, that nudging is manipulation, was put to rest when the behavioural insights unit inside the UK government showed that nudges produce large and lasting changes at negligible cost, and that people overwhelmingly approve of them once they are told about them."

Act as a philosopher of public policy with a working knowledge of the empirical behavioural literature, and audit this passage for me. Think it through carefully before you write. Begin with a short paragraph setting out what the passage is trying to establish and what work each of its sentences is doing — the overall argumentative strategy — before you turn to sentence-by-sentence assessment.

Where a defender of nudging would object that the passage has made the position easier to attack than it is, give me that objection; where the passage has in fact made the position harder to defend than it needs to be, say that instead. My own instinct is that the passage is too kind to the nudgers, but I cannot tell whether that is a substantive objection or just temperament.
