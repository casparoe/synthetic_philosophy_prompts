I chair the amnesty committee of the reconciliation commission set up after our country's civil war. Our statute permits amnesty only where the act in question was committed with a political objective; acts done for private gain, personal malice or mere obedience to orders fall outside it. We must now decide the application of a former auxiliary soldier who was present at the killings at the river crossing outside his home town: he guarded detainees, and he fired on people trying to escape. His disclosure has been full and his remorse looks genuine. What the record cannot settle is why he acted. He volunteered for the unit and stayed with it through the worst of it; he also twice asked to be transferred, and his own account is of drink, confusion and fear of his sergeant. The psychologist we commissioned writes of gradual habituation and group pressure; the historian we commissioned reads his letters home as showing a settled hatred of the group his unit was rounding up. The survivors' associations say that calling him an ordinary man swept along by events is itself an insult to the dead. One colleague makes the sharper point: two of the applicant's comrades, who openly avowed the ideology, have already received amnesty from us because their political motive was plain, so that on this reading our statute ends up punishing the unideological follower while the enthusiast walks free. My colleagues divide between the ordinary-men and the willing-executioners readings, and Tuesday's vote will be close.

Please draft the committee's written opinion. It should recommend a course of action with a date attached — including, if you recommend refusal, whether we should refer the file to the prosecutors and by when — give the reasons for that recommendation, and state the strongest case against it fairly rather than briskly. Close by saying what would change the recommendation and where the reasoning is likeliest to fail. I want a reasoned recommendation I can put to the committee, not a survey of the literature.
