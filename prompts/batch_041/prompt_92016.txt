I am a novelist, and for several years now I have been trying to finish a book that follows one family across four generations — and the question that keeps pulling me away from the manuscript is what, exactly, a reader is supposed to take from a life. I have arrived at a position about this, and I would rather have it stress-tested than flattered. Please take the role of a philosopher working at the intersection of moral psychology and the history of biography: someone who has thought hard about exemplarist ethics, about the ancient practice of writing lives in pairs, and about the conventions of hagiography. Reason carefully before you answer, and begin by brainstorming rather than concluding — set out the candidate objections in a list before you rank or judge them.

My position, as plainly as I can state it, is this. Moral learning from a life is neither exemplarity nor sympathy. It is what I would call frame rehearsal: the reader is made, temporarily and at no cost, to occupy an evaluative framework that is not their own, and the good that comes of it is the discovery that their own judgments are contingent on a frame they never chose. Two corollaries follow. First, the moral yield of a life is proportional to the resistance the reader feels, not to the admiration — a subject who is sympathetic, coherent, and admirable teaches least, because there is no friction where the reader's frame already fits. Second, hagiography and comparative biography — the saint's life on one side, the paired lives of Greek and Roman worthies on the other — are the same experiment run at opposite settings: hagiography removes resistance by presenting a single frame as simply true, while the comparative method manufactures resistance by forcing incommensurable standards into one judgment. Hence, on my account, the first has largely failed as moral education and the second is the most effective moral technology the ancient world produced. One reason I trust biography over the novel as the test case is that a novelist can tune the resistance at will while a biographer inherits it; if the thesis is true, it should be most visible in lives that were never shaped for our edification.

Now the request, which is the main event. Tell me what would count against this. I want the defeaters sorted by how decisive they would be, and I want no bibliography: assume I can look nothing up, and write so that nothing in your answer depends on my being able to. Distinguish what would genuinely refute the position from what would merely be awkward for it. Say what my view predicts that its rivals predict differently — plain exemplarism, the empathy-and-identification view, and the view that moral learning from lives is nothing but the accumulation of a repertoire of cases — and where we could in principle look to adjudicate. Tell me which defeaters a committed defender of my position would concede and which they would be entitled to wave away. Press as well on the framing: is what I am describing moral learning at all, or only the acquisition of a tolerance that has no bearing on conduct? And be honest with me about insulation. I am aware that I can always rescue the thesis by declaring the resistance internal, or by saying that a failed moral education is simply one in which the reader's frame was never really occupied. Is my position in danger of becoming unfalsifiable — and if so, is that a defect for a claim of this kind, or just the ordinary fate of claims about what reading does?

I would be most persuaded by either of the following, if you can supply it: a case in which a life produced a moral change in a reader with almost no resistance — an unimpeachably admirable subject who nonetheless moved someone to act — or, in the other direction, a clear instance of a comparative or ironic biography that generated nothing but aesthetic pleasure. If neither can be had, say so, and say what that does to my position. I would also like to know what would count against the second corollary on its own, since it seems to me the more exposed half of the pair.

Commit to a verdict. I do not want a survey in disguise; I want your judgment about whether the position survives contact with its best objections, which part of it I should surrender if I must surrender something, and how confident I should be. If you conclude that the thesis is true but trivial, or that it quietly collapses into one of the rivals I named, tell me so plainly.
