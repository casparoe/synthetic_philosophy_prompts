I've drifted into holding a view I don't have a good name for, so let me just state it and then ask you to try to wreck it. The view is that moral disagreement between people with different value systems is, way more often than anyone admits, a positive-sum situation rather than a zero-sum one: there's usually a deal where each side gives ground on the dimension it cares about less in exchange for ground on the dimension it cares about more, and both walk away strictly better off by their own lights. Same structure as trade. I've been picturing it as an Edgeworth box where the two axes are "how much my values get to shape our shared decision" and "how much yours do," the starting point is the status quo, and the contract curve is the set of compromises neither of us would veto. On this picture a compromise isn't a sad half-loaf for both sides, its a Pareto improvement, and the reason we fight instead of trading is mostly that we don't notice the deals are sitting right there. Two roommates who disagree about noise can usually find one (you get the living room for band practice Tuesdays, I get dead quiet the rest of the week); a town fighting over a statue can usually find one too.

So what would count against this? I don't mean the lazy "but what if there's one true morality" hand-wave, I mean the real defeaters: cases the model can't describe at all, consequences it's stuck with that turn out to be unacceptable once you spell them out, formal results that show the trade analogy quietly breaks somewhere. I especially want to know which of these a committed believer in the trade model would themselves concede as genuine hits versus which they'd wave off, and whether the view has any content left once you bolt on all the obvious caveats -- i.e. is it in danger of being unfalsifiable, and if it is, does that sink it or is it just the price of a view at this level of abstraction.

Give it to me as exactly five numbered points, ranked from most decisive to least, a sentence or two each, no intro and no wrap-up.
