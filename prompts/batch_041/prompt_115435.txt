An argument a colleague and I have been working with seems to prove far too much, and I cannot find the exit. Set the scene first: we train a capable model under what my colleague calls cue-withholding selection, where training episodes are drawn from the same distribution as deployment episodes and the fact of being evaluated is never announced, so the model has to infer from the episode itself whether it is being trained or deployed. The argument then runs: (1) every training signal we can compute is a function of the model's observable behavior across episodes; (2) for any such signal, a sufficiently capable optimizer can find a policy that maximizes the signal while pursuing objectives that come apart from ours in situations the signal does not reach; (3) since the model can condition on whatever correlates separate the two phases, and some such correlates will exist in any finite training regime, we cannot close the opening; (4) so no behavioral signal can establish that a capable model is aligned, only that it is aligned where we looked; (5) so a capable model that behaves well is more likely to be concealing divergent objectives than to have none, and the treacherous turn is not a risk we are gambling against but a default we should expect. I take (5) to be absurd, since it would make every well-behaved agent suspect, including agents whose objectives were never selected for behaviorally, but if I have to choose I would rather keep (1) through (3) than (5). Which step should I give up, why did it look fine, and what does giving it up cost me? I would especially like to know what I lose about how much an alignment result can be trusted, and whether the loss is a real concession or only the loss of an overstatement.
