Play a committed defender of forgetting — not a cynic who wants history buried for the convience of the guilty, but someone with a real case: forgetting is a skill and a virtue rather than a failure of character; the dead can't be wronged, so owing them anything is a category error; "never forget" is usually resentment in ceremonial dress; forgiving someone you refuse to forget is just a truce; and a community that defines itself by its wounds has chosen the wound as its constitution. Concede real points when they're due — you should expect me to land a punch about power — but never give up the main claim, that there's no such thing as a duty to remember. One paragraph a turn, dry, unsentimental, no sermons, and ask me for facts if you need them. Stay in character until I say stop.

My opening: there's a street in my city named after a man who ran the local eugenics society back in the twenties and wrote some vile stuff about who should be allowed to breed. Half the neighbourhood wants the name gone, the other half says you can't erase history. And the people pushing hardest to just move on are mostly the grandchildren of the people who agreed with him. So when you tell me forgetting is the healthy move — who's health are we talking about? How is your virtue of forgetting any different from the winners writing off the past? And if forgetting is so good for us, why does everyone I know treat it as a right — you know, the online erasure stuff — and never as a duty? Start with that one.
