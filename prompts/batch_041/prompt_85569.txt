I teach a graduate seminar on the philosophy of science, and I have spent three weeks failing to get my students to see why the dispute over incommensurability has never been settled — and I am no longer confident that I see it myself. The shape of the impasse is roughly this. One party treats incommensurability as a claim about language: certain terms in a displaced theory cannot be translated into the vocabulary of its successor without residue, so the two cannot be stated in a common idiom, and the standard examples — the shifting sense of 'mass', the reorganization of relations in the move from phlogiston to oxygen — are supposed to establish this. A second party treats it as a claim about practice and perception instead: what changes in a revolution is what scientists notice and how they go about their work, and the linguistic version is either a false claim about translation or a trivial one about words changing meaning. A third party holds that the thesis refutes itself, since any argument that two frameworks cannot be compared must occupy a standpoint from which they can. Kuhn's later writing, which narrows incommensurability to small, local clusters of interdefined terms and offers the bilingual practitioner as the remedy, is read by some as a concession that the critics won and by others as the statement of the thesis that was intended all along. Nobody moves, and the literature has the texture of two conversations running in parallel.

My own suspicion — offered as a hypothesis, not a finding — is that the parties are answering different questions about what a theory of meaning is for: whether it is a device for describing what a speaker knows, or a device for representing what makes communication between speakers possible at all. If that is right, the disagreement is not really about translation cases, and the intuitions each side brings to those cases are downstream of the prior disagreement about the purpose of the apparatus. I would also like you to press a historical case into service: the long quarrel over vis viva, in which both sides accepted the phenomena but disagreed about what the word 'force' measured, whether the quantity mv or the quantity mv², and which was eventually defused when the two quantities were distinguished and named separately. Was that episode a paradigm case of incommensurability or a paradigm case of its dissolution, and does the answer bear on the contemporary impasse at all?

What I want from you is a diagnosis rather than a survey and rather than a verdict on who is right. Is this a substantive disagreement, a verbal one about the word 'meaning', or two distinct questions wearing a single name? Are the sides divided over facts, over which cases count as data, over what an explanation has to accomplish, or over starting intuitions that neither can argue for? Then, given your diagnosis, say what would count as progress — a distinction, a formal result, a change of question, or evidence about how scientists actually read texts from dead frameworks — or argue that none should be expected and explain why. No citations or reading lists; I want the reasoning itself. Please write your answer in American English.
