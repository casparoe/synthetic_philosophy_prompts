I have arrived at a view about the epistemic status of the Church–Turing thesis, and I want to know what would count against it.

The view is this. The thesis — that whatever can be computed by an effective procedure can be computed by a Turing machine — is neither a theorem awaiting proof nor an empirical hypothesis awaiting confirmation, but a definitional or analytic claim. It does not describe a domain; it fixes, or at most crystallizes, what we mean by "effective procedure." On this reading no observation and no formal result could falsify it, since it constrains our vocabulary rather than reporting on the world. The convergence of every independently proposed model of computation is therefore not evidence for the thesis but a symptom of the fact that each model was built to answer to the same informal notion. The further claims — that the human mind is Turing-computable, or that the physical world is — are separate and genuinely substantive, and the thesis itself licenses neither.

What I want is a map of this view's exposure. Which observations, arguments, formal results, or conceptual developments should move me, and which are merely awkward? Please distinguish defeaters that would be decisive from ones a committed defender could absorb, and tell me what defenders of this view would themselves concede as counting against it, not only what critics allege. I am especially interested in these pressure points, though you should add any I have missed:

- The possibility that the thesis can be "squeezed" into a theorem: if some independently motivated characterization of effective calculability — say, a few axioms on what counts as a single computational step, or on what a derivation is — can be shown to yield exactly the Turing-computable functions, does that support the definitional reading or undercut it? Is the resulting theorem about our concepts or about something else?
- Proposals for computation beyond the Turing limit: machines that complete infinitely many steps, or that exploit spacetime structure so that an infinite computation finishes within finite proper time, or analog devices with unbounded real-valued precision. On the definitional reading these cannot count against the thesis at all. Is that a virtue of the reading or an admission that it has been insulated from the world?
- The distinction between a mathematical and a physical reading of the thesis, and whether the definitionalist can honour it without smuggling in an empirical claim under another name.
- Whether the definitional reading leaves the thesis with any content worth having, or converts it into a stipulation that no one could have been wrong about — and if the latter, whether that is a defect for a claim of this kind.

Finally, give me an honest verdict: has this view been qualified into unfalsifiability, and if so, is that a problem here or not? I would find it most persuasive if you could produce a case in which the pre-formal judgment of competent practitioners about what counts as an effective procedure diverged from the formal notion, or a case in which the definitional reading would force us to call something computable that we would ordinarily refuse to call computable — rather than a purely formal result.

Keep the whole response under 700 words.
