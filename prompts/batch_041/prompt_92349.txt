I'm trying to figure out what theory of lying is baked into the way the legal system treats deceptive but literally true statements. There's this old Supreme Court case, Bronston, where a guy was asked if he had ever had a Swiss bank account, and he said the company had an account there for about six months, which was true, but it implied he personally didn't. He couldn't be convicted of perjury because his answer was literally true. But then you have the federal false statements statute, which says you can't conceal a material fact by any trick or scheme, and courts have said even denying guilt to an FBI agent can be a crime. And police are allowed to lie to suspects during interrogations. So the system seems to say: if you're under oath, literal truth is a shield, but if you're talking to a federal agent, misleading silence or evasion can send you to prison. And the police can lie to you, but you can't lie to them. What view of lying does that presuppose? Is it that lying requires a false statement, while deception is broader? Or does it presuppose that lying is always wrong but deception is sometimes okay, like Augustine? I remember Augustine thought every lie is a sin, but he also thought you could conceal the truth without lying, like by not saying anything or maybe by saying something true that misleads? Actually I'm not sure if he thought misleading true statements were lies. If he didn't, then Bronston might be okay on his view, but the false statements statute would be punishing something Augustine might not call lying. If we took Augustine's absolute prohibition on lying seriously, what would have to change in these practices? Would police be allowed to lie? Would suspects be allowed to mislead? And is the legal system's implicit theory coherent, or does it just cobble together different views for different contexts? I'm especially interested in whether the practice presupposes that lying is about the falsity of the statement or about the intention to deceive, and whether it treats deception as a separate category. Also, if Augustine distinguished between lying and merely concealing truth, does that distinction map onto the legal distinction between perjury and obstruction or false statements? I'd like a reconstruction of the implicit theory, what it rules out, and what would change if we replaced it with Augustine's view.
