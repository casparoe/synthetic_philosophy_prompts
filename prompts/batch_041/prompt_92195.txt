Two colleagues of mine — a philosopher of mind and an evolutionary biologist, both of whom I like, neither of whom I take to be a fool — spent most of a long train journey arguing about a taxonomy they both accept as the ordinary starting point. On it, minds come in four grades. A Darwinian creature is fitted to its world by natural selection alone: it arrives with a fixed repertoire and cannot learn within its lifetime. A Skinnerian creature learns by reinforcement, its own successes and failures doing the selecting, so that the environment shapes the individual rather than the lineage. A Popperian creature can model its surroundings internally and try its hypotheses against the model, so that when the model is wrong it is the hypothesis that dies rather than the creature. A Gregorian creature is one whose mind is furnished largely by culturally transmitted tools and thinking-tools: much of what it knows and of how it reasons was invented by others and inherited rather than discovered for itself. Neither of them questioned where the labels come from, and I am not interested in that question either. I want to know what the fourfold division is worth.

They agree completely on the definitions and disagree about what the definitions describe. Their exchange ran roughly as follows.

(1) The biologist: The four grades are not a ladder of degree but three genuine discontinuities, and what marks each one is the currency in which error is paid for. A Darwinian creature pays for its errors in offspring. A Skinnerian pays in its own pain and lost opportunity. A Popperian pays in the cheap internal currency of a disconfirmed model, which is why it can afford thousands of trials where the Skinnerian can afford a handful. A Gregorian creature pays in a currency that is not its own at all, since the tool it uses was paid for by someone else's errors, possibly centuries earlier, possibly by someone it never met. Every step down that ladder is a step in the cost of being wrong, and cost of being wrong is not a matter of degree; it changes what kind of thing a creature can be.

(2) The philosopher: Every one of those discontinuities is a continuum with a plausible intermediate case. Where exactly does an internal model begin? A bacterium swimming up a sugar gradient is, if you like, testing a hypothesis about where the sugar is; a crow that re-hides its food only when it has itself been watched is doing something very like attributing a perspective to another. If you cannot say where hypothesis-testing starts without simply legislating a threshold, then Popperian names a region on a scale rather than a kind. Your criterion has the same trouble: cost of error varies continuously with body size, lifespan, and ecology, so cheap enough to make simulation worthwhile is a threshold you are choosing, not finding. And there is a second problem. Grades are ascribed to behaviours, not to minds. A human is Darwinian about the suck reflex, Skinnerian about her fear of one particular dog, Popperian when she rehearses an argument, Gregorian when she does long division. If every creature is all four at once, the four grades are a taxonomy of activities, and the claim that there are four kinds of minds has quietly disappeared.

(3) The biologist: That the grades co-occur does not make them the same grade, and nothing obliges a taxonomy to sort whole organisms. What matters is whether the boundary marks a change in the kind of explanation called for. Explain the fit of a Darwinian creature and you need population genetics; of a Skinnerian, reinforcement; of a Popperian, its own model of the world; of a Gregorian, the history of the tools themselves — an inheritance with its own variation and selection, not tied to anyone's genes. Four explanatory projects, four kinds. You also have the argument backwards when you insist that I locate the first hypothesis. A kind can have vague edges and still be a kind; nobody thinks the concept of life is worthless because viruses are awkward. And the Gregorian grade is not the Popperian grade with a better memory. It runs in a loop — minds make tools, tools make minds — and the loop ratchets, because a tool that works is preserved and improved by people who never met its inventor. That is a new kind of thing in the world, not a faster version of an old one.

(4) The philosopher: Four explanatory projects is a fact about our convenience, not about nature. You have told me that different questions have different answers, which is true of everything. And the ratchet is not unique to culture: genes build environments that then select genes, and that loop has been running since the first organism modified its surroundings, so if the criterion is a feedback loop between a lineage and its products, the Darwinian grade already has one. What remains distinctive is only that the Gregorian inheritance is not genetic — a claim about the channel, not about the mind. Which brings me to the objection I would press hardest. Take away the notation, the instruments, the library, and the division of cognitive labour, and what is left is an ordinary Popperian animal in an unusual environment. If the scaffolding is doing the work, you have not discovered a fourth kind of mind; you have discovered that some minds are well equipped. You are crediting the notebook to the thinker.

(5) The biologist: The test is not whether the competence survives the removal of the scaffolding but whether the same creature could have acquired it without the scaffolding, and the answer is no. Someone who has learned arithmetic in a place-value notation can afterwards do in her head what no one who never learned it can do; the notation has not been added to the mind, it has reorganised it. Nor is it merely that she stores facts elsewhere. The tools supply the shapes of her thoughts, the categories she thinks in, and the standards by which she judges a thought finished. You can drop the phrase the same creature into the counterfactual, but there is no fact about what that creature would have been, because the tools were part of what made it.

(6) The philosopher: Then notice what you have conceded. If a grade is fixed by what a lineage can accumulate and transmit rather than by anything in the individual, Gregorian is a property of lineages, and by your own reasoning so is every grade — no individual is Darwinian or Skinnerian either, except as a member of a lineage whose history is doing the explaining. So the taxonomy is not four kinds of mind at all; it is one kind of process running at four speeds. And the price of your answer in (5) is that most of what we call thinking turns out not to be a property of the mind at all, which strikes me as a reductio, though I admit I cannot say precisely why.

Please adjudicate. Go move by move through (1) to (6), rating each from clearly mistaken to clearly right, with a line or two of justification for each rating, and separating distinct claims where a single move contains several. Tell me which of the disagreements are substantive and which are verbal — that is, which concern the taxonomy itself and which concern what the word kind is supposed to mean. Say what each of them is missing that the other sees. Then answer the question they never quite put to themselves: what should the criterion be for individuating a kind of mind, and does the fourfold division survive it in any form? To keep that answer honest, apply whichever criterion you defend to two cases they never raised — a corvid that manufactures tools and re-hides its caches when it has been watched, and a language model trained on a large corpus of human writing and then tuned by reinforcement from human ratings — saying which grade each falls in and what the fact that you can answer at all shows about the taxonomy. Finally, say who is more nearly right overall, and on which points each of them would have to give ground.

I do not need to know who said what, or what has been written about any of this. Assess the arguments on their merits.
