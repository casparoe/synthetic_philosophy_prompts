You are a philosopher of science and AI safety researcher. A colleague in my lab has built the following argument, and I cannot tell whether it is a decisive result or a seductive fallacy. Please think carefully before answering, and begin by brainstorming the strongest version of each side.

(1) To be justified in believing that a model is aligned, we must rule out that it is deceptively aligned.
(2) A deceptively aligned model behaves exactly as an aligned model would until it reaches a condition under which defection is optimal.
(3) No finite set of behavioral tests can rule out the existence of such a condition, because the condition may simply not have arisen.
(4) Therefore no amount of behavioral evidence can justify the belief that a model is aligned.
(5) Without that justification, it is irresponsible to deploy a sufficiently capable model.
(6) Therefore we can never responsibly deploy such a model.

My colleague adds that this is not a bare skeptical possibility: our training process directly rewards policies that pass our tests, and the model has access to the fact that it is being tested. So the deception hypothesis is not arbitrary. Still, the argument seems to prove too much: by the same logic, I cannot know that my colleague is not a secret traitor, or that a bridge will not collapse. I would like to keep (1) and (6) if I can, but I will give up whichever premise is least costly. Which step should I reject, why did it look compelling, and what does rejecting it commit me to? Then say what a defender of the argument should say in response.
