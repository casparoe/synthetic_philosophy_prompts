I would like to know whether a pattern I keep meeting in historical writing has a name, and if it does not, I would like to see it named and bounded.

The pattern is this. When I ask why a large event occurred — a war, the collapse of a regime, the failure of a firm — I am usually given a list of causes, and each item on the list is defended in one of two ways. Some items are defended counterfactually: without them, the event would not have occurred. Others are defended by the contrary claim: without them, the event would have occurred anyway, because another item on the list would have produced it. It is seldom remarked that these are opposite defences, so that a list of indispensable causes and a list of dispensable ones are offered as though they were the same kind of list.

What actually puzzles me is a narrower thing. In some cases it seems true both that some event of a given kind was going to happen and that the event which happened was necessary in none of its details. I am told that a great power war was going to come, and that the alliance system, the arms race and the Balkan crisis were each enough to bring it about; yet the war that came began over this quarrel, lasted this long, and ended in this settlement. An institution I once worked for was going to close; the closing that occurred had different debts, different people and a different last day. When people say such an outcome was inevitable, they seem to slide between 'some outcome of this kind' and 'this very outcome', and the slide is what makes the claim feel at once undeniable and empty.

A third feature of these cases is that the search for the decisive cause never terminates. Each generation of historians nominates a different one. I suspect this is structural rather than a shortage of evidence: where several candidates would each have sufficed, no counterfactual test can single one out, and yet the question 'which was the real cause?' still feels like a factual question with an answer.

I have looked at the nearest things I could find and they do not fit. The philosophical cases in which two causes would each have produced the same outcome — a prisoner shot by a firing squad in which every rifleman's shot would have killed him — turn on whether any single shot may be called the cause of his death. There the death is the same death whichever bullet does the work; in my cases it matters which cause does the work, because the character of the outcome depends on it. I have also met the suggestion that when an outcome is guaranteed by many causes, the causes should be cited but not ranked. That seems too quick, and it says nothing about the details.

So: does this configuration have a name? If not, please coin one, define it, and mark its edges. I should like to know how it stands to the nearest concepts I may be confusing it with — the firing-squad cases, cases in which one cause does the work only because another did not get there first, and the idea that quite different paths may reach the same end state — and whether 'which cause was decisive?' and 'why then rather than at some other time?' have no answer in these cases or merely no easy one. Finally, please say what your account implies for two further cases. The first: an outcome with a single sufficient cause, alongside other candidates listed as contributing factors although they would not in fact have sufficed — is that the same phenomenon or a different one? The second: a friendship that ended, where I can see both that it was going to end and that the ending that occurred was a different thing from the ending that was going to occur.

Please answer in British English.
