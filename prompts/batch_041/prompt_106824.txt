I've spent the past couple of years reporting on the moral-uncertainty literature — the project of deciding what to do when you can't tell which moral theory is correct — and I've been asked to lay out my own settled position for a panel, so before I commit it to print I want it audited. Here is where I've landed. (1) When I'm unsure which of two options is morally better, I decide by expected moral value, weighting each candidate moral theory's verdict by my credence in that theory. (2) I don't think the major moral theories are commensurable: I can't say that a world which fully honors Kantian duty is worth more or less than a world with greater aggregate welfare, because these aren't amounts of a common currency. (3) I'm not a moral realist, and I don't believe there's a mind-independent fact that settles which theory is right. (4) I refuse to be a fanatic: I won't let a vanishingly small probability of some enormous value dictate how I live, which is why I give to well-evidenced global-health charities rather than liquidating my savings for a speculative long-shot cause. (5) I think the correct response to moral uncertainty is to favor the options that are acceptable by the lights of every theory I take seriously — the safe, overlapping choices.

What I want from you is an audit, not a survey. Tell me which of these commitments genuinely pull against each other and which only appear to, and rank the genuine tensions by severity rather than handing me an undifferentiated list of worries. For each real tension, say precisely what would have to give and what the cheapest repair would be, along with what that repair costs me elsewhere. Then tell me which single revision would resolve the most of the trouble at once, and what I'd be surrendering to make it. Assume I'm competent and that every one of the five is defensible on its own; the question is whether they can be held together.

Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give the reasoning. No preamble, and don't restate the question back to me.
