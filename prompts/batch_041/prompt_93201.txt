In 2031 a research consortium announces that it has reconstructed the lost Venetian and papal dispatches of the 1520s by training a generative model on the surviving diplomatic correspondence of the period. The synthetic documents are indistinguishable from authentic ones under every philological test the consortium can devise, they corroborate independently known events, and they fill gaps that historians had long treated as irrecoverable. The conference convened to debate this practice has asked you to write the keynote that Leopold von Ranke would deliver if he were alive to give it — not a pastiche of his prose, but the argument he would actually make, to an audience that is half archival historians and half machine-learning researchers.

Getting this right requires holding three things apart. First, Ranke's stated programme, which is well documented: the 1824 preface to his Histories of the Latin and Teutonic Peoples, with its refusal to have history judge the past or instruct the future and its insistence on rigorous presentation of the facts, and the lecture formula that every epoch is immediate to God and has its value in its own existence rather than in what issues from it. Second, his working practice, which is genuinely disputed: he prized eyewitness accounts, memoirs and diplomatic dispatches, and he is often taken as the founder of source-critical history, yet he also built sweeping narratives and held providential views about God's action in history. Third, the question actually at issue, which is whether the Rankean criterion for admitting a source is provenance — a chain of custody reaching back to an eyewitness — or whether it is corroboration and explanatory fit with the rest of the archive. Invent your own name for that distinction, and let the argument turn on it.

Then let the two later critics press him. Carr, in the 1961 Cambridge lectures collected as What Is History?, treats a historical fact as something the historian manufactures out of the facts of the past by selection in the light of present concerns, and treats history as an unending dialogue between present and past. White, in Metahistory (1973), argues that the historian prefigures the field and emplots it, so that even Ranke's realism is a literary mode rather than an escape from narrative. Decide which of these, if either, the reconstructed Ranke has the resources to answer, and where he simply loses.

Write the keynote as a reasoned reconstruction rather than a verdict, and let it concede at least one point to each critic — making clear which concession costs him more. Separate what is settled among historians of historiography from what is genuinely contested, and do not present a contested reading of Ranke's practice as the consensus. Do not put words in his mouth as direct quotation unless you are certain of the exact wording; where you are not, paraphrase or use indirect speech, and do not invent citations.
