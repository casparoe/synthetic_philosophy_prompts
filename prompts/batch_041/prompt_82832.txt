I hold a position about the timing of conscious experience and I want it stress-tested. Please reason from the substance rather than steering me toward a literature; I am after the argument, not a reading list.

The position concerns the color phi phenomenon. Two dots are flashed in different locations in rapid succession; when they differ in color, subjects report not only a single dot traveling between the two locations but a color change that seems to occur partway along the path — earlier than the second dot was presented. The report therefore carries information about the second stimulus before that stimulus existed. (The labels "Orwellian" and "Stalinesque" have been attached to the two halves of the fork below, and the same fork opens for related cases in which a later stimulus appears to erase or rewrite the experience of an earlier one.)

Two readings of such cases suggest themselves. On the post-hoc reading, the brain registers both stimuli and then edits the record of the earlier experience so that the color change is included: what is remembered as seen was never seen, and the revision happens after the experience. On the pre-emptive reading, the brain withholds the earlier experience from becoming conscious until the second stimulus arrives and then produces a single already-consistent experience: the revision happens before the experience. The two fit the behavioral data equally well.

My position is that the choice between them is not a real choice. Each presupposes that there is a determinate moment at which a given content becomes conscious — a privileged finish line in the brain at which the lights come on — and once that presupposition is dropped, "before the experience" and "after the experience" no longer pick out different hypotheses; they are two descriptions of one process by which content becomes available to the systems that use it. So I claim there is no fact of the matter as to which reading is correct.

Map this position's exposure for me.

- What would count against it? Give me concrete items — observations, experimental designs, conceptual or formal results — ranked from those that would force a genuine retreat to those that would be merely awkward.
- What would it take to give the distinction empirical content without begging the question, that is, without already assuming that some particular process is the finish line? Is there a marker, behavioral or neural, that could play that role and be defended non-arbitrarily — or does every candidate either presuppose the conclusion or reduce to "the finish line is wherever the theorist puts it"?
- What does the position predict that its rivals do not? If nothing beyond the predictions the rivals share, say so.
- Where does it risk self-undermining? If there is no fact of the matter about when a content becomes conscious, is the difference between genuinely undergoing an experience and merely seeming to remember or judge that one did also threatened? If so, is that a reductio or a consequence I should accept?
- What would the position's own defenders concede? I want the objections they must grant, not only the ones they can wave away.
- Finally: is the position falsifiable at all, and if not, is that a defect here or simply the price of denying that a distinction is well formed?

To be concrete about what I would find most persuasive: suppose someone demonstrated that some downstream system, one whose output we can measure, consumes the unrevised first color before the second stimulus arrives, while the eventual report is still interpolated. That seems to drive a wedge between the content that was available and the content that was experienced. Does it really? Or does my position have a principled way to absorb it?
