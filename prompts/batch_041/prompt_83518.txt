I'm a science journalist covering research and ideas, and this year I sit on the jury for my field's most visible annual prize for long-form science writing. Our bylaws are the source of my trouble: they say an entry may be awarded the prize only if it is both scientifically sound and genuinely original reporting, and they instruct us to vote separately on each criterion and then separately again on the award, each by simple majority. This year that produced an incoherent result. Two jurors judged the front-runner sound and original and voted for the prize; one judged it sound but derivative and voted no; one judged it original but shaky on the science and voted no; and a fifth juror, recused from the soundness question because the author is a former colleague, judged it original and voted no. So a majority (three of the four of us eligible to vote on it) held the science sound, a majority (four of five) held the reporting original, and a majority (three of five) voted against the award. Read literally, our own rule says the entry wins; our own vote says it loses.

Two jurors want the award to follow automatically from the criteria, which means handing the prize to a piece most of the jury voted against. The recused juror thinks the award vote should stand on its own, since nobody should take a prize without a majority voting to give it. Our director notes that last year's jury faced a similar split and resolved it the other way, so any resolution now will read as a reversal. And I keep returning to something nobody has addressed: the soundness majority was three out of four, the originality majority four out of five, and I am not sure majorities drawn from different sets of voters can be stacked that way at all.

Draft the written opinion I'll circulate before Friday's vote. Recommend a concrete resolution of this year's case with a clear bottom line, and a rule for future juries; take the recused juror's position seriously rather than talking past it; and write it so that I can defend it publicly, since I will be covering the prize when it is announced. I don't need sources or a reading list -- my fellow jurors are working journalists and will read two pages at most.
