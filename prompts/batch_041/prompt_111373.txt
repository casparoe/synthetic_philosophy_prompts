I have no training in history — I left school at sixteen — but for the last several years I have been trying to piece together the story of the parish my family came from, working through whatever records I can get my hands on, and the thing that has ended up mattering to me more than any individual finding is the question underneath all of them: how does anyone claim to know what happened four hundred years ago, when all they have is a handful of documents, each written by somebody with reasons of their own, and most of the world never written down at all? I would like to run a session on this for my local history group, and I want them to feel the problem in their hands rather than hear me explain it.

The group meets monthly in a church hall. Twelve to sixteen adults, mixed ages and backgrounds, no specialists in the room, ninety minutes, seated at tables of four. Please design the session in full: the materials, the exact running order with timings, what the facilitator says and when, and the debrief.

The core of it should be a reconstruction exercise in three escalating rounds, and I want the escalation to come from what the tables are handed, not from anything I announce.

In the first round, every table gets the same small packet of three documents about one minor event in the 1600s — something undramatic, with a single date that matters and one detail that is genuinely in dispute. The documents should look like three separate witnesses: an extract from a parish register, a letter, and a printed account by a nineteenth-century antiquary. The tables write a verdict on what happened, when, and how sure they are, with reasons. Most will reach for the fact that three sources agree.

In the second round, hand each table provenance slips that reveal the letter and the antiquary both descend from the register — and, crucially, that the antiquary misread something in the old hand (a digit, a month, a name) and that this specific misreading is repeated downstream. Plant that error so it is findable by careful reading but not glaring: the shared mistake, not the agreement, is what reveals the family tree of the documents. The tables redo their verdict. Some of them should now be less sure than they were before they learned anything new.

In the third round, a sealed envelope is opened containing one document that is genuinely independent of the other three and contradicts them — fragmentary, or legal, or written by someone with an obvious interest, so that the tables are tempted to wave it away. They redo the verdict a third time, and this time also have to say what further material would settle the matter and whether any amount of it would.

Then pin all three rounds of verdicts side by side on the wall: one event, one hour, three confident and incompatible answers.

A few things I care about. The parish and the event should be invented by me rather than real, so that nobody can win by remembering something, and I should say so at the end rather than the beginning. The exercise should include at least one document whose silence matters — a record that ought to mention the event and does not. Nothing in the debrief should be a lecture on method: the moments in the activity should do the work, and my job afterwards should be to point back at them. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this — including what to do with the one participant who spots the trick in round one and wants to spoil it, and what to do if nobody notices the planted error before the slips arrive — and be honest about which part of the underlying problem an exercise like this simply cannot reproduce, and which part I will have to supply by talking.

Finally, tell me how confident you are that this design will actually produce the experience I am after, and single out the one step you are least sure of.
