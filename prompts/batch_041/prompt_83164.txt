I'm writing something arguing that the paradox of deontology only bites because deontologists borrow a harm-based justification for constraints and then refuse to follow it through, so I need a case where that borrowing is visible. Build me one where a deontologist has to say: I may not violate this one constraint myself, even though doing so is the only way to stop a much larger number of violations of the very same constraint by other people. Constraints on the case: the constraint shouldnt obviously protect any particular person -- something like a promise made to someone who's now dead, or a rule about what you're permitted to do with a body after death -- so nobody can just wave the "constraints protect rights" story at it; the violation she'd commit mustn't be a causal means of preventing the others, so she isn't using anyone as an instrument; she can't get somebody else to do the violating for her; and it has to be somewhere a normal person could plausibly end up, no trolleys and no ticking bombs. Also make the number of prevented violations big enough that any threshold which says "no" starts looking arbitrary, but not so big that it degenerates into a straight numbers game. Then walk me through: what the deontologist says and why, which single feature of the case is doing the real work, and what happens if you swap the victimless constraint for an ordinary one like not killing -- does the squeeze get tighter or looser? Finish with the strongest thing a deontologist can say to show this isn't a paradox at all, just a badly described case.
