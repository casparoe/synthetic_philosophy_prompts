QUESTION 2. Our seminar had a visiting speaker who argued roughly as follows: the thermodynamic arrow of time is not a real feature of the world, it's an artefact of our ignorance. Entropy increase is a statistical claim, and probability claims are made from a point of view that already has a past and a future, so the statistical-mechanical story presupposes the very asymmetry it claims to explain.

(a) Lay out the standard statistical-mechanical account of the arrow of time. Be explicit about which parts are dynamical laws and which are assumptions about boundary conditions, and say where exactly the asymmetry enters.
(b) Assess the speaker's circularity charge. Is the account viciously circular, or can the asymmetry be located in a contingent fact rather than in the notion of probability?

STUDENT ANSWER.

Start with the laws. Newton's second law, Hamilton's equations, Schrödinger's equation — none of them privilege a direction. If a sequence of states satisfies the equations, so does the time-reverse of that sequence. As far as we know this holds for every fundamental interaction, so the arrow can't be coming from the dynamics.

So it has to come from the initial conditions. This is Boltzmann's insight, and it's why he proposed the Past Hypothesis: the universe began in an extremely low-entropy state, and since then entropy has been climbing toward its maximum. Two things follow. First, the second law isn't a law in the same sense as the dynamical laws; it's a statistical regularity that holds because we're starting from an atypical corner of phase space, and the overwhelming majority of trajectories out of such a corner lead to higher entropy. Second, entropy increases in both directions away from a low-entropy state, which means the Past Hypothesis is doing all the directional work. If we ever found ourselves near a low-entropy state we'd call the low-entropy direction the past, and that's exactly what we do, because our memories and our records are themselves entropy-increasing processes.

Boltzmann tried to derive the second law directly with the H-theorem, but Loschmidt's reversibility objection — the one about Poincaré recurrence — shows the derivation is mathematically invalid: since the equations are reversible, for every trajectory along which H decreases there is a time-reversed trajectory along which H increases, so the theorem cannot be right as stated. The modern consensus is that you simply have to put the low-entropy boundary condition in by hand.

On the circularity charge: I don't think it lands. The Past Hypothesis is a contingent claim about how the universe actually started. Given that condition and the dynamics, everything else follows, and you don't need to smuggle in a prior notion of "the future". The probabilities involved are just combinatorial facts about how many microstates realise a given macrostate and what fraction of those evolve into what, and counting microstates doesn't presuppose a direction of time. So the asymmetry is located in a contingent boundary condition rather than in the concept of probability, and the account isn't circular. If anything the speaker has it backwards: it isn't that our probability talk presupposes a past, it's that we have a past, a low-entropy one, and that's what makes the probability talk useful.
