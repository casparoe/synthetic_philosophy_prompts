I'm part of a graduate reading group that is working through Wittgenstein's later remarks on religion, and each of us has to bring a short position paper to spark discussion. I've drafted mine below and would like your comments on it. I'd ask you to engage with the argument as it stands rather than send me a reading list or citations; I want to know whether the paper is any good, not where to look next.

Pictures, Not Hypotheses: What Wittgenstein Gets Right About Religious Belief, and Where He Overreaches

The most useful thing in Wittgenstein's later treatment of religion is the refusal to treat the believer's utterances as hypotheses. When a religious person says that God exists, or that the dead will be raised, we are not, on this view, being handed a prediction that further evidence could confirm or embarrass. We are being shown a picture that regulates a whole life — a way of taking the world, of weighing what matters, of holding one's own death. Wittgenstein's habit of describing a practice rather than explaining it, together with his insistence that the sense of a word is fixed by the use it has in a form of life, suggests that the religious language game has its own grammar, and that importing the standards of empirical inquiry into it is a category mistake rather than a refutation.

This is a genuine advance over the flat evidentialism that treats belief in God as structurally like belief in an undiscovered planet. The evidentialist picture makes the religious person's confidence look like a failure of reasoning, and it cannot explain why the practices — prayer, fasting, ritual — are not mere decorations on a proposition but the very thing that gives the words their life. On the picture view, the practices are what make the words mean anything at all.

But the view overreaches when it is asked to do too much. If religious belief is a picture that regulates a life, it becomes hard to see how one religious life could be criticized from within another, or how a believer could ever discover that she had been wrong — not merely that she had lapsed, but that the thing she had trusted was not so. Wittgenstein himself tells a parable that seems to cut against his own doctrine: a man is sent on a pilgrimage and made to perform a series of devotional exercises, only to be told at the end that the point was never the itinerary but the prayer it was meant to produce, with the ring he carries in his own pocket serving as the emblem of the whole business. The story suggests that the exercises are instrumental to a state of the person, not reports about the furniture of the world. If that is the model, then religious disagreement starts to look like a disagreement about which diet to follow, and the believer's own sense that she is answerable to something beyond herself quietly evaporates. The account, in other words, risks collapsing into a non-cognitivism that no actual believer would recognize as her own position.

My attempted repair is to keep the picture account but to restrict its scope. I want to say that some religious utterances function as regulative pictures while others genuinely make claims, and that the difference is not one of content but of role in the life of the speaker — a distinction I can gesture at but cannot yet draw cleanly.

What I'd like from you is three things. First, an accuracy check: have I represented Wittgenstein's position fairly, or have I quietly made him say something he would have rejected? Second, an assessment of the objection itself — is the charge of overreach strong, or am I attacking a straw man that a careful reader of the later work would not recognize? Third, if you think my repair is salvageable, tell me what the strongest objection to it would be, and whether the distinction between a picture and a claim can really be drawn without smuggling back in the very theory of meaning Wittgenstein was trying to dissolve.
