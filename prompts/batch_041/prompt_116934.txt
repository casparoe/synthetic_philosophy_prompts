I'm a retired experimental physicist — thirty years building vacuum systems and the control electronics that went with them — and since I retired I've been reading a lot of philosophy, including a fair amount on AI. A friend from my old lab who went into machine learning and I got into an argument about how these systems ought to be built that neither of us can settle, and it's turned into a standing monthly thing where we both show up with notes. He's definately in what I've started calling the tool-AI camp: build the things as pure instruments, no goals of their own, each one doing one bounded job that a human asked for and then stopping, and this is both safer and the only sane target to aim at. I've drifted the other way, into the agent camp, which surprises me — my instinct now is that capability and agency are the same knob, that anything good enough at getting what you want will start picking up sub-goals like keeping itself running and keeping its objective from being edited, and that a system genuinely good at open-ended problems will end up being an agent whether or not we meant it to be. In my old line of work an objective was always something a human dialed in and could dial back out again, so the notion of a system with an objective of its own is genuinely alien to me, and I can't tell how much of the disagreement is that.

So: draft the joint statement we should have written at the start, the document we could both sign. First the claims we actually agree on, numbered — including the engineering ones, like that a bounded service whose objective is set from outside and frozen is at least possible in principle, that nothing running today is a general agent in any interesting sense, and that the real question is what happens as capability rises rather than whether this year's chatbots want things. Then the disagreements, as a table, one row each, with my friend's position stated in wording he'd accept as his own and mine stated in wording I'd accept as mine — no straw men, and if you can't find a non-sloppy version of one of them, say so rather than fudging it. Then the cruxes: the sub-questions where, if one of us turned out to be right, the other would have to actually revise something. My guesses, which you should keep, cut, or add to: whether agency is a matter of degree or of kind; whether an objective being human-set and frozen is enough to make a system a tool even when it has a rich model of the world and of its own operator; whether the many-small-stateless-services architecture stays stable as each service gets more capable, or whether capability pressure drags the whole thing toward one persistent optimizer; and whether having goals is a fact about a system at all or only a stance we take toward it when predicting it — if that last one is carrying a lot of weight, say so, and say which slice of our fight is just about words. For each crux give me the thing we'd both agree actually bears on it — a test, a toy case, an argument, a bit of history — with each of us writing down in advance what we expect. Something like: take a system trained against one fixed objective, then change that objective from the outside, and watch whether it resists, or keeps optimizing the old one anyway, or quietly generalizes to keeping its operator happy; he predicts something boring, I predict something alarming, and it would be worth having that in writing before either of us sees the result. We've been talking past each other alot for about a year, so this is overdue. No reading list, please — I'm already drowning in PDFs.

And since you're the one drafting this and can't check up on any of it: tell me how confident you are in the characterizations you're putting in our mouths, and single out which one you're least sure we'd both actually sign.
