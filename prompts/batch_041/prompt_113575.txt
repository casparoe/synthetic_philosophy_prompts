For most of my adult life I have treated deduction as a way of extending what I know: if I know a premise and I know that the premise entails a conclusion, I have assumed I know the conclusion, and I have acted on that assumption, sometimes at work and sometimes about my own life. That assumption was shaken a few weeks ago, during an argument with a friend about why conspiracy theories are so hard to talk someone out of, when she explained Nozick's tracking account of knowledge to me — that you know something only if your belief would not have survived the thing's being false, and would have been there had it been true — and I found, to my considerable annoyance, that I agreed with it, and that it gives the sceptic the worst of it while leaving me with my hands and my laptop. The trouble is that it also seems to sever knowledge from its consequences, and I now notice that I do not have a principled view about which of the two I would rather keep. I would like to settle this as a specification problem rather than go on trading intuitions.

So please write me the desiderata for an adequate theory of knowledge in this region — a theory that has to say what knowledge is and also what becomes of knowledge under entailment, where adequacy is measured by whether the resulting account can be used by someone who has to act on deductive conclusions and not merely admired from a seminar table. What I am after is the requirements list, with the motivating case carried in each entry. The cases it must get right: the asymmetry that lets ordinary knowledge stand while the sceptical hypothesis remains unruled out; the subject who believes a true thing on evidence that would have produced the same belief had the thing been false; the subject who is right about what she is looking at, though she would have been fooled by any of the convincing imitations nearby; the deductive chain whose premise is known and whose conclusion, by the theory's own lights, is not; the necessary truths, which threaten to satisfy a subjunctive condition by default and so make knowledge of them free; and knowledge that arrives by testimony or memory, where the belief seems to survive counterfactual changes in the facts precisely because it tracks the informant rather than the world. The distinctions it cannot collapse: between knowing and knowing that one knows; between the failure of closure and the failure of knowledge; between a warrant that is carried across an entailment and one that merely fails to be destroyed by it; between a possibility one cannot rule out and a possibility one has reason to take seriously; and between a theory of what knowledge is and a theory of when we are entitled to say that someone knows. And the fixed points it must live with: that we do extend knowledge by deduction and by deference often enough that a theory forbidding it is describing something other than our epistemic lives, and that the tracking conditions are evaluated relative to a belief-forming method, which any such theory must individuate in some non-arbitrary way or else remain tunable to whatever verdict one wants.

For each requirement, say what consideration motivates it and whether you regard it as non-negotiable or as a prejudice of one camp, and tell me which single requirement does the most to exclude otherwise attractive theories. Then say which subset of the list you believe cannot be jointly satisfied, and commit: name the requirement you would surrender, and tell me what the surrender costs in the cases above. I would much rather have a definite position I can argue with than a survey of options.
