I've fallen down a rabbit hole on the Chinese School of Names — Gongsun Long, Hui Shi, Deng Xi, that crowd — and I keep coming back to the white horse thing. I think I understand the machinery of it: horse picks out something by shape, white picks out something by color, and a word that picks out color isn't a word that picks out shape; then the sharp bit, where if you ask for a horse a brown one will do, but if you ask for a white horse a brown one won't, so the two phrases can't be picking out the same thing. What I can't get is what any of that would feel like from the inside, whether it's a genuine discovery about language or just an extremely tidy trick. So: write me a short story that puts me inside it.

Setting, if it helps: a toll station on a mountain road at the edge of a collapsing emipre, late autumn, everything wet. The posted rule is that anyone bringing a horse through pays, and the toll is punishing. A traveler arrives leading one white horse. The clerk on duty has spent the season reading a book of dialectical puzzles and has concluded, honestly and without any smirk, that a white horse is not a horse, and therefore nothing is owed; he will not be moved by common sense. I'd like the argument to actually unfold on the page, the way it does in the dialogue — the clerk making his moves, the traveler or a bystander pushing back the way anyone would push back, and the clerk having a reply ready. Please work in at least one pass over whether "is not" means "isn't the same as" or "isn't a kind of", one over whether the whiteness in the horse is a thing in its own right or just the horse, painted, and if you can fit it, one over whether hardness and whiteness can both be truly in a single stone at once. Then bring in a third figure: an old friend of the clerk, a retired magistrate who went through the same books and came out the other side convinced that all distinctions are conveniences and that things are ultimately one, so the two of them can fall out over whether names carve anything real at all.

Aim for around 1,500 words. No lectures dressed up as dialogue — it has to matter that a particular man is standing in the rain with a particular animal and definately no money. And don't resolve it at the end; I'd rather finish unsure whether the clerk is a sophist, a saint of consistency, or just someone who's found a loophole and enjoys it.
