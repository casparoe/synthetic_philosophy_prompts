I'm writing a novel whose central figure is a philosopher closely modelled on Leibniz, and I need him to rule on a question that never occurred to him. I have kept his doctrine that God must create the best of all possible worlds, his criterion that a world's perfection lies in the greatest variety joined to the greatest order, his claim that existence is itself a perfection, and his view that the best world contains the fullest set of compossible things.

In the scene a modern interlocutor puts two worlds to him. The first holds a modest number of people whose lives are rich and various — deep inquiry, great music, the kind of harmony he prizes. The second holds an astronomical number of people whose lives are only barely worth living: no wretchedness to speak of, but no excellence either, nothing he would call order or variety. By every aggregate measure of welfare the interlocutor can name, the second world wins enormously; by every measure of excellence per life, the first does. He then presses a harder case: take the first world and trade one of its finest achievements — a symphony, a proof — for a great many additional merely adequate lives, and continue until nothing excellent is left.

Rule for him. Does he rank the second world above the first, and by what measure exactly — does the doctrine that existence is a perfection, and that the best world is the fullest compossible one, drive him toward the aggregator's answer, or does his perfectionism block it? If it blocks it, give me the shape of his ordering — lexicographic in order and variety, or something else — and say whether the trade-off is better modelled as a compossibility constraint, the symphony and the extra lives being unable to belong to one and the same world, and whether that changes which world comes out maximal. Give me a committed reading: choose the verdict his system actually yields, defend it against the obvious opposing reading, tell me which of his commitments you had to bend to get there, and what the novel loses if the opposing reading is the right one.
