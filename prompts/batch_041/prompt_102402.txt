Ok, so I'm a novelist. Literary-ish stuff mostly, but the book I'm trying to get off the ground is this philosophical thriller about a machine that watches people and works backwards from what they do to what they actually want. Think a camera network that never asks anybody anything, it just infers. The whole thing dies if the ideas underneath it are fake, so I've been poking around the field that actually does this — inverse reinforcement learning, preference inference, whatever you want to call it — and now I'm stuck on where to put the weight.

Here's my ask. Of all the open questions in this area, which ones should the book actually hinge on? I don't want a reading list and I don't need citations, I want a ranked argument. Give me the two or three questions most worth building a plot around, in order, and then tell me which ones are overrated — the ones that sound profound in a seminar but are basically settled, or that nobody who actually works on this loses sleep over. I'm less interested in the list than in why: which questions are upstream of the others, which would actually change the shape of a story, and what would move something up or down your ranking.

Some things I've been chewing on, just so you get the register I'm after. One, the machine sees a person do something and there are apparently a hundred different wants that would explain it equally well — so how do you even say which want the person really has, or is that just not a fact about them? Two, everybody in my book is being watched and knows it, so they're partly choosing in order to be seen choosing, which seems to break the whole idea that behavior reveals desire. Three, I keep reading that people don't discover their preferences so much as build them in the act of choosing, which would mean my machine isn't reading anyone, it's writing them. Four, once people know the machine is scoring them, they start wanting whatever gets them a better score — so the thing it's trying to measure is chasing its own tail. Five, whether a single person even has one stable ordering of wants, or whether that's a fiction we tell for convenience.

The machine in my book can never ask anyone what they want. It only ever gets behavior. So keep that in mind — if a question only exists because some lab can run surveys, it's no use to me.

Also, one constraint on the answer: no bibliography, no names of papers, no dates. A friend of mine reads everything I write and she can't look anything up, and I want her to be able to follow this too. American English please, I'm publishing in the States.
