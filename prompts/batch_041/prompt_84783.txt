I edit a section on science and ideas, and for a special issue built around the theme of small acts and large arithmetic, I am commissioning a short story of roughly 2,000 to 4,000 words that dramatizes the paradox of voting rather than explaining it.

The paradox, in brief: because a single ballot almost never decides an election, the expected instrumental payoff of voting appears to be smaller than the cost of casting it, and yet hundreds of millions of people vote anyway. Fiction that takes this up tends to exit through one of two doors — the voter decides that her ballot is expressive, a declaration of who she is rather than a lever on the world; or she decides that the expected-value calculation is a category error, because duty, habit, or the collateral pleasures of participation settle the matter. I would like a story that either refuses both doors or walks through one of them and shows what it costs.

I would like the story built around two ideas that are further into the weeds than the standard telling, and that should be dramatized rather than lectured about:

First, the famous figure — the one you get by treating each voter as an independent coin flip, which puts the odds that a single ballot decides a national election at one in many millions — is not the number that governs anything. When the shocks that move voters are correlated, when a plant closing, a storm, or a scandal breaks the same way across every precinct at once, the probability that any given ballot is decisive rises by orders of magnitude, into the thousands or the hundreds. Whether your vote matters is not a fixed property of the world; it is an artifact of how the errors are correlated. Give me a character who learns that the number she has been treating as a punchline is contingent in exactly this way, and who has to decide what to do with that knowledge.

Second, decisiveness and informativeness pull in opposite directions. A vote decides something only in the worlds where the contest is close, and a contest is close precisely when the people who share your information and your circumstances are split evenly against everyone else — which means that the situation in which your ballot weighs most is also the situation in which your reasons for choosing as you did are least likely to be representative of anything. Run that logic through a person, not a theorem: a well-informed citizen who works out that the states of the world in which she is pivotal are the states in which she is most likely to be wrong, and who has to live with the conclusion.

A few constraints. Invent the jurisdiction, the office, and the question on the ballot; do not set the story in a real election or lean on real politicians, parties, pollsters, or the familiar recount anecdotes. Nobody in the story should explain the paradox to anybody else, and no character should be handed the author's conclusions. I would rather have a concrete situation — a canvasser, a county clerk, a forecaster who sells pivotal-probability estimates, a woman standing in a booth with her phone out — than a debate.

Please close with a brief afterword addressed to me rather than folded into the fiction. It should say which contested reading of the paradox the story commits to, where the story takes liberties with the ideas (invented mechanisms, numbers that have been massaged, correlations asserted rather than demonstrated), and what would change your mind: which argument, piece of evidence, or counterexample about the paradox would push you toward a different ending or a different fate for the central character, and where you think the story's implicit reasoning is most likely to be wrong.
