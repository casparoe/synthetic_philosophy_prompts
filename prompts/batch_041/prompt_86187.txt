I have been reading the statistics-and-foundations arguments that circulate on the rationalist blogs, in the metascience literature, and in the comment threads of methodology papers for something like a decade, and I have watched the same fight about the replication crisis fail to terminate for as long as I have been watching it. The two camps are familiar. On one side are the Bayesians and likelihoodists, who argue that the original error was treating a p-value as a measure of evidence, that a p-value answers a question nobody asked — how surprising the data would be if the null were true — and that the remedy is to report something proportional to the likelihood or to a posterior, so the reader can see how far the data should move their belief. On the other side are the error-statisticians, who argue that the only guarantee available before seeing data is a long-run one, that a posterior is only as trustworthy as its prior, and that the practical fix is to repair the machinery rather than replace it: preregister the analysis, lower the threshold, correct for multiple comparisons, and above all control the rate at which the community asserts falsehoods. Both sides agree the published literature is badly unreliable. Both sides agree that p-hacking, selective reporting, and chronic underpowering are real and pervasive. Neither defends the naive practice of running a test and declaring victory because p came in under .05. And yet the prescriptions do not converge, and the argument recurs with the same moves every few years.

What makes me doubt this is a clean philosophical split is how weak the sociological correlation is. I know committed subjectivists who preregister their analyses and report confidence intervals as a matter of course, and I know frequentists who reason quietly in likelihood ratios and would never deny that a surprising result should shift their belief. The people who hold the incompatible-sounding philosophies do not, in practice, behave as though they were running incompatible research programs.

Consider the oldest bone of contention between them: whether the stopping rule matters. The likelihoodist says that once you have the data, how you decided to stop collecting it is irrelevant to what the data tell you; the error-statistician says the stopping rule is precisely what fixes the error properties of the procedure, and ignoring it is how you fool yourself. Each is right about his own question, which is exactly why the exchange never resolves.

My working hypothesis, which I would like you to attack rather than accept, is that what presents itself as one foundational dispute is really three disputes wearing a single name, and that most of the heat comes from confusing them. The first is verbal: a disagreement about what the word probability ought to denote, whether a degree of belief, a long-run frequency, a propensity, or something else. The second is genuinely formal: a disagreement about which procedures have which properties, whether a given interval is calibrated, whether a given update rule is coherent, whether a given estimator is admissible under a stated loss. The third is decision-theoretic, and I suspect it is where the real action is: a disagreement about which properties we should want, given that a working scientist ordinarily gets one shot at one dataset and cannot literally average over hypothetical repetitions of an experiment that will never be run again. If that is the structure, then the first layer is nearly empty, the second is decidable by mathematics, and the third is a genuine but different kind of disagreement about what statistical practice is for.

So tell me: what kind of disagreement is this, really? Is it verbal, substantive, or two questions under one heading — how a single agent should update, versus how a scientific community should regulate its collective error rate? Is a hidden empirical question doing the work, for instance about the actual distribution of effect sizes and the actual base rates of true hypotheses in a given literature, such that one camp's defaults are simply better calibrated to the world and the other's to a different world? Is the impasse a sign that the question is ill-posed, or merely that it is hard? I want the diagnosis grounded in what each side actually claims, not a caricature of either, and I want you to say plainly which parts of my three-layer hypothesis survive contact with the positions and which do not. Then tell me what would count as progress: a theorem, a simulation, a conceptual distinction, a change in what we are trying to do, or nothing at all. And be honest about the question I care about most — is the foundations fight even load-bearing for the replication crisis, or would the crisis persist more or less unchanged under either philosophy, because its real drivers are incentives, base rates, and the file drawer rather than the interpretation of probability?
