ok so this started as a dumb argument in my group chat and it's now eaten a whole week. Someone claimed that when you genuinely have no information about a continuous quantity, you should just put a flat prior on it. I said fine, flat on what — the side length, or the volume, or the log of the side length? — and the answer came out different everytime, which I'm pretty sure is just Bertrand's chord thing wearing a hat. Then someone else said the fix is to be careful and pick the parametrization that "matches the symmetry," which sounds great until you notice that for a scale parameter, like an unknown upper bound, the symmetry-respecting prior goes like 1/x, which isn't a probability distribution at all, so now the fix needs a cutoff that you just made up. We also got stuck on a coin: flat on the bias p, flat on the log-odds, Laplace's add-one rule, Haldane's 0/0 — all "uninformative," all giving different answers on the same data, and the differences don't wash out as fast as I'd like them to. At some point someone said "principle of indifference" and we all just stared at each other.

So before we keep going, what I actually want is the desiderata. What does any theory of "uninformative" or "objective" priors have to deliver to count as adequate? Give me the full spec — the cases it has to get right (the chord thing, the factory-that-makes-cubes-with-an-unknown-max-size business, the coin, and honestly I want to know whether it even has to say anything about a prior over hypotheses in an infinite-dimensional space like the ones the Solomonoff-flavored people wave around), the distinctions it can't collapse (real ignorance vs. a symmetry you actually know you have; the prior vs. the likelihood; an actual probability distribution vs. a merely formal measure you never normalize), and the results it has to stay consistent with (the usual probability axioms, conditionalization, maybe Dutch-book-style arguments). For each requirement, tell me the case or consideration that motivates it, and mark which ones are non-negotiable vs. which are just habits of one camp — I suspect "flat prior" is a habit and "invariance" is doing the real work, but I could be wrong. And the part I most want: tell me which subset of these can't be satisfied together. I have a strong suspicion that "makes no arbitrary choices / is invariant under reparametrization" and "is actually a normalizable distribution" are in direct conflict for any unbounded parameter, and if that's right I want to know which one people quietly give up and what it costs them. A short scorecard at the end — flat, maxent, Jeffreys-type, group-invariance, Solomonoff — is fine, but don't make that the bulk of it; I care way more about the criteria than about the contestants.
