I direct the historical society in a mid-sized town that holds a Founders' Festival every August: a costumed procession, a charter read aloud on the square, an "ancient" harvest rite performed by schoolchildren. My own research, which the society published last year, showed that the whole thing was assembled in the 1930s by the chamber of commerce — the charter is a fabrication drafted by a schoolteacher in 1934, the procession was copied from a village two counties over, and the rite first appears in a tourism brochure. Worse, and less well known, the 1930s invention displaced an older and genuinely local observance that no one now remembers. I have been asked to give this year's keynote, in front of two audiences at once: townspeople for whom the day is sacred and who have read my research, and visiting historians who will be listening for whether I debunk or whitewash. Write the speech. It must honor the festival and the community it actually makes without asserting anything false about its age or origins, and without letting the exposure do the work of contempt; the audience should leave with a way of understanding how a tradition can be recent, deliberately manufactured, and still genuinely binding — and what is lost when one layer of forgetting is replaced by another. About eight minutes. Let the argument about invented tradition and imagined community shape what the speech can and cannot say; do not quote or cite any scholarship, and do not have me name any theorists — the audience needs the thought, not a reading list.
