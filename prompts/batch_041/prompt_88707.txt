Me and a friend have this recurring thing: we've read the same material, we're both careful, and we still land on opposite sides of a question, and then I never know what to do with the fact that she disagrees with me. So I've been collecting the policies people actually seem to use. Tier them, S down to F, for how defensible each is as a general rule for what to believe when someone you respect, and who's about as well placed as you are, tells you you're wrong. Short note on what's carrying the weight in each case:

a) move to the midpoint between your credence and hers
b) keep your credence, but cut your confidence that you're the sort of person who gets these things right
c) keep both, and treat the disagreement as evidence about the subject matter but not about your own competence
d) ignore it — you looked at the evidence, that's the end of it
e) adopt the credence of an average member of everyone who's looked into it, yourself included
f) keep believing the thing while also believing you shouldn't believe it
g) move toward her, but by less than half, on the grounds that you can see your own reasoning and only her conclusion

Then tell me which of these can be applied by both of us at once — where neither of us has to be wrong about the other's competence for the policy to even make sense. I suspect that cuts the list differently than the tiers do and I want to know if I'm right.
