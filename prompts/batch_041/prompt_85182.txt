A friend and I spent most of last night arguing about whether an agent can be built to avoid side effects, and whether an impact measure can stand in for a specification of value, and he kept falling back on surgery. A surgeon, he said, cuts as little as possible, takes only what the operation demands, and leaves the rest of the body untouched; restraint there is a matter of technique rather than any commitment about what is good, and she needs no complete account of a patient's well-being to know not to nick an artery. His conclusion was that low-impact behavior is conservative by construction rather than by value judgment, and that this ought to make us more relaxed about leaving the objective unspecified. I have heard versions of the same move from several people, and I do not trust it. Audit the analogy for me: give the correspondence table — patient, operation, healthy body, the surgeon's training and standard of care, the complication, the consent — then say where the mapping breaks, which features of the surgical case it quietly imports that an agent acting in the world lacks, and above all what does the work there that the body does in the operating room, since something has to fix which changes count as side effects at all. Then tell me whether his conclusion survives once the disanalogies are on the table, separating the ones that bear on his claim from the merely decorative, and whether a better analogy is available.
