I am presenting my term paper at the end-of-term workshop for my course on decision theory and formal epistemology, and I want to walk in ready for the discussion. The paper argues that the familiar theorem that the expected value of information is never negative — the result usually attributed to Good — is not a general truth about rational choice but rests on an assumption its standard statements leave unstated: that the agent's preferences over the consequences of her options do not depend on what she has learned. I use cases in which the utility of a consequence turns on the agent's beliefs (someone who would rather not learn a genetic risk; a reader who enjoys a mystery less for knowing the ending) to argue that even a fully Bayesian expected-utility maximizer can face a negative value of information, and that the usual proof — that she can always ignore what she is told — fails here precisely because the information changes the very beliefs that enter her utility.

The room will be my instructor, whose own work is on state-dependent utility; three or four PhD students in formal epistemology; two economists who apply decision theory; and two philosophers of science who are skeptical of importing decision-theoretic machinery into questions about evidence. Prepare my Q&A prep sheet: the objections I will actually get from these people, ordered by how likely and how damaging they are, phrased the way each of them would put it, each with a two-minute reply and a note on where that reply is weakest. Include the question I am most hoping no one asks.
