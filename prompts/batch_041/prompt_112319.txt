Two members of the drafting committee for a post-conflict settlement are arguing over its amnesty clause. The clause grants immunity from prosecution to anyone who gives the commission a full account of their acts and whose acts satisfy a political-objective test — the commission must decide whether an act was done in pursuit of a political aim or instead for private gain or cruelty — and it makes the commission's findings inadmissible in any later criminal proceeding. One drafter helped design the clause and defends it as the least bad way of buying the truth that a fractured society needs in order to hold together. The other was appointed to the committee at the insistence of the victims' associations and holds that the clause is an injustice that compounds the original wrong.

Write their dialogue. Both are competent and neither is a fool; each must answer the other's actual arguments rather than restate a position. Let each press the strongest version of their case, and let the exchange work its way to at least three hard points: whether the state can forgo what is not only its to forgo — that is, whether amnesty waives the state's own claim to punish or instead extinguishes the victims' claim against the perpetrator, and whether those two are really separable; whether conditioning immunity on disclosure puts a price on a wrong and so misdescribes what was done; and whether the political-objective test is a genuine standard or merely the state choosing which crimes it finds forgivable. Do not resolve the dispute tidily — leave the crux exposed.

Present the dialogue as exactly five numbered exchanges, each consisting of one speech by each of the two speakers, each speech a short paragraph. No scene-setting and no closing commentary: nothing but the five exchanges.
