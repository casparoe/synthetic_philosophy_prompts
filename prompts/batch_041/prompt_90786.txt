I'm a novelist — second book, first one did fine — and I've been stuck for about eight months on a manuscript whose whole spine is an argument between two characters about whether an ant colony can have a mind. One is a semi-retired entomologist who insists on calling the colony 'her'; the other is her nephew, a computational-something grad student who thinks the entire idea is sentimental nonsense dressed up in systems talk. Years ago somebody lent me a book of philosophical dialogues with an ant colony character called Aunt Hillary, and it's been rattling around in my head ever since, but I don't want the fight in the book to be two people restating the obvious for and against each other. So what I need from you is a decision tree that starts from a commitment both my characters would actually sign, something like: the colony's behavior is vastly more sophisticated than any single ant's, and nobody is in charge — and branches out to the actual positions a person can end up holding. The branch questions have to be answerable by a reader who has no idea which camp the answer drops them into, so nothing phrased as 'do you believe in group minds?'. The distinctions I want it built out of, roughly: whether the colony's states are about anything or merely co-vary with things; whether there's one computation going on or a lot of loosely coupled ones; whether the pheromone trails and the nest are part of the mind or just the medium it runs on; whether it matters that individual ants are interchangeable and constantly replaced; whether the colony's interests (if it has any) are anything over and above the summed interests of ants; and whether the whole thing counts as a mind only when somebody is there to interpret it. Push hardest on that last one, because I'm fairly sure that's where my two characters actually disagree and neither of them has noticed yet.

Build the nodes out of concrete cases, since I'll be stealing them for scenes: (a) a colony where the real ants get swapped one at a time for identical mechanical ones, with the trails topped up by a pump so the behavior never changes; (b) two colonies whose pheromone fields get bridged by a fallen branch, after which they behave like a single colony; (c) a colony where every ant is following two local rules and nothing else, and those rules fully explain everything the colony does; (d) a colony being steered in real time by a bored technician with a joystick — whose mind is that, exactly?; (e) a colony kept going for forty years, so that every ant in it has been replaced many times over. Use those where they fit and invent more if a branch needs a case I haven't thought of. As for the leaves, I want named positions rather than vibe words, and I want these among them: colony talk is just useful predictive shorthand with nothing it's like to be a colony; the colony realizes the right kind of organization and therefore really does have a mind; the mind is spread across ants and environment, so 'the colony' is the wrong boundary to draw; there's a genuine collective subject with its own goals, not reducible to ant goals; and there's no fact of the matter, or it's a matter of degree all the way down. I also want at least one leaf where the colony turns out to be a proper subject of moral concern and one where the mind question gets settled but the ethics is said to come apart from it entirely. This is for my writing group, by the way — literary people, not philosophers, though a couple of them read a lot.

Give it to me in this order: Starting point (two or three sentences, the commitment everything hangs off). The branch questions (numbered; for each, the answer options and one line on what choosing it commits you to). The leaves (one short paragraph each — name of the position, the path of answers that lands you there, and its single most awkward commitment). Unoccupied combinations (short: which answer-patterns nobody actually holds, and whether that's because the view is incoherent, unattractive, or just unexplored). Pairs this tree can't separate (flag them honestly instead of papering over). Keep the whole thing under about fifteen hundred words — I'm printing it and taping it above my desk, and my desk is small. I've definately got the cases, I just don't have the logic of it.
