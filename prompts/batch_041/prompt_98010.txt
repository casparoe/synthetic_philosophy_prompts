I am assembling the specification for a theory of how the number of people whose lives are at stake should weigh in decisions about whom to rescue, and I want the desiderata set out before I commit to any of the proposed answers. Treat the standard of adequacy as this: the theory must be good enough to hand to a rescue coordinator or a hospital triage committee and actually guide them. Write the list of requirements any adequate theory in this area has to meet.

For each requirement, give the case or consideration that motivates it, and say whether you take it to be non-negotiable or merely the preference of one camp. I want the list to cover at least the following, and you should tell me which of them are genuine desiderata and which are prejudices of a particular camp dressed up as requirements:

Cases the theory must get right.
(a) You can save one stranger or five strangers, not both.
(b) You can save A, or you can save A and B; in the second option nobody is worse off and one person is better off.
(c) You can save two people now or three people later, and one of the two is among the three.
(d) You can save one person for certain, or attempt a rescue that gives each of three people a one-in-three chance of surviving.
(e) The one is your child, or someone to whom you have made a promise; the many are strangers.
(f) You are yourself one of the people who would be saved or left to die.
(g) You could kill one healthy person and distribute their organs to five who will otherwise die.
(h) A thousand separate incidents, each pitting one against two, against a single incident pitting one against two thousand.

Distinctions it must respect, and must not collapse into one another: numbers as a tie-breaker between equal claims versus numbers as a weight in the balance; counting lives versus counting life-years; killing versus allowing to die; the first-personal standpoint versus the third-personal standpoint; what is permitted versus what is required; the ex ante appraisal of a risky choice versus the ex post appraisal of its outcome.

Commitments it must not contradict: that every person's life is of equal value to every other's; that in (a) the five may, or must, be saved; that the surgeon in (g) must not act; that the verdict must not turn on how the incident is parcelled up or described.

Constraints on the form of the theory itself: it must be action-guiding rather than merely classificatory; it must compose with a decision theory that can handle the uncertainty in (d); it must return a determinate verdict in each case rather than declaring the cases incomparable; and if it rests on a controversial metaphysical thesis, such as the separateness of persons, it must say so rather than smuggling it in.

I am most interested in two things. First, any pair or subset of these requirements that cannot be jointly satisfied — I suspect the demand that the five be saved, the demand that the surgeon not kill, and the demand that no person's claim be aggregated with another's pull against each other, and if so I want to know precisely which has to give. If the list as a whole is unsatisfiable, say so; that is itself the result I want. Second, which single requirement does the most to exclude otherwise popular theories.

Then, briefly, how the main contenders fare against the list — the aggregative view that each counts for one, the view that numbers do not count at all, the view that they count only as a tie-breaker among equal claims, and a contractualist account of what we owe to each other. Keep the weight on the criteria rather than on the scoring, since I can run the scorecard myself once I trust the list.

Do not give me a reading list, citations or suggestions for further reading; reason from the cases themselves. Please write in British English.
