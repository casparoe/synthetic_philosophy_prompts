I run a weekly 50-minute "life studies" seminar for about thirty 16-year-olds at a school with no philosophy department, and the whole gimmick is that we read one biography or one saint's life a week and try to get something out of it for our own lives. The problem is the kids (and honestly me) end up either saying "wow, inspiring" or "well, different times," and nothing lands. I want to steal the actual machinery behind this instead of reinventing it: the way Plutarch wrote paired lives and then a comparison of the pair, the way hagiographers built a saint's life so it could be imitated on a feast day rather than just admired, and the older idea that reading a life is holding a mirror up to yourself rather than watching someone else.

So turn that into a proceedure I can actually run in a session. Give me the questions to ask in order -- about the person, about the particular deeds, about the gap between their situation and ours -- what counts as a good enough answer to each, where the procedure should stop and say this life has nothing to teach us here, and where it should stop and hand the thing over to my own judgement because it's a values question the method can't settle. I don't want a lesson-plan template or a worksheet, I want the reasoning in order with the failure modes named.

Then run it on two cases. First the standard edifying saint's life, the Francis of Assisi kind of story, where the whole point of the genre is that you're supposed to imitate it. Second a morally mixed modern life: a genuinely great scientist or reformer who was, by all accounts, a monster to his own family. I suspect the procedure will tell me the first is less usable than it looks and the second more usable than the kids expect, and if it doesn't, tell me why I'm wrong.

Also be straight with me about what this can't do. If the Plutarch-and-hagiography machinery only works because those authors already knew what a good life looked like and were just illustrating it, say so, because then my seminar is doing something different from what they were doing and I need to know that.
