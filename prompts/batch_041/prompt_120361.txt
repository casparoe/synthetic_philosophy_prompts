I am writing on al-Ghazālī's treatment of causality, and I want to work through a reconstruction that the secondary literature cannot do for me. The exercise is this: suppose al-Ghazālī were confronted with causal questions that arose only long after his death, and tell me what he would have said. I want a reasoned reconstruction and a verdict, not a survey. No citations or reading suggestions, please -- I know the literature and need the argument.

Begin from the familiar exegetical fork. On the stronger reading he is a thoroughgoing occasionalist: no created thing has any efficacy whatever, God alone acts, and fire is related to burning only as a regular accompaniment. On the weaker reading he concedes created things whatever efficacy one likes, but denies that the connection between them is necessary -- the regularity rests on God's free decree and could have been otherwise. This fork is usually adjudicated by close reading of the Tahāfut al-Falāsifa. I want to try the reverse: adjudicate it by pressure-testing each reading against four developments he never saw.

(i) Quantum indeterminacy. Fundamental physics appears to be irreducibly probabilistic. Does indeterminism vindicate the denial of necessity, or does it embarrass a view on which the reliability of our expectations is underwritten by divine habit (al-ʿāda)? A habit that issues in a given outcome thirty percent of the time is not obviously a habit. Consider a single unstable nucleus decaying with no preceding change anywhere in the world: on the stronger reading there is no puzzle, since God simply acts; on the weaker reading, is there anything left for a cause to be?

(ii) Darwinian natural selection. Here is a process that produces the appearance of design without a designer, and that biology treats as an autonomous causal process. Which reading can accommodate a science that proceeds perfectly well without ever mentioning God as a cause, and what does the accommodation cost him?

(iii) Probabilistic causation. Epidemiology and the difference-making tradition treat X as a cause of Y even when X raises the probability of Y only slightly. Does al-Ghazālī have anything like a partial or difference-making cause, or does his account flatten into the claim that whatever happens is equally the act of God?

(iv) The contemporary metaphysics of causation. Compare the Humean regularity view, dispositionalist or powers views, and interventionist counterfactual accounts. The interventionist denial that causal connections are logically necessary looks like al-Ghazālī's own move, yet it analyses causation in terms of what would happen under manipulation, which presupposes that the manipulated variables have real efficacy. Is he interventionism's ally or its target?

Then give me four things. First, a verdict on the fork: which reading the modern material supports, and whether his position survives as a distinctive fourth option or collapses into one of the three just named. Second, what his own commitments require -- in particular whether he needs God's concurrence in each creaturely act or only conservation, and whether the Ashʿarite account of time as composed of discrete instants makes continuous re-creation mandatory or is being asked to do too much work. Third, which of (i) through (iv) is most damaging and what the cheapest repair would be. Fourth, one case I have not raised where you think the reconstruction is genuinely underdetermined by anything he wrote, together with what further evidence would settle it.

If some part of the exercise is ill-posed, say so rather than manufacturing a verdict.
