Our faculty senate's free-expression working group is drafting a policy on protests that disrupt invited speakers, and I've been asked to write the memo on how Mill's harm principle should bear on it. In two successive meetings the same maxim did all the work, in opposite directions — that your right to swing your fist ends where my nose begins. One member cited it to argue the university should cancel a talk that would make some students feel unsafe; the next week another cited it to argue the university may never cancel a talk, since being offended is not a nose. Set out for me the distinct precise claims that sentence could be making — a boundary-of-liberty claim, a claim about causation and directness, a claim about which harms count, a claim about who may do the restricting (the state alone, or also employers, platforms, and crowds), a claim about prior restraint as against after-the-fact punishment — and say for each whether it is a platitude, a substantive and defensible thesis, or simply false, and whether it would actually license either member's conclusion. Add a section on where the fist-and-nose picture breaks down once the thing at the end of the arm is speech rather than a blow, and say which reading people usually mean when they use the line to end an argument, including whether that reading supports or undercuts the objection to a heckler's veto.
