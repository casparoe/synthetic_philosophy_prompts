Six months ago I was elected steward in my department at a large public hospital, on the strength of our first contract campaign, which ended in a nine-day strike I helped run. I have 90 minutes at the next stewards' training day with about twenty new stewards, most of whom have never voted in an authorization vote, and I want them to feel two things that took me a strike to learn and that I still cannot convey in a speech: that the union's power is a public good each member has a private reason to free-ride on, and that a strike works only if it binds the members who voted against it. Please design the session. I want a simulation with roles, a payoff scheme that uses no real money, a timeline, and a vote structured so that the minority is visibly bound by the majority's decision and has to decide, in role, what it owes a colleague who crosses. Build in at least one role whose objection to striking is principled rather than self-interested, so the fairness question cannot be waved away as selfishness. Then a debrief that maps what happened onto the difference between organizing as a right of association and striking as coercion of one's own side, including what to say to members who want the gains without the discipline. Tell me what predictably goes wrong the first time — I expect the free-riding to be underplayed and someone to feel personally accused — and be frank about which part of the idea the simulation cannot reproduce and I will have to state myself.
