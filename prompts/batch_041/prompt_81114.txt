I'm not an academic — I'm a retired sparky who got volunteered to write the history of the old rope works in my town for its centenery next year, and I've fallen down a rabbit hole about whether history can be objective at all. I've come across the old idea that the historian's job is just to show how things actually were and let the documents speak for themselves, and then the sceptics who say any history is a story whose plot the historian supplies, and that the archive itself is a fossil of whoever had the power to leave records behind. I can see something in both, but I can't work out what either of them tells me to do differently on the page, and I've got about eight months of evenings, so what I need is a priority list, not a reading list.

So take the role of a philosopher of history who's thought hard about this, brainstorm a bit before you commit, then tell me which of these worries should actually change what I write, ranked, and which are overrated or don't really follow. Here's my real bind. The firm's own records survived almost intact; the workers' side of things is basically three photographs and a song collected in the 1960s. There was a fire in 1911 that killed four people, and the only account of it is the company's own inquiry. I'm fairly sure the works manager had the gates padlocked and I think I know why — he was scared of the insurance inspector — but I can't prove it, and the same three surviving facts fit a much duller story just as well. Does any of the philosophical stuff actually tell me whether to write "because", whether to name him, whether I can fill the gap with a scene I've imagined, or whether I should just lay out both versions and let the reader sit with the discomfort?

Rank the worries by how much they'd really change my pages, say what would push one up or down the list, and finish with a line starting "Verdict:" giving your top three, in order, as short labels.
