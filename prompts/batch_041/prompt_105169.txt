I've just come out of a long and rather heated argument with a friend about Turing's 1950 paper, and I want to write down where I've landed before the shape of it slips away. My friend thinks Turing's treatment of extrasensory perception is an embarrassing footnote — a lapse into credulity that we should quietly ignore when we teach the imitation game. I think that is too quick, but I am no longer sure my own set of commitments hangs together, and what I want is an audit rather than a defense. Here is roughly where I stand. (1) I think the imitation game is a genuinely good test: if a machine reliably passes a long, rigorous, text-only interrogation against competent interrogators, we should conclude that it can think, or at least that it is intelligent in the only sense we can operationalize. (2) I think Turing was right to take the empirical possibility of telepathy seriously in that paper, and right to suggest that the conditions of the game would need to be shielded from it — his proposal that the interrogator be placed in a room proof against telepathy, however fanciful as engineering, strikes me as a legitimate piece of methodological hygiene. (3) I think telepathy almost certainly does not exist; the statistical evidence Turing gestures at has not survived scrutiny, and I would bet heavily against any parapsychological effect. (4) I think the test should be run over a teleprinter, or some equivalent text-only channel, precisely so that the interrogator cannot use voice, appearance, or other embodiment cues. (5) I am a physicalist: I think all mental states are physical states and all causation is physical causation.

What I want from you is a consistency check. Which of these are actually in tension, which only look like they are, and which one would have to give? I am especially unsure about the interaction among (1), (2), and (5). If the test just is the criterion of thinking, why should I care whether the interrogator might be using telepathy — and if I do care, am I secretly treating the test as evidence for something behind the behavior rather than as a criterion? Conversely, if I am a physicalist, does (2) even make sense, or does it require me to entertain a phenomenon whose usual description is non-physical? And is there a reading of Turing on ESP that makes the whole package coherent, or was he himself pulled in two directions at once? Please rank the tensions by severity, tell me which apparent ones dissolve on inspection, and identify the cheapest single revision that would clean up the most — rather than listing everything as equally bad.
