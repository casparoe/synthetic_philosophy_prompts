I run the risk-assessment and authorisation unit at a national environment and chemicals agency. About fifty proposals a year cross my desk, each one a request either to approve a substance or technology with conditions attached, or to restrict or withdraw something already in use, and each has to be closed out as a signed decision note within ninety days, with a twelve-month review available if we take the conditional route instead of a flat yes or no. My staff are scientists and lawyers rather than philosophers, the notes we write get judicially reviewed, and the reasoning has to survive a judge asking why we treated the uncertainty in this file differently from the uncertainty in the file two desks over. I came into the job a believer in the precautionary principle and I still am, but I have watched our own unit invoke it in opposite directions on nearly identical evidence — as a reason to act at once and as a reason to keep studying — and I have been made to look foolish in cross-examination for it. What I want from you is a procedure, not an essay on the principle: an ordered set of questions my unit can actually run, with entry and exit conditions at each step, a statement of what counts as a sufficient answer to each, the points at which the procedure returns a definite no, and the points at which it must hand the file to a person because the remaining question is one of values rather than evidence. Note that our minister can override us on social or economic grounds; I would rather the procedure said so openly at the right step than have that override smuggled in earlier as though it were a scientific judgment.

The procedure has to be honest about the structure of the principle instead of smoothing it over. It must separate the two quite different jobs the principle is normally asked to do: lowering the standard of proof required before a restriction is justified, by shifting the burden onto whoever wants to introduce or keep the substance; and licensing action on grounds that would fail a conventional cost-benefit test. It must distinguish, and prescribe different treatment for, at least four epistemic situations — probabilities known, probabilities unknown but the outcomes enumerable, outcomes themselves contested, and the case where we suspect we have not yet thought of the relevant outcome at all — and it must say which of these the principle was actually built for and which it was not. It must confront the fact that waiting for better evidence is sometimes the genuinely precautionary move and sometimes precisely the vice the principle exists to prevent: I want an explicit test for when a delay is a legitimate purchase of information and when it is a decision to let the harm keep running, and I want the procedure to name the error it is committing either way. It must also force us to price the risks of our own intervention — the substitute chemical, the lost benefit, the displaced production, the harm done by the restriction itself — without letting that requirement become a general licence to do nothing, since I have now seen both failure modes and need a procedure that can be accused of neither.

Practical constraints: ninety days, no new primary research, a unit of six with one economist available two days a week, and a director who will not sign a note whose conclusion is merely that the science is uncertain. Give me the procedure in a form I can put in front of the unit — steps in order, the question to ask at each, what a sufficient answer looks like, where we stop, where we escalate, and a one-page form the case officer fills in. Then run it end to end on three files: a class of insecticides where the laboratory evidence is suggestive, the field evidence is contested, and the registrants argue that every available alternative is worse; a proposal to release a self-propagating engineered organism, where the central problems are irreversibility and the absence of any way to recall what has been released; and a third case I have chosen because I expect the procedure to tell me something I will not want to hear — a widely used flame retardant whose most likely substitute is probably more dangerous than the thing we would be banning, so that I can see whether the procedure really does return a refusal to act, and what it says we owe the people who remain exposed in the meantime. Finally, tell me where this procedure will misfire: the moves that can be gamed by whoever frames the file first, the inputs that look like measurements but are actually choices, and the questions it will keep handing back to me that no procedure can settle.
