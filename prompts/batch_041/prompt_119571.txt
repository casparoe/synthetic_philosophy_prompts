I am a high school student preparing for a state-level civics and appellate advocacy competition, and our team has been assigned to produce a model adversarial collaboration. Two of our members have argued all season: one is a committed textualist, the other a committed purposivist. They agree on almost nothing about how to read a statute, and they keep talking past each other. I need the document they could both sign. Please act as a professional philosopher of law and an experienced neutral facilitator. Think carefully about the difference between a genuine disagreement and a merely verbal one, and about what each side could accept without abandoning its core commitments.

Before you draft, do a private brainstorming pass. List the plausible readings of each disputed provision in the fictional statute below; the strongest textualist and purposivist arguments for each; and at least two places where the dispute may be about words, burdens of proof, or institutional roles rather than about substance. Then discard the brainstorming and write only the joint statement.

Use only the fictional statute and legislative history below as the authoritative record. Do not include a bibliography, reading list, or citations to real cases, statutes, or scholarly works. Refer to provisions by section number.

**The Community Automated Decision Notice Act (CADNA)**

*Section 1. Short Title.* This Act may be cited as the Community Automated Decision Notice Act.

*Section 2. Findings and Purpose.*
(a) The Legislature finds that automated decision systems are increasingly used to make consequential decisions about individuals, and that individuals often lack meaningful notice of the factors and logic underlying those decisions.
(b) The purpose of this Act is to ensure that individuals receive a plain-language explanation of consequential automated decisions before those decisions are finalized, while avoiding undue compliance burdens on small entities and preserving legitimate trade secrets.

*Section 3. Definitions.*
(a) "Covered automated decision system" means any computational process that uses machine learning to make or materially inform a consequential decision.
(b) "Consequential decision" means a decision concerning employment, credit, housing, insurance, or education that produces legal or similarly significant effects for an individual.
(c) "Plain-language explanation" means a description of the principal factors and logic used to reach a decision, sufficient for a reasonable person to understand the basis for the decision. It need not include trade secrets or confidential business information.
(d) "Small entity" means a business with fewer than 50 employees and less than $5,000,000 in annual gross receipts.
(e) "Operator" means a person or entity that operates or materially controls a covered automated decision system.

*Section 4. Notice and Explanation.*
(a) An operator shall provide a plain-language explanation to an individual before a consequential decision is finalized.
(b) If providing an explanation before finalization is impracticable, the operator shall provide the explanation within 30 days after the decision is finalized.
(c) An operator that is a small entity is exempt from subsection (a), unless the operator has previously been found to have violated this Act.

*Section 5. Enforcement.*
(a) The Attorney General may bring a civil action for violations of this Act.
(b) Nothing in this Act shall be construed to create a private right of action.
(c) In an action under this section, a court may award injunctive relief and civil penalties of up to $10,000 per violation.

*Section 6. Construction.*
(a) This Act shall be construed narrowly to avoid imposing undue burdens on small entities.
(b) If any provision of this Act is held invalid, the remaining provisions shall not be affected.

*Section 7. Effective Date.* This Act takes effect 180 days after enactment.

**Fictional Legislative History**

- Committee Report: "The small-entity exemption is intended to be temporary and narrow. It should not swallow the rule. The Attorney General should focus on egregious cases."
- Floor Manager's Statement: "The bill is not meant to create a new bureaucracy. The heart of the bill is the pre-decision explanation; the 30-day provision is a fallback for genuine impracticability, not a routine option."
- Sponsor's Statement: "A plain-language explanation means an individual should be able to understand why the decision went the way it did, not just that an algorithm was used."
- Dissenting Report: "The term 'machine learning' is vague and may sweep in ordinary statistical tools. The exemption for small entities will create a loophole."

**Your task:** Draft the joint statement of an adversarial collaboration between the textualist and the purposivist. It should contain the following sections, in this order, with the approximate lengths indicated:

1. **Title and Scope** (100–150 words): Name the two camps, state the question they are jointly addressing, and say what materials both accept as the record.

2. **Agreed Claims** (numbered, 10–14 claims; 700–900 words): Claims both sides can sign. Include claims about the Act's structure, the enforcement scheme, the status of the purpose clause, the burden of proving impracticability, the difference between a statute's purpose and the legislature's intentions, the role of legislative history, and the limits of the plain-language explanation requirement. Each claim should be substantive and non-vacuous.

3. **Disagreements Table** (6–8 rows; 500–700 words): A table with columns: Interpretive question; Textualist position in wording a textualist would accept; Purposivist position in wording a purposivist would accept; Why this is not merely a verbal dispute. The rows should cover at least the meaning of "impracticable," the scope of "machine learning," the phrase "previously been found to have violated this Act," the small-entity exemption as applied to corporate affiliates, and the use of the purpose clause and legislative history.

4. **Cruxes** (4–5 numbered cruxes; 150–250 words each): For each, state the sub-question; why settling it would move at least one side; and the case, argument, or piece of evidence both sides agree would bear on it. Make clear which side would be moved and in what direction.

5. **Joint Tests and Predictions** (table with 4–6 rows; 400–600 words): A table with columns: Test case; Textualist prediction; Purposivist prediction; What both agree would count as disconfirming evidence for each side. The test cases should be concrete applications of the fictional statute, not abstract restatements.

6. **Residual Disagreement and Joint Commitments** (200–300 words): State what remains genuinely unresolved, and list the procedural commitments both sides will follow in future arguments—for example, stating their interpretive theory before applying it, distinguishing verbal from substantive disputes, and identifying what would change their mind.

Write in a formal, polished register suitable for a public competition. Do not resolve the disagreement in favor of one side; the deliverable is a text both camps could sign, with the residual disagreement made exact.
