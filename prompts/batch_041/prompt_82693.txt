For some years now I have been caught in a recurring argument about corruption that never moves, and I would like you to diagnose it rather than settle it. The participants are, roughly, development economists and governance practitioners on one side and anthropologists and area specialists on the other.

The first side holds that corruption is the abuse of entrusted public office for private gain; that bribery, patronage, and clientelism are corruption wherever they occur; and that the country rankings and governance indicators they publish measure one thing across very different societies. Within this side there is a further split I find telling: some treat corruption as a principal–agent problem, in which a venal official betrays a well-intentioned principal, while others treat it as a collective-action problem, in which everyone is trapped in an equilibrium no individual can exit alone.

The second side holds that much of what the first side calls corruption is a system of reciprocal obligation — gifts, favours, loyalty to kin and patrons — with its own internal moral logic, and that to call it corruption is already to have applied a market-state, Western standard that the participants do not share. On this view the indicators measure little more than distance from an idealized Western bureaucracy.

Both sides know each other's arguments by heart. The universalists say the contextualists excuse what is plainly theft from the poor; the contextualists say the universalists mistake one institutional form for a universal moral truth. No one has moved in years.

I want a diagnosis of the impasse. Is it a normative disagreement about whether these practices are wrong, a conceptual disagreement about the extension of the word 'corruption', an empirical disagreement about their consequences, or a disagreement about which cases count as data — the ethnographer's thick description against the economist's aggregate index? Are the two sides perhaps answering different questions under a single heading, one asking how to rank states and the other what a practice means to those who live it? My own hypothesis, offered as a hypothesis, is that the dispute is largely conceptual, and that the two camps apply different criteria for applying 'corruption' — one functional, one procedural — so that they could agree on every fact and every value and still contradict each other; but it may instead be a straight clash of moral starting points about whether the claims of kinship can ever outweigh the claims of office.

Given your diagnosis, tell me what would count as progress — a distinction, a reformulation of the question, a piece of evidence — or argue that none should be expected and why.
