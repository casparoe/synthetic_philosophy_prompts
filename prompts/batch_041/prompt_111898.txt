I need a pair of constructed cases from the ethics of moral worth, built to a specification, for use in a graduate seminar. The point of the exercise is to see which requirement on acting for the right reasons is actually doing the work, so the verdicts must fall out of the views rather than being built into the descriptions.

The distinction to isolate is between two readings of acting for the right reasons. On the de re reading, the consideration that in fact moves the agent is in fact the consideration that makes the act right, whatever the agent takes that consideration to be. On the de dicto reading, the agent is moved by the thought that the act is right, or required, or demanded of her. Build two cases, each a single paragraph of no more than about 150 words, set in ordinary contemporary life:

1. A case in which the agent satisfies the de re reading and fails the de dicto reading.
2. A case in which the agent satisfies the de dicto reading and fails the de re reading.

Both cases must meet these constraints. The act must be right — the interesting question is not whether the agent did the wrong thing. The agent's operative reason must be statable in one sentence of the form "I did it because ...", and you must supply that sentence. No case may turn on the agent's being deceived about a non-moral fact, unless you flag the deception explicitly and show that the verdict survives its removal. No trolleys, no runaway trains, no hospital triage, no murderers at the door, and no character whose name already belongs to a well-known example in the literature; the cases must not be recognisable as rehearsals of cases students will already have met.

Then do the following.

(a) For each case, identify the act, the feature that makes it right, and the agent's operative reason. Then give the verdict under each of three views of moral worth: (i) the view on which moral worth requires that the agent be moved by the thought that the act is right or required; (ii) the view on which it requires that the agent be moved by the very feature that makes the act right, and on which being moved by the thought that the act is right neither suffices nor is necessary; (iii) the view on which it requires that the act's rightness not be a matter of luck for the agent — roughly, that she would have acted rightly for good reasons across a range of nearby variations in the situation. State each verdict in a sentence and say what in the case drives it.

(b) Say which case discriminates between which pair of views, and whether any one of the three gives the same verdict in both cases. If one does, say what it must say about each case and whether that is a cost to it. If none of the two cases discriminates between the third view and the other two, describe what a case that would discriminate among them would have to look like.

(c) For each case, name the single feature doing the work, say what happens to the verdict if that feature is removed, and say whether the case then collapses into something uncontroversial.

(d) Say how close the two cases are to a minimal pair. Where they differ in more than one respect, say why that is unavoidable given the distinction being isolated.

(e) If neither case separates moral worth from praiseworthiness, add a third short case that does: an agent who is fully praiseworthy and whose act nevertheless lacks moral worth.

(f) Finally, take whichever of the three views fares worst across your cases and give the strongest reply its defender could make — in particular, the reply that your cases stipulate their verdicts rather than generating them from the view — and say how much of the case survives that reply.
