I'm in the fourth year of a philosophy PhD, my dissertation is on truth, and I keep getting told that deflationism is basically a settled area — that all the interesting moves were made decades ago and what's left is bookkeeping. I dont believe it, but I also can't tell which of the loose threads are actually worth pulling, and my advisor is no help (he thinks truth is either boring or trivial, depending on the day). I've got about a year of funded writing time, a decent background in formal semantics and proof theory, and not much patience for the metaphysics of properties. So: what are the real open questions in deflationary theories of truth right now, and if you had to pick two or three for me to spend that year on, which ones, and why? I want the ordering defended — what makes a question worth a year of someone like me — not a survey of the literature, and I'd rather hear why one thing is upstream of another than get a list of topics.

Some things I've been circling, in case they help you see what I mean. Whether a deflationist can say anything about why truth is worth caring about without quietly reinstating a substantive property — I keep worrying the answer is no and that everyone knows it. Whether deflationism about truth generalizes to reference and content, or whether the generalization is exactly where the view stops being deflationism. Whether the whole thing should be recast with true as an operator rather than a predicate, since a friend swears the operator-versus-predicate choice is the fork everyone pretends not to see and that the usual fights (opaque contexts, generalizations, the value of truth) look different from the other side of it. Whether deflationism is even allowed to have a favored response to the paradoxes, or whether that's a separate issue people wrongly use as a litmus test. And a hunch of my own I'd like stress-tested: that the real test isn't meaning or metaphysics but the theory of validity — that a deflationist can coast on truth-as-a-device-of-generalization for semantics and then has a much harder time explaining why truth-preservation is what makes an argument good, and that this is upstream of more than people admit. Tell me where I'm wrong about that, which of these is actually tractable in a year with my toolkit, which are neglected versus just hard, and what evidence over the next year or two would move any of them up or down the list. If you think the framing is off and the important work is somewhere else entirely, say so and defend that instead.
