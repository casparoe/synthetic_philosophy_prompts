I have recently had two experiences that pushed me toward a subject I had previously encountered only as an undergraduate survey, and I would be grateful for your comments on a short essay I have drafted about it. The first is that two close friends asked me to officiate their wedding this spring; I was certified online for the purpose, and the entire business — the rehearsal, the license, the moment in the ceremony when I was supposed to say the words that make the thing official — left me thinking about Austin far more than I had expected. The second is that I have spent the past year as a volunteer moderator of a fairly large online forum, where I have discovered that locking a thread is a surprisingly fraught act. I am not a philosopher by training, but I have been asked to present something to a small reading group of former classmates, and I would like the draft to be accurate before I stand up in front of them.

The draft is titled "Saying Is Doing," and its argument runs as follows. Austin's central insight is that some utterances do not describe an action but are the action, and he called these performatives. Unlike constatives, they are neither true nor false; they are happy or unhappy. I then distinguish two ways an utterance can be unhappy. The first is a misfire, which occurs when the speaker is insincere — someone promises to help and has no intention of helping, or says "I congratulate you" while privately wishing you had lost. The second is an abuse, which occurs when the surrounding machinery is missing: a bystander with no standing whatsoever declares a couple married, or a ship is "christened" by someone who was never given the authority to do it. Because the second failure is a failure of the world rather than of the speaker's heart, I argue that it is the more interesting of the two, and the second half of the essay is devoted to it. My central example is the rehearsal: the couple and I ran the ceremony through twice, and if performatives are acts, it is not obvious to me why we did not thereby marry them, even if only in some thin, etiolated way. That nobody treats a rehearsal as producing a marriage suggests to me that performative success rests less on the words than on a shared willingness to treat them as counting. I close with the forum, where I argue that what makes "This thread is locked" a performative is its illocutionary effect — the fact that the posting actually stops — and I add that Austin's later willingness to set aside the performative/constative distinction in favor of his three-part locutionary/illocutionary/perlocutionary scheme was, in my view, a step backward.

I would like your comments, please, and I would much rather be corrected than encouraged. Above all I want to know where I have gotten the machinery wrong: if I have misdescribed, swapped, or misattributed a distinction, say so plainly and tell me what the correct version is. Beyond accuracy, I would like to know whether my central claim — that the interesting kind of failure is the one for which the world, rather than the speaker, is at fault — is a claim worth making, or whether it collapses into something obvious once stated carefully. Finally, please suggest two or three concrete revisions that would sharpen the essay, and write your response in American English.
