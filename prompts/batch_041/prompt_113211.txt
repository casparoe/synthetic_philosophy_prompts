I am a software engineer with no formal philosophy background, and I have been trying to work out what the Frankfurt cases really imply for responsibility in engineering practice. My rough understanding is that in these cases an agent acts on their own reasons, but a counterfactual intervener would have forced the same action had the agent wavered; because the intervener never acts, the agent could not have done otherwise, yet we are invited to hold them responsible. If that is right, the principle of alternative possibilities cannot be the whole story about moral responsibility. I work at a company that has adopted blameless postmortems, but I have noticed that the reviews still tend to turn on counterfactual questions: could the engineer have caught this, could they have chosen the safer path, would a different person have acted differently. I want the most important implications of the Frankfurt cases for how we run those reviews.

I am particularly interested in the implications that are easy to miss: what follows for the difference between causal contribution and blameworthiness; what follows for treating the existence of a safer alternative as the test of responsibility; what follows when a system design makes an error practically inevitable but the engineer still exercised their own judgment; and what follows for the distinction between being caused to act and acting for reasons. Please rank the implications by how much they should change actual postmortem practice, explain each in plain language, and note where the Frankfurt lesson is being stretched beyond what it can bear. No citations or reading list are needed; I want the implications themselves.
