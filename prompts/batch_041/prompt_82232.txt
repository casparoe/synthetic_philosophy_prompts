I sit on the curriculum and assessment committee of a large mixed comprehensive, and I have been handed a decision I did not expect to find genuinely hard. For three years the school has run what we call the spot test: at the start of each half-term, every Year 11 class is told that there will be one test during that half-term, that nobody will be able to predict the day, and that the point is to keep revision continuous rather than crammed into the fortnight before the mocks. Last half-term, a top-set maths class produced a proof — two sides of A4, and not stupid — that the announcement cannot be true. They reason backwards: the test cannot fall on the last day, because by the evening before it would be the only remaining day and so would be foreseen; and once the last day is ruled out, the same argument rules out the second-to-last, and so on until no day is left. The announcement, they conclude, is either false or empty, and the school has been issuing an empty threat. The proof was printed in the school magazine, so by the time the test came round it was common knowledge among the students — everyone knew, and knew that everyone knew — that the test could not come at all; it was set on the Wednesday of the final week and surprised almost everyone, which the students say shows only that they did not believe their own argument. The students' council now wants the announcement withdrawn as dishonest, the head of maths wants to keep it as the only thing that gets the bottom set working in week one, and the governors want a written recommendation from us before term ends.

The wrinkle is a colleague's proposal to replace the announcement with a sealed-envelope draw: on the first morning of half-term the student council chair draws a date in front of the class, the slip is sealed and held by the head of year, and the class teacher learns the date only on the day. Nothing about that draw is foreseeable in advance, so a promise of an unpredictable test would be straightforwardly true and nobody would have to be told anything false. I am drawn to it and suspicious of it in roughly equal measure, and I keep circling a worry I cannot quite formulate: that the point of the spot test is the students' knowledge that it might come on any day, not the metaphysical fact of unpredictability, so the envelope may solve a problem we do not have while quietly dropping the one we do. (I have been trying to taboo the word surprise and say what we actually want, which I think is that the test should not be reliably foreseeable, but that formulation has its own difficulties.)

I would like a memo I can circulate before the committee meets: a clear recommendation — keep the announcement as it stands, withdraw it, adopt the envelope, or something I have not thought of — with the reasoning, the date by which we should adopt it, and, if we keep some form of announcement, the wording I should put to the governors. Please say plainly what the students' proof shows and where, if anywhere, it goes wrong; I have read enough blog-era discussion of this puzzle to know that the brisk replies are less decisive than they look, and I would rather be told what I am committing the school to than be reassured that it is only a curiosity. I should add that two of my colleagues have already staked out positions I will have to answer — one holds that any announcement of an unannounced test is self-defeating by definition, the other that the students' argument is a schoolboy howler — so the memo needs to give each of them something they can actually respond to, not just a verdict.
