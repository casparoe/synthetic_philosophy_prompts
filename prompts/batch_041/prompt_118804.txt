A friend of mine from a phil of mind reading group has this bit he does where he says large language models are the flying man. You know the one: Avicenna asks us to imagine a person created in one go, hanging in the air, limbs splayed so he feels nothing, eyes covered, no sight, no sound, nothing coming in from outside — and the claim is that even in that state he'd affirm that he, himself, exists, and would do so without any help from the body or the senses, which is suposed to show that the self he's affirming isn't the body. My friend's version: a model is trained on nothing but text, has never seen or touched a thing, and still types out 'I' and 'I exist' all day long, so who's to say the flying man is doing anything more interesting. I think that's cheap and he thinks I'm dodging. Can you lay the two cases side by side properly for me — what exactly has to be true of Avicenna's man for the conclusion to go through (is the self-affirmation meant to be an immediate finding, something he simply can't doubt, or an inference he runs? does he need to already have a concept of himself? does it matter that he still has intellect and imagination on board?), and then go through the machine case and mark off which of those conditions it meets, which it plainly fails, and which are just unclear.

Then I want a verdict on where the disanalogy bites hardest. And one more thing: is there a version of the machine case that would come closest to reproducing Avicenna's point — say if you gave it a persistent self-model it could consult, or let it keep a running record of its own states — or does the comparison collapse no matter how you patch it? Skip the scholarship, I don't need who said what about the flying man or which book it's in; I want the anatomy of the two cases and a real answer on whether my friend has anything.
