I am a high school student, and this year I chair our Student Handbook Interpretation Committee: five students and one teacher, ten minutes per contested item, meeting weekly through the fall activities season, none of us with any training in philosophy or law. Our job is to rule on questions of the form "does X count as a Y under the handbook?" — whether a smartwatch is a phone for the no-devices rule, whether a skateboard is a vehicle for the hallway rule, whether a club's off-campus meetup is a school event for insurance purposes — and our rulings decide whether a club gets its funding and whether a student gets written up, so the stakes are real even though the questions sound silly. The teacher can overturn us and the assistant principal can overturn her, which I mention because I need to know which of our rulings are arguable and which are simply wrong.

In my world history class we read a translation of the White Horse Dialogue, a Warring States text attributed to Gongsun Long, in which one speaker defends the claim that a white horse is not a horse: "'Horse' is what designates shape. 'White' is what designates color," he says, and when you ask for a horse a yellow or a black one will do, while when you ask for a white horse it will not. Our teacher told us the argument turns on a word that can mean either "is not the same as" or "is not a member of," and that the two speakers may simply be using different senses of it. That is precisely the fight we have every week: half the committee hears "a skateboard is not a vehicle" as the claim that skateboards are not vehicles, and half hears it as the claim that the word "skateboard" and the word "vehicle" are not interchangeable.

Please take the role of a philosopher of language who knows classical Chinese disputation and the logic of definitions and category terms, and think carefully before you write anything. Begin by brainstorming, in a scratchpad I do not need to see, which distinctions from the dialogue are load-bearing for a committee like ours and which are scholarly detail we can safely drop, then compress the load-bearing ones into a procedure. Give me the result as a numbered list of exactly five items, each a sentence or two, with no introduction and no conclusion: (1) the one distinction from the dialogue that does the real work, stated so that a student who has never heard of the text can apply it; (2) the questions we ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, and where the procedure stops with a no; (3) the point at which the procedure hands the decision to a person because the question has become one about what the handbook ought to say rather than what its words mean, together with which steps are judgment calls dressed up as procedure; (4) two worked cases, one of which comes out in a way I will not like; and (5) what the procedure cannot do, including the case most likely to make it misfire. It has to be runnable in ten minutes by people with no philosophy background, and it should not depend on any source I would have to go look up.
