ok so my reading group is wrapping up a term on non-classical logic and for the last session, instead of a handout, I want to give them a poem. Topic is dialetheism -- the view that some contradictions are actually true, not just that people contradict themselves sometimes -- and the form I want is a palinode, i.e. one of those poems where the speaker takes back what they said in an earlier poem. Only this one retracts the retraction as well, and I dont want that landing as a joke or a cheap paradox. Both halves have to stand.

So: a palinode, 14 to 20 lines, meter whatever you like, but the first and last lines have to be identical so the thing loops back on itself, and the two halves should mirror each other line for line, the second half being the first half turned around, negated where the first asserted. The hinge is where it all lives: where a classical reader is waiting for a BUT that would let one half win, you put an AND. That AND is the whole point -- it's the difference between a reductio and a true contradiction, and I want the same poem to be readable as both depending on whos holding it. A classical logician should be able to take the back half as the reductio that kills the front half. A dialetheist should be able to take them as standing together, front half undefeated. Dont tip your hand either way.

Second thing, and this is the part I actually care about: once the contradiction has landed, the poem has to keep going for a few lines with perfectly ordinary, uncontroversial true statements, the kind of thing no one is argueing about, and those have to survive intact. Thats the paraconsistent bit. In a logic where explosion holds, one contradiction makes every sentence true and the poem turns to mush; I want the poem built so that doesn't happen and the reader can feel that it doesn't. So no drifting off into everything-is-both-true-and-false territory -- thats the trivialist, not the dialetheist, and it wrecks the poem.

No philosophers names, no epigraph, no footnotes, and no line that just announces its own falsity. The self-reference should sit in the structure -- the looping first and last line, the mirrored halves, the retraction that retracts itself -- not in a sentence that comments on itself. Plain title. Its for grad students who've read the Liar into the ground, so dense is fine, just dont make it a lecture in verse.
