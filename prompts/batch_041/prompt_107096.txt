I am a retired experimental physicist. Late in life I have begun reading moral philosophy on my own, and I have run into a puzzle I cannot resolve by myself. I would like you to explain a conceptual issue to me in some depth, and I would prefer an answer that works through the structure of the problem rather than merely summarizing the published literature.

The issue arises from John Taurek's 1977 article Should the Numbers Count? in Philosophy and Public Affairs. I understand the broad shape of his argument: that when one must choose between saving one person and saving several, each person has a claim of equal weight; that the mere fact of there being more people on one side does not by itself give that side a stronger claim; that Taurek is willing to save the larger group but denies he is required to do so by the numbers; and that he suggests a random procedure, such as a coin flip, as a way of giving each person's claim its due. What I cannot get clear on is what exactly the coin flip is supposed to be doing, and whether it is consistent with the rest of his position.

Here is the specific difficulty. A coin flip between saving the one and saving the five gives the single person a one-half chance of being saved and gives each member of the five a one-tenth chance. If the underlying moral idea is that each person's claim has equal weight, then the procedure seems to weight the one person's claim five times as heavily as any of the others. A lottery over the six individuals, by contrast, would give each person a one-sixth chance. So there appear to be at least two different things one might mean by treating claims equally: equalizing the chances of each individual, or equalizing the chances of each side in the choice. I would like you to explain how these two readings relate, whether one of them is the one Taurek actually needs, and whether the distinction is a real one or merely verbal. I would also like to know whether Taurek's position is better classified as a deontological constraint on aggregation, as a form of moral particularism about individual claims, or as something else entirely.

I would also like you to consider a variant I have constructed, because it is the case that made me realize I did not understand the position. Suppose the same rescuer faces the same kind of choice repeatedly, with different people each time, and suppose the rescuer consistently uses a coin flip between the smaller and larger group. Over many such choices, the expected number of people saved will be lower than if the rescuer always saved the larger group. Does Taurek's position require accepting that consequence, or does it have the resources to resist it? And if it does require accepting it, is that a reductio of the position or a bullet that a defender can bite?

Finally, I would like you to distinguish carefully between what is settled among people who have thought hard about this and what is genuinely contested. I do not want a confident verdict presented as though it were the consensus of the field. If there are several live positions, please say so, and say which parts of the discussion are relatively uncontroversial and which are still disputed.
