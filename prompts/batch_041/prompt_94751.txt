I'm putting together a handout for a graduate reading group on decision theory, and after an hour of arguing about whether paying in counterfactual mugging is a mark of sophistication or a confusion, we decided we need something more useful than a list of -isms. Please act as a decision theorist and construct a decision tree that takes someone from their most basic commitments about what makes an act rational to a position on acausal problems -- problems where an agent's choice is correlated with, but does not causally influence, a payoff-relevant fact. The tree should sort people by what they say about cases, not by which label they already like, so every branch node must be a question answerable without knowing where it leads. Build it from the logical space rather than from a roster of names: first the questions and the combinations of answers they permit, then the actual positions located in that space, with any unoccupied combinations noted and briefly diagnosed -- is nobody there because the view is incoherent, because it is unattractive, or merely because nobody has bothered to defend it?

The cases the tree has to handle are: Newcomb's problem; counterfactual mugging, both in the one-shot form where you are told which way the coin landed and in the form where you can set a policy in advance; and the family of blackmail cases where a threat is armed if and only if (you comply) XOR (some independent fact obtains), so that your compliance is evidentially but not causally connected to the threat -- the blackmailer's letter tells you the arming condition and claims a near-perfect prediction of your response, and you have to decide whether to send the money. Please make sure the leaves include at least: a purely causal view; a purely evidential view; a view on which you evaluate decision procedures rather than acts; a view that evaluates policies across all branches of the counterfactual; a view on which pre-commitment is the only legitimate solution; and a view on which the problem is ill-posed. Use the standard names where you know them and descriptive labels where you don't. Flag any pair of leaves your tree cannot separate, and say why the separation fails. If your tree forces a choice that the literature usually leaves open -- say, whether one-shot counterfactual mugging is a genuine problem or only a problem for agents who cannot pre-commit -- say so explicitly rather than papering over it.

Before you draw the tree, brainstorm for a few sentences about which distinctions actually do the work here, and discard any distinction that turns out to be verbal. Then give the tree, then the scoring key that maps answer patterns onto leaves. Throughout, state how confident you are in each substantive claim you make, and single out the one point in the whole construction you are least sure of -- I would rather have a flagged uncertainty than a confident blur.
