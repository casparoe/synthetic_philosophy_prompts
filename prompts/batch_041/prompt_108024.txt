I chair the research-subjects committee at a neuroscience institute. Our policy runs on two tracks: animal protocols, which require analgesia, humane endpoints, and a harm–benefit review, and human-subjects protocols, which require consent and withdrawal rights. Cultured tissue has always fallen outside both. We register it as biological material and move on.

Next week we review a protocol from a lab that cultures human stem-cell-derived neural tissue on a multi-electrode array and closes a feedback loop between the tissue and a simulated body, so that the culture learns to avoid a stimulus. The lab's position is that this is tissue in a dish rather than an animal, so neither track applies and no welfare conditions attach. One member objects that the only thing the animal track protects is the capacity to suffer, and that if mental states are multiply realizable then substrate cannot be what settles the question — so we either extend the track or concede we never meant it. Another replies that multiple realizability is a claim about mental-state types, not about which systems have them, and brings us no closer to whether this culture feels anything. A third warns that any criterion loose enough to catch the culture will also catch the calibration software driving the simulated body, and possibly the lab's own controller.

Please draft the decision memo I will circulate before Thursday's meeting. It should give a clear recommendation with conditions rather than a survey of views; state a criterion the committee can apply to the next case and not merely this one; specify concrete protocol requirements — what, if anything, stands in for analgesia and humane endpoints here — along with a review date; give an honest account of the strongest argument on the side we reject; and rule on the calibration software and the simulation itself, saying whether our criterion reaches them and, if not, on what principled basis we exclude them.
