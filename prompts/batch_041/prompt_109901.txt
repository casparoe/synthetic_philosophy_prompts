I am assembling a three-year research program on supererogation — acts that are morally good but not required — and I need to defend its agenda to a funder who will not accept "it is a classic puzzle" as a justification. I want a ranked agenda of the open questions in the area, with the reasoning made explicit, rather than a survey of what has been written.

The program has three people. One is a deontic logician, one works on virtue ethics and the aretaic vocabulary, and one works on moral demandingness and the limits of obligation. Assume no empirical budget and no collaborators outside philosophy; the deliverable over three years is conceptual, so tractability for people with these tools should weigh heavily.

What I need from you:

1. A ranked list of the open questions that actually matter, ordered by how much other work depends on them. For each, say what it is upstream of, how tractable it is for this group, and how neglected it is relative to its importance.

2. An honest judgment about which questions in the area are overrated — the ones that generate papers but not progress — and which are underrated.

3. The dependency structure itself: which questions are prior to which, and which apparent disagreements are really downstream of one unresolved choice.

I have three suspicions that I want you to test rather than assume, and to use as constraints on the ranking:

First, I suspect the field's most famous puzzle — that an act can be optional and yet morally better than some act that is obligatory — is largely an artifact of treating obligatory and optional as an exhaustive binary deontic partition. If that is right, the paradox dissolves and the real work lies elsewhere: in the boundary question of what obligation covers and why. If it is wrong, I want to know what the paradox is actually tracking that the boundary question misses. Tell me which way you come down and why.

Second, I suspect the concept is quietly relational. The same rescue can be supererogatory for a bystander and obligatory for a lifeguard, and supererogatory for a stranger and obligatory for a parent. If so, supererogatory may be a predicate of acts-under-descriptions-relative-to-agents rather than a property of acts, which would change what a theory of it is even a theory of. I want to know whether this relational reading collapses the category into a theory of role and relationship duties, or whether it leaves something distinctive for supererogation to name.

Third, I suspect the category may not survive a scalar or threshold conception of obligation at all — that it is parasitic on a binary deontic framework and quietly disappears once obligation comes in degrees or once there is a threshold above which everything is optional. Consider whether that is a reason to keep the binary framework, to abandon the category, or to reconstruct it.

Beyond those, work in at least these candidate items and tell me where they fall: whether omission can be supererogatory or whether the category is essentially act-involving; whether heroism and saintliness are one category or two; whether praiseworthiness is necessary, sufficient, or neither; whether the supererogatory and the suberogatory are genuinely symmetric or whether the symmetry is an artifact of the formalism; and whether anything turns on the recipient's desert or on the agent's risk of failure.

Finally, tell me what would move items up or down this ranking over the next two years — what a decisive result on any one of them would look like — and, if the funder would only support a single line of work, which one you would pick and what you would cut. I care more about the defense of the ordering than about the length of the list: state the criteria you are using, where they trade off against each other, and where your ranking is hostage to a judgment I might reasonably reject.
