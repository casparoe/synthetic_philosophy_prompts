Okay, I need help with something that's been driving my reading group nuts. We're five people, mostly grad students plus one philosopher of mind, and we've spent about four months on the wrong kind of reasons problem and nobody has moved an inch. I don't want another proposed solution -- I want a diagnosis of why the arguement never terminates.

Here's the shape of it as we keep fighting it out. Start with the fitting-attitude picture: something is good, or admirable, or lovable, just in case it's fitting -- appropriate, correct, called for -- to have the relevant pro-attitude toward it: to value it, admire it, love it. The picture is attractive because it demystifies value and explains why lovable and fitting-to-love seem to say the same thing. The objection: a demon threatens to torture you unless you admire him. That definately gives you a reason to admire him, but he isn't admirable, so the analysis seems to say something false. Then the moves we keep rehearsing:

- The threat isn't a reason for the attitude, only a reason to want to have it, or to bring it about that you have it; and the analysis only cares about reasons for the attitude, the object-given ones, not reasons that bear on the state of having it (state-given).
- Or the threat isn't a reason for the attitude at all, because what makes something a reason for an attitude is that it bears on the question the attitude is an answer to, and the threat says nothing about whether he's admirable.
- Or, if you're a buck-passer, value was never a reason in the first place -- only the natural features that make something valuable are reasons -- so the example misses your view.
- Or the right-kind reasons are exactly the ones that bear on whether the attitude gets its object right, represents it accurately, and that's the whole distinction.

Why I think we're stuck: the demon case is too easy. Everyone already agrees the threat is a wrong-kind reason, so it can't discriminate between the views; it only shows that some distinction is needed, which nobody denies. So we went looking for cases that actually decide something, and they all turn into swamps. (1) Self-fulfilling ones. If believing you'll pass the exam makes you study and so makes passing likelier, is that a reason to believe you'll pass, and is it right-kind or wrong-kind? The consideration is about the state of believing and about the object at the same time -- the belief making itself true -- so neither label fits cleanly. Same with trusting a partner who becomes more trustworthy because you trust them. (2) The regress. If the fix is that the threat is a reason to want to admire, the problem just climbs a level: now we need the kind of reason we have for the higher-order want. (3) The criterion. The obvious test -- right-kind reasons are the ones bearing on whether the attitude is fitting -- seems to reuse the very notion of fittingness the analysis was supposed to explain. We also notice the same structure shows up for belief (money for believing something) and for emotions, and we can't agree whether that's one phenomenon or several.

So: not a verdict on which solution wins, but a diagnosis of the disagreement itself. Is this a substantive fight about the nature of reasons, or a verbal one about two readings of a reason for an attitude -- a reason for it versus a reason to have it -- that the sides keep sliding between? Or are the camps answering different questions under one heading, one doing the analysis of value and the other the metaphysics of reasons, so their arguments only bite if you already grant the other's framing? Is there a hidden formal or empirical question doing the work, or is the question ill-posed?

My own hypothesis, and I could be wrong: it's stalled because both sides treat the attitude's being fitting as the thing to be explained, but nobody can say what makes an attitude fitting except by pointing at the reasons, and nobody can sort reasons into right and wrong kind except by pointing at fittingness. So the distinction carries weight it can't cash out, and the WKR problem is really the old circularity worry about fitting-attitude accounts seen from another angle. If that's right, say so; if not, say where it goes wrong.

And tell me what would actually count as progress: a new distinction, a formal result, a case that genuinely discriminates, a change of question -- or nothing, with an argument for why nothing should be expected. American English, please.
