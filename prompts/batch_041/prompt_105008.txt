I am preparing materials for a graduate seminar on intention, foresight, and the morality of killing in war, and I want to use the atomic bombings of Hiroshima and Nagasaki as a stress test rather than rehearse the familiar debate. I would like the seminar to rank the standard defences of the bombings by how well each withstands the critique G. E. M. Anscombe pressed against Truman: that he chose to kill the innocent as a means to his end, and that no accumulation of good consequences can license doing so.

Consider the following five defences, each stated in what I take to be its strongest form:

1. The consequentialist defence: the bombings shortened the war and saved more lives, Japanese as well as Allied, than any available alternative.
2. The double-effect defence: the destruction of a military-industrial target was intended, the civilian deaths were foreseen but not intended, and the intended good outweighed the foreseen evil.
3. The supreme-emergency defence: in a crisis of this magnitude the ordinary prohibitions are suspended or overridden, and the leader who orders the attack does wrong but is justified, or at least excused, by the stakes.
4. The reciprocity or total-war defence: the enemy had already embraced the targeting of civilians, so the moral baseline had shifted and the bombings were no worse than what was already being done on all sides.
5. The legitimate-target defence: the two cities were genuine military targets — ports, industrial plant, military headquarters — and the civilian deaths were incidental collateral damage.

For each defence, assign a score between 0 and 1 for how well it survives an Anscombian critique, where 0 means it collapses completely and 1 means it fully answers her. Then rank the five from most to least defensible. For each, in a few sentences, state precisely where her critique bites if it does; say whether the defence depends on the intention/foresight distinction, and if so whether it needs that distinction to do moral work or only descriptive work; and say whether the defence would survive if one granted her absolutism about never doing evil so that good may come of it.

Finally, supply a second ranking: which of the five Anscombe would have judged the most dangerous, in the sense of the most likely to be accepted by reflective people, and explain why that ranking need not coincide with the first. I am less interested in a verdict on Truman than in seeing where each defence actually gains its force.
