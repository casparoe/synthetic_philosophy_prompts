The Hart–Devlin debate on legal moralism, paired with Hawthorne's The Scarlet Letter.

I'm putting together an essay on Hawthorne's The Scarlet Letter for a seminar on the Hart-Devlin debate, and I keep going back and forth, so I want a proper reading rather than my own muddle. Here's the setup I'm working with. Devlin's line was that a society is held together by a shared morality the way a body is held together by its members, so a breach in one part of the moral code weakens the whole thing -- his so-called disintegration thesis -- and that this is why the community may use the criminal law to enforce morality even when the conduct harms nobody else, since the ordinary person's feelings of intolerance and disgust are themselves a legitimate ground for legislation. Hart's reply, roughly, is that the disintegration thesis is an empirical claim nobody has ever backed up, that the law should not enforce morality as such, and that Devlin smuggles in the majority's distaste as if it were evidence of harm. This was all fought out over the Wolfenden Report and the question of decriminalizing homosexuality and prostitution, so the paradigm case is consensual conduct between adults that offends the community's moral code.

Now Hawthorne. A Puritan community publicly punishes Hester for adultery -- the scaffold, the letter, the exclusion -- and the whole point is to reaffirm the moral order. But the thing that actually rots the community from the inside is Dimmesdale's concealed guilt, and Chillingworth's private revenge, neither of which the law touches. So my hunch is that the book is a kind of test case: does it vindicate Devlin (enforcement restores the shared morality) or does it quietly show the enforcement doing nothing for social cohesion while the real corrosion happens off the books?

What I want from you: read the novel through the debate, seriously in both directions. Map the characters onto the positions -- is the Puritan magistracy Devlin's reasonable man, is Dimmesdale the case Hart would point to, is Hester's punishment the enforcement Devlin defends -- and then tell me honestly where the book resists the reading. I know one obvious objection is that adultery isn't the paradigm case, since there's a spouse who's arguably harmed, so you'll have to say whether that wrecks the analogy or not. And I'd like you to end by saying what the novel adds that the argument alone doesn't -- whether it dramatizes something about legal moralism that neither Hart nor Devlin saw, maybe about concealment, or about who actually pays.

Don't reduce the book to an illustration and don't bend the debate to fit it. One more thing: tell me what would change your mind. If the novel turns out to cut the other way, or if the adultery-vs-homosexuality objection is fatal, I want to know what argument or consideration would flip your verdict, and where you think your reading is most likely to be wrong.
