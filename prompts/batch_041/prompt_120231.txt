Discussions of whether the quantum wavefunction is real are usually framed as a contest between two readings: the ontic reading, on which the wavefunction is a physical entity or field existing independently of anyone's beliefs, and the epistemic reading, on which it encodes information, knowledge, or credence about some deeper state of affairs. Consider a third position. Call it the nomological reading: the wavefunction is not an entity in the world at all, but a law. On this view it belongs to the theory's dynamical structure — like a force law or a Hamiltonian — and its job is to describe how some other, more ordinary ontology (particles, fields, pointers) behaves. It is real only in whatever sense laws of nature are real, which a proponent will insist is a robust sense.

The attractions are easy to see. It disposes of the puzzle of locating a function defined over a 3N-dimensional configuration space in the three-dimensional world we inhabit. It explains why we never measure the wavefunction directly, but always particles, fields, or pointer readings. And it softens the oddity of quantum probability, since a law that assigns chancy behaviour to a system is unremarkable in a way that a physical object with irreducibly probabilistic properties is not.

The objections are equally easy to see. Laws are supposed to be general and not to be tampered with; the wavefunction of a given system is a particular, contingent, time-dependent object that we prepare in a laboratory and adjust with knobs. Laws are not prepared. And if the wavefunction is a law, what is it a law of? In a theory with particles as its ontology this has an answer, but in other theories it is far from clear, and the view threatens to slide into the claim that the wavefunction is merely a calculating device for predicting outcomes — instrumentalism under a more dignified label. Conversely, if one insists that laws are themselves genuine features of the world, it is hard to see how the nomological reading differs from the ontic reading at all; the dispute may reduce to a preference between two words for the same real structure. There is also a no-go theorem, much cited in this area, showing that under assumptions including preparation independence, two distinct pure quantum states cannot correspond to overlapping distributions over underlying physical states. Does that theorem tell against the nomological reading, or does it simply presuppose the point at issue, namely that the wavefunction is a state of a system rather than a law governing one?

Reach a verdict on whether the nomological reading is a stable third position: either it is genuinely distinct from both ontic realism and instrumentalism, or it collapses into one of them (say which, and why), or the question of whether the wavefunction is real turns out to be verbal once the three options are laid out this way.
