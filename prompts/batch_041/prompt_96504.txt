I am defending, at a joint anthropology and history seminar next month, a narrow rehabilitation of national-character talk. My thesis is that a claim such as 'the Danes are unusually trusting' should be read not as a claim about an essence but as three separable empirical claims: that a measurable trait has a distribution shifted relative to an explicitly named comparison set; that the shift survives controls for composition and is stable across cohorts; and that the shift is sustained by institutions, in the sense that the population occupies a self-reinforcing equilibrium of expectations rather than expressing a deep culture. On that reading, I argue, such claims are no more suspect than claims about regional subcultures, and the reading carries a testable implication: if character is institutional, the descendants of migrants should converge on the host equilibrium within a few generations, whereas a deep-culture reading predicts persistence.

The audience will be several anthropologists who treat 'culture' as unbounded and contested, two historians of nationalism who date the nation to the modern period, a survey methodologist who works on ecological inference, and one social psychologist who studies the accuracy of national stereotypes. Prepare my Q&A sheet: the objections I will actually get from this audience, ordered by how much damage they do, each phrased the way that person would put it, with a two-minute reply and a note on where the reply is soft; what I should concede up front and what I should hold; and the one question I am hoping nobody asks.
