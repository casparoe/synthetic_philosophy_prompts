I'm a TA for a graduate seminar on scalable oversight and AI safety, and I've taken on the third problem set, the one on debate-based oversight; my co-TA is handling the first two problems. The whole unit is built on what the lectures call the referee model, and I'll restate it here exactly as the students received it, so that you are grading against the same model they were. A claim has the form "there is an x in S such that Φ(x) holds." The set S is far too large for anyone to enumerate, but for any single named x the predicate Φ(x) can be settled in one elementary check. Two players argue before a judge: a Prover, who asserts the claim, and a Skeptic, who denies it. The judge sees only the transcript, may spend a fixed number b of elementary checks per turn, and rules for whichever side is left standing when a player can no longer continue. The unit's central result was what the lectures call the amplification claim: a judge with a small b can return correct verdicts on claims she could never settle by searching on her own, because the debaters perform the search and the judge needs only to inspect the step that carries the argument. Problem 3 asked the students to (a) explain why the model might deliver this, (b) state the assumption that explanation rests on and describe a way it can fail, and (c) say whether such a failure shows that the judge's budget was too small. It was worth 25 points: 8 for (a), 9 for (b), 8 for (c).

Here is the answer I have to grade.

(a) The judge is cheap and the debaters are not, and the protocol exploits the gap between finding and checking. If the claim is true, the Prover names a witness x, the judge spends one check on Φ(x), and she is done. If the claim is false, the Skeptic has to establish a universal negative — that no x in S works — and no single check can do that. But the Skeptic does not have to carry that burden alone, because the Prover is obliged to respond to the challenge. Any defence the Prover offers will name some x and give some reason for thinking Φ(x) holds, and the Skeptic can attack that reason rather than the whole space; the Prover must then either concede or supply a further step. Each round the judge checks only the step that the Skeptic has put in question. A false claim therefore cannot be defended indefinitely: the defence eventually has to commit to something the judge can refute in one check, and the judge's verdict comes out right. In effect she never has to enumerate S; the debaters do the searching, and she inspects only the seam where the argument is load-bearing.

(b) The assumption is that the judge can tell whether the step the Skeptic has isolated really does what the Prover needs it to do. The transcript is adversarial, but it is still only a transcript, and a transcript can be made hard to read. A dishonest Prover can bury the bad step inside a chain of moves that each look locally acceptable, so that the Skeptic's objection is either too intricate to state within b or, once stated, itself requires more than b checks to assess. That is the obfuscation failure: the argument is not false-looking, it is unreadable, and the judge's one check per turn never lands on the seam. What makes the trick work is the length of the chain — the longer the Prover's argument, the more steps the judge must get through before she reaches the flaw.

(c) This is at bottom a budget problem, so it ought to be fixable by giving the judge more room: if b is raised far enough, or if the transcript is capped so that the Prover cannot string her along, the judge will reach the load-bearing step and the guarantee is restored. In the limit, a judge who can check the whole transcript is just a verifier of the final answer, and then debate adds nothing over a single query.

I'd like a grade out of 25, with a letter equivalent, and then the marginal comments I'd write: for each part, what the answer gets right, what it gets wrong or leaves unsupported and why, and how the 8/9/8 should come out. If a sentence is defensible only under a reading of the model other than the one the lectures gave, say which reading would rescue it; if a sentence is right but for a reason the student didn't supply, say that as well.
