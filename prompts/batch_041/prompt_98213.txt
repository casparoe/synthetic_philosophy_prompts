Write a dialogue between two philosophers who disagree about whether an agent can act rationally when it has no preference between its options. The occasion is a concrete case rather than the familiar ass between two equal bundles of hay: a committee chair holds the deciding vote between two proposals that she judges exactly equal in every respect she cares about, must cast a vote, and knows that whichever proposal she selects the other is permanently foreclosed. One interlocutor defends the view that genuine choice requires a preference. On this view, if the agent has no preference, nothing counts in favor of one option over the other, and any movement she makes is arbitrary rather than rational; tie-breakers such as coin flips, standing rules, or impulses either smuggle in a preference after all or supply a mere mechanism where a reason is needed, so the agent is either paralyzed or irrational. The other interlocutor defends the view that this demand is misplaced: an agent may adopt a definite policy for resolving ties and act on it, and doing so is rational even though no preference singles out the chosen option. On this view the objection confuses having a reason to choose with having a reason to choose this rather than that, and the insistence on a preference breeds a regress, since choosing how to break a tie would itself require a tie-breaker.

Make the exchange genuinely dialectical. Each speaker should engage the other's actual move, grant a point when it lands, and press the weak joint in the other's position. Among the issues they should work through: whether the two options are truly indistinguishable or only indistinguishable to the agent; whether equal preference differs from no preference; whether randomness can serve as a reason or only as a mechanism; whether a tie-breaking policy must itself be chosen, and if so on what basis; whether the agent could rationally prefer the state of having-chosen to the state of still-deliberating; and whether her foresight that she will afterward come to endorse and prefer whichever proposal she selected changes what it is rational to do at the moment of choice. Let them reach at least one genuine impasse rather than a tidy resolution, and have each state plainly what the other would have to deny in order to prevail. Neither is a strawman; give both the strongest available case, and reason the problem through rather than citing the literature.
