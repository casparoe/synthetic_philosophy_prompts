**Three Questions About Heritability**

**1. The received clarification, and what it leaves open**

Discussions of heritability outside specialist settings begin with the same clarifications, and every one of them is correct. Heritability is a property of a population, not of a trait or a person. It is a ratio of variance components, and so is indexed to a particular population at a particular time living in a particular range of environments; change the environment distribution and the number changes. A high heritability does not mean a trait is fixed, or that feeding, teaching or medicating people cannot change it; it means only that, in the population as it happens to be constituted, differences among individuals trace disproportionately to genetic differences. And the heritability of a trait within a group places no constraint on the causes of a difference between groups: mean adult height is highly heritable within well-nourished populations, and the differences in mean height between well-nourished and poorly nourished populations are overwhelmingly environmental.

I accept all of this. My complaint is that, as usually deployed, it has hardened into a ritual rather than an explanation. The ritual answers one question, implies that the others are closed, and makes them harder to ask. What I want to propose is a way of separating the questions that the word *heritability* is used to ask.

**2. Three questions, one word**

*Q1, the variation question:* within this population, in this range of environments, how much of the variance in the trait is attributable to genetic differences among individuals? This is what heritability estimates answer, and they answer it well.

*Q2, the intervention question:* if we changed the environment, or the relevant genotypes, for everyone in the population, how far would the distribution of the trait move? This is a question about counterfactuals. Heritability estimates do not answer it. They bear on it only indirectly, and only under assumptions that are rarely stated: that the intervention does not take the environment far outside the observed range, that interaction and genotype-environment correlation are limited, and that we are not asking about the tails of the distribution.

*Q3, the gap question:* what would happen to the difference between two populations if some feature of their environments, or of their genotype distributions, were equalised? This is not one question but a family, one member per feature chosen. Heritability answers none of them.

The three are not independent, but they are not interderivable either, and nearly every dispute I have read turns on sliding between them without noticing. The hereditarian move runs from a high Q1 estimate to a Q2 or Q3 conclusion. The environmentalist move runs from the correct observation that Q1 is uninformative about Q3 to the conclusion that Q3 has an environmental answer. These are the same inference with opposite signs, and both fail for the same reason: the number does not settle the counterfactual.

**3. The gap question has a shape, not a value**

Q3 is often treated as though it had a single true answer that one side or the other is suppressing. It has no answer at all until the counterfactual is specified. Ask how much of the difference in a trait between two populations is genetic, and you have not yet asked anything; ask what would happen to that difference if the two populations were equalised on nutrition, or schooling, or exposure to a particular pathogen, and you have asked something answerable in principle. What the within-between argument establishes is that Q1 is the wrong evidence for Q3. It is frequently over-read as establishing that Q3 is unanswerable, or worse, as establishing a default answer. Neither follows. What follows is a specification demand, and it cuts both ways: an environmental answer to Q3 is a claim about a specific counterfactual and needs exactly as much specification as a genetic one.

**4. Variance-heritability and intervention-heritability**

Here is the proposal I care most about. I want to distinguish two quantities that the single word *heritability* currently covers.

*Variance-heritability* is the familiar ratio: the share of observed variance in a trait, in a population, attributable to genetic differences among its members. It is a fact about the variation that is actually present.

*Intervention-heritability* is indexed to an intervention rather than to a population: for a specified intervention on genes or on environments, the fraction of the population's current trait variance that the intervention would remove. It is a fact about a counterfactual.

Both are legitimate, they answer different questions, and they come apart in both directions. Where nutrition is adequate and uniform, height is highly variance-heritable, and an intervention that equalised nutrition still further would remove almost none of the variance. Where nutrition varies widely, variance-heritability is lower, and a feeding programme would remove a great deal of it. Nearsightedness is the more interesting case, running the other way: it is among the more strongly variance-heritable of common traits, and it also responds to a simple environmental intervention, with prevalence rising steeply within a single generation in some populations. High variance-heritability did not make it hard to move. Variance-heritability is a function of which sources of variation happen to be present; intervention-heritability is a function of what we can do.

Once the two are separated, the slogan that heritability is irrelevant to policy comes out false in one direction and true in the other. It is false as a claim about intervention-heritability, since the variance decomposition of a population does tell you something, though not much, about where the leverage lies. It is true as a claim about the gap, since no fact about the distribution of variation inside a population can tell you what would happen if you equalised something across populations.

**5. Why the confusion persists**

Both parties to the dispute have an interest in the ambiguity. One wants a high number to mean a genetic cause; the other wants the fact that the number cannot license that inference to mean that the cause is environmental. The distinction above shows why the second inference is no better than the first, and why the conciliatory response — that the trait is partly genetic and partly environmental — is not a solution either. That response treats the decomposition as a report of two ingredients mixed in the trait, when it is a report of two sources of variation in a population, relative to an environment range, for a specified measure. The reason this keeps needing to be said is that the decomposition looks like a recipe.

I would like comments in the following order of priority.

1. **Is the distinction real?** The load-bearing claim is that variance-heritability and intervention-heritability are two quantities rather than one. Give me the strongest reason to think they collapse — that the second is just the first applied to a counterfactual population, or that it is a relabelling of the familiar point that the observed environmental range may be narrower than the possible range. If the distinction survives, tell me which of the two the word *heritability* should be reserved for, and whether my definition of intervention-heritability as the fraction of current variance a specified intervention would remove is tight enough to be used, or whether it behaves badly in cases I have not considered.

2. **Accuracy.** Flag anything a quantitative geneticist would call wrong or misleading. I am most worried about three places: the claim that heritability estimates bear on the intervention question under stated assumptions; my treatment of interaction and genotype-environment correlation, which I gesture at and then drop; and my description of the gap between heritability estimates and the variance accounted for by identified genetic variants, which I deliberately left unnamed. I would rather be told I am wrong here than anywhere else in the piece.

3. **Is the target real?** I claim the standard clarification has hardened into a conversation-ending ritual. State the strongest version of the position I am attacking — not the version I wrote — and then tell me whether anyone actually holds it at that strength, or whether I am arguing with a straw man. 4. **The gap question.** Is section 3 right that Q3 has no answer until the counterfactual is specified, or does that make the point trivial, since on that reading every causal question needs specification and nothing is special about the group case?
