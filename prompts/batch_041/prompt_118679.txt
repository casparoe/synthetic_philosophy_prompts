my reading group has one guy who insists the EU is just a holding company with subsidiaries. member states = subsidiaries, brussels = head office, the commission = the parent's management team, the ECJ = the group's internal legal dept, budget contributions = intercompany transfer pricing, cohesion funds = capital allocation, the eurozone and schengen opt-outs = the parts of a subsidiary that only get partly consolidated, art 50 = the sell button. and he says this settles subsidiarity: it's just the delegation rule any competent firm uses -- push the decision down to whoever has the local knowledge, centralize only when the centre is genuinely cheaper or when one unit's choices spill over onto the others. so all the closer-union rhetoric and the shared-destiny stuff is sentimentality; the union is a joint venture for mutual gain, and if a member's net position goes negative they're entitled to walk, no betrayal involved.

how far does it actually hold? i want the correspondence table first -- parent, subsidiaries, shareholders, assets, transfer pricing, audit, board, exit -- then the disanalogies, sorted into the ones that actually matter for his conclusion and the ones that are just scenery. i especially want to know what the firm picture quietly imports: a single objective function? ownership of the parts rather than their consent? the idea that the delegation rule is set by the principal and can be rewritten whenever it likes? that exit is a sale and not a defection? that 'lowest level competent' is an efficiency question with no legitimacy dimension at all?

and does the conclusion survive? subsidiarity came out of catholic social thought, where the point of it wasn't efficiency -- it was that the smaller unit has a standing the larger one must not absorb. if thats right then the firm analogy is importing the wrong criterion from the start. also there's that early-warning thing where national parliaments can object to a draft law and the commission has to go back and look again (a yellow card?), and in germany the länder mostly just administer federal law while in switzerland the cantons have their own tax base and can't simply be overruled -- do those differences tell us which features of the firm picture are load-bearing? and if the analogy can't carry his conclusion, whats a better one -- i've heard club, marriage, family; do any of those do better, or do they just smuggle in a different set of assumptions?
