I'm a second-year PhD student in philosophy of biology — thesis is a tip, don't ask — and I also sit in on a small fiction night some friends and I run, where each month we all write something to a theme. This month the theme is reasons without reasoners and I've bagsied free-floating rationales. The phrase is Dennett's, as I understand it: the thought that you can honestly say what a bit of behaviour is for, and give the rationale for it — it does X because X reliably brings about Y, and that's why the behaviour is there at all — without there being anybody, anywhere, who holds that reason. The stock illustrations are the cuckoo chick and the digger wasp and so on, and I'd quite like to get away from the seminar-room examples, partly because they've gone stale and partly because I want a story, not an illustration. Can you write me a short story (call it 1,800–2,500 words) that actually dramatises the thing rather than name-checking it? Take the role of a philosopher of biology who is also a good literary short-story writer, and think carefully about the shape before you start — it might be worth spending a page or two brainstorming openings and premises before you commit to one, though I only want to see the story itself, with maybe three or four lines at the top saying what you're going for.

For the setting, here's a seed if it helps: a fictional rewilding scheme on the site of a disused reservoir somewhere in the Pennines, run by a small charity and a hopelessly overstretched council committee, with a graduate student doing a season of invertebrate surveys, a management plan that nobody has read since it was written, and a grant report due. I'd like the story to hold two registers at once. In one, a non-human process — beetles, a fungus, a reedbed, a parasitoid, whatever takes your fancy — should be described in a way that genuinely licenses means–ends talk, where you can say what it's for and be right, but where it would be absurd to say anyone is trying to do it. In the other, a human institution should turn out to have a rationale of the same free-floating sort: its procedures do something, reliably, for a reason no member of it could articulate, and the characters should keep mis-locating the reason — one of them puts it in a designer, one in the animals' little minds, one in the ecosystem "wanting" things. Please resist giving me a narrator who settles the question, and please don't have a character deliver a lecture on Dennett; if anyone gets close to the idea, let them get it slightly wrong. What I want dramatised is the difference between a rationale being real — it explains, it predicts, it earns the phrase in order to — and a reasoner existing, and I'd like the story to make the pull of the opposite assumption visible rather than just refuting it. Finish with a short afterword, 120 words or so, saying which bits of the story you think carry the concept and where it strained or went thin on you.

British English throughout please, spelling and idiom — I'm British and it reads oddly otherwise, and one or two of the friends are insufferable about it. No need for epigraphs or quotations from anyone, and don't worry about citing Dennett anywhere; I know the literature well enough, I just want the fiction.
