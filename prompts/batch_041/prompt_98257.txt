I want the specification, not a survey: what would an adequate theory of generations as real social kinds have to deliver? Lay out the requirements such a theory must meet, with the case or consideration behind each — covering at least the cases it must get right (a birth-year cohort fixed by nothing but an arbitrary interval; a "generation" said to share a formative experience; a label that people take on as an identity and thereby become more alike; a generational term that travels across societies with very different histories and still seems to pick something out); the distinctions it must not collapse (cohort as a statistical construct versus generation as a sociological one; being a member versus being representative or self-identifying; a real kind versus a merely useful label versus a bare aggregation); and the findings it must be consistent with (that age, period, and cohort effects are notoriously confounded in longitudinal data, so a cohort effect cannot simply be read off a snapshot). For each requirement, say why it is a genuine requirement rather than a preference of one camp, mark which are non-negotiable and which negotiable, and flag any pair you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied. Then tell me which single requirement does the most to exclude the usual candidates, and whether the full list is satisfiable at all — that last question is the one I most want answered.
