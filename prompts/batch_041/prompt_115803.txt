I've been trying to work out a position on overdetermination in history and I'd like you to try and knock it down. Roughly: when an event is overdetermined -- two independent causes, each on its own sufficient, say a harvest failure and a military defeat either of which would have brought down a government -- I don't think we should say the event has no cause or that neither cause explains it. I think each cause explains a different dimension of the outcome: one explains that it happened, another explains its timing, manner, or the contrast class it falls under. So 'why did the regime fall?' has no single answer, but that's not a failure; the event just has a modal profile with several dimensions, and different causes answer different contrastive questions.

What would count against this? I want the observations, arguments, intuitions, or formal results that should move me, sorted by how decisive they'd be -- fatal, merely awkward, or just cosmetic. Which historical cases make the contrastive/division-of-labour move look ad hoc? What would a defender of the view themselves concede as a cost? Does it predict anything its rivals don't, or is that the real weakness? And be honest about whether it risks anything at all or has just been qualified into unfalsifiability -- and if it has, whether that's a problem for a view of this kind. British English, please.
