I'm a hospital pharmacist — eleven years on the wards, and before that two years in a community shop that ran a needle exchange out the back door, so I've seen this from both ends. My brother-in-law is a public defender and we've been going at each other for months about drug policy, and his favourite move is to tell me I'm smugling medical assumptions into a moral question. Fair enough, maybe. But it occured to me that the reverse should be possible: take the whole debate — prohibition, the Portugal-style decriminalization thing, full legalization of something like cannabis — and restate it, as faithfully as you can, in the vocabulary I actually think in. Indications and contraindications. Dose and dose-response. Therapeutic index. Tolerance vs. dependence vs. addiction. Prescription-only vs. behind-the-counter vs. over-the-counter. Adulteration, cold chain, pharmacovigilance, off-label use, drug interactions, and the difference between treating the patient in front of you and managing a whole population. Translate the actual arguments, not the slogans: deterrence, the claim that decriminalization doesn't raise use, the claim that legalization just hands the market to corporations, the claim that the user is a patient rather than a criminal, the claim that using a drug for no medical reason is nobody else's business. I don't need a reading list or names to drop at dinner — I need the translation itself.

Then the part I actually care about: the residue report. Which of those arguments comes through intact, which only comes through mangled, and which I simply cannot say at all? I suspect, for instance, that I have no way to express 'a person is entitled to get high' — in my language every drug is either indicated or contraindicated, and someone who takes a drug without an indication is non-adherent, not free. If that's right, tell me whether it's a hole in my framework or evidence that the original claim was doing less work than it sounded like it was. Same question for anything you have to fudge to make the translation go through — don't fudge it quietly, flag it.

One more thing, and this may be me being too clever. In my world the therapeutic index is the gap between the dose that does the job and the dose that poisons you, and a drug with a narrow index is one where the margin for error is small: you monitor the patient constantly, you keep it on a tight leash, you don't hand it out casually. It seems to me that the prohibition-versus-legalization fight is really a fight about the width of that index at the level of a whole population rather than one patient — narrow social index, keep it illegal and closely watched; wide index, sell it at the corner shop. Does that actually hold up as a translation, or does it collapse the moment you push on it? Push on it. If it breaks I'd rather know where.
