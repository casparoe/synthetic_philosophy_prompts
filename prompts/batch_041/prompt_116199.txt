I've talked myself into consequentialism, but the cluelessness problem has started to eat at me: the causal ripples of anything I do run on indefinitely, most of what determines their value happens long after I'm gone, and I have no way to estimate any of it. What I want from you is not a defence of consequentialism but a reconstruction of what I actually think, so here are my verdicts on some cases.

1. Fifty pounds to a charity that distributes bed nets versus fifty pounds on drinks for my friends: the bed nets are better, and not by a little. I feel no need to trace either option through the next three centuries in order to say so.
2. Rinsing a yogurt pot before it goes in the recycling: I do it, but I don't think anyone acts wrongly by skipping it, and I'd be embarrassed to call it a duty. Whatever it accomplishes, it does through councils, markets and norms whose net effect on the next few hundred years I cannot begin to assess.
3. Voting in a national election: this feels different from the yogurt pot even though the structure looks identical — my vote almost certainly decides nothing, and its knock-on effects are as opaque as the pot's. I still think there is a real reason to vote and something faintly shabby about not bothering.
4. Chicken sandwich versus bean burger: the sandwich is worse, and the reason is the chicken's life, which is the kind of thing I can actually see. It never occurs to me to ask what either lunch does in 2200.
5. Deworming charity versus a museum's new wing: deworming, on the strength of the near-term effects I can identify.
6. Moving my pension out of fossil fuels: I feel good about it and would press a friend to do it, even though its effect on emissions is a rounding error and its effect on prices, norms and politics is anyone's guess.
7. Reading to my child for an hour instead of working that hour and donating the money: fine, and not self-indulgent, though I cannot see how to get that verdict out of a calculation, since the donation has identifiable beneficiaries and the bedtime hour mostly benefits us.
8. Having a child at all: neither required nor forbidden, even though adding a person, and then their descendants, dwarfs everything else on this list.

Two things bother me. Cases 2 and 3 look like the same case with different verdicts, and cases 1, 5 and 6 seem to lean on the visible part of the causal chain while 2 refuses to. The principle I would like to hold is roughly this: compare acts by the difference they make in the portion of the causal neighbourhood I can actually model, and treat the vast unknowable remainder as a common factor that cancels between the options. Call it the cancelling-far-future view.

Does that survive the list? If not, I want the strongest principle that saves as many of the eight verdicts as possible, with the case that motivates each clause of it, and an honest statement of which verdict it cannot accommodate. Please also say what it implies for cases I have not written down — a career choice between medicine and finance, or whether to fly to a wedding — and whether the far future can be treated as cancelling only when the two options' unknown effects are similar in distribution rather than merely unknown, which I suspect is the weak point. Finally, tell me whether the 2/3 pair can be saved by anything short of a rule-consequentialist move, since I have a feeling I would find that ad hoc.
