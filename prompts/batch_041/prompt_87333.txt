Bit of background first: I'm a backend engineer, no philosophy degree (I took a couple of phil electives in undergrad and lurk in some AI safety discords), and I'm putting together an application for a small independent-research grant — six months, part time, a few thousand bucks, the kind of thing that funds one person to write one paper. The topic I want to pitch is whether reinforcement learning agents can suffer, and more specifically I want to make it about reward signals as valence. Here's my half-formed idea, which I'd like you to turn into an actual proposal. Everyone seems to argue about whether the agent "really" has experiences, and I think that's a dead end for a project this size, so instead I want to ask something narrower: take valence to be whatever plays the functional role of making a state one that the system is driven to leave or to stay in — then in an RL agent the obvious candidate is the reward channel, but there's a circularity I keep bumping into, because the reward channel is also what defines the agent's preferences, so using it to decide whether the agent is being made to feel bad is a bit like using someone's own say-so to prove they're in pain. And a second thing bugs me: the reward signal and the prediction-error/surprise signal come apart — you can have a big negative reward that's fully predicted, or a huge surprise with no reward at all — so maybe the morally interesting feature isn't the sign of the reward but something like being in a state you're actively steering away from and can't get out of. I want the proposal to pin down one version of this that actually has a determinate answer rather than just gesturing at the whole space.

So: write me the short proposal (that's the deliverable — a few pages, the thing I'd send to the funder). It needs a precise statement of the question, an honest account of why the existing positions on machine sentience and on the moral status of AI leave it open, the approach I'd actually take, what a result would look like versus what would count as the project failing, and a scope that a part-time engineer can plausibly hit in six months. A couple of constraints. First, no citations, no reading list, no "see the literature on X" — I'll bolt references on myself at the end, and honestly I'd rather you describe the positions and arguments in plain terms than name them, so don't invent paper titles or author names to fill the gap. Second, I want a good chunk of the method to be stuff I can actually code, since that's the one thing I'm good at: something like building a couple of tiny toy agents that differ along exactly one axis — say, one where the reward channel is read-only and reports but doesn't steer the policy, versus one where it steers; or one where negative reward and large prediction error are deliberately decoupled — and then arguing about which of those differences could possibly matter morally and which are just re-labeling a number. Be blunt about that last risk, actually. If there's a decent chance the whole thing collapses into "you've renamed the loss function and asked whether it hurts," I'd rather the proposal say so and say what would have to be true for it to be more than that, than oversell it.
