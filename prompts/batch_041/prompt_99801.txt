Two members of my reading group spent most of a session arguing about whether qualia are an incoherent posit, and I genuinely could not tell who had the better of it. Here is the exchange as faithfully as I can reconstruct it.

(1) One of them began with the concept itself: a quale is supposed to be ineffable yet something we report, intrinsic yet causally responsible for our reports, private yet compared across persons, and immediately known yet exactly the sort of thing that the motivating cases (inverted spectrum, knowing what it is like) turn on our possibly being wrong about. No state could carry all four at once, so the notion is not merely hard to verify but internally confused.

(2) The other said this proves too much. Terms like gene, species, and electron all rest on folk glosses that do not survive scrutiny, yet we refine them rather than declare them incoherent. An inconsistent folk definition shows a concept needs sharpening, not that it has no referent.

(3) The first replied that the disanalogy is that the scientific concepts get sharpened by causal-explanatory work, whereas qualia are introduced as precisely the remainder left after every functional, causal, and representational fact has been subtracted. A posit whose entire brief is to be explanatorily idle cannot be rescued by its explanatory successes, which is what makes it uniquely vulnerable.

(4) The second called this circular, since it assumes functionalism is the test of intelligibility, and offered a case: a person whose color processing is permuted but whose discrimination, naming, memory, and aesthetic responses are all compensated, so that nothing observable differs; she nonetheless insists that red now looks the way green once did. If that is even conceivable, he said, the refutation is coming from the no-causal-work premise, not from the concept.

(5) The first said that "even conceivable" is the problem: the case is stipulated to be undetectable, so nothing could count for or against it, and a difference that makes no difference is no difference. The second said that is just the verificationism the first had disowned at the outset, and that "makes no difference" is itself a functionalist slogan.

(6) The second then pressed a further charge: the whole argument equivocates between qualia as the felt character of experience, which nobody denies, and qualia as intrinsic, non-relational properties. Quining the second does not quine the first, so the conclusion that there are no qualia is a bait-and-switch. The first answered that the felt character just is a certain representational-cum-functional state, and that calling it qualia adds an item where none is needed.

Please adjudicate this point by point: for each of the six moves, say what is right and what is mistaken, and rate it from clearly mistaken to clearly right. Note where either party concedes too much or claims too much, and finish by saying what both of them are missing.
