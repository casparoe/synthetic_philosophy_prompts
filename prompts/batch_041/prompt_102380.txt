I have spent much of this year in and out of hospital with a back problem, and I have become rather fixated on a small piece of bureaucracy that seems to me to have a great deal of philosophy hidden inside it. Every few hours a nurse asks me to give my pain a number from nought to ten, and almost everything downstream of that number is decided by it: at seven or above a doctor is supposed to come and see me within half an hour, at three or below I become a candidate for discharge, and what I am given for it is adjusted according to where the number sits. The trust has lately added a second question — how much the pain is interfering with what I can actually do that day — which is scored separately and can send me off to a different clinic altogether, and there is a row of faces on the wall for people who would rather point at a picture than say a number. A physiotherapist, trying to be kind, told me that the scans often show very little wrong with people who are in agony and a good deal wrong with people who are perfectly comfortable, so the number is more or less all anybody has to go on. And yet the same hospital will not take my word for how much alcohol is in me, or how much paracetamol, or whether I have a temperature; for those things there are machines. Meanwhile a relative of mine was refused a disability payment after an assessment in which the assessor decided, largely from how she looked and moved, that she was not in nearly as much pain as she claimed — the exact opposite arrangement, in which my report of myself counts for nothing and a stranger's observation of me counts for everything.

What I want to know is what this whole apparatus assumes about how well any of us can know our own minds. When the number I produce by introspection is the thing that unlocks a doctor or a dose of morphine, the hospital appears to be treating my report as the final word on a fact about me — indeed as a fact about which I could not be mistaken — and treating my own access to my own pain as better than any observer's or any instrument's. But if that is the assumption, I cannot see why anyone would bother hunting for a machine that measures pain, or why my relative's assessor was entitled to overrule her, or why we should need to ask about interference with daily life in addition to intensity, or why a nurse sometimes looks doubtful when the same patient reports a seven every single time. If, on the other hand, the assumption is only that I am the most convenient informant available, or that saying I am in pain is not so much a report of an inner state as a way of asking for help — a move in a game rather than a description — then I do not understand why the number is permitted to trigger a rule as rigid as the half-hour one, or why anybody would think that a scale from nought to ten could mean the same thing for me as for the man in the next bed.

Could you reconstruct for me the theory of self-knowledge that this practice presupposes? I should like to know which parts of it are genuinely required by the practice and which parts it merely happens to sit comfortably with — I suspect a good deal falls into the second category — whether the various bits of it are consistent with one another, and what would have to change in the hospital if we adopted a rival view instead: that introspection is a fallible faculty rather like eyesight, that it is a story we construct after the fact rather than a window onto anything, or that avowals of pain are not descriptions at all. Please write as though explaining this to someone who has read no philosophy but is not stupid.
