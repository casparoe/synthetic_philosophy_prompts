So I've been hanging around animal-welfare and EA-adjacent corners of the internet for years now, and theres this pattern in how we actually regulate animals that I can't stop turning over. The law basically draws its line at vertebrates — and in a few places it's only just started reaching out to octopuses and other cephalopods. If you're a mouse in a lab, there are committees, enrichment requirements, pain relief, rules about the method of killing. If you're an insect — and by the rough estimates we're killing something like a trillion-plus a year in agriculture and pest control, maybe orders of magnitude more — there are basically no rules, no one has standing, nobody has to think about it at all. We'll spend real money making a few thousand lab mice comfortable and precisely zero on the welfare of astronomically more bugs. And sure, maybe bugs don't feel anything. But that's the part that nags me: nobody actually knows that, the science is genuinely unsettled, and yet the practice acts as if the question were closed. It feels like a very specific philosophical decision is being made quietly in the background, and I want to know what it is.

So: what view of sentience and moral standing does this regulatory pattern presuppose? Does it require thinking sentience is all-or-nothing and tied to a particular kind of nervous system, so that anything under the line gets exactly zero weight and the numbers don't even enter? Or could you run the identical practice off a graded view — expected welfare = probability of sentience × welfare — and just claim the bug probability is so low it rounds to nothing, and if so is that actually consistent given how hard the numbers explode when you multiply across a trillion individuals? Does the practice commit you to a threshold in probability below which no quantity of individuals can compensate (so that a mouse at high confidence outweighs a billion insects at low confidence, forever), and is that threshold defensible or just an unexamined artifact of how our empathy scales? I'd also like to know where it's philosophically underdetermined — which rival theories (all-or-nothing neural-substrate views, graded expected value, a "capacity for a life" view, or frankly something about charisma and relatability) would license exactly the same regulations, so that the pattern can't tell us which is true. And finally, if you swapped the presupposed view for a straight expected-value-over-insects view, what would concretely have to change — a revolution in how we farm and exterminate, or mostly the same laws with a much bigger research budget for figuring out whether bugs suffer?
