I'm writing a paper on Dennett's heterophenomenology and there's one objection I keep failing to get to the bottom of, so I want you to walk me through it properly. Take the role of a philosopher of mind who knows the consciousness literature cold and is not going to just recite the standard talking points back at me. Before you answer, think it through carefully -- maybe start by spelling out to yourself, step by step, what the method is actually supposed to deliver, because I suspect the trouble is hiding in one of the middle steps.

Here's the method as I understand it. You collect what a subject says about their experience. You bracket the question of whether the experience is real. You treat the reports as a kind of text to be interpreted, you build up a picture of the world as the subject takes it to be, and then you ask what's going on in the subject that makes them say all of that. The subject is always authoritative about what they say, and never automatically authoritative about what's actually the case.

Now here's my case. A subject under hyponosis, in total good faith, reports in enourmous detail that they were taken aboard a craft and examined. They're not lying and they're not muddled about what they're saying. The heterophenomenologist does the standard thing: treats the report as data, builds the world in which the subject really was abducted, then explains the report as a product of suggestion and confabulation. Fine.

Then take the boring case: a subject reports a red apple on the table. Same procedure -- treat it as data, build the world with the apple in it, explain the report by pointing to the apple and the visual system. Also fine.

My question is what actually separates these two. In both cases the report ends up explained by something other than the thing reported. If the experience is just whatever explains the report, then we've explained the report in both cases and there's nothing left over -- which seems to mean the method can never tell us anything about consciousness, awkward for a method that's meant to be the science of consciousness. But if in the apple case there's something left over -- the actual redness, the what-it's-like -- then why isn't there the same left-over thing in the abduction case, and what in the method would ever let us detect it? I've been calling this the parity problem: ordinary and confabulated reports get handled identically, so either both leave an experiential residue or neither does, and the method seems unable to say which.

So please explain the issue to me properly. I don't want 'Dennett would reply that...' as a slogan -- I want the structure: what work the picture of the world as the subject takes it to be is actually doing, whether the abduction case really is parallel to the apple case or whether I'm missing a disanalogy, and where (if anywhere) the method legitimately draws a line between reports that do and don't leave something over. And tell me plainly if I'm just confused. Rigorous, but pitched at someone smart who's new to this.
