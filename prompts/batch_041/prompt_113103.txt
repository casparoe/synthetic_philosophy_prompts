Can you go through it and tell me whats actually right, what's the kind of simplification a popularizer makes, and what's just wrong? Here's the gist, the way I'd repeat it back:

'Acausal trade is when two agents who can never affect each other still manage to cooperate. Picture two civilisations in seperate galaxies, so far apart that nothing either one does can ever reach the other. Each would rather the other do X than Y, and each is willing to do the other's preferred thing in return. Since neither can reward or punish the other, the trade only works if each agent simulates the other and works out what it would decide — the same way you treat your own choice as evidence about what a copy of you will do, like Omega in Newcomb's problem. But it only works if the two agents are exactly identical, because only then does your decision determine theirs. So it's basically Hofstadter's superrationality applied to games you never actually get to play, which makes it basically Kant's categorical imperative with math. And the whole thing rests on the assumption that the universe is infinite, so that every possible agent exists somewhere, which is why the people who believe in acausal trade also tend to believe in the multiverse. The standard objection is that it's unfalsifiable, and that in practice it's just a licence for weird commitments — paying off hypothetical blackmailers, doing favours for a god you don't believe in. If you accept it though, it explains why a rational agent should cooperate with copies of itself, and why we should care about far-future people who can't do anything for us.'

Honestly my instinct is that the whole idea is a bit silly and only works if you assume every agent is a perfect reasoner with the same priors.
