Some friends and I have spent the past month working through the Gongsun Longzi together, and next week it falls to me to open our session on the White Horse Dialogue. I can follow the exchange sentence by sentence, but I keep losing the thread of the argument as a whole, and what I want is a careful reconstruction of what each speaker is actually doing—not a verdict on whether the dialogue is serious philosophy or an elaborate stunt. The translation we have been working from renders the central exchange as follows:

"Can 'a white horse is not a horse' be asserted?"
[Advocate] "It can."
[Objector] "How so?"
[Advocate] "'Horse' is what designates shape. 'White' is what designates color. What designates color is not what designates shape. Therefore I say: a white horse is not a horse."
[Objector] "If there is a white horse, one cannot say there is no horse. If one cannot say there is no horse, does that not mean there is a horse? Since having a white horse counts as having a horse, how can the white [one] not be a horse?"
[Advocate] "If one seeks a horse, yellow and black horses may all be delivered. If one seeks a white horse, yellow and black horses may not be delivered. Suppose a white horse were [just] a horse. Then what is sought [in the two cases] would be the same. If what is sought were the same, then 'white' would not differ from 'horse.' If what is sought does not differ, how is it that yellow and black horses are acceptable [in one case] and unacceptable [in the other]? That acceptable and unacceptable are mutually opposed is clear. Therefore yellow and black horses are the same in that they can answer to 'there is a horse,' but cannot answer to 'there is a white horse.' This is a white horse's not being a horse: it is certain."

The trouble is that the Advocate appears to give two quite different arguments, and I cannot see how they hang together. The first turns on what words designate—"horse" picks out shape, "white" picks out color, and what picks out color is not what picks out shape—and moves straight from that to the conclusion. The second drops designation entirely and reasons from the fact that a yellow or black horse will satisfy someone seeking a horse but not someone seeking a white horse. Meanwhile the Objector's objection looks like a point about entailment rather than designation or seeking: if having a white horse counts as having a horse, then surely the white one is a horse. I cannot see that either of the Advocate's arguments actually touches that objection, and I suspect the dialogue drifts between different senses of the word translated "is not" without ever admitting it.

One of my friends has proposed a way to unify the whole thing, and I would like your help deciding whether it works. His suggestion is that both arguments are instances of a single test of the kind Leibniz gives for identity: two things are the same only if they can be substituted for one another without changing the truth of what is said. On his reading the "seeking" argument is the decisive one, because it exhibits a context—"x is what is sought"—in which swapping "horse" and "white horse" flips the truth value, so the two cannot be identical; and the shape/color argument is just a clumsy preliminary gesture at the same point. What I would like from you is a reconstruction that (i) states precisely what the Advocate claims in each argument, and in particular what notion of "what is sought" the second one requires; (ii) says whether that second argument does or does not meet the Objector's "having" objection, and whether the two speakers may simply be talking past each other; (iii) assesses my friend's Leibnizian reading, above all whether it genuinely unifies the two arguments or disguises a shift between them; and (iv) tells me whether the dialogue must be read as using "is not" in a single sense at all, or whether the two speakers can be assigned different senses without either being obviously mistaken.
