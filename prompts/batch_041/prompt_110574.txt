In three weeks I am presenting a paper at a departmental colloquium, and what I need from you is a question-period preparation sheet, not a defense of the paper. My thesis is that the Meno problem is a real problem with a misplaced demand. The problem asks why knowledge should be worth more than true belief, and its shape presupposes that the surplus value attaches to the state of believing. I argue that it cannot attach there. The epistemic evaluation of a belief supervenes on its content and its truth; any further property one might add (being justified, being reliably produced, being safe, being non-accidentally true) either adds nothing beyond truth or adds something that belongs to the belief's history rather than to the belief. The surplus is therefore the value of a transition: what is worth more is not the true belief but having arrived at it by a route that would have arrived at it again. Two subjects holding the same true belief have, as believers, the same epistemic standing; they differ only in the value of the histories that produced their beliefs. Three commitments I want to keep: the Meno problem is answered rather than dissolved; the difference between those two subjects is real and not merely pragmatic; and knowledge licenses assertion and practical reliance in a way that lucky true belief does not.

The room is as follows. Seven epistemologists, most of whom work on the value of knowledge or on virtue epistemology, and who will assume without argument that the surplus must be located in some property of the belief itself. Two philosophers of science who are Bayesians, hold that the sole epistemic good is accuracy, and regard any distinction that does not show up in the credence as epiphenomenal. One philosopher of language working on testimony and epistemic injustice, who will ask whose transitions are being credited and whether my account makes testimonial knowledge derivative or second-hand. And one historian of philosophy, who will ask what my position does to the ancient formulation and whether I am answering the problem or changing the subject.

Give me the objections I will actually get from this room, ordered by how much damage they would do in it, weighing both how likely each is and how costly it would be. Phrase each one the way its author would phrase it, in that author's vocabulary, not in mine. For each, give me a reply I can deliver in about two minutes and a single sentence on where that reply is softest. Where two objections are the same underlying worry in two vocabularies, say so rather than padding the list. Tell me the two or three things I should concede at the outset of the Q&A rather than be pushed into. Tell me which reply has to land for the paper to survive the hour, and if the Bayesians and the virtue epistemologists are making demands I cannot satisfy at once, tell me which of them I should disappoint and why. End with the question I am hoping nobody asks.
