For a university symposium next spring on transformative experience — the problem that an experience which changes what you can know also changes who you are, so that the self who chooses it cannot know in advance what it is choosing — I have been asked to give a lecture on my own craft, and I want to be ready for the discussion afterward. I don't need the familiar thought experiments recited back to me.

My thesis: for fifteen years I have written interiority from the outside — research, interviews, imagination — and the result, I have come to believe, has a specific defect: it is assembled rather than inhabited. For my next book I intend to undergo, deliberately, a transformation of the kind I mean to depict, and I claim that for a certain class of character this is not an aid to the work but a condition of it. The test I use is the two-manuscript test: if the book could have been written by someone who had only read about the experience, the experience was incidental; if not, it was constitutive — and constitutive transformations cannot be faked from the outside.

The room: four philosophers who work on the epistemology of transformative choice; two critics who write on autofiction and on the ethics of representing other people's lives; three working novelists from the MFA faculty; one developmental psychologist — the philosophers skeptical that any such choice can be made rationally, the critics alert to extraction and appropriation, the novelists unsentimental about method-talk.

Draft my Q&A prep sheet: the seven objections I will actually get from this room, phrased in each faction's own vocabulary, ordered by how much damage each would do, with a two-minute reply and a candid note on where each reply is weakest. Tell me what to concede up front, which objection is really a disagreement about values rather than facts, and the one question I am hoping nobody asks. End with a single line beginning 'Verdict:' that names, in one sentence, the objection most likely to sink the thesis and why.
