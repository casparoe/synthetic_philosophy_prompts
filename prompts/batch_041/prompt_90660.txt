For the last few weeks a reading group I'm in has been working through decision theory under extreme uncertainty, and we keep circling the same uncomfortable thought: once you allow that the far future might contain vastly more beings than the present, even minuscule probabilities of getting things right can swamp everything else — which is roughly what people mean when they accuse this style of reasoning of fanaticism. A friend of mine has been arguing that the simulation hypothesis is the cure: if we're probably living in a computer simulation, the far future is a subroutine that could be deleted at any moment, so the stakes shrink and the fanaticism should subside. I'm not convinced, and I suspect his argument is doing something more interesting than he thinks. Before our next meeting I want to lay out the actual space of positions here so we stop talking past each other.

Please build me a decision tree — or, if it works better, a taxonomy organized as a short quiz with a scoring key — that takes a reader from commitments they can hold without already knowing any of the labels down to named positions on whether the simulation hypothesis gives any reason to temper fanaticism about the far future. Drive the branching from the logical joints of the question rather than from a list of authors or schools. I'd like the first split to be over where the alleged dampening is supposed to occur: in the expected number of future beings whose welfare is at stake (a simulated world cut short, a future confined to a subroutine); in the moral weight we assign to simulated versus non-simulated minds; in the probabilities themselves, where the hypothesis shifts credence toward scenarios of very different size; or in the decision theory, for instance in whether the simulators' values enter our own expected-value calculation. Each later node should likewise be a question a reader can answer on its own terms. Please make sure the following positions appear somewhere as leaves: the view that simulation implies a shortened future and so genuinely lowers the expected value; the view that simulated minds count for less; the view that the hypothesis merely redistributes value without reducing it, leaving fanaticism untouched; the view that it amplifies the stakes by multiplying the worlds that matter; the view that only specific versions of the hypothesis dampen anything; and the deflationary view that the whole question conflates credence with value. I'd also like one leaf for the thought that the simulation hypothesis is itself a tiny-probability, enormous-consequence scenario, so invoking it is an instance of fanaticism rather than a remedy — and, if it fits, a leaf for the self-undermining worry that a simulated reasoner's credences about the far future are themselves suspect.

Use concrete cases rather than abstractions wherever you can: the subroutine that might be switched off, the question whether a simulated mind's suffering is as bad as a biological one's, the possibility that we are being run as a test so that our choices matter more rather than less, and the structural parallel between the simulation hypothesis and the far-future reasoning it is meant to restrain. Build the space first and place the positions in it second, and tell me which combinations of answers are unoccupied and why — is the view incoherent, merely unattractive, or just unexplored? Flag any positions your tree cannot separate rather than forcing them apart. Aim for somewhere between six and nine leaves. Finally, since this is going onto a handout, keep clear the line between what people who actually work on this agree about and what is genuinely contested; do not present any contested verdict as the consensus, and say where the tree is drawing a distinction that is itself disputed.
