I chair the performance-measurement review board of a mid-sized state human-services agency. Twice a year the board approves, renews, or retires the roughly forty measures that govern our contracts with private providers, our federal reporting, and, indirectly, the annual bonuses of regional directors. Each measure gets about ninety minutes of meeting time; I have no analyst of my own; the standing membership is two program directors, a lawyer, a data manager, and a community representative; and whatever we decide has to be defensible in writing to the state auditor. I would like a procedure the board can run on a proposed or an existing measure to decide whether it is safe to use as a target.

Build it on Goodhart's law and on what is actually known about specification gaming, but do not give me a restatement of the law. I want the questions, in the order we should ask them; what counts as a sufficient answer to each; the point at which the procedure returns a flat no; and the points at which it must stop and hand the decision back to the board because the question is one of values rather than of measurement. Assume the procedure's user understands percentages and averages and nothing more. Mark plainly which steps are judgment calls wearing the costume of measurement, and say which distinctions in the underlying idea have to survive this translation into a committee procedure and which we can drop without losing the point.

The procedure has to survive five cases, because all five come up every cycle.

- A measure that cannot be gamed and still tells us nothing we care about. Goodhart's law has nothing to say about this failure, and I need the procedure to.
- A measure whose gaming is the behaviour we wanted. When we began counting caseworker visits, visits rose, and part of the rise was real. The procedure must distinguish corruption of a measure from the intended response to it.
- A measure we are required to report federally, which we may not retire whatever the procedure concludes. It needs an output meaning keep it, but do not let it steer anything, and it needs to say how that is enforced when the governor's office reads the same dashboard we do.
- A measure reported to us by the providers whose money depends on it.
- The case I expect to dislike: the board's favourite measure, the one everyone in the room is proud of. I want the procedure to be capable of ruling against it.

Two further matters to settle. First, we have observed a failure mode I have not seen named. The measure keeps tracking what we care about, but over the years the agency reorganizes around it: hiring favours people who move the number, programme goals get rewritten in the measure's vocabulary, and what we care about gradually comes to mean whatever the measure captures. If your taxonomy of risks has no slot for this, say so and say what slot to add. Second, a colleague proposes that we sort every risk into drift, capture, or saturation — drift being the measure ceasing to track the target because the world moved, capture being agents optimizing the measure instead of the target, saturation being the measure ceasing to discriminate once everyone reaches the ceiling. Assess that three-way cut: is it the right division, does it conflate things that should be kept apart, and is your procedure better with it, with a repaired version of it, or without it?

Then run the finished procedure from beginning to end on two measures — monthly caseworker visits per family, and the share of children returned home within twelve months — showing the questions asked, the answers, the output, and the objection you would expect from the board. Finish by telling me where the procedure will misfire, what it cannot decide, and which of the five cases above it handles worst.
