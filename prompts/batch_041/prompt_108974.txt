I've been drafting a paper on individual duties in collective-harm cases and have ground to a halt; my suspicion is that the literature has stalled for the same reason, and I want that suspicion tested rather than confirmed. The shape of the standoff is this. Where a harm materialises only if enough people act — emissions, meat, a vote in a district decided by tens of thousands of ballots — the individual act either makes no difference at all to whether the harm occurs, or makes a difference with a vanishingly small probability. One camp concludes that the act is not wrong, or at least not wrong for the reason usually offered, and treats the generalisation of that result — no duty to vote, no duty to abstain from meat, no duty to shrink one's footprint — as a welcome clarification of what morality can demand. The other camp holds that the act is wrong anyway and relocates the wrongness: into the act's expressive or symbolic significance, into complicity with a harmful collective, into the breach of a duty to do one's part in a just scheme, into the agent's maxim, or into the claim that the act is a genuine but small additive contribution to a divisible harm. Both sides accept the same facts about thresholds and marginal effects. Neither has moved in a decade.

What kind of disagreement is this, actually? I want a diagnosis with reasons, not an adjudication. Consider at least these candidates and say which is doing the work, and in what proportion: (a) a hidden disagreement about causation — whether making a difference is a counterfactual notion or a contributory one, so that the sides never meant the same thing by the phrase; (b) a disagreement about the object of the moral predicate, act-token versus pattern of action versus social practice, which would make the two sides' central claims compatible under different readings and the dispute verbal; (c) an empirical disagreement about the shape of harm functions — sharp threshold versus additive and divisible, certain versus probabilistic — which could in principle be settled by finding out how the relevant harms actually work; (d) bedrock intuitions about complicity, about what it is to be implicated in a wrong one did not intend and could not have prevented; (e) a disagreement about whether ought can attach to an agent who cannot affect the outcome at all.

Test your diagnosis against a battery of cases and report where each side's verdicts diverge: a harm with a sharp threshold that has already been crossed before I act; a harm that is purely additive and evenly divisible among millions of contributors; a case where my act raises the probability of a catastrophe by a minuscule amount; and a case where my act makes no causal difference whatever but is of a kind that would produce the harm if sufficiently many acted likewise. If the two sides' verdicts track the causal features across these cases, the diagnosis is one thing; if they track something else, it is another. Say which pattern you find.

My hypothesis, offered as a hypothesis and not as something I want you to flatter: the causal machinery on both sides is largely epiphenomenal, and the real dispute is about whether wrongness attaches to act-tokens or to patterns, with the inefficacy objection serving each side as a respectable causal costume for a conclusion it already held. If that is right, tell me what follows. If it is wrong, tell me where it breaks.

Finally, given your diagnosis, say what would count as progress: a distinction that dissolves the standoff, a reformulation of the question, a formal result about harm functions, an experiment or measurement that would settle the empirical residue — or nothing, and why nothing should be expected. If you conclude the dispute is verbal, you owe me an account of why a verbal dispute has survived this long and what would be lost by resolving it one way rather than the other.

Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give the reasoning. No preamble, and do not restate my question.
