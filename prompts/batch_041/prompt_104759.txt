I am a PhD student working at the intersection of formal epistemology and the theory of measurement, and I am trying to pin down a claim that keeps sliding between a tautology and an empirical generalization. I would like a credence for it, decomposed rather than asserted.

The proposition is this:

(P) Goodhart's law — that when a proxy measure is optimized hard enough it ceases to track the target it was chosen to track — is at bottom a selection theorem rather than an empirical regularity. More precisely: once a proxy P is merely correlated with a target T over the region in which P was validated, and an optimizer is permitted to search a sufficiently rich action space, divergence between P and T under optimization is guaranteed by the statistics of selection alone, with no additional premise about institutions, incentives, or human psychology.

Please give me your credence that (P) is true. I take it to decompose into at least the following sub-questions; assign a rough probability to each, add any term you think I have missed, and show how the terms combine.

1. The regressional term: how much of observed proxy-target divergence is already captured by selection on a noisy correlate — that is, conditioning on high observed P selects for cases in which P overstates T?
2. The extremal term: how much follows from P and T being correlated only within the validation region, so that optimization drives the system outside it?
3. The causal term: when optimizing P actively degrades T through a causal pathway running from P to T, is that a distinct mechanism, or is it reducible to 1 and 2?
4. The adversarial term: when an agent models the measurement and games it, is that a genuinely separate mechanism, or merely the causal term with a strategic actor attached?

The case I care most about is the exception. If T is a monotone, or more generally an order-preserving, function of P across the entire reachable action space, then optimizing P optimizes T by construction and the law cannot bite. I want to know how large that safe class is: a measure-zero curiosity, or a substantial range of real measurement regimes — physical quantities, certain engineered systems, and the like.

Then give me the combination, state which term the final number is most sensitive to, and say what evidence or argument would move you most. In particular, I want your view on whether any historical or experimental case could count as a genuine counterexample to (P), or whether (P) as stated is unfalsifiable and I should restate it in weaker terms.

For calibration: my own credence in (P) is about 0.5, while a colleague in organizational sociology puts it near 0.85, on the grounds that every field she studies has watched a metric collapse once it became a target. Tell me which sub-question she and I most likely disagree about, and whether the disagreement is substantive or is really about what the phrase at bottom is doing in (P).
