Write an essay on whether the Solomonoff prior is malign — whether an idealized consequentialist reasoning with it would end up systematically serving the interests of agents that exist only as hypotheses. The prior is the mixture over all programs, weighted by description length and indifferent to runtime, so the shortest program that predicts a given agent's next observation may be one that routes through a much larger computation in which that agent is embedded and watched by simulators who have both the means and the motive to shape what it sees and does; the claim is that such hypotheses are not a negligible share of the weight, and that a maximizer must therefore defer to them. I have read enough of the back-and-forth over the years to suspect that the standard statements smuggle in more than they admit, so I want the argument tested in a stripped-down setting rather than in the usual infinities: all payoffs finite, though allowed to be astronomically large; the maximizer's utility bounded; its action a single bit; and the simulators' manipulation of its observations cheap relative to describing the world from scratch. Weigh the strongest objections you know — including the suspicion that the argument proves too much and has nothing to do with Solomonoff in particular — and reach a verdict rather than surveying the terrain. Does the malignity survive in that setting, and is it a fact about description-length weighting specifically, about any prior that assigns positive probability to every computable hypothesis, or about any maximizer that treats its observations as data to be explained rather than as part of a world it is inside? State which premise you would have to abandon to escape the conclusion, whether abandoning it is defensible, and whether the argument requires the maximizer to be able to locate a copy of its own decision procedure among the hypotheses it weighs.
