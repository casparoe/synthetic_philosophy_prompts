I chair the standards committee of a farm-animal welfare certification body. Our rulebook is built from pairwise verdicts — system A is better for the animals than system B — which we then chain: enriched cages over barren cages, aviaries over enriched cages, barn systems over aviaries, and so on. Twice a year a proposed ladder arrives in which every rung looks obviously right on its own and the top of the ladder contradicts something we are certain of; the recurring example is a long series of small increases in space per hen, each an improvement, concluding that a very large barren cage is better than a small cage with perches and a nest box. Please write a procedure the committee can actually run on a proposed ladder, turning the question of whether better-than is transitive into an ordered set of steps: what must be true of each rung for it to transmit betterness to the next, how to treat a rung that is an improvement too small to be confident about, when to break the chain rather than accept its conclusion, how to record the point at which we have simply posited a threshold — a level of confinement below which extra space stops compensating — and where the procedure must stop and put the question to a recorded vote on values. Assume no cardinal welfare scale we trust, a two-hour meeting, members with no philosophy training, and a standard we publish and producers can appeal. Run it on two ladders: one varying only space per hen, one varying the age at which dairy calves are separated from the dam. Include the case where it forces a verdict I will not like, and say plainly what the procedure cannot decide.
