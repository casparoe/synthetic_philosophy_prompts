Construct a thought experiment for a graduate seminar on the limits of aggregation. Its purpose is to isolate a variable that the textbook utility-monster case leaves confounded, so that a reader cannot wave the case away by rejecting any single premise.

In the textbook version, one being at once (a) converts any contested unit of resource into vastly more welfare than anyone else does, and (b) has its welfare counted on a par with everyone else's in the sum. Those two premises travel together, which is exactly why the case is so easy to dismiss. I want a case in which both (a) and (b) are stipulated true, and yet the being's claim to the contested resource still comes out false under an independently motivated rule — so that the verdict has to turn on some third feature, which I want you to name and defend as the load-bearing one.

Constraints: keep it realistic (a hospital ward, a workplace, a family, or a public budget — not a science-fiction setting); the being must be a recognizable person or group of persons, not an exotic alien; the rule that defeats the claim must be one you would endorse independently of this case, not one reverse-engineered to yield the verdict; and the disputed resource must be the sort of thing that rule actually governs in ordinary life.

Then do the bookkeeping. Give the scenario in one paragraph. For at least three aggregation rules — hedonistic total utilitarianism, prioritarianism with diminishing marginal value, and one further rule of your choosing — state what each delivers and which premise of the case it is responding to, making sure they do not all land on the same verdict. Say which single feature of the case is doing the work, whether removing that feature flips the verdict back, and how a defender of the rule that fares worst would try to defuse the case.

Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence: state whether the case succeeds in isolating the variable, then supply the reasoning. No preamble, no restatement of the question.
