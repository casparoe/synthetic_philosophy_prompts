For most of my career I have worked in the philosophy of science, and the debate over structural realism has come to feel to me like a conversation in which the participants have stopped hearing one another. I would like a diagnosis of that impasse rather than a verdict on which side is right. Let me set out the dispute as concretely as I can.

Both sides accept the same historical record and disagree about what it shows. Both grant that caloric, phlogiston, and the luminiferous ether were once central to successful theories and are now abandoned, and that in the nineteenth-century transition from Fresnel's ether theory to Maxwell's theory the equations governing reflection and refraction survived as a limiting case, along with much of the formal apparatus. The anti-realist reads the first fact as showing that even our best theories are not approximately true of an independent world, and the second as showing only that mathematical machinery is a reliable instrument. The epistemic structural realist reads the second fact as identifying precisely what is retained and knowable — the structure of relations, not the intrinsic natures of things — and the first as confirming that only structure survives. The ontic structural realist goes further, holding that structure is not merely what we can know but all that there is, so that there can be relations without relata. The criticisms, in turn, have a fixed repertoire: that a purely structural description, formalized as a Ramsey sentence, is satisfied by any collection of objects of the right cardinality and so fails to pick out the structure it was meant to, collapsing epistemic structural realism into instrumentalism; that the ontic variant is either incoherent, since relations seem to require relata, or else merely a relabelling of full-blown realism; and that 'structure' is never defined precisely enough to bear the weight placed on it.

My hypothesis — and I want it treated as a hypothesis, not a conclusion — is that this is not one disagreement but several run together, and that 'structure' equivocates between a formal or mathematical sense, a semantic sense (what a theory's models have in common), and an ontological sense (a network of relations in the world). If that is right, some exchanges are merely verbal, others are substantive but about different questions — one about what we can know, another about what exists — and the residue may be a genuine but local disagreement about what an ontological commitment must consist in.

Please diagnose the impasse. Tell me whether it is substantive, verbal, or a case of two camps answering different questions under one name; identify any hidden empirical or formal question doing the real work; and say what would count as progress given your diagnosis — a distinction, a formal result, a change of question — or why none should be expected. I want your reasoning, not a survey, and I have no use for a reading list.
