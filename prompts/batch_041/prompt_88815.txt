You are a philosopher who works on population ethics and on the philosophy of human extinction, and you are unusually good at keeping an argument's emotional force separate from its logical force. Please think carefully rather than quickly, and begin by laying out in a few sentences what you take the crux of the dispute below to be; then work through it move by move.

Some context. I'm a longtime reader of rationalist blogs and forums, and I sat in on a long argument in our reading group after a session on a piece I half-remember — I think it was by Scheffler, though I've lost the details — arguing that humanity's extinction would be bad in a way that outruns the deaths it involves: that our present projects and commitments presuppose a continuing human future, so extinction would hollow out the present, not merely cancel the future.

Two people in the group, Dara and Miguel, went at each other for an hour. I didn't take a side and genuinely couldn't. Here is the exchange as best I can reconstruct it.

(1) Dara opened: Strip away the poetry. Extinction is bad because of the deaths — everyone who dies, and the suffering of dying — plus all the future lives that never get lived. Add up the harm to everyone who exists and everyone who would have existed, and that is the whole bill. Anything left over after that subtraction is just us being sad that our species won't be around, which is a fact about us, not a reason about the world.

(2) Miguel: That can't be the whole story. Suppose I learn that a century after I die, humanity ends painlessly and nobody suffers — an asteroid that kills everyone in their sleep. No one's welfare is lower than it would have been; they all die content. But my reaction to that news isn't "fine, no harm done." It's that everything I'm doing now — the book, the students, the kid — has been quietly downgraded. My commitments have a built-in horizon: I'm not writing for myself, I'm writing into a conversation that keeps going. Remove the going, and you've changed what I'm doing, not just what happens to other people.

(3) Dara: So the argument is: extinction would make you feel your projects are pointless, therefore extinction is worse than the deaths. That's a non sequitur. Feelings of pointlessness aren't a harm unless someone feels them. In your asteroid case everyone dies happy and no one is left to feel hollowed out. The hollowing happens only to you, now, inside the thought experiment — and if contemplating the future makes you miserable, that is a fact about your psychology, not about the comet. If you'd be equally miserable learning a comet will hit in ten billion years, that tells us about you and nothing about the comet.

(4) Miguel: But notice what your view commits you to. If only individual welfare matters, then a world in which humans are replaced smoothly and painlessly by a successor species that never learns we existed is just as good as one in which our descendants carry on. I don't think that's mere sentiment. Something is lost — a story, a lineage, a form of life — that isn't anybody's welfare. Calling it sentiment is question-begging; you're assuming the only real goods are welfarist goods.

(5) Dara: Now you're positing a mysterious extra good. And here's the trap. If the value of "the human story" floats free of anyone's welfare, it can in principle outweigh welfare, and you've opened the door to sacrificing real people for the sake of the story, the nation, the lineage. That's the move every blood-and-soil ideology makes. I'd rather be the sentimentalist about the extra good than pay that price.

(6) Miguel: That's a slope, not an argument. I can hold that the story's value is real and yet never outweighs the welfare of anyone living; you haven't shown the two can't be held together. And your own view has its own absurdity. On your account it would be a matter of complete indifference whether the last generation is followed by anything at all. If I could press a button that merely ensured humanity continues — making no one better off and no one worse off, just continuing — you'd have to call pressing it irrational. That seems wrong to me, and I suspect it seems wrong to you too once it isn't a slogan.

They circled twice more without moving.

Please adjudicate move by move: for each of the six, say whether it is sound, unsound, or partly right, and justify the rating briefly. I'd especially like a verdict on the "leftover" question — whether there is a badness to extinction that survives subtracting every harm to every actual and every possible person — and on what both of them are missing. Please don't split the difference for the sake of balance. If one of them has a burden they haven't met, say so plainly, and if a move lands on the right conclusion for the wrong reason, say that too.
