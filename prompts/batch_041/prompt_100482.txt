I retired from the lab a couple years ago and have been working my way into philosophy of religion, and the fine-tuning argument keeps leaning on the same picture: you're standing in front of a firing squad of fifty rifles, they all fire, and every shot misses. You wouldn't call that luck — you'd figure somebody meant for you to be alive. Now swap the squad for the constants of nature: the odds of them landing in the life-permitting window are so absurdly small that 'we just got lucky' is supposed to be like saying the squad missed by chance. I want an audit of that analogy, since it seems to be doing most of the work. Give me the correspondence table first — what plays the squad, the volley, the misses, the surviving, the 'someone meant it'. Then tell me where the mapping breaks. A few things nag at me: I've seen firing squads hit what they aim at, and I've never seen another universe; and there was nobody around to be surprised in the universes that fizzled. Are those fatal to the analogy or just scenery? Also, when people answer 'no design, just a multiverse and we're in the lucky branch', does the firing-squad picture help that story or quietly undercut it? And finally: does the conclusion the analogy was wheeled in to support — that chance alone won't do and something else is going on — actually survive once the disanalogies are on the table, or was the analogy carrying the conclusion all along? If you think there's a better picture for the situation, tell me what it is. (Sory for the ramble — old habit from writing grant reports.)
