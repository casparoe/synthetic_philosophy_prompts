For a graduate seminar I'm running on historiography and memory, next week's session pairs the two great theoretical flashpoints of the 1980s — the Historikerstreit and Zygmunt Bauman's argument in Modernity and the Holocaust — with Art Spiegelman's Maus, and I'd like you to give me the reading I'll build the discussion around. My students will have read both volumes of Maus and know the debate in outline: on one side the claim that the Nazi genocide is singular and resists assimilation to the general history of mass atrocity; on the other the insistence that it can and should be compared, and, in Bauman's version, explained as a sober product of modernity itself — of bureaucratic rationalization, the division of labour, and the moral distance that institutional machinery opens between those who act and those who are acted upon — rather than as a reversion to premodern barbarism. I don't want a summary of either position, and I don't want Maus used as a mere illustration of them; I want you to read the book against both, taking seriously that it is a book and not a case study.

Two features of the work seem to me to put pressure on both positions, and I'd like you to say what you make of them. First, the animal scheme — Jews drawn as mice, Germans as cats, Poles as pigs — is itself a gesture of comparison, one that maps whole peoples onto a common grid of predator and prey; does that formal decision sit comfortably with a claim of uniqueness, or does the book's own grammar commit it to comparability, and does Spiegelman register the difficulty or leave it unexamined? Second, the frame tale, in which the middle-aged cartoonist interrogates his ageing father in Queens and turns the interviews into the book you are reading, keeps intruding on the historical narrative: the camps are interrupted by present-day quarrels, by the father's ordinary cruelties and frugalities, by the inset comic about the mother's suicide and by the father's destruction of her written account of Auschwitz. That reflexivity might be read as a dramatization of Bauman's point about the ordinariness of the perpetrators and the bureaucratized distance of the killing, or as a device that pulls the book away from the modernity thesis altogether, insisting on contingency, personal resourcefulness, and a suffering that no structural account captures. Which reading does the text support, and where does it resist the one you favour?

Close by telling me what Maus adds to this debate that the arguments alone cannot — what it makes visible or felt that the Historikerstreit and Bauman's thesis leave out — and name two or three specific moments I should put in front of the seminar to start the argument. Please keep it under 900 words, and don't quote the book at length; I'm after the argument, not the passages.
