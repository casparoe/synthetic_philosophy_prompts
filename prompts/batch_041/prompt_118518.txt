Last weekend a cousin of mine visited from a country where tipping is neither expected nor particularly welcome, and over dinner a friend of mine spent a good twenty minutes insisting that he had been morally in the wrong to leave nothing on the table, wrong again to drift into the middle of a queue at the airport rather than join the back, and wrong a third time to cross against the pedestrian signal on our walk home — not merely ill-advised or rude, she kept saying, but wrong, the kind of thing one could legitimately resent him for. When I asked why any of that was a matter of morality rather than of manners or prudence, she produced the proverb 'When in Rome, do as the Romans do' and treated it as settling the question. I came away suspecting both that the proverb is doing quite different work in the three cases she lumped together, and that in at least one of them it is doing more work than it can bear.

What I would like from you is a disambiguation, not a verdict on my friend. Take that single sentence and lay out the distinct precise theses it could be expressing, arranged as a menu that runs from the nearly vacuous to the genuinely radical: for instance, the prudential reading on which it merely warns that nonconformity invites friction; the descriptive reading on which it reports what counts as polite in a given place; the jurisdictional reading on which it says that the norms applicable to an act are fixed by where the act occurs rather than by the agent's home norms; the conventionalist reading on which the local practice itself constitutes the standard of correctness for the act-type; the reciprocity reading on which the visitor owes his hosts a debt of conformity; the reading on which the visitor is bound only where the local convention happens to track an independent moral reason that would bind him anywhere; and the pragmatic reading on which the sentence is not a claim at all but a way of declining to argue. For each reading, say whether it is a platitude, a substantive but defensible thesis, or something false or incoherent, and — the point I actually care about — say whether that reading, if true, would get my friend her conclusion that my cousin was morally in the wrong. Then tell me which reading she was most likely invoking, and which of the three cases (tipping, queueing, jaywalking) the proverb genuinely supports and where it breaks down, distinguishing in each case between what the local convention requires and what morality independently requires.

Please keep the whole reply under 650 words: a numbered list of the readings, each with a one-line verdict and a one-line note on whether it supports her conclusion, followed by a short closing paragraph identifying the reading she probably meant and the cases it does and does not cover. I don't need a survey of the anthropology of tipping, and you needn't assume I share her view — I want the menu and the verdicts, tight enough to bring back to the argument.
