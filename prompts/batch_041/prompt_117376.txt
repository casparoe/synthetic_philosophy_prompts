You are a philosopher of history and social ontology who specializes in how moral norms rise and fall, and who can tell a real causal explanation apart from a just-so story. I want a diagnosis of an impasse, not a survey of the literature.

In my seminar on moral revolutions we keep covering the same ground without converging. Three families of explanation are on the table for the fall of practices like slavery, dueling, and footbinding, and each can point to cases the others seem unable to absorb. The materialist family (the Williams thesis) says such practices died when the interests sustaining them collapsed. The honor family (in Appiah's vein) says they died when they became dishonorable for the elites who kept them alive. The argument family says they died because someone finally made the moral case well enough to be heard. At this point the disagreement isn't really about the evidence; it has the texture of people talking past one another.

My hypothesis, which I'd like you to test rather than assume: the camps are answering different questions under a single word. "End" conflates a practice's *cessation* (people stop doing it) with its *delegitimation* (people stop believing it needs a defense), and these need not coincide or share a cause. On top of that, interests and honor may be *selection* mechanisms that determine which practices survive, while arguments are *legitimation* mechanisms that determine which already-dead practices get retrospectively justified.

Diagnose the dispute. Is it a substantive causal disagreement, a verbal one about the sense of "ending," or three parties answering three different questions under one heading? Is my selection/legitimation split doing real work, or does it merely relabel the positions? Say what each side is actually committed to, identify which concrete cases would genuinely discriminate among the stories, and say whether any form of progress is available—a distinction, a decisive case, a better-posed question—or whether the impasse is permanent and why. Begin by laying out the candidate diagnoses before you commit to one, reason step by step, and flag your uncertainties rather than smoothing them over.
