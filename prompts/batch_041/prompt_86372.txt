I'm a doctoral student in science and technology studies and the graduate representative on my university's task force on artificial intelligence. The task force's draft report leans hard on the printing press: the press, it says, flooded Europe with destabilizing material, the resulting upheaval took about a century to work itself out, and what ultimately settled it was unplanned institutional adaptation — copyright, licensing regimes, an emergent public sphere — rather than any deliberate attempt to steer the technology at the outset. From this the report draws a practical conclusion: the university should not try to shape how AI develops, but should build what it calls "absorptive capacity" and wait for institutions to catch up. It also claims the press analogy beats the nuclear analogy, on the ground that AI resembles print — a diffuse, general-purpose technology — rather than a point-source weapon, and so cannot be governed by anything like arms control.

I think the analogy is doing more work than it can bear, but I can't yet say where. Please audit it: lay out what maps onto what; identify the features of the printing-press case the report is quietly importing (what actually did the settling, and how the timing of harms compared with the timing of remedies); say where the mapping breaks for AI; and judge whether the report's hands-off conclusion survives once the disanalogies are on the table. If it doesn't, tell me what the analogy can still legitimately support, and whether a better comparison — nuclear, industrial, or something else — would serve the task force's actual purpose.
