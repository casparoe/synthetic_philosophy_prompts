ok, so I'm a PhD student working on philosophy of biology, and I've been circling the species problem for about a year and a half now, and I've started to suspect the reason I can't get anywhere is that there isn't one problem — there's several wearing the same coat. I don't want you to take a side or tell me which species concept is right. I want you to diagnose the disagreement itself.

Here's the fight as I see it. On one side: people who think there's a fact of the matter about which groups of organisms are species — species are lineages, or gene pools, or the tips of the tree of life, or whatever — and we just haven't landed the right concept yet. On the other: pluralists, who say different biological processes carve life up in different, incompatible ways, so no single concept can be right, and we should use whichever one suits the job at hand. And then a third crowd saying species aren't kinds at all but individuals — particular historical entities, organisms writ large — which is meant to dissolve a lot of the fuss. Nobody in this argument is surprised by anything anyone else says anymore. It's been decades. What gets me is how little of it is about the organisms: when I actually talk to these people, they agree on the biology down to the last messy case — ring species, asexuals, bacteria passing genes sideways — and the disagreement is entirely downstream of facts they share. And yet its ferocious and it doesn't budge.

My hunch, and please stress-test it rather than nod: it's a pile-up of different questions under one name. One person is asking what kind of thing a species is — furniture-of-the-world metaphysics. Another is asking what we should count when we run a biodiversity survey or write a conservation law — a question about our purposes. A third is asking whether 'species' earns its keep in evolutionary explanation. All three get published as answers to the same question, so they look like they're contradicting each other when they may just be talking past each other. I know the standard move is to split it up — the category vs. particular taxa, which groups are species vs. what makes them species — but that never seems to make the fight go away, it just relocates it, and I want to know why.

Where I stop trusting my own hunch: some of it does look substantive. The realist and the pluralist seem to genuinely disagree about whether the living world has the kind of structure that makes one correct taxonomy possible at all, and that doesn't sound verbal to me. So — what kind of disagreement is this, actually? Which parts are verbal, which are about facts, which are about purposes or standards, and which are bedrock intuitions neither side can argue for? Is there a hidden question doing the load-bearing work — kinds vs. individuals, say, or whether a concept that's good for explaining has to be the same one that's good for cataloguing? Given your diagnosis, what would progress look like: a distinction, a decision to split the question, an empirical result, a verdict that one side was answering the wrong question all along, or a case that the impasse is permanent and why? And tell me which version of the question I should actually be working on, because right now I can't tell if my thesis is about the world or about our vocabulary, and I have to pick one.
