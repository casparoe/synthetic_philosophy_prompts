I'm a journalist at work on a long feature about how we judge the past — the arguments over monuments, curricula, and the language historians use — and before I file I want to get one conceptual knot straight, because I keep watching intelligent people talk past each other on it. The knot is this. Almost everyone grants that we should not read the past through present-day categories, and almost everyone, pressed, also grants that no historian can wholly avoid doing so. What I cannot make out is what is really being asserted on each side, and whether the demand to escape the standards of one's own time is even a coherent one. Please give me a careful conceptual explanation rather than a survey of opinions.

Begin by sorting the several different things that travel under the label of presentism: the anachronistic imposition of present-day moral verdicts; the use of present-day concepts as analytical instruments for understanding a past that did not possess them; and the present-driven selection of which past events are deemed worth explaining at all. Tell me which of these the objection is actually aimed at, and why the others are so often confused with it. Then set out the strongest version of the reply — that a historian's standards of evidence, relevance, and intelligibility are themselves historically conditioned, so that the instruction to transcend them is either empty or quietly self-defeating — and tell me how much of the original objection survives it.

There is a further wrinkle I would like you to address directly, because it seems to undercut one side of the argument. The prohibition on judging the past by present standards is itself a present-day standard, and it is offered as a rule we should now adopt; the anti-presentist appears to exempt himself from his own injunction. Is that a genuine contradiction, a harmless reflexivity, or something that can be dissolved by restating the position more carefully? I would like to know which.

Finally, I want your judgment on whether there is a defensible middle position here, or whether we are stuck between an unattainable neutrality and a presentism so complete that it dissolves the very object the historian is supposed to be studying. Keep it concrete — a historical case or two will help — and make clear where the real philosophical difficulty lies, as distinct from where the rhetorical heat is.
