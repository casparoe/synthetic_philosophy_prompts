Somewhere along the way I've ended up a Carnapian about politics and planning, and I'm not sure I should be. The position, as I'd state it: the choice of a framework — a language, a constitution, an economic order — is a practical decision rather than a theoretical one, so it isn't true or false, only more or less useful given the ends you already have (same move as the tolerance idea, where in logic we lay down conventions rather than prohibitions). Once the framework is fixed, the questions inside it are perfectly objective: whether the plan gets the steel to the right place, whether the policy raises output, whether the institution does what it says on the tin. And disputes about ultimate ends aren't disagreements in belief at all — they're expressions of attitude. So a fascist and a socialist could in principle agree completely about the engineering and the means, and what separates them would be nothing either of them could be right or wrong about.

What counts against this? Not the standard "but values feel objective" grumble — I want the defeaters that should actually move me, ranked, with the decisive ones separated from the merely awkward. Things I'm already worried about, which I'd like you to take seriously rather than wave at:

1. The means/ends split. Is there a case where posing a means-ends question already settles something about the end, so that "what the end is" is itself something we can get right or wrong? If so, is that fatal, or just proof that the split is a matter of degree?
2. The calculation debate. Does the history of planned economies count against any of this? My instinct is no — that's an argument about dispersed information, i.e. a feasibility problem, not a problem with the framework/practice distinction. Tell me if I'm wrong to quarentine it like that.
3. Tolerance. If framework choice is conventional and ends are non-cognitive, what exactly is supposed to rule out the Nazi framework? "Because we don't like it" sounds like a concession that the view has nothing to say here. Or maybe that's just what the view says and it isn't a defeater at all. I want a straight verdict on which way that cuts.
4. Whether it risks anything. If every political arrangement can be absorbed as "a different practical decision", is the position unfalsifiable — and if it is, is that a problem for a view of this kind, or am I asking the wrong sort of question?

And tell me which of these a sympathetic Carnapian would concede as genuinely counting against them, as opposed to dissmissing. I'd be most moved by anything showing that a technical question can't even be posed without settling a value question, or by a case where the ends themselves turn out to be something we can be wrong about. If it turns out the position is well insulated, say so plainly and tell me whether that's a strength or an evasion. I don't much care whether this is the real historical Carnap — if I'm misreading him, note it in passing and move on.
