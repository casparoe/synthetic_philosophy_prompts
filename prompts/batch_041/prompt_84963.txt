I retired last year after thirty-odd years as an experimental physicist — instrument work, measurement, a lot of arguing about error bars — and I've spent the time since trying to catch up on philosophy, mostly philosophy of probability. The bit I've latched onto is Keynes: the idea that a probability isn't a fact about the world and isn't just a feeling, it's a relation between a body of evidence and a conclusion, so that the same proposition can have different probabilities for two people and neither is being sloppy. And then the thing he adds on top, which I've been groping for my whole career without having a word for it: the weight of the argument, the amount of relevant evidence behind the judgement, which can go up even when the probability goes down. So here are the verdicts I actually find myself holding. I'd like to know what principle, or small set of principles, they add up to.

1. Two reports on the same question. One gives me 0.62 with a tight interval, off a huge dataset and a model I don't believe. The other gives me a vaguer range from less data, on assumptions I do believe. I take the second more seriously. The sharp number isn't more informative, it's just more precise about something I don't care about.

2. I'll happily put a number on a well-characterized physical process coming out one way. I won't put a number on whether a particular country has a coup in the next five years — not because I'm especially ignorant, but because I don't think my ignorance there has a size. And I refuse "so it's 50/50, then" as anything but a joke. Though I notice I do act on such things, and act differently depending on how they feel, which suggests I'm smuggling in some kind of comparison after all.

3. When an independent lab replicates a result, the number doesn't move but my position does. I want to say the probability stayed where it was and something else improved. That something else is the weight.

4. I don't think how likely it is that a coin is biased and how likely it is that a drug works in a given patient sit on a common scale. Within a question I can rank; across questions I can't. But if two uncertainties genuinely can't be compared at all, I don't see how I choose between two actions, and I do choose, every day.

5. A theory flexible enough to accommodate almost any observation can be highly probable on the evidence I've got and still worth nothing — low weight, I'd say. And I want to say that's a defect in the support the theory has, not just a fact about my psychology, which I can't make work if probability is a relation and weight is just amount of evidence.

6. And the one I keep going back and forth on: whether to run the experiment again, or take an umbrella, when the evidence genuinely doesn't favour either option — I think it's sometimes rational to just pick, and even rational to pick capriciously, but I also think if it's rational then there must be a reason, and if there's a reason then the evidence did favour something.

My own guess at a principle is something like: probability is only defined where I've got a model, and weight is whatever's left over — but that makes weight unmeasurable, and it makes my confidence in my own instruments a mystery, which I don't like. So: is there a coherent account that gets all six, or close? Say precisely what it says, tell me which of my verdicts it forces me to drop (I suspect it's one I'm attached to), give me a case I haven't listed where it tells me something I won't like, and be straight with me about which of the six it simply can't accommodate.
