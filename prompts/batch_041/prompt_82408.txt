Eleven months ago I found a lump and did nothing about it — not as a decision, since I never decided anything; I simply kept my evenings full, and the appointment kept not getting made, and when I finally went and the result was benign I could not say what I had been doing all that time, except that I do not think I ever believed I was fine. A friend tells me this shows self-deception to be a fiction: to deceive someone you must know the truth, to be deceived you must not, and no one can hold both positions at once. I think she has the paradox right and the conclusion wrong. Self-deception need not be a state of holding two contradictory beliefs; it can be work done upstream, in what one allows oneself to notice and how long one leaves a question open, and in that version there is no moment at which the lie is told. Please write me a pantoum about it: four quatrains in which lines two and four of each stanza return as lines one and three of the next, and the closing quatrain brings back the two opening lines not yet reused, so that the poem ends on the sentence it began with. The recurring lines should be the small, ordinary things I actually said to myself, the ones that sound innocent and must go on sounding innocent while becoming something else without a syllable changing. If it works, no reader should be able to say whether the speaker is the deceiver or the deceived, and the poem should end flat, on the line I used to buy myself another week, with no moral attached.
