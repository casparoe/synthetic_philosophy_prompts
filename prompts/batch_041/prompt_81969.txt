I am in the middle of a graduate seminar on confirmation and induction, and I have to hand in a short position paper next week. The seminar has already chewed over the problem of old evidence in the usual way, so I am trying to write something that does not simply restate the puzzle. I would like your comments on the draft below. My audience is comfortable with the formalism, so I have kept the exposition of the puzzle compressed and spent the space on the argument. I am not looking for a literature review or a reading list; I want to know whether the argument holds up.

Draft:

Bayesian confirmation is normally stated as a relation between a hypothesis H, evidence E, and a credence function P: E confirms H for an agent just in case P(H given E) exceeds P(H). The familiar difficulty is that when E is already known, P(E) equals 1, and conditioning on a certainty does nothing, so P(H given E) equals P(H). Yet we plainly treat theories as supported by the very facts they were constructed to fit. The standard fix in the material I have read is to say that what does the work is not E itself but the discovery that H entails E, since conditionalizing on that discovery can raise the probability of H and the puzzle appears to dissolve. I want to argue that this fix is unstable, and to replace it with an explicitly agent-relative account of confirmation.

The instability is this. Either the discovery is treated as a piece of evidence in its own right, in which case we need a prior over discoveries, and that prior is exactly as mysterious as the thing we were trying to explain; or it is treated as a psychological event that merely licenses a change of credence, in which case the account has quietly abandoned the idea that confirmation is a relation in which an agent stands to a hypothesis and a body of data, and has become a story about when agents are permitted to change their minds. Neither horn is attractive, and I do not see how the fix can choose between them without further help.

My proposal is to stop treating novelty as a property of evidence and to treat it instead as a relation between evidence and the agent's actual path. Define the confirmation relation for an agent not on her current credence function alone but on a pair: her current credences together with the class of credence functions she could have had, given the inquiries she actually conducted. E confirms H for her, roughly, when conditioning on E raises the probability of H above the level H would have had in the nearest counterfactually available credence function in which E was not yet in hand. On this account no evidence is ever old; what varies is how much of the probability of H is inherited from E rather than earned elsewhere. I call what remains the accommodation residue: the portion of the posterior that is due to E having been used to build H rather than to test it.

Two consequences follow. First, confirmation becomes agent-relative in a strong sense: two agents with identical current credences can stand in different confirmation relations to the same hypothesis and the same evidence if they arrived by different routes. I take this to be correct rather than embarrassing, since it is the same fact that makes an accusation of circularity apt in one case and idle in another. Second, the account predicts that the old-evidence problem should be most acute exactly where a hypothesis left itself many free choices that the data then constrained, that is, where the residue is large, and should vanish for a hypothesis fixed in advance of the data. I think this matches how we actually apportion credit for a fit that was engineered rather than anticipated.

Please give me pointed comments rather than encouragement. In particular: is the instability argument fair, or can the standard fix be reformulated so that it escapes both horns, for instance by treating the discovery of the entailment as a fact about the agent's language rather than as evidence? If it can, does my proposal differ from that reformulation in substance, or have I only relabelled it? The agent-relativity is the part I trust least: can you construct a case in which two agents with the same current credences but different histories obviously ought to be treated alike, and if so, does that sink the account? Does the accommodation residue do independent explanatory work, or is it a name for the phenomenon rather than an account of it, and if the latter, how would you make it measurable in a toy case, say one in which a hypothesis was formulated after seeing half the data and then applied to the other half? Finally, tell me where the essay is weakest and what the strongest objection to it is; if you think the whole framing is misguided, say what you would put in its place.
