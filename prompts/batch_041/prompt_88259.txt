I'm a third-year epi PhD student, so basically my whole world is causal inference — DAGs, do-operators, potential outcomes, exchangeability, positivity, consistency, target trial emulation, that whole toolkit. When my lab says X causes Y we mean something like: there's a well-defined intervention that sets X, and if we could randomize it the outcome distribution would shift, and here's whether we can identify that shift from the observational data we've got. Anyway, I ended up next to a philosopher at a wedding last month who works on causation, and he spent most of the reception explaining to me that all of this is just a special case of the counterfactual theory of causation — the David Lewis possible-worlds thing, where A causes B if, very roughly, in the closest possible world where A doesn't happen B doesn't happen either, and the closest world is one that's identical to ours right up to just before the event and then diverges with a tiny local violation of the laws of nature. I nodded a lot. But I have a nagging feeling that a translation of his picture into my vocabulary would either be basically trivial or would quietly lose the entire point, and I can't tell weather it's the first or the second. (I have zero philosophy training, for what it's worth — I read half of a philosophy of science paper once and gave up.)

So please do the translation properly, as faithfully as you can, into the vocabulary I actually use: interventions, do(X=x), potential outcomes Y^x, exchangeability/ignorability, consistency, positivity, SUTVA, target trials and their protocol components, identification, estimands. I want an honest mapping, not a vibe — don't just say it's basically the do-operator and move on. Then give me the residue report: which parts of his picture carry over cleanly, which only carry over with distortion, and which have no counterpart at all in my framework.

Three places I especially want you to press, because I suspect that's where it comes unstuck. First, preemption — we spend a lot of time on competing risks: someone dies of the thing we're studying, but he was going to die of something else within the year anyway, so if you'd removed our exposure he'd still have died. My friend says his view has a famous hard time with the rock-throwing version of that case, but in my world we'd still quite happily report that the exposure caused the death, just with a different estimand, and I want to know if that's a real disagreement or us talking past each other. Second, exposures you can't intervene on — race, sex, age, SES — where my field gets visibly nervous about saying causes at all, and I want to know whether his framework handles those better or just relocates the weirdness (a closest world where I'm a different race?).
