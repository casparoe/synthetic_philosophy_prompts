I am a working software engineer with no formal training in philosophy or probability theory beyond what the job has forced on me, so I would like a careful audit of the piece rather than a general introduction to the subject.

The explainer runs roughly as follows. Take a circle and draw a chord at random: what is the probability that the chord is longer than the side of the inscribed equilateral triangle? If you pick two random points on the circumference, you get 1/3; if you pick a random radius and then a random point along it, you get 1/2; if you pick a random point in the disk to serve as the midpoint, you get 1/4. Because the principle of indifference tells us to assign equal probability to possibilities we have no reason to distinguish, and it here delivers three incompatible answers, the paradox refutes the principle of indifference and shows that mathematical probability is at bottom subjective — all three answers are equally defensible, and the correct one is simply whatever prior you happen to hold. The explainer adds that this is a curiosity rather than a real difficulty, because in any genuine application the physics determines which chord-selection procedure is in play, and that procedure is the random-radius one, so the answer is 1/2. It closes by noting that Jaynes later settled the matter by maximising entropy, which shows the principle of indifference was sound all along once it is applied properly.

Please tell me which parts of this I should trust. Work through the claims and say what a probabilist or a philosopher of probability would accept, what they would regard as a misleading simplification, and what is simply wrong; and then say whether the explainer's larger conclusions — that the principle of indifference is refuted, that the answer is subjective, and that Jaynes resolved the problem — survive once the accurate parts are separated out.
