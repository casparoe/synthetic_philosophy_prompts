I'm the philsophy-of-science guy in my friend group, which is why my friend — she runs a tiny newsletter that's all deadpan finance parody, fake fund letters, mock analyst notes, that kind of thing — has asked me to write the year-end piece for her. So: draft it. A quarterly letter to limited partners from a fictional investment firm whose entire thesis is that a certain famous long-run prediction about population and subsistence was right, just early. The firm has been waiting since the late 18th century, its founder was a country clergyman, and it's now run by a descendant who is preternaturally calm about all of this. Around 700 words, in the flat, self-congratulatory register these letters actually use: "Dear Partners," a thesis paragraph, a section along the lines of "Why We Are Early Rather Than Wrong," a table of past predictions with the original horizon in one column and the revised horizon in the next (give it at least four rows, including one from the 1960s), a risk-factors list, and a short memorial note about the founder. And a disclosure footnote at the end.

The comedy has to come out of the epistemics, not the finance. Someone who's read Popper and Lakatos should be able to point at each paragraph and name the move the letter is making — the auxiliary hypothesis that saves the core, the prediction that can only ever be "premature," the horizon that gets rewritten rather than abandoned — while the letter itself never uses the words falsifiable, ad hoc, immunizing, or unfalsifiable. Meanwhile someone who's never heard of any of that but vaguely knows the famine never came should still laugh, so don't lean on jargon or on a history lecture; the past is only in the letter so it can be re-dated.

A few specifics so it doesn't come out generic. The flagship product should be some kind of perpetual note that matures every 25 years and automatically rolls over unless the prediction has come true in the meantime, and the letter should be genuinely proud of that clause — treating the rollover as a mark of intellectual seriousness, maybe pitching a "patience premium" to investors who accept the longer lock-up. It has to confront the obvious objection, that the prediction has had two centuries and hasn't landed, and deal with it by converting the objection into evidence of the firm's patience; that's the heart of the piece, so give it real room. There's a rival school it's snide about, the people who think technology keeps outrunning the constraint, but the snideness stays in-house — they're "short-termists," they "mistake a trend for a law" — rather than turning into an actual argument. Don't quote anyone real, and if you want to wave at the famous line about how in the long run we're all dead, paraphrase it rather than attribute it; I don't want a misattributed epigraph sitting inside a fake fund letter. End with the footnote doing the quiet part out loud — that the fund benchmarks itself against a horizon it sets itself. Deadpan throughout, no winking, no narrator stepping outside the letter. (Sorry this got long, I've been in the weeds on it all week.)
