For about a year now I have been reading about automated decision systems — the kind that decides which patient gets the last bed, which driver gets the next delivery slot, which applicant gets a second look — and I keep running into a claim I cannot get comfortable with: that when one of these systems causes serious harm, there may be no one who is at fault. My instinct is that this is usually a polite way of letting people off the hook, so I want you to build me a case where it is not, and to build it to my specifications rather than reaching for the usual illustrations. I want a realistic contemporary scenario, no more than one paragraph long, with no science fiction, no rogue machine, and no villain or reckless actor anywhere in it; please stay away from self-driving cars, which I have read about to death. The harm must be serious and must fall on one identifiable person to whom the organisation plainly owed something — a patient, a tenant, a passenger — so that no one being at fault cannot be purchased cheaply by there being no duty in the first place. It must arise from the system doing the job it was deployed to do, not from a component failure, a bug any competent engineer would have caught, or sheer randomness, and it must be a kind of harm the designers knew systems of this sort sometimes cause, even though nobody could have foreseen this particular instance of it.

The part I care most about is where the gap comes from. I do not want it to come from the machine being opaque: the system should be fully documented and its behaviour explicable after the fact, and every human in the chain should have made a decision that was defensible on the information available to them. The gap, if there is one, has to come from the way the work was divided up and the individual choices composed, not from a black box.

Once you have the scenario, do four things. First, show that the case earns the claim that nobody is at fault rather than stipulating it, by naming the candidate defendants one at a time and saying why each is off the hook. Second, run it against three ways people propose to close such gaps — strict liability on whoever operates the system, a duty of care on whoever built it, and collective responsibility on the team as a whole — and tell me for each whether it genuinely closes the gap or merely relocates it onto someone who is not at fault. Third, tell me which single feature of the case is doing the work, then remove exactly that one feature and show me fault snapping back into place. Finally, give me the strongest case a sceptic could make that the gap is an illusion, and say what you think survives of my scenario afterwards. If you find the case cannot be built without stipulating something rather than showing it, say so and tell me what would have to give.
