I retired three years ago from a career in experimental physics, and since then I have been working my way, slowly and somewhat idiosyncratically, through formal epistemology. I keep circling back to a contrast that I cannot fully get straight, and I would like you to compare the two positions for me in some depth. On one side stands the ordinary rule of updating: when you become certain that some proposition is true, you replace your prior credences with your prior credences conditional on that proposition. On the other side stands the more permissive rule for cases in which the evidence itself arrives only probabilistically — where, say, an instrument reading leaves you seventy per cent confident that a proposition holds rather than certain of it — and you update by taking a weighted average of your old credence in a hypothesis and your old credence conditional on the proposition, weighted by how confident you have now become in the proposition. I can manipulate both rules mechanically; what I cannot articulate is what genuinely separates them, and I would like a careful account of whether the second is a genuine extension of the first, a disguised special case of it, or something else altogether.

Several specific puzzles are lodged in my mind. First, the permissive rule appears to require that my conditional credences given the proposition and given its negation stay fixed across the update — an assumption I have seen called rigidity — and I want to know whether that assumption is innocent or whether it covertly does all the work. Second, I have been toying with a distinction of my own devising between uncertainty about the world and uncertainty about what my evidence actually is, and I suspect the two rules divide along that line; tell me whether that distinction is doing any real work or whether it collapses under scrutiny. Third, if I update in two small probabilistic steps rather than one, does the order of the steps matter, and can a sequence of small shifts end somewhere different from a single combined shift? Fourth, is the permissive rule really an independent principle, or can it always be reconstructed as ordinary conditioning once I enlarge the space of possibilities to include the evidence-gathering process itself?

To make this concrete, consider a small case. My prior credence in a hypothesis is one half. An experiment then leaves me nine-tenths confident in a proposition, and, holding the relevant conditional credences fixed, my credence in the hypothesis given the proposition is four-fifths while my credence given its negation is one-fifth. I can see that the ordinary rule, were the proposition certain, would move me to four-fifths, whereas the permissive rule moves me only to something lower; I would like you to work through what each rule prescribes, explain precisely why they diverge, and then reach and defend a verdict about which of the two is the more defensible account of how a rational agent ought to revise belief in the face of uncertain evidence. Please do not end on a non-committal note; I am looking for an argued position, even if it is a qualified one.
