I spent most of last night arguing with an old friend, a logician, about Skolem's paradox, and I am still not sure whether she won the argument or merely wore me down. We were at a bar near the university, and she kept sketching the model on a napkin: a countable universe, a set that the model calls the real numbers, and an arrow she labeled the missing bijection, drawn outside the box because the model itself cannot see it. Her line was that there is no paradox at all, just a fact about first-order model theory that people dress up as a scandal: if ZF has a model, it has a countable model, and yet that model can satisfy the sentence that says there is an uncountable set, because the bijection that would witness countability from the outside is not itself an element of the model. I kept saying that a countable model containing an uncountable set sounds like a contradiction no matter how you slice it, and she kept saying that uncountable is not an absolute property but a property relative to a model, and that the whole thing dissolves once you distinguish what is true inside the model from what is true about the model. We went around for hours. Now I want to see the mess turned into a satirical piece.

Write a deadpan Lonely Planet-style travel-guide entry, around 600 words, for a destination called Skolem's Paradox: a charming, countable model of set theory whose residents insist that their set of real numbers is uncountable, that their power set is strictly larger, and that the hotel is full even though it has countably many rooms. The humor should come from getting the technical details right: internal versus external countability, the missing bijection, the difference between a model's satisfaction of uncountability and the metatheoretic fact of countability, and the way the paradox dissolves once you see that uncountable is relative to the model. Include a sidebar on local customs, perhaps the annual festival of the missing bijection; a warning about trying to count the residents; and a closing note on whether the paradox is a genuine contradiction or just a tourist trap. The piece should be funny to a set theorist and legible to someone who only knows the rough idea, and it should aim its satire at the way non-logicians and careless logicians treat uncountable as an absolute property rather than a model-relative one.
