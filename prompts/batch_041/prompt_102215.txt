I'm a journalist who covers philosophy of mind and religion, and for a piece on what Buddhism actually says about whether you stay the same person over time, I've been sitting in on a seminar taught by a Buddhist philosopher — someone who works on the early Buddhist material but also reads a lot of contemporary analytic philosophy of personal identity. Her verdicts on a string of cases have been piling up and I can't quite reverse-engineer the rule she's running, so I want your help. Take the role of a philosopher of mind who knows both the Buddhist literature and the analytic work on personal identity, and please start by brainstorming a list of candidate principles before you commit to anything — I'm less interested in a label than in the actual machinery.

Here's what she's said so far. A man who over forty years goes from gentle to bitter and forgets most of his youth: still the same person, she says, just a long stream. A woman who comes out of a coma with big gaps in her memory and a changed temperament: still her, the stream wasn't broken. A body whose cells all get replaced one at a time over the years, Theseus-style: same person. A case where someone's body is destroyed and an exact copy, memories and all, is assembled on Mars a moment later: she's adamant that's a different person, the first one died. And when the same person is split into two beings, each carrying all her memories and traits: neither one is the original, she says; both are heirs, but not identical to her. She also likes to add, half-jokingly, that of course none of this shows there's a self — which is the part that keeps tripping me up.

The new case I want to run past her, and past you, is this. A woman has a degenerative condition and agrees to a one-shot procedure: her brain is kept biologically alive, but in a single operation every one of her memories and character traits is erased and replaced with those of a volunteer donor — whose own brain is then destroyed. So you end up with one living brain that used to be hers, now running the donor's mind, memories, and personality. Do you think she'll say the resulting person is the same as the woman who went in, the same as the donor, both, or neither? More than the verdict, I want to know which rule she seems to be runing — is it causal continuity of the physical stream, continuity of the psychological/narrative stream, plain conventional imputation where there's no fact of the matter and we just label, or the no-self reduction where the question gets dissolved — which of those her earlier answers actually pin down, which other rules fit her verdicts just as well, and what further case I should put to her that would tell the surviving candidates apart. If her earlier answers genuinely underdetermine the new case, say so, and tell me what she'd be committing herself to either way.
