I am a novelist, and I am perhaps two-thirds of the way through a book whose narrator insists from the opening page that he is entirely in possession of his own mind — I write him in the first person, so the reader has only his word to go on, and is meant to distrust it without ever being handed a flat contradiction. To get that voice right I went back to Poe's 'The Tell-Tale Heart,' which I had remembered as a story about guilt and found on rereading to be something closer to a stress test in epistemology. The narrator opens by denying that he is mad and then does the very thing that would settle the question: he reports his own mental condition with serene authority, separates his own clear-sightedness from the blindness of the mad, and seems to know that he knows these things about himself — while the whole effect of the story depends on our concluding that he is wrong about precisely this. It has put me in mind of two theses I half-remember from a course and would like to see tried against the story rather than merely recited: the luminosity thesis, that a mental state is luminous when being in it puts you in a position to know that you are in it; and the KK principle, that if you know something you are in a position to know that you know it. There is also, I think, an argument in the background which holds that no such state can be luminous, because knowing requires a margin of safety and there will always be a borderline case in which you are in the state but cannot tell; and I want to know whether Poe's narrator is meant to be that borderline case or something else.

Give me a reading of the story through this material, and make it a reading rather than a proof-text — I don't want the story reduced to an illustration of a thesis, nor the thesis bent to fit the story. Does it dramatize a failure of luminosity, the narrator's own mind opaque to him exactly where he claims perfect access to it? Or does it show only a first-order failure, a man who does not know his own condition, which would leave the KK principle untouched, since a belief that does not amount to knowledge never satisfies the antecedent of KK in the first place? Those are different readings, and I want to know which the story supports and whether it can be made to support the other without distortion. Point me to the moments that decide it: the opening disavowal of madness and the confidence with which he separates his insight from the blindness of the mad; the filmed, pale eye that so offends him and what he takes himself to perceive in it; the heart he alone hears; the confession that seems to force its way out of him against his will. And if the story turns out to bear on KK rather than on luminosity, tell me which moment does the cleaner work — whether there is a place where the narrator genuinely knows something first-order and yet fails to know that he knows it, which would be a counterexample of a different shape than the one the arguments usually run on.

Then tell me where the story resists the philosophical reading — where Poe's interest is in dread and voice rather than in the epistemology of self-knowledge — and, if it can, what the story adds to the debate that argument alone does not: whether fiction can put a reader inside a mind that is, by hypothesis, not fully available to itself, in a way that no counterexample can. I am not writing a paper; I want the reading to be usable in the novel, so I care less about who has said what than about what the story actually shows.
