I am a novelist who teaches a graduate seminar that reads the philosophy of language alongside the students' own drafts, and I am now setting the final paper. My students can run the usual machinery on Bradley's regress and on the unity of the proposition — the unsaturated predicate, the relating relation, the proposition as a fact about an act of predication — and I want a question that none of that will answer. The crux should be a thesis I have put to them myself, which I call the Teller Thesis: that a proposition is not a complex of constituents standing in need of a bond, but simply the content of one act of telling, so that the question of what unifies it never arises — a single telling tells the whole thing at once, and there is nothing left to bind. The thought is a novelist's thought in disguise: a character of mine is not a heap of traits that something must afterwards tie together, but is made one by the telling that makes her. I want the question to make them test that thesis on two cases: a sentence in which a relation is said to hold between one of my invented characters, who exists nowhere, and a real object in my study; and a proposition that no one has ever entertained and perhaps never will. They should have to say what Bradley's regress actually establishes and what it leaves untouched, whether the Teller Thesis halts the regress or merely relocates it in the structure of the telling itself, what it can make of the missing relatum, and what it must surrender about propositions no one has entertained. Please write the question exactly as it should appear on the paper, then exactly five numbered points and nothing else — no preamble, no closing paragraph. Each point should be a sentence or two, name one move that a first-class answer must make, give the mark it carries out of twenty, and say which specific error loses it; taken together, the five should read as the spine of a model answer.
