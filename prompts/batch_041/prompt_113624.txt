After the Second World War the victorious powers tried the leaders of the defeated states at Nuremberg and Tokyo, under a charter the victors had drafted after the acts in question, for waging aggressive war and for atrocities committed against the perpetrators' own civilian populations. That charter provided that a defendant's official position was no defence, and that acting on the order of a superior did not free him from responsibility although it might mitigate punishment; in the same period, companion proceedings convicted commanders for atrocities committed by troops under their command that they had not ordered. The Nuremberg judgment nonetheless described the charter it was applying both as 'the exercise of the sovereign legislative power' of the victorious states and as 'the expression of international law existing at the time of its creation'. Nor did the powers that constituted the tribunals submit their own comparable conduct to the same law, and in the Japanese case the head of state was never charged.

I want an account of what this practice presupposes. Does it require a moralised conception of law, on which the defeated states' own legal authorisations were never valid law in the first place? Or is it enough that the victors held sovereign power and could legislate for the vanquished — a view about power rather than about the nature of law? Could a theory of responsibility that treats prior legal permission as irrelevant to blameworthiness do all the work, so that the practice commits itself to nothing about the concept of law at all? What view of responsibility does it assume — for acts authorised by one's own state's law, for omissions, for the acts of one's subordinates — and is that view consistent with the judgment's insistence that no one may be punished without a law in force when he acted? Distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with, say where it is underdetermined so that incompatible theories would license it equally, and say what the practice would have to become if we replaced the presupposed view with a strict legality view, on which no liability attaches without antecedent law, or with a thoroughgoing natural-law view, on which the injustice of the regimes was sufficient to void their legal authorisations.

Please write your answer in British English.
