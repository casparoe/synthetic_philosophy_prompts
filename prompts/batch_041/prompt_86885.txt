I spent forty-one years as an experimentalist — most of it in precision measurement and low-temperature physics, chasing things like the anisotropy of inertial mass and the thermal noise floor of torsion balances — and since retiring I have been sitting in on a philosophy of physics reading group at the local university, where we have been working through the absolute-versus-relational debate about motion. The graduate students in the room are, without exception, competent physicists, and yet every discussion collapses within twenty minutes into a two-box picture: either you are a substantivalist, in which case the bucket proves that space is a thing, or you are a relationist, in which case you have to explain Newton's experiment away. I am convinced this is an artefact of the labels rather than a fact about the positions, and I would like an instrument that dissolves it. Please act as a philosopher of physics with a specialist's command of the substantivalism/relationism literature and of the debates about spacetime structure in general relativity, and begin by thinking carefully about the logical space before you commit to any tree; a short brainstorming pass in which you enumerate the questions that could possibly separate the views, before you draw anything, would be welcome.

What I want is a decision tree of the sort one could hand out as a single page, which takes a reader from a starting commitment we all share — that there is a fact of the matter about whether the water in the bucket is rotating, and that the concave surface is evidence for it — and routes them, by a sequence of questions they can answer without already knowing which label sits at the end, to a named position. I would like the branching nodes to be genuine fault lines rather than slogans. Three that I think must appear: first, whether the fact of the matter about rotation is fixed by the relative motions of material bodies alone, or whether it requires something over and above them; second, for those who say something more is required, whether that something is best understood as a thing — a manifold of points, a field, a set of worldlines — or as a constraint inherited from the form of the dynamical laws, so that what is real is a structure of inertial frames rather than any entity that possesses them; and third, whether the points of spacetime have primitive identities of their own, or whether the manifold's structure is defined only up to a rearrangement of its points, so that a world and its shuffled twin are one and the same world. That third question is the one I care most about, because I suspect it is what actually separates the views people call substantivalism into two families that disagree about almost nothing else, and I want the tree to force the reader to answer it rather than let them hide behind the word. Feel free to add a fourth or fifth node if the logical space demands it — for instance, if you think the explanatory question (what makes the bucket argument a good inference to the best explanation) comes apart from the truthmaker question (what in the world makes a rotation claim true), then give them separate nodes, because I would like to see whether any of the resulting combinations are occupied.

At the leaves I would like the positions named and located: ordinary Newtonian substantivalism; Leibnizian relationism; the Machian variant on which inertial effects are determined by the distribution of matter in the universe; the anti-haeccity version of substantivalism on which spacetime is real but its points are not individuals; the dynamical or structuralist option on which inertial structure is real but not spatiotemporal in any naive sense; the supersubstantivalist identification of bodies with regions of spacetime; and the deflationary leaf occupied by those who think the bucket argument shows nothing, either because the dispute is verbal or because there is no fundamental fact about motion at all. For each leaf, give one sentence naming its most awkward commitment and one concrete case that would distinguish it from its nearest neighbour — I suggest the rotating bucket, the two globes joined by a cord, a single body rotating in an otherwise empty universe, and a universe containing nothing but a rigid ring. Where the tree leaves a combination of answers unoccupied, tell me so explicitly and diagnose it: is the view incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored? And where two leaves cannot be separated by any question on the tree — I expect at least one such pair, probably between the anti-haeccity substantivalist and the structuralist — say so plainly instead of inventing a distinction to keep them apart. Do not pad the answer with appeals to what many philosophers are said to hold; every branch and every leaf should be stated as a claim someone could agree or disagree with, and if you find yourself unsure whether two positions are really distinct, flag the uncertainty rather than smoothing it over.
