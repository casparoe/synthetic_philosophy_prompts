Ok so I'm 68, retired six years ago after 34 years as an entomologist, mostly moths, plus a lot of soil by-catch I never had the heart to sort properly. Two years ago I joined a philosophy reading group and now I can't sleep. Here's the problem.

Most of my working life I killed insects. Light traps, pitfall traps, Berlese funnels, and a great many more just by walking across a meadow. I never thought twice about it. Now I keep running into what people call the scale argument -- that because there are so many insects, even a tiny probability that each one can suffer makes the expected amount of insect suffering enormous, maybe larger than everything we do to farmed vertebrates -- and I genuinely can't tell whether it's the most important argument of the century or a pile of arithmetic wearing an ethics costume.

So give me a number and show me the arithmetic.

Question: what is your credence that a typical moth -- say a noctuid, since thats what I actually caught -- has valenced experience, in the thin sense that there's a fact of the matter about whether things go well or badly for it from the inside? Decompose it, roughly:

- the probability that the correct account of sentience requires something like a centralized brain or thalamocortical-style recurrent loops (or whatever you think the right criterion is), versus the probability that it tolerates a distributed or much smaller nervous system;
- conditional on each of those, the probability a moth's nervous system meets the bar -- and be straight with me about how much of the behavioural evidence (wound grooming, opioid and anaesthetic effects, motivational trade-offs, avoidance learning) can be explained with no felt quality at all;
- and then how you get from that to a per-insect welfare number, if welfare survives the translation at all.

Two wrinkles I want you to take seriously rather than skip.

First, I keep wondering whether the individual is even the right unit of count for social insects. I spent years watching ant colonies behave like one slow animal. If the colony is the thing that has interests, the numbers move by orders of magnitude, and nobody I've read seems sure which way to go. Is that a real fork or am I being sentimental about my study organisms?

Second, the soil stuff. The by-catch in my funnels -- mites, springtails, things I never identified -- always outnumbered whatever I was actually after, tens of thousands per square metre in good forest soil, or so the soil people told me. If the scale argument is right, my light traps were a rounding error next to one spadeful of leaf litter. Do I have to extend the same credence to a springtail? What breaks first, the argument or my intuitions?

For what it's worth I'd put myself at about 0.1 for the moth and 0.01 for the springtail, and I couldn't tell you why they differ by a factor of ten. So the part I actually want is this: tell me where the number is doing real work and where it's arbitrary. If you land somewhere in that range, what would move you -- a new result from insect neurobiology, a new argument, or nothing short of a theory of consciousness?

And please seperate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who work on this. I'm new enough that I can't tell which parts are textbook and which are one lab's opinion, and I don't want a contested view sold to me as the consensus. If your decomposition is mostly guesswork with two or three real anchors in it, just say so.
