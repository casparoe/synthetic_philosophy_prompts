I sit on the graduate medical education committee at our teaching hospital, and on Thursday we vote on the simulation center's proposal to move our surgical residents to proficiency-based progression: no resident would place a central line or perform the first three laparoscopic cases on a patient until she has cleared a benchmark of three consecutive simulator runs that meet the checklist standard with no verbal prompting from an attending. The director of the simulation center argues her case in the vocabulary of a four-grade scheme of minds I have been reading about lately — creatures whose behavior is fixed by selection and needs no learning at all; creatures whose behavior is shaped by reinforcement, so that they must buy their competence with their own history of consequences; creatures able to run the trial inside the head and let the hypothesis die instead of the creature; and creatures whose thinking is done partly with inherited tools, symbols, and notation, so that a large part of the mind is acquired rather than grown. Her claim is that the apprenticeship model we have used for thirty years treats a surgeon as a creature of the second grade, and that the whole point of possessing the third-grade capacity is that we are not obliged to purchase skill with other people's complications. The division chief of surgery, who holds two of the committee's votes, is unmoved: he says operative judgment is not a simulator output, that our outcomes are at or above the national benchmark and always have been, and that the residents he has seen come out of the simulation-first pathway freeze at the first unscripted bleed.

What the proposal does not mention is that for the past year the simulation center has been running an adaptive coaching system that watches each resident's runs, predicts the next error, and calls it out in real time. The residents who score best on the benchmark are the ones who have leaned on the coach most heavily; with the coach switched off, their runs are unremarkable. Our informatics representative has put a paragraph in the file arguing that the director's scheme has no name for what our trainees have become, since they are not paying for their errors, because the environment has been rigged to forgive them, and they are not doing the simulating either, because the machine is doing it for them; she calls them prosthetic Popperians and says the benchmark is therefore measuring the wrong creature entirely. I am inclined to approve with conditions, but I want a memo the chief can actually answer rather than a restatement of the director's case.

Please draft the short written opinion I will circulate before the vote. It should recommend a concrete course of action — approve, reject, or approve with conditions — and the conditions, if any, should be specific enough to write straight into the policy: what the assessment runs must be run on, what happens to the coaching system during assessment, who signs off on a resident's clearance, and when the committee revisits the whole arrangement. It should state plainly which grade of creature the policy is assuming the trainee to be at each stage of training, and what that assumption licenses us to demand of her. It should give the chief's position its strongest formulation and say what would have to be true for him to be right. And it should take a clear position on the informatics representative's objection: whether this committee should treat the individual trainee or the trainee together with her tools as the unit of competence, and what follows from that choice for the four-grade story the director is telling.
