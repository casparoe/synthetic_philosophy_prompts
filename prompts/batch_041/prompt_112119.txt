Assume the role of a philosopher of science whose specialisms are the demarcation problem, Popper's falsifiability criterion, and the philosophy of biology. Think carefully before you write, and open with a brief section setting out how you take the dispute to be structured; I would rather have your own map of it than a survey of the literature, and I am not asking for references.

I am a retired evolutionary biologist — forty years of field and modelling work, now reading philosophy of science in earnest for the first time — and I have been trying, without much success, to get a clean grip on the following dispute.

Popper, whose falsifiability criterion I had always treated as the sensible line between science and pseudo-science, argued in the 1970s that Darwinian natural selection is better described as a metaphysical research programme than as a testable scientific theory, on the ground that it appears to forbid no possible observation: whatever survives is, by definition, the fittest. He later partly withdrew the claim, insisting that natural selection is testable and that his objection applied only to a stripped-down version of the principle. Around him, critics and defenders have kept the argument going. The defenders say the theory does forbid things — that it excludes complex adaptation arising with no selection at all, and that it constrains what the fossil and biogeographic records may look like. The critics reply that any observation can be accommodated by adjusting the auxiliary assumptions about heredity, variation, and population structure. Neither side seems to gain ground.

What I cannot tell is what the disagreement is actually about. My working hypothesis is that at least two equivocations do most of the work. First, falsifiability is used in two different registers — as a purely logical property of a statement (does some possible observation contradict it?) and as a methodological standard for appraising a research programme (do its practitioners actually expose it to risk?). Second, Darwinism names at least three different objects: the bare logical schema that if there is heritable variation in fitness then the fitter variants will tend to spread; the empirical research programme that claims such variation and selection are the principal causes of adaptation; and particular adaptationist explanations of particular traits. If the two sides are not naming the same object or are using different senses of the criterion, the dispute may be largely verbal; if they are, it may be substantive.

Please do two things. First, diagnose the dispute: is it verbal, a disagreement about standards of evidence, a case of the two sides answering different questions under one name, or a substantive disagreement about what makes a theory empirical? Where, if anywhere, is the real crux? Second, assess whether a deeper disagreement is hiding underneath — for instance about whether there can be laws in biology at all, or about whether a theory that is schematic and borrows its content from auxiliary assumptions can be genuinely empirical.

Then tell me what would count as progress given your diagnosis: a distinction, a reformulation of the question, or an argument that no resolution should be expected and why. Be concrete and commit to a view.
