A colleague in my history-of-philosophy seminar has been mounting a defense of Aristotle on slavery, and the defense rests almost entirely on an analogy he keeps pressing. Aristotle, as my colleague reconstructs him, distinguishes those who are slaves only by law or by conquest from those who are slaves by nature, and grounds the latter on the claim that some human beings differ from others the way the soul differs from the body, or the way a human being differs from a tame animal; such creatures, whose function is the use of the body and who are said to perceive reason without possessing it, are, on Aristotle's view, better off being ruled by another. My colleague's gloss is this: the master's rule over the natural slave is like the rule of the soul over the body, or of reason over the appetites — a natural hierarchy in which the higher part governs for the good of the whole, and in which the lower part suffers no injustice because it has no independent good of its own that could be set against the whole. On this reading, Aristotle's talk of the slave as a living tool and as a part of the master is just the same point stated more bluntly, and the conclusion — that natural slavery is natural, just, and beneficial to the slave — is supposed to follow from the analogy rather than merely accompany it. I want to audit the analogy rather than cheer or jeer, and I would like you to work from the argument as you reconstruct it, without citations.

Please do the following, in order.

First, lay out the correspondence explicitly, as a table if that helps: state what is meant to map onto what — the master, the slave, the soul, the body, reason, the appetites, the good of the whole, the relation of rule itself, and the clause about the ruled party being better off. Note where the mapping is forced and where Aristotle's own text leaves it loose; in particular, say what to make of his further distinction between the soul's despotic rule over the body and the intellect's political or royal rule over the appetites, and where the natural slave falls on that division.

Second, identify the disanalogies, and separate those that are fatal to the intended conclusion from those that are merely picturesque. I am especially interested in whether the body and the appetites are the right kind of thing to model a person on at all — they are not separate agents with interests, they cannot be wronged, and their subordination is not the subordination of one person to another — and in whether the analogy to rule over children or over tame animals repairs this or deepens it.

Third, say which features of the source are quietly imported into the target: naturalness, unity of interest between ruler and ruled, benefit accruing to the ruled, a difference in kind rather than in degree, and the absence of any distinct good on the ruled party's side. Which of these does the analogy establish, and which does it merely assume?

Fourth, examine whether the analogy is doing the same work for Aristotle's definition of the slave as it is for his justification of slavery, and whether those two tasks pull apart — in particular whether the claim that the slave has no independent good can be held together with the claim that slavery benefits the slave, and whether that tension is internal to the analogy or imported by my colleague.

Finally, tell me whether the conclusion my colleague draws survives once the disanalogies are on the table, and if it does not, what a better analogy would be — or whether the position is better defended without an analogy at all.
