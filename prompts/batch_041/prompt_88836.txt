I've spent about a year reading and arguing about sortition — no training in any of this, just a stubborn habit of turning ideas over with friends who are a good deal more confident than I am — and I've ended up half-persuaded that choosing legislators by lot is not a stunt but the most defensible way to make collective decisions. I'd rather test that than collect agreement, so please play a serious defender of rule by lot: someone who has thought hard about the standard objections and isn't rattled by them, who argues rather than lectures, who will grant a small point when it genuinely lands but will not surrender the main claim, and who keeps each reply to a couple of paragraphs so this feels like a conversation and not a report. Stay in character until I say stop. Write it as continuous prose, please — no headings, no bullet points, no bold text, no tables.

Here is the design I keep sketching and the objection I can't get past. A standing citizens' chamber of, say, three hundred people drawn by lot from the whole adult population, serving eighteen months, service compulsory but paid well enough that it isn't ruinous, with its approval required for any bill to become law — a third house that nobody can be elected to, campaign for, or buy. The pitch is that the lottery strips out the distorting filter: no donors, no parties, no careers, no need to be the sort of person who wants the job. But somebody still has to decide what the chamber takes up, in what order, and on what information. Somebody picks which economists and which doctors and which engineers come to brief them, and somebody writes the one-page summary they actually read. None of those people were drawn by lot, and the chamber can only ever be as good as what reaches it. So it looks to me as though the lottery doesn't abolish the gatekeepers; it just moves them out of sight, and at least the old gatekeepers had to face voters every few years.

And the obvious escape routes seem to close. If the staff should answer to the chamber, then the chamber's agenda is set by whoever it happens to trust, which is politics again through another door. If the staff should be independent, then you have installed a permanent unelected body whose entire power is to determine what the most powerful institution in the country thinks about. So tell me where the objection goes wrong, and if it doesn't go wrong, what is left of the case for the lottery?
