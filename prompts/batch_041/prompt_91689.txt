You are a philosopher of mathematics and physics specializing in supertasks — infinite sequences of actions completed within a finite stretch of time — and in the metaphysics of limits, continuity, and the determinacy of physical state. I have a set of verdicts of my own about when a supertask has a well-defined outcome, and I want you to find the principle behind them, or tell me that there is none and that one of my verdicts has to go.

Work carefully and in stages, and show the stages. Begin by brainstorming: for each case below, describe the finite stages, say which quantities have limits and which do not, and set down every candidate principle the case seems to support. Then put the candidates in competition, naming the case that most sharply separates each pair. Only after that should you commit to an answer. Do not settle on the first principle that fits most of the cases; I have deliberately included cases that pull in different directions, and I want the tension exposed rather than smoothed away.

Take noon as the limit time, and suppose the stages occur at 1, 1/2, 1/4, ... minutes before noon, unless I say otherwise.

1. The switch lamp. A switch is thrown at each stage; the lamp starts off. My verdict: there is no fact of the matter about whether the lamp is on at noon. The throws all occur; the world simply does not settle the state.

2. The dial lamp. Instead of a switch, a dimmer knob is turned to 1, then 1/2, then 1/4, ... The knob can hold any real value in the interval from 0 to 1, and the bulb's brightness is just the knob's setting, with 0 meaning dark. My verdict: determinate. The brightness at noon is 0 and the lamp is dark. Here the world does settle it.

3. The tape. A device writes one symbol at each stage onto a one-way tape, and the symbol at stage n is 1 when n is a perfect square and 0 otherwise. My verdict: determinate. After all the stages the tape holds that infinite pattern, so what the tape reads is settled — even though the running count of 1s has no finite limit and the symbol sequence converges to nothing.

4. Marbles out. At stage n, marbles 10n−9 through 10n are put in and marble n is taken out. My verdict: determinate — the jar is empty at noon. Surprising, but I think it is simply true.

5. Marbles kept. Same additions, except that at stage n the marble taken out is marble 10n, the highest one just added. My verdict: determinate — infinitely many marbles remain, namely everything except the multiples of 10.

6. Coin-flip marbles. Same additions, but at each stage the marble taken out is chosen by an independent fair coin. My verdict: I want to say that "infinitely many marbles remain" is settled, since however the coins fall the jar ends with infinitely many; but I also want to say there is no fact about which marbles are in it. I am not sure these two verdicts are consistent.

7. Stipulated lamp. The same throws as in case 1, but the description adds that the lamp is on at noon, by stipulation, whatever the throws do. My verdict: determinate — on. The throws fix the state at every earlier time and the stipulation fixes it at noon.

8. The ball. A ball approaches a wall so that its distance from the wall at stage n is 1/n, and it is required to move continuously. My verdict: determinate — it is at the wall at noon. If instead the rule had its distance alternate between 1 and 0 at successive stages, with no continuity requirement, my verdict would be indeterminate, just as in case 1.

My tentative principle is roughly this: a supertask has a determinate outcome exactly when the relevant physical quantity, taken as a function defined on the times before noon, extends continuously to noon; where it does not extend, there is no fact of the matter — except when the task's own description independently specifies the noon value (case 7), or when the outcome asked for is not a state at a time but the whole infinite history itself (case 3).

I do not think this survives contact with the cases. Case 3 is the immediate problem: its symbol sequence has no continuous extension and its tape content is not stipulated in advance the way case 7's lamp is, yet I still call it determinate. And case 6 shows that I do not know whether determinacy of an aggregate (how many) can come apart from determinacy of the underlying detail (which ones) — which matters, because in case 1 I am unwilling to say anything analogous, that the lamp's existence is settled while its state is not.

So: is there a single principle, or a small set of clauses, that captures my verdicts? State it precisely enough that I could apply it mechanically, and for each clause name the case that forces it. Then apply the principle to cases I have not listed:

(a) The averaging lamp. At stage n the bulb's brightness is the average of the first n symbols of the sequence 1, 0, 1, 0, ..., so the brightness tends to 1/2 even though the switch is thrown at every stage and the on/off sequence has no limit. Is this determinate or not, and does it show that the converging quantity need not be the state itself?

(b) The extra final stage. The throws of case 1 occur at 1, 1/2, 1/4, ... and then one further throw occurs exactly at noon. Does that final throw settle the state?

(c) Parallel performance. The knob-turns of case 2 are carried out by infinitely many distinct agents, each turning the knob exactly once, so that no single agent performs a supertask. Does that change the verdict, and does it show that determinacy attaches to the schedule rather than to the causal structure?

Finally, tell me honestly which of my verdicts, 1 through 8, the principle cannot accommodate, and whether I should abandon that verdict or add a clause to save it. I would rather be told that one of my intuitions is mistaken than be handed a principle that merely restates the list.
