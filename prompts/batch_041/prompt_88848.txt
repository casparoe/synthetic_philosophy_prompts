I'm a backend engineer with no real philosophy training, and at work I've somehow become the person who maintains the weighted-score rubric our team uses to rank vendors, project proposals, and occasionally job candidates: every option gets a number for cost, technical-debt risk, user impact, team morale, and so on, we multiply each by a weight somebody made up two years ago, and the highest total wins. A colleague and I have been arguing about whether that rubric is doing something legitimate or papering over a problem, and we keep circling the suspicion that some of these goods cannot be traded off against each other at all — that no amount of saved cost buys a bit less user harm, that morale is not worth some quantity of technical debt, and so on. I have been reading around on the side, mostly blog posts and a couple of textbook chapters I only half follow, and I would like to pin down what my colleague and I actually disagree about rather than trading intuitions, so please build me a decision tree that sorts the positions on when two options can be ranked against each other and when they cannot. Start from the commitment that at least some hard choices are genuinely hard rather than merely inconvenient, and build the tree out of the logical space first: work out which questions carve the space up, arrange those questions as branch nodes, and only then locate the positions in the resulting structure. Every branch node should be phrased as a question my colleague and I could answer without already knowing which position the answer commits us to — ideally a question about one of the cases below, or about what we would say if the rubric and our gut disagreed, rather than a question about which theory we hold. Please make sure the tree can separate at least these six: someone who thinks every pair of options is rankable in principle and that our rubric merely needs better weights; someone who thinks a scale exists but our comparisons on it are vague at the edges, so the ranking is imprecise rather than absent; someone who thinks two options can be comparable and yet neither better than the other nor exactly equal, so that choosing between them is neither forced by reason nor arbitrary; someone who thinks the difficulty is in us rather than in the options, so that a fully informed and unlimited agent would have a unique answer; someone who thinks there is no fact of the matter about which is better and that the choice is a commitment we create rather than discover; and someone who thinks the right response to unrankable options is to stop producing a single number at all and hand the decision to a human or to a procedure such as a Pareto front with an explicit tie-break. If two of those turn out to be the same leaf, say so instead of faking a distinction.

Please anchor the tree in concrete cases rather than abstract formulations, and use at least these: two job offers where one is clearly better on money and the other on meaningfulness; a hiring rubric containing a floor you never go below, so that almost any amount of one score is traded for crossing a threshold on another; a pair of options where nudging one of them up by a hair leaves you unable to say it is now better, while nudging it a little further clearly does make it better; a choice between taking a demanding promotion and caring for an ageing parent; and two options that feel roughly level without your being indifferent between them. For each leaf, give me the name of the position (or the word unnamed if there is no standard label for it), one sentence on its most awkward commitment, and one line on what it implies for a tool like ours — a weighted sum with arbitrary weights, a lexicographic rule with thresholds, a Pareto front plus a human tie-break, satisficing against a bar, or a refusal to rank at all. Then list the answer combinations that no position occupies and say briefly whether each is empty because it is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored, and flag any two leaves the tree cannot really separate. Keep the whole thing to roughly seven branch nodes and eight or nine leaves, and do not bother with author names or paper titles; I would not be able to check them, and what I care about is the shape of the space rather than the bibliography.
