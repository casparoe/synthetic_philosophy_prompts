My idea, as best as I've got it: Dennett's line (as I read him) is that a pattern is real when a description that uses it buys you a big compression win over the raw description of the data, and that this is what makes belief-desire talk about people, thermostats and chess engines legitimate rather than merely useful fiction. I was happy with that until I noticed that compression and causation seem to come apart, and that every example in the usual line-up — the glider in Conway's Life, the chess program, the thermostat — is a case where they happen to line up. Two cases where they don't: Benford's law, which is a gorgeous compressible pattern that turns up in all sorts of unrelated datasets and doesn't seem to cause anything; and a chaotic system, where the fine-grained trajectory is causally doing all the work but is essentially incompressible, so the pattern-language has nothing to grab. I suspect the framework is quietly trading on 'real' meaning 'causally efficacious', and that if you say that out loud you either have to drop the compression criterion for something else or let in far more patterns than Dennett wants to. So my half-formed question is whether the realism of the intentional stance depends on which criterion of pattern-realism you adopt, and whether the framework can be run on a causal criterion without smuggling back in exactly the Cartesian commitments it was built to avoid.
