Consider a pattern of argument — often called transcendental — in which some proposition P is said to be a condition of the possibility of a practice that one's interlocutor is already engaged in (asking questions, making assertions, demanding to be taken seriously, blaming, promising, deliberating), so that P may be treated as established on the ground that denying it would undermine the very act of denying it.

Now suppose the following three points hold. (1) At its strongest, the pattern shows only that one cannot coherently assert the denial of P while continuing to engage in the practice; that is a claim about what we can say, not a claim about how the world is. (2) Whether a practice genuinely commits its participants to P, rather than merely being accompanied by a belief in P, is typically the very matter in dispute, so the premise of the argument has a way of assuming what it was meant to establish. (3) The pattern is usually available to both parties at once: whoever denies P can often give a comparably plausible argument that the same practice presupposes something incompatible with P.

Please describe the most important implications of these three points, ordered by importance, for the following: (i) how much weight to give such an argument when the interlocutor is sincere but rejects your premise — a relativist, say, who holds that all professed reasons are rationalisations of interest; (ii) the use of the pattern in public and political argument, where the charge of inconsistency — that one demands tolerance and yet withholds it — is a staple of polemic; and (iii) what a presupposition argument of this kind can still legitimately accomplish if it cannot prove its conclusion. For at least one of these, work through a concrete example in detail, and wherever your answer rests on a premise that a determined opponent could reject, say so rather than assuming it.
