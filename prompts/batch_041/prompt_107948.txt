Next month I am to give the Founders' Day address at Marrow College, where I took my degree and where I have taught regional history for twenty-two years. I am also the person who, four months ago, published the metallurgical report on the Founder's Spoon and the transcription of the diaries of Dean Aldis — the man who in 1958, with the college nine weeks from insolvency, assembled the entire ceremonial apparatus that Founders' Day now consists of: the candlelight procession, the vigil, the oath in the dog-Latin that Aldis wrote himself, and the "seventeenth-century" spoon, which is a 1920s electrotype of a Victorian copy of a colonial original that never existed. The trustees know. The alumni magazine runs the story in the spring. By the time I stand up, most of the hall will know, and my address will be read as my statement on the matter whether or not I make it one.

The room will hold four kinds of people: the older alumni, for whom the vigil is the most serious thing in their lives, several of whom have written to ask that I not spoil it; the students, who mostly find it embarrassing and are half hoping I detonate it; the trustees, who would like me warm, brief, and vague; and one very old man, a member of the first vigil cohort, who will stand for the whole thing whether or not anyone else does. I want six or seven minutes that are honest and that do not end in the usual shrug — that it doesn't matter whether a tradition is true, only what it means to us — because I don't believe that shrug, and I don't think anyone in that room who has thought about it for five minutes does either.

The conceptual spine I want you to use, and to make visible without lecturing: the threefold division of invented traditions into those that establish social cohesion, those that legitimize institutions and authority, and those whose work is socialization — together with the question of which of the three the vigil is actually performing now, as against which one Aldis intended it to perform; the insistence that an imagined community is not thereby a fabricated one, and that the interesting question about any community is never whether it is real but what style of imagining holds it together; and the observation that the rigidity of a ritual — the unvarying wording, the fixed order of the steps, the insistence on exactly the same observance every year — is itself the fingerprint of recent invention, since living custom is flexible and it is precisely invented tradition that cannot be allowed to drift. The speech should therefore be able to say something true, and even consoling, about the very features of the vigil that the report has just exposed, and to say it so that a person who has stood in that procession for fifty years can hear it without being made a fool of.

Constraints: no citations, no scholars' names, no books — this is a hall, not a seminar. Do not use the word "myth" as a dismissal. Do not dissolve the tension by declaring that the ritual's meaning is independent of its history; engage the question of what, if anything, the fabrication costs the community it built, and whether the binding power of the vigil rests on the belief in its antiquity or on the practice itself. Leave the old man something to stand for. End somewhere I could stand without either lying or apologizing. Then, in a few lines after the speech, tell me which sentence is doing the load-bearing work and what would collapse if it were removed.
