I'm working through Book V, chapter 10 of Aristotle's Nicomachean Ethics for a seminar on moral generalism and particularism, and I've hit a passage I cannot get a grip on. In W. D. Ross's translation it reads: "when the thing is indefinite the rule also is indefinite, like the leaden rule used in making the Lesbian moulding; the rule adapts itself to the shape of the stone and is not rigid, and so too the decree is adapted to the facts." A little earlier in the same chapter, in another public-domain rendering I have been comparing, Aristotle says that what is equitable "is not identical with, but a correction of, that which is just according to law", and that its essence "is that it is an amendment of the law, in those points where it fails through the generality of its language." I think I follow the individual moves: law must speak in general terms, general terms cannot capture every particular case, so the equitable judge adjusts the verdict to the case. What I do not understand is what this is supposed to be a theory of.

My seminar is reading the chapter alongside the contemporary debate between moral generalists, who hold that morality is or can be captured in principles, and particularists, who deny that the moral features of a situation are exhaustively codified in any set of rules. Some of my classmates want to claim Aristotle for the particularist side, on the strength of the leaden-rule image and the thought that the rule "adapts itself to the shape of the stone". Others say that is a misreading, because equity in Aristotle looks more like a second-order general principle — a rule telling you when to depart from first-order rules — and that a device for correcting over-general laws presupposes exactly the generalist picture it is meant to undermine. I can see both readings, which is presumably why I am stuck. Could you explain what Aristotle is actually doing in this chapter, with close attention to what "correction" and "adaptation" can and cannot mean here, and then say plainly which side, if either, the text supports? I would much rather you tell me that the whole "was Aristotle a particularist?" framing is anachronistic — if that is what you think — than manufacture a verdict to fit the seminar. And if the leaden rule is doing less philosophical work than the image suggests, say so; I want to know what Aristotle's account of equity adds to the generalism/particularism debate that a modern reader might miss, and where it leaves the question genuinely open.
