I'm running a session for a small reading group that's been working through the Löwenheim–Skolem material, and instead of lecturing at them I want to hand out a decision tree that sorts people by what they actually think about Skolem's paradox, not by which label they've read somewhere. Everyone starts at the same place: I accept that if ZF is consistent it has a countable model, and that ZF proves there are uncountable sets. From there, split them with yes/no questions until they hit a leaf.

The nodes should be the real pressure points rather than the names of schools. Stuff like: is uncountability a fact about a set, or only a fact about a set relative to a language or a model or a metatheory? Is the culprit first-order logic specifically, so that moving to second-order or plural quantification gets you out, or is it something about the intended interpretation instead, or neither? Does the paradox tell us anything about sets at all, or is it a technical result about what an axiom system can pin down and nothing more? Does it generalise into the indeterminacy-of-reference conclusion some people pull out of it? And two concrete cases I want as nodes because they make people commit: the tourist, who steps outside the countable model, watches its reals get enumerated, then steps back in — has she learned something the model can't say, or just changed her language? And the two-model case, where two countable models are each convinced they've got all the reals — is one of them simply wrong?

The one I care about most: is the whole thing just the same phenomenon as nonstandard models of arithmetic, where a model's natural numbers include ones we'd call infinite? I want the tree to make people notice the wrinkle — ZF can define 'uncountable' internally and then get it wrong from our vantage point, whereas PA can't define 'standard' at all, so is that an analogy or a disanalogy? Don't give that question an obvious right answer.

Leaves get named positions where a name exists and 'unnamed' where it doesn't, each with one line on its most awkward commitment. I want at least these to turn up somewhere: the full relativist who denies there's any absolute uncountability; the person who thinks the paradox is benign and first-order set theory just fails to pin down the intended model; the one who thinks richer logic or full semantics fixes it; the structuralist who says the question was never about an absolute fact; the one who reads it as an argument that reference is indeterminate; and whoever thinks uncountable sets should just be given up. Build the tree from the logical space first — lay out the questions and the combinations of answers they permit, then locate the actual positions in that space, and note any combination that comes out empty and why (nobody there because it's incoherent, or unattractive, or merely unexplored?). Also flag any two leaves the tree can't seperate, I'd rather that be said out loud than fudged.

Size: about eight nodes, ten leaves at most. Audience is phil-of-math grad students plus one stubborn set theorist. No citations or reading lists, they're no use here, and don't put a person's name on a position unless your sure — descriptive names are fine.
