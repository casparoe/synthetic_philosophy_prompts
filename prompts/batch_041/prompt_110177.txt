I'm covering for a colleague next Thursday and I've ended up with ninety minutes on the statue and the clay — material constitution, apparently — in front of a room of second and third year art history and conservation students. No philosophy at all in their background, but they've spent a term handling objects and arguing about whether a restored vase is still the vase, so they're not blank. Don't explain the topic to me, plan the session: the order the pieces have to go in and why each one depends on the one before it, the single case that should carry the whole thing and what that case has to have and has to avoid, the misconceptions these particular students will arrive with or develop along the way, and exactly where in the sequence I should head each one off. Plus one question I can put to the room at the end that tells me whether it actually landed.

Some decisions I've already made so plan around them. I'm not using the Ship of Theseus — I think that's a different puzzle wearing the same coat, tell me if I'm wrong. The case I was going to run it on is the plain one: a lump of clay that's a figure on Monday, gets squashed flat on Tuesday, gets re-formed into a different figure on Wednesday. I want a second case in reserve though, and I keep coming back to this one: a bronze figure melted down and poured back into the same mould, so what comes out is the same metal, the same shape, made in the same mould. Is that the same statue, the same lump, both, neither? My colleague says that case is a distraction and that what the students need first is the picture of the statue as a shape the clay is wearing. My instinct is that that picture is the thing that causes the trouble rather than the thing that cures it, but I can't say precisely why. Work that out as part of the plan.

The misconceptions I'm expecting — tell me which are real, which is me being paranoid, and what I'm missing: that 'statue' and 'lump' are just two words for one thing and the puzzle is verbal; that two things can't be in the same place at once so one of them must be fake or a mere aggregate; that the lump is a part of the statue; that this is really the identity-over-time problem, which is why everyone reaches for the ship; and that statues are abstract — a shape, a plan, a form — so there's no material object here to argue about at all. Also alot of them, I suspect, will want to say the statue is 'borrowing' the clay. I can't tell whether that instinct is a good foothold or a trap, so say.

And tell me what I can safely leave out and say so out loud: the answer that treats the statue and the lump as two four-dimensional things that overlap for a stretch and then come apart, anything about what grounds what, and the whole 'how many objects are there really' business about what our words for objects commit us to. If cutting those guts the session, say that instead. Keep the final check to one question I can ask the room, not a quiz. They won't be reading anything.
