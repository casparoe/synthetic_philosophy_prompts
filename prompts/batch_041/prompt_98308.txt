I am in a reading group on eighteenth-century moral philosophy, and last week a participant tried to settle a dispute about whether Smith's ethics can ground any criticism of a community's accepted norms by quoting Smith's sentence, "We endeavour to examine our own conduct as we imagine any other fair and impartial spectator would examine it." He took that sentence to establish a purely social or conventionalist reading of Smith: on his view, the impartial spectator is simply the internalized voice of one's community, so Smith has no resources for saying that a whole society's moral code is wrong. I suspect the sentence can be read in several very different ways, and that some of those readings would support his conclusion while others would not. Lay out the precise claims that this one sentence could be making—about who or what the spectator is, what makes the spectator impartial, whether the spectator is imagined or real, whether the spectator's verdict is discovered or constructed, whether the spectator is a device for self-approbation or for moral discovery, and what authority the spectator's judgment is supposed to have. For each reading, say whether it is a platitude, a substantive and defensible thesis, or something Smith would reject, and whether it actually gives my interlocutor the conventionalist conclusion he wants. I want a menu of readings, not a single interpretation, and I want to know which reading is doing the work when someone uses that sentence to end an argument about whether Smith can criticize a society's morals.
