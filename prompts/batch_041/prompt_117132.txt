I want to know whether the underdetermination in Thomson's lamp is an artefact of its bare two-state description or something that survives once a supertask is fleshed out physically. So construct a supertask of the lamp family to the following specifications, and then show that it meets them.

The setup must be a discrete state changed at stages whose times accumulate at a completion time t*, with no last stage and the task finished in finite time. In addition to the discrete state, the system must carry an underlying continuous magnitude that converges to a definite value at t* — and that value must be exactly the critical value at which the discrete state is not fixed by the magnitude. The case must therefore not be resolvable by taking limits: continuity of the underlying quantity leaves the terminal state open, and the discrete sequence itself does not converge. The terminal state must nonetheless be determinate, fixed by an independent physical consideration that you state and defend as a fact rather than a stipulation. If on inspection the only tie-breaker available is a convention — a threshold the physics does not itself draw — say so, and say whether that vindicates the no-fact-of-the-matter view rather than refuting it. Keep it bench-top: no infinite energies, speeds, or forces, and no exotic entities.

Then give the verdict of each of the following: the limit/continuity view; the view that there is no fact of the matter about the terminal state; and the view that the task is impossible. Say which the case refutes and which survive. Identify the single feature doing the work, and give the minimal variant with that feature removed, showing exactly what the case collapses into. Say how a defender of the no-fact-of-the-matter view would attack the construction, and finish with what it establishes that Thomson's original lamp does not.
