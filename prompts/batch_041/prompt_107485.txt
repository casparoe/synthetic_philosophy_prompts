I'm putting together the final essay question for my second-year seminar on Wittgenstein's later stuff, and I need one on language games and family resemblance. The problem is every year the students write some version of "games don't share one essence, they're a family, meaning is use" and then just stop — pure recitation, and I honestly can't tell who gets it from who memorized it.

I want a question that makes them actually do something with it. Two things keep coming up and I want the exam to force them to sort them out. One student last term wrote that Wittgenstein's point was really just that game is a cluster concept — tick any four of seven features and you're in. And another said a philosophy-of-sport guy had actually *defined* a game, something about voluntarily taking on unnecessary obstacles on purpose, and that this shows Wittgenstein was wrong because now we've got the definition. I want students to work out whether these are the same as family resemblance or a totally different kind of move, and what work the appeal to forms of life / training / use is doing that a feature checklist can't.

Concrete hook I want them to chew on: suppose someone invents a thing people start calling a game that you play alone, with no winning or losing and no rules you could actually break — you just wander around. And separately, someone hands you a checklist that says any 3 of 6 features makes it a game.

So write me one essay question (they get 45 minutes, no notes), a model answer around 550–650 words, and a marking scheme built on five must-hit points worth 20 marks total, with a one-liner for each on how students usually get that point wrong. Make the scheme separate an A from a B too — name the specific move a top answer has that a solid-but-not-great one won't. And please don't let the whole thing be answerable by just paraphrasing the games passage back at me.
