Next month I am presenting a paper at a small decision-theory workshop, and I need a question-and-answer preparation sheet. The audience is mostly economists and game theorists in the causal-decision-theory tradition, who reason in terms of commitment devices, signalling, and equilibrium selection; plus one Kantian; plus one person who works on collective intentionality and team reasoning. My paper concerns Parfit's hitchhiker: a traveller stranded in the desert is offered a ride into town on the condition that he promise to pay on arrival, though once in town he can neither be compelled nor punished, and Parfit's point is that the traveller's self-interest is better served by his being the sort of person who would keep such a promise. My thesis is narrower and less comfortable than the usual one. I argue that this so-called rational irrationality is not reason surviving its own suspension but the acquisition of a binding mechanism, which is precisely what Parfit's own pill example reveals; and that where no mechanism is available, as in the case as stated, the advice to become the sort of person who keeps his word is advice no one can take, so the verdict that the traveller should pay is either a moral duty dressed as prudence or an artefact of the standpoint of someone choosing his own character, a standpoint that no one inside the case occupies. Prepare me for the objections this room will actually raise, not the ones a Parfit seminar would raise, ordered by how much damage they would do, each phrased in the vocabulary of the camp that would raise it, with a two-minute reply and an honest note on where the reply is thin. Tell me what I should concede at the outset and the single exchange I have to win for the paper to survive, and include the question I am hoping nobody asks.
