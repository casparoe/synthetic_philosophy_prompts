I build stateful distributed systems for a living: replication, live migration, schema evolution, and the difference between object identity and value equality. I know the philosophical literature on personal identity only as a well-read layperson — the ship, the teletransporter, psychological continuity, animalism — but I know my own vocabulary cold: primary keys versus value equality, serialization and deserialization, backup and restore, replication and failover, rolling upgrades and canary deploys, hot-swapping a node while the service stays up, split-brain and consensus.

Take the debate over mind uploading and gradual replacement — the claim that a person survives when her memories and dispositions are carried over, whether by scanning and reconstruction or by replacing neurons one at a time until no original tissue remains — and restate it as faithfully as you can in my vocabulary. I want the translation to run in both directions: not merely what a scan looks like in my terms, but what each party to the debate is actually asserting, said in terms of record identity, continuity of service, migration without downtime, and what it would take for two replicas to count as the same object rather than as two objects with equal contents.

Then give me the residue report. Which claims translate cleanly? Which translate only under distortion, where I must pretend a metaphor is doing work it cannot? And which have no counterpart in my vocabulary at all — the first-person fact of being the one who wakes up, the question of whether the successor inherits obligations, the distinction between information and the medium carrying it, or the suspicion that being the same person is a decision we make rather than a fact we discover? For each residue, say whether the fault lies with my framework, which may be systematically unable to express something about identity, or with the philosophical claim, which may be trading on a distinction my vocabulary exposes as empty. Be explicit about where the translation flatters me by making a hard problem look like routine engineering, and where it flatters the philosophers by dressing a conventional choice up as a discovery.
