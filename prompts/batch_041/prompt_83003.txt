I teach a graduate seminar on practical rationality and the philosophy of action, and I'm writing the final exam. For years my toxin-puzzle question has been answered by students reciting the standard moves — that intentions are not reducible to beliefs and desires, that one cannot intend what one knows one will not do — without their ever having to work out which features of the setup are load-bearing. I want this year's question to force them to isolate those features, so I've built it around two modifications of Kavka's original case that students must work through and compare. For reference, the original: an eccentric billionaire will pay you one million dollars tomorrow morning if at midnight tonight you intend to drink a vial of toxin tomorrow afternoon; the toxin will make you painfully ill for a day but has no lasting effects; the money will already be in your bank account before the drinking is due, and you are free to change your mind; assume you know your own mind and that the billionaire can reliably detect a genuine intention; and so, since you will have no reason left to drink once the money is banked, you cannot intend tonight, and cannot collect.

The first modification adds a clawback: the midnight payment stands exactly as before, but a separate clause obliges you to pay the billionaire a penalty, large enough to outweigh the illness, if you have not drunk the toxin by the appointed time. The second removes your will from the matter altogether: at the appointed time you will be restrained and the toxin administered by force. I want the question to ask, for each modification, whether the agent can now form the intention at midnight and collect the money, why or why not, and what the answer shows about which assumptions in the original are doing the work; and then to assess the claim that the original puzzle is better understood as a problem about diachronic rationality — about the agent who re-optimizes at the moment of action and so cannot carry out a plan whose rationale lay in the past — than as a challenge to the belief–desire model of intention. I expect a strong answer to notice that the two modifications come apart, and to be able to say which one dissolves the puzzle and which leaves it intact.

The students are doctoral candidates; the exam runs three hours, and I want this essay to be answerable in about an hour and a quarter. Assume they know the original puzzle cold and have read nothing beyond it, so the question must be self-contained, must not require them to recall any particular author's position, and must not ask for citations of any kind. Please give me three things: the question exactly as it will appear on the paper; a model answer of roughly seven hundred words; and a marking scheme worth twenty marks, built from five must-hit points — the ones that genuinely separate an A answer from a B answer — each with its mark value, plus a one-line note on the characteristic way candidates miss each point. The model answer should open with its bottom line — a single sentence stating the verdict it will defend — and then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restatement of the question. Write in American English.
