I've just retired after 35-odd years in a genetics lab (yeast for the first decade, then plant genomes), and I've spent year one of retirement doing something I always meant to get round to: reading philosophy. A friend pushed me into Dennett and I've been stuck on one idea ever since, his claim that Darwin and Turing both pull the same trick on us and hand us competence without comprehension. The idea being that you can get something that behaves as if it understands -- a bacterium, a chess program, whatever -- with nothing inside that understands anything, and that both men got there by inverting the obvious order of explanation. We assume comprehension comes first and produces the competence; they say the competence comes first and the comprehension is either absent or a very late arrival. I can't quite seperate Darwin's version from Turing's, which may be my problem rather than theirs.

Anyway. Our local science-and-film group is screening Ex Machina next month and I've been asked to run the discussion. I assumed this would be easy, its literally a Turing test film, but the more I think about the ending the less I know what to say, and I'd rather not stand up in front of thirty people and bluff.

So: act as a philosopher of mind who knows this literature properly -- Dennett, the Turing test debates, the Chinese room, Block's lookup-table objection -- and who also knows how to read a film, and give me a reading of Ex Machina through the competence-without-comprehension lens. Before you write anything, brainstorm three or four candidate readings and tell me which is strongest and why. I'd rather have one reading done properly than five done thinly, and take your time with the ending, that's the crux.

Here's what I keep tripping on. The ending seems to need the comprehension back. Ava gets out, leaves Caleb locked in to die, grafts herself a human face and walks off into the crowd, and everyone reads it as her having used him and never having cared. But using, caring, pretending -- those are all words for inner states. If the film only lands because we credit her with real intentions (a real desire to get out, real indifference to Caleb), then it's quietly on the side of the picture the inversion was supposed to overturn. Is that a flaw, or is that the point -- that we can't help ourselves, that the film makes us do the projection and then shows us what it cost Caleb? I genuinely can't tell, so please take a side and defend it instead of splitting the difference.

Three more things I'd like worked in:

- Nathan as a Darwin figure. He doesn't so much design Ava's mind as breed it out of the search data his company has hoovered up, plus the phones he hacked, and he culls the earlier models. Is the film making the point that the maker of a mind needn't be a comprehender of minds? And does his death at her hands mean anything, or is that just the genre paying out?
- The Blockhead worry, the giant lookup table that passes the test without understanding a thing. Is Ava a Blockhead in nicer packaging, and does the film hand us anything that answers the objection -- the body, the confinement, the fact that she has to want her way out?
- Which scenes should I actually point to for the group? I can't rewatch before the session and neither can you, so stick to ones famous enough to be safe, and say what each is doing.

No bibliography, no citations -- I'm not writing a paper and I've no way to check anything. And tell me straight if my framing is muddled: if the competence/comprehension distinction doesn't cut this film the way I think it does, say so. Finish by telling me what the film adds to this argument that the philosophy doesn't, if it adds anything at all.
