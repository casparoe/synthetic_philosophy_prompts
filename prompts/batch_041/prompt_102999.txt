I spent a good part of last night arguing with a friend about heterophenomenology — the idea, associated with Dennett, that a science of consciousness should take a subject's first-person reports as data, interpret them charitably under something like the intentional stance, build up an account of the world the subject is describing, and simply suspend the question of whether the experiences were really as reported. My friend is a committed defender of the method, and the analogy he kept returning to was anthropology: the heterophenomenologist, he says, is just the anthropologist of the mind. You write down what the informant says, you reconstruct the world he is reporting on, and you stay neutral about whether that world exists — exactly as an ethnographer can produce a faithful account of a tribe's gods without taking a position on whether the gods are real. On his view this shows the method is presuppositionless, that it can serve as the neutral foundation of a science of consciousness, and that objections to it are really objections to anthropology as such, which no one accepts. I think he is getting more out of the analogy than it can carry, but I couldn't say precisely where it fails, so I want the audit done properly.

Please lay out the correspondence between the two cases as explicitly as you can — a table is welcome: what plays the role of the informant, the gods, the ritual and the artifacts, the ethnographer, the ethnography, and the neutrality itself. Then tell me where the mapping breaks. I am especially interested in which features of the anthropological situation the analogy quietly imports. Does the anthropologist's ability to corroborate the natives' claims against a public world — checking whether the ritual actually heals, whether the prayer is answered, whether the statue is where they say it is — have any counterpart in the heterophenomenological case, or is the only available check more reports? Does the fact that the anthropologist's subject matter is the natives' beliefs rather than the gods themselves carry over cleanly, and if it does, what does that make the heterophenomenologist's subject matter — the experience, or merely the belief about the experience? And does the informant's potential for systematic error match the subject's? An informant is not normally mistaken about what he himself believes, even when he is mistaken about the world; a subject may be mistaken about his own experience in just the way the method is designed to handle, and I want to know whether that asymmetry matters for the neutrality claim or is a distraction. Separate the disanalogies that bear on my friend's conclusion from the ones that are merely picturesque, and then tell me whether the conclusion survives: is the method as neutral as the analogy makes it look, or does the analogy smuggle in a substantive and contested view of what experience is? If the anthropological comparison is the wrong one, say what a better analogy would be, and what it would show.
