Ok so I volunteered to lead next month's session of our reading group and I'm now regretting it. It started as a Discord offshoot of the local LessWrong meetup -- people who can code and argue and have read the Sequences twice but not much actual philosophy -- and it's drifted into reading real texts. Next up is David Lewis on convention, the 1969 book, roughly the first couple of chapters plus the stretch at the end where he does language and meaning. 75 minutes over Zoom, everyones read it, at least three people skimmed it. What I want from you isn't an explanation of Lewis, I think I basically have that, it's a plan for how I explain it: the order the pieces have to go in and why, where the dependencies actually are. My instinct is that the coordination-problem setup and the idea of a salient solution have to be in place before I can even state 'common knowledge' in a way that means anything, and that if I say 'common knowledge' in the first ten minutes everyone nods and nothing lands -- but tell me if thats wrong, or if there's a dependency I'm not seeing (I keep wanting to do the 'why should I conform at all' part early and I suspect that's a mistake).

The example should carry the whole thing, and I'd rather not just use Lewis's own (the interrupted phone call, Hume's rowers, meeting someone in a city with no way to communicate) because our crowd has heard a version of the drive-on-one-side-of-the-road thing a hundred times and it makes the whole thing sound like a truism. Candidates I'm considering: the reply-all / reply-to-sender mess in a big email thread; the convention that whoever dropped the call calls back; the thing where you don't take the seat next to a stranger on a mostly-empty bus. Pick one if you can, or tell me none of them will bear the weight, and say what a carrying example here has to have and has to lack -- my guess is it needs both options live and nobody ever having chosen, but you tell me what's actually doing the work, and flag any example that smuggles in something Lewis wouldn't accept, like an enforcer, or a rule somebody wrote down, or a moment where somebody picked.

Then the misconceptions, which I can mostly predict from the inside. First, everyone in that room hears 'convention' and reaches for the Searle-ish picture -- the community collectively counts something as something else, with a shared intention doing the work -- so they'll assume Lewis needs agreement or collective intentionality, when the whole move is that a regularity sustained by mutual expectation of conformity is enough and nobody has to agree to anything. Second, 'arbitrary' gets read as 'no reason to prefer it', which then makes the normative grip look like a magic trick. Third, and this is the one I most want placed precisely, people will think the account only works for agents who can run the reasoning every time, and that it's therefore circular or unrealistic -- which is where the evolutionary / hawk-dove-ish stories (conventions of first-occupancy, or of property, just emerging in a population without anyone understanding them) do work Lewis's own machinery doesn't, and I want to know whether to bring that in as a rescue, as a rival, or hold it back entirely. Each of those should get headed off at the right point in the sequence rather than dumped in a pile at the end, which is my usual failure mode. Finish with the one question to ask in the last ten minutes that separates people who actually got it from people who just learned the vocabulary, and a short list of things I should announce out loud that I'm deliberately not doing at this level -- I'm already planning to skip the formal definition of common knowledge as an infinite hierarchy of iterations, and probably most of the convention / norm / custom taxonomy, but you tell me what else has to go.
