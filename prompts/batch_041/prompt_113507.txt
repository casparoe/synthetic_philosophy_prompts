So me and a friend got into it for about three hours last night over her pet view, lexical-threshold negative utilitarianism. Her version goes: suffering is bad, pleasure is good, but suffering above a certain severity — the really bad stuff, the kind of pain or grief that wrecks a life — has lexical priority over pleasure, meaning no amount of pleasure or relief, however large, can justify it. Below that threshold, though, pains and pleasures are commensurable and just add up the normal way, so a mild pain can be outweighed by a big enough pleasure. She also insists that the relief of pain isn't itself a pleasure, it's just the removal of a bad, so the ledger never actually goes positive.

What I want from you is a case analysis: take the turn in American medicine in the nineties and early two-thousands toward aggressively treating pain — pain as a fifth vital sign, the prescribing guidelines, the marketing of long-acting opioids — and the overdose epidemic that followed, and run the whole thing through her view. Map it. Who are the parties, what were the options and payoffs in her terms, what does the view say about the policy choice and about the individual doctor with a patient in front of her who's suffering now. Then be honest about where it goes silent or just makes stuff up. My suspicion is that this is a case of pain versus pain, which means the lexical clause about pleasure never fires and the entire verdict is being carried by where she put the threshold — tell me if I'm wrong about that. And I want the commensurability thing nailed down: she needs a single scale to trade off mild pains against mild pleasures below the threshold, so isn't she secretly committed to a common currency, and does that leak back up and undercut the lexical claim? Finish with whether this counts as her view doing well or badly, how she'd fight back, and what plain hedonistic utilitarianism says instead.
