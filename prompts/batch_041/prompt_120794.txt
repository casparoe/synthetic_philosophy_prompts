I am a novelist at work on a book about a truth commission, and I have spent the better part of a year reading the arguments that have grown up around the truth-for-justice bargains struck in South Africa, Argentina, and Rwanda. The image that recurs throughout that rhetoric — in commissioners' speeches and in the editorials defending their reports — is medical: a nation that has endured atrocity is a body carrying a wound. Silence, on this picture, is infection; testimony is the opening and cleaning of the wound; amnesty is the scar that lets the tissue close; prosecution is the reopening of a wound that had begun to knit. I have given this image to one of my characters, a commissioner defending the bargain to a room of furious survivors, and I have let him push it further than I have ever seen it pushed. Because the nation is one body, he argues, the perpetrators are not an invading organism but the patient's own tissue; to punish them is therefore not to cut out a foreign growth but to turn the body's defenses against itself — an autoimmune disease. On his account, healing requires that the whole body be treated as a single patient, that nothing be amputated (which he takes to rule out purging the security forces), and that the wound be granted its natural time, so that the demand for prosecution now is a species of impatience.

I need to know how much of this the image will bear before I write the rebuttal. Please do four things. First, give me the correspondence table: what, in the transitional-justice case, plays the part of the wound, the infection, the debridement, the scar, the surgeon, the patient, the immune system, the pain, and the passage of time — and flag every point at which the source has a feature with no target, or the target has a feature with no source. Second, tell me which features of the medical picture are being quietly imported rather than earned: health as a natural norm with one proper end state; healing as automatic, gradual, and time-bound; the body's indifference to meaning and memory; the unity of the organism, which has a single boundary, a single physiology, and no dissent; and the fact that we do not hold scars morally accountable. Third, separate the disanalogies that matter for my commissioner's conclusion from those that do not, say plainly whether his conclusion survives them, and tell me which single disanalogy his argument is most sensitive to. I am especially interested in whether punishment-is-self-harm follows from the analogy or is smuggled in by it — note that real immune systems do attack their own tissue, that we call the result a disease, and that debridement literally consists in removing tissue. Fourth, if the image cannot carry his conclusion, propose a better one: an analogy that preserves what is worth preserving here — the temporality, the demand for acknowledgment, the sense that the past is not simply closed — without the organic unity doing the illegitimate work, and tell me what that better analogy would license instead.

Please write in American English, in continuous prose with the correspondences set out as a table, and keep the register closer to an essay than to a memorandum. I am a novelist; I need to hear the argument, not receive a briefing.
