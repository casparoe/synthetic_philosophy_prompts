Essay question: Buddhist thought denies that there is a permanent, unchanging self (anattā), yet it also affirms rebirth, the efficacy of karma, and the possibility of liberation. Write an essay that (a) explains why the Pudgalavāda (Vātsīputrīya) school judged the reductionist account of the person—the view that "person" is merely a conventional designation for the five aggregates—to be inadequate; (b) explains how the doctrine of the pudgala was intended to solve this problem, including how the pudgala is said to relate to the aggregates; and (c) discusses what the Buddha's silence in the Ananda Sutta, when the wanderer Vacchagotta asks first whether there is a self and then whether there is no self, is meant to show about the limits of the question. Refer to at least one primary text or simile in your answer.

Student's response:

Buddhism is often summarized as the doctrine that there is no self, but this summary is misleading, since the Buddha also taught that beings are reborn and that actions have consequences across lives. If there is no self, what is reborn? What suffers, accumulates karma, and finally attains nirvāṇa? The Pudgalavāda school, also known as the Vātsīputrīyas, took this problem seriously and argued that the reductionist account of the person could not do the required work.

The reductionist view, defended by the Theravādins and by many Sarvāstivādins, holds that when we say "person" we are not referring to anything over and above the five aggregates of form, feeling, perception, volitional formations, and consciousness. On this view, "person" is a designation (prajñapti), not a real entity. The Pudgalavādins objected that if the person is nothing but the aggregates, then when the aggregates break up at death, the person would simply cease to exist. That would make rebirth impossible. It would also make it impossible to say that the same person who performed an action is the one who experiences its result, which is essential to karmic justice. And it would undercut the practice of loving-kindness, since there would be no being toward whom compassion is directed.

The Pudgalavādins therefore posited a pudgala, or person, which they described as neither identical with nor different from the five aggregates. The pudgala is the bearer of the burden: the aggregates are the burden, and the person is the one who carries it. This person is what transmigrates and what attains nirvāṇa. To explain the relation between the person and the aggregates, the Pudgalavādins used the simile of fire and fuel. Fire depends on fuel and cannot be said to be the same as the fuel or entirely different from it. In the same way, the pudgala depends on the aggregates but is not reducible to them.

It is important to be clear that the pudgala is not the ātman of the Brahmanical schools. The Pudgalavādins accepted the Buddha's denial of a permanent, unchanging self-substance. Their pudgala is not a sixth aggregate or an unconditioned dharma like nirvāṇa; it is inexpressible (avāktavya), and this is precisely what allows it to play the role of the person without falling into the error of eternalism. The Milindapañha's chariot simile makes the same point: just as "chariot" is a designation for the parts in a certain arrangement, "person" is a designation for the aggregates, and neither term names an extra entity.

The Ananda Sutta is often cited in this connection. When the wanderer Vacchagotta asks the Buddha whether there is a self, the Buddha is silent. When Vacchagotta asks whether there is no self, the Buddha is silent again. Later, Ananda asks why, and the Buddha explains that to answer that there is a self would be to side with the eternalists, while to answer that there is no self would be to side with the annihilationists and would leave Vacchagotta bewildered, thinking that the self he used to have no longer exists. The point of the silence, then, is that the question whether there is a self is framed in terms of categories the Buddha rejects. The Buddha's silence is not a refusal to answer a well-formed question but a refusal to endorse either of the two available metaphysical positions. What the sutta shows is that the self is ultimately ineffable and cannot be captured by either assertion or denial.
