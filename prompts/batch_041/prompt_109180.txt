I am assembling materials for a seminar on whether the anti-corruption norms that international bodies promote are genuinely universal or merely the moral furniture of one kind of state. For that purpose I need a case that a serious defender of the opposing view would accept as fair, so please treat the following as a construction task rather than an invitation to editorialise.

Take three frameworks for identifying corruption, each of which has able defenders:

1. The principal–agent framework: corruption is the misuse of entrusted authority for private benefit, where "private" is measured against the purpose for which the authority was entrusted.
2. The institutional framework: corruption is conduct that degrades the impartial functioning of an office or institution, whether or not the agent profits personally.
3. The relational framework: the morally basic obligations are those of kinship, clientship, and reciprocity, incurred by the help one has received and the help one may need; conduct is judged by whether it honours or betrays those obligations, and the claims of the impersonal office are derivative and comparatively weak.

Construct a single mundane administrative scenario — a permit, a school place, a pension form; nothing life-or-death, no violence, no judge, no police officer — in which one and the same act, described once and without stacking the facts in advance, is corrupt, and perhaps gravely so, under framework 3 while frameworks 1 and 2 either acquit it or positively commend it. The person at the centre must obtain no private benefit whatever: no money, no favour, no career advantage, not even the private satisfaction of having done right. The reversal must fall out of the structure of the obligations rather than from the official being a bad character, a crank, or a status-seeker, and the community whose judgement condemns her must be depicted so that a thoughtful defender of framework 3 can endorse that judgement rather than merely explain it away.

Then, in the analysis:

- State the verdict each framework returns and the premise that drives it.
- Identify the single feature of the case that carries the reversal, and show that altering only that feature restores the ordinary pattern, in which frameworks 1 and 2 convict and framework 3 acquits.
- Say whether the three frameworks are disagreeing about the facts, about the proper use of a thick evaluative term, or about the underlying values, and say which of them has the better claim to the word "corruption."
- Give the strongest objection available to a defender of whichever framework comes out badly, and say whether the scenario survives it.

Constraints: the scenario itself should be one paragraph; the analysis may run as long as it needs to; keep the stakes modest enough that urgency cannot settle the matter; and offer no citations, real or invented, since I have no way to check them and do not want them.
