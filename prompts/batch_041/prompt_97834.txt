I'm in a political theory reading group that has spent the term on Mill, and one of our members circulated a short essay on Mill's relationship to British India that we're supposed to discuss next week. His account runs roughly as follows. Mill spent his entire working life inside the East India Company and, unlike the more squeamish liberals of his generation, was untroubled by its work: he regarded Indian society as stagnant, its inhabitants as lacking the habits of deliberation and self-direction that representative institutions presuppose, and he therefore defended British rule there not merely as a practical necessity but as a justified despotism. The essay then makes two further claims. First, that Mill's celebrated defense of individual liberty was never meant to be universal — that the famous principle of On Liberty was silently restricted to peoples the essay calls "civilized," so that Mill's liberalism turns out to be a liberalism for Europeans only, and his career is a cautionary case study in how a philosopher's theory can be quarantined from his practice. Second, that when the Company was wound up and India passed to direct Crown rule in the wake of the 1857 upheaval, Mill accepted the change as the inevitable triumph of liberal principle over a commercial monopoly, which the essay presents as an admission that his defense of the Company had always been professional loyalty rather than conviction.

Something in this nags at me and I can't locate the trouble. I have a vague memory that Mill allowed despotism to be a legitimate mode of governing a society not yet capable of the discipline of liberty, provided the arrangement aims at that society's improvement — so the concession itself doesn't surprise me, and I don't think the essay is simply inventing a view. But the memory also suggests that Mill treated this as a temporary and tutelary arrangement rather than a verdict about what Indians were permanently capable of, and I have a competing and perhaps false recollection that he did not welcome the 1858 transfer to the Crown at all, but rather fought it. I may be mixing him up with his father, or with someone else entirely, and I don't know how much of this is my own reconstruction rather than anything Mill actually held.

What I want from you is an audit rather than a summary of your own. Go through the essay's claims one at a time and tell me, for each, what a careful student of Mill would accept as fair, what is a distortion or a conflation of two things Mill kept apart, and what is straightforwardly wrong — including, if it comes to that, my own half-remembered concessions. I'd like to know which of the essay's charges survive once the inaccurate parts are corrected, and which of them depend on the inaccuracies; in particular, whether the hypocrisy conclusion follows from anything Mill actually wrote, or whether the real tension in his position lies somewhere else. Assume I know On Liberty and On Representative Government reasonably well but nothing about the India correspondence, the Company's internal culture, or the politics of the 1858 legislation, and don't send me off to a reading list — I need the substance of the corrections, not citations.
