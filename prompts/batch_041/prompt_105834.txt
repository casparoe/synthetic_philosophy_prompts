so I'm writing a short essay arguing that the preference/bias distinction in ethics is doing less work than people think, and I want you to poke holes in it. here's the gist, roughly how i'd write it:

Most people draw the line between a moral preference and a bias in one of three ways. First, statistically: a bias is just a deviation from what most people in the group feel, so if you're the only one who's squeamish about something, that's your bias. Second, epistemically: a bias is a feeling that won't budge in the face of evidence, whereas a preference is responsive to it. Third, normatively: a bias is just a preference we happen to disapprove of, and "preference" is the polite word for the ones we like. I think all three are bad. The statistical one can't be right because a whole society can be biased, so deviating from the norm can't be the whole story. The epistemic one collapses because plenty of moral commitments are supposed to be evidence-insensitive — if you stop caring about cruelty the moment someone shows you a study, we don't congratulate you on your rationality. And the normative one is circular: it tells you bias = bad preference, and then you still need to know what makes it bad.

So I want to propose a different test, which i'm calling the cost-shift test. Take the agent's preference P and ask: if P had to be borne as a cost by the agent themselves rather than by someone else, would they still hold it? If they'd drop it, it's a bias; if they'd keep paying, it's a preference. The hiring manager who likes candidates "like us" calls it a taste; make him the one who gets passed over and it turns into a bias in his mouth too.

And then the part i'm least sure about: I want to say bias is a relational property, not a psychological one — it's a fact about who bears the cost of your preference, not about what's going on inside your head. Two people could have identical feelings and identical beliefs, and one is biased and the other isn't, purely because of where the costs land. Which feels right to me but seems to make "bias" not a mental-state concept at all, which is weird.

Can you give me comments? I care most about (1) wether the cost-shift test actually distinguishes anything or whether it's just universalizability in a trenchcoat, (2) whether the relational claim is coherent or whether i've just redescribed power and called it a day, (3) anything that's flatly wrong, and (4) whether it's interesting enough to bother finishing.

Format: exactly five numbered comments, each one or two sentences, no intro or conclusion.
