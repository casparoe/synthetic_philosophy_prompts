Im in a third year ethics seminar and we've spent the last two weeks on moral luck, which I thought I understood until I started writing down my actual reactions to cases instead of the reactions I think I'm supposed to have. Heres the list, and what I want is the principle, if any, that they add up to. (1) Two friends drink the same amount, drive home equally recklessly, one hits a kid on a bike and kills her, the other gets home and goes to bed. I blame the first one way more and I'm fine with him getting a much longer sentence; when my TA says they're equally culpable and the difference is just luck I nod, but I don't believe it. (2) A guy I know grew up in a house where fists were the normal way to end an argument and put someone in hospital in a bar fight at 19; I definately blame him, but honestly not that much, and a lot less than I'd blame myself for doing the same thing. So here luck about how you were raised lowers blame, and luck about how things turned out raises it, which seems like it should be one phenomenon or the other. (3) Two identical people in 1940, one stays in her country and ends up informing on neighbours under the occupation, one happens to have emigrated in 1938 and lives blamelessly. I don't blame the emigrant at all, so whatever I'm throwing at the informer seems to hang on a coin flip she didn't call. (4) A friend gave her kid an ordinary over the counter medicine and the kid had a freak reaction and nearly died; nobody could have known, and I don't blame her in the slightest, but I do think she owes the kid something -- months of hospital sitting, whatever -- and it would be bizarre to say she owes nothing just because she's blameless. (5) Two doctors both miss the same rare presentation; one because anyone would, one because he was rushed and didn't look properly. The second I blame a lot, the first not at all, even though the patient dies either way.

My own stab at a principle, which I'd rather you test and repair than just replace: blameworthiness = how bad your will was given what you knew, full stop, and harm is a seperate matter that only fixes what you owe and how much of a response other people are entitled to make. On that, the unlucky driver isn't worse than the lucky one, he just owes more; the informer is worse because of what she chose in her circumstances, not because of how it turned out; my friend with the medicine owes but isn't blameworthy; and the rushed doctor is blameworthy while the other isn't. The trouble is that I don't only think the unlucky driver owes more, I think he's worse, and I have the same feeling about a pair like the painter who walks out on his family and turns out to be brilliant versus the one who walks out and paints nothing -- the failure seems to say something about him as a person that the success doesn't, which is exactly backwards from the principle.

So: is there a version of this that keeps most of my verdicts, which ones does it force me to drop, and what does it predict about a case I haven't listed -- the same small lie told in a job interview, once in a world where it happens to cost another applicant the job and once where it costs nothing?
