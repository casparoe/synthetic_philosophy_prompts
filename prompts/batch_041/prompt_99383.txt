Ok so I've got a works-in-progress talk in three weeks in my dept's phil of science group and I'm the only grad student presenting, so it's basically a firing squad. I need you to build me a Q&A prep sheet.

The thesis: free-floating rationales (Dennett's thing — reasons that nobody actually has, that selection "endorses") are legitimate explanations in biology, and I want to defend them against the mechanists. My twist, which is the part I actually care about, is a criterion I'm calling the counterfactual-upkeep test. Roughly: a rationale counts as genuinely operative — not just a story we retro-fit — only if the system's behavior gets adjusted when the rationale's recommendation changes while the proximal cue stays fixed. So the wasp that re-drags the cricket forever when you move it fails the test (cue fixed, no upkeep), but a corvid that re-caches food after it's been watched passes, because the behavior tracks something the wasp's does not. I argue the mechanists are wrong to demand a mechanism before allowing the rationale, but I also think the Dennett crowd is wrong to think you never need a test at all.

The audience is specific and I know them: mostly New Mechanist types who want every function cashed out as a mechanism and are allergic to anything that smells like teleology, two evo-devo people who think adaptationism is a disease and will jump on any just-so story, and one phil of mind guy who's a Dennett fan and thinks I've watered down the original position. Give me the objections I'll actually get from these people — in the order they'll come and the way each of them would phrase it, not the generic objections from the internet.
