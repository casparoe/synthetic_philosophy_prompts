I'm a third-year PhD student in philosophy working on AI safety, and for the past few months I've been sitting in on a reading group where two colleagues have gotten stuck in a recurring argument about low-impact AI. The question is whether an AI can be instructed to avoid side effects -- to leave the world roughly as it would have been, or to avoid disturbing things that are not part of its task -- without first giving it a full specification of what is valuable. Ana thinks yes: we can define impact in a value-thin way, for example as the degree to which the system's actions reduce the set of future options available to other agents relative to a baseline of inaction, or as the degree to which it changes the world along dimensions that are unnecessary for its task. Ben thinks no: any such definition must choose a baseline, a set of agents, a distance metric, a time horizon, and a list of which features count as relevant, and every one of those choices encodes substantive values. They have been going back and forth for weeks, and I would like an adjudication of the actual exchange rather than a general essay on alignment.

Here is the exchange as best I can reconstruct it. (1) Ana says the whole point of a low-impact constraint is that it can be specified before you know the AI's final values: you do not need to know whether someone prefers a red wall or a blue wall, only that you should not foreclose their ability to choose. She offers a case: an AI tasked with making coffee notices a paint bucket and a brush; if it paints the wall, it has changed the world in a way no one asked for, and a measure based on option-preservation flags that as impact without deciding whether red or blue is better. (2) Ben replies that the case only looks value-free because we have already agreed that painting the wall is a side effect; if the wall's owner had been about to paint it themselves, the AI's painting might preserve their option to have a painted wall, and if the owner is indifferent, then calling it impact at all seems to require a preference for the status quo. He adds that an AI could reduce its measured impact by doing nothing while a fire spreads, or by freezing all options in place, which suggests the measure is tracking deviation rather than harm. (3) Ana answers that no measure is perfect and that low-impact is meant to be combined with a task objective and a separate safety check, so the do-nothing case is ruled out by the task; she also says Ben is conflating a thin value -- do not destroy other agents' option sets -- with a full value specification, which is the thing she denies is needed. (4) Ben says that is exactly the sleight of hand: do not destroy option sets is itself a substantive value claim, and a controversial one, since some options are worthless, some harms do not consist in lost options (he mentions pain), and some option-preserving actions are paternalistic or unjust. He says that if you add a task objective to rule out do-nothing, then the impact measure is no longer independent of the objective, so the claim that it can be defined without a full specification of value is false as stated. (5) Ana responds that Ben has set the bar impossibly high: if any value-laden choice counts as a full specification, then no safety property short of full alignment is definable, and low-impact was never meant to be that. She says the useful question is whether the value commitments are thin enough to be shared across many value systems, not whether they are literally value-free. Ben replies that this is a retreat to thin enough for whom, and that without a principled account of thinness the view collapses into we will know it when we see it.

Please adjudicate move by move: for each of (1) through (5), say who has the better point and why, and rate the move from clearly mistaken to clearly right. Then say what each party is missing -- in particular, whether there is a coherent middle position between Ana's value-thin constraint and Ben's no-free-lunch argument, and what that position would have to look like. I am not looking for a literature review or citations; I want the argumentative structure assessed. If you think the disagreement is partly verbal -- about what counts as a full specification of value -- say so and explain what turns on the distinction.
