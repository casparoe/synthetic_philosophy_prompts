I'm writing the final for my upper-level seminar on rational choice and epistemology, and I need one long essay question on Dutch book arguments. My problem is that every version I've written can be answered striaght off the shelf: the student recites that if your credences violate the probability axioms then a bookie can offer you a set of bets, each of which you find fair, whose combined result loses you money for sure, and that's a wrap. Full marks, no thinking. So this year I've built a specific case I want them to actually grind through, and I'd like you to turn it into the question, plus a model answer and a marking scheme.

The case. An agent is thinking about whether it will rain tomorrow (call it R). She says she is 0.55 confident it will rain and 0.5 confident it won't — yes, incoherent, that's the point. She's an expected utility maximiser over money with utility u(w) = ln w, and she currently holds $4. A bookie who knows her utility function and her wealth, but who cannot see her credences, offers to sell her a $1 bet on R and a $1 bet on ¬R, each at whatever price makes her exactly indifferent between taking it and not taking it.

I want the question to force them to (a) work out both prices and say whether the classic two-bet Dutch book actually works against her; (b) if it doesn't, name precisely which premise of the Dutch book argument has broken, and say whether the bookie can repair it by changing the stakes — and if so, what that repair costs the argument; and (c) say what any of this should do to the claim that incoherent credences are irrational, and whether a Dutch book was ever the right thing to be worried about in the first place. It should be answerable in about 50 minutes, and it needs to include the case, not assume the students already have it.

Then give me a model answer of about 700 words, showing the arithmetic rather than just asserting the numbers, and a marking scheme out of 20. For the marking scheme I want the must-hit moves listed separately with the marks each is worth, plus a line on what should cost marks — students who just restate the theorem, students who think the example shows incoherent credences are harmless, students who never mention the utility assumption, that kind of thing. It needs to seperate a 15 from an 18.
