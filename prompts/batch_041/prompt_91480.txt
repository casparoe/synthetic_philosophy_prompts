So I've been working on a piece about self-improvement culture and I've fallen down a Franklin hole. Here's the position I've basically talked myself into, and I want you to try to take it apart: that old Ben's project of arriving at moral perfection isn't just a quaint self-help stunt, it's actually a defensible ethics of how a person should try to be good — better, even, than either following rules or just trusting your gut about what a decent person would do. The scheme is the interesting part. Thirteen virtues, one per week, cycling through the whole list four times a year, and he keeps a little book with a grid where every slip gets a black mark. He puts Temperance first on purpose, on the theory that a clear head makes the rest of the list easier. He adds Humility only at the end, after a friend tells him he comes off as insufferably proud. And he's upfront that he never got close — says the whole point was that he ended up a better and happier man for the trying.

The bit I keep chewing on is Order — the schedule-and-tidy-desk virtue, the one he flat-out admits he could never keep — and how breezily he shrugs it off. I can't tell if that shrug is the most honest thing in the whole account or a total cop-out, and honestly I want to know which.

So play it straight. What would count against the view that this method is a genuinely good model for trying to be good? Not the lazy "he was a hypocrite" stuff — I want the real defeaters, sorted by how much they'd actually cost me. Things I'd want you to press on: does the whole thing collapse into mere habit-training and never once touch moral understanding — and if so, is that fatal or fine? Is a virtue list like that just arbitrary — why those thirteen, why that order, why Temperance first — and what would have to be true for the list to be more than one man's preference? Is the Humility-at-the-end move self-undermining, since you can't set out to be humble the way you set out to be tidy? And does treating your own character as an engineering project miss the point that alot of what makes us good or bad is structural and not a matter of individual willpower at all?

Also tell me honestly whether this view can even be falsified, or whether the defender just falls back on "well, he was happier" and makes it untouchable — and if it is untouchable, is that a fatal problem for a view like this, or is that just the kind of thing a view like this is? I'd like to know which defeaters a committed Franklin defender would themselves concede as counting against them, and which they'd wave off.

I'll say up front what would move me most: any case where the method, followed properly rather than sloppily, reliably produces someone we'd recognize as worse rather than better — a smug, tidy, self-satisfied person who's technically hitting his marks. If the machine can crank that out, that's the thing I can't get around, and I'd want to know if that's a real risk or just bad luck with the operator.
