I am a novelist, not a mathematician, and for the past year I have been trying to think clearly about a problem that keeps surfacing in my working life: how much of myself to stake on the long-shot book. Someone recently described to me a way of thinking they called geometric rationality, and I have not been able to shake it. The gist, as I understood it, is this: when you face a sequence of decisions whose outcomes compound, the thing to maximize is not the arithmetic average of your outcomes but the geometric one — the product, so to speak — and once you do that, a whole family of familiar intuitions about risk flips over. My interlocutor said this is the same principle behind the betting strategy known as the Kelly criterion, in which you stake a fraction of your capital proportional to your edge, the fraction being chosen to maximize the expected logarithm of your wealth rather than its expected value. I understand the slogan; I do not understand the machinery, and I suspect the slogan is doing a great deal of hidden work.

Please act as a patient expert in decision theory, probability, and information theory and explain this conceptual issue to me from the ground up, as if I were intelligent but had no formal training beyond school algebra. Before you begin writing, think carefully about the several distinct things that might be going on here — the difference between the average of a quantity and the average of its logarithm, the difference between the outcome of a single bet and the outcome of a lifetime of bets, the sense in which a sequence of bets is one object rather than many, and whatever it is that information theory has to do with any of this — and decide which of those threads actually carry the argument and which are red herrings. Do not rush to reassure me that it is all very simple.

What I want is an explanation, in continuous prose, of why maximizing the geometric mean of one's wealth, or equivalently the expected logarithm of it, produces a different and in some sense more defensible policy than maximizing expected wealth, and why that policy is the one a person would want who expects to make many bets rather than one. I want the intuition rather than a proof, but I want the intuition to be honest: where the argument depends on an assumption, name it; where the famous advice is genuinely contested, say so. I have heard both that Kelly betting is dangerously aggressive and that it is absurdly timid, and I would like to understand how one rule can attract both complaints at once.

Then turn the machinery on my own case, because that is why I am asking. I am deciding how to divide the next three years between a safe, contractually guaranteed piece of work and an ambitious novel that will probably fail and might, if it succeeds, succeed enormously — and I will have to make further such decisions with whatever I have left afterward. I do not want a formula or a number. I want you to show me how the geometric way of thinking reframes the choice: what it treats as the unit being maximized, whose interests it is really protecting, and what it says about the temptation to stake everything on the one great book. Be concrete about where the analogy between a career and a sequence of bets illuminates and where it breaks down, since I am suspicious that a novelist's life is not a casino.

Write to me throughout as one person writing to another: continuous prose only, no headings, no bullet points, no bold, no tables — the way one would write a long and careful letter. Take the space you need. I would rather have one thing explained well than five things gestured at.
