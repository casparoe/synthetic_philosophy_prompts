I'd like you to act as a philosopher with expertise in normative ethics and moral psychology, and to think carefully before you answer. I'm not a philosopher by training, but I read a fair amount of ethics, and I keep running into a shape of objection that I have no name for. I'd like to know whether there's an established concept for it; if there isn't, I'd like one coined. Either way, the label matters less to me than getting the shape right.

Here is what I keep seeing. Someone attacks an ethical theory, but not by denying that its verdicts about what to do are correct, and not by complaining that its demands are too heavy. The complaint is aimed at what the theory requires the agent to be thinking. Three instances I've collected:

1. Two old friends, one of whom has been visiting the other every week for years. The visitor says: I come because I promised, and I keep my promises. The friend says: I would rather you came because you wanted to. The visits are identical, and the promise-keeper is arguably the more reliable friend; the objection is not about the behaviour at all.

2. A parent who loves their child decides how to treat the child by consulting a manual of parental duties, and consults it correctly, so that the verdicts reached are the ones a good parent would have reached anyway. Something still seems defective here, and it is not the verdicts.

3. A theory tells you to be generous, and then adds that you should be generous because it is required. The second half seems to undermine the first in a way that has nothing to do with whether generosity is in fact required: it asks the agent to stand outside the disposition in order to act on it.

What these share, as I see it, is that the theory is granted the action and convicted on the accompaniment. It is faulted for inserting a justifying thought where the agent's attachment should simply be doing the work, or for requiring the agent to have a reason for something that ought not to need one. And the objection gets slippery in a way that bothers me: sometimes it sounds like a complaint about the theory, sometimes like a complaint about a particular person who happens to deliberate that way (the manual-consulting parent is a person, not a theory), and sometimes like mere distaste dressed up as argument.

So: does this have a name? I have seen words like integrity and self-effacement used in this vicinity, but I cannot tell whether either is the concept I am after, and I suspect my three cases may not all be instances of the same complaint. Please begin by brainstorming the candidate concepts that come closest, then compare each one against all three cases and tell me which fits, which does not, and why. In particular, sort out whether the complaint concerns how much is demanded or what is demanded; whether it is an objection to the theory itself or only to the theory's use as a decision procedure; and whether it can be pressed against a theory that is entirely indifferent to the agent's motives. Then, whether or not an established name exists, give me a precise formulation of the phenomenon together with the boundary cases that separate it from its neighbours, and tell me plainly if I am conflating two or more different complaints.
