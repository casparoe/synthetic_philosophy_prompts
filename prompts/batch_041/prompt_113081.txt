OK so I've been roped into giving the opening remarks at this one-day symposium our department runs every spring, for the Medieval and Renaissance Studies program — which the dean keeps hinting he wants to fold into a generic "Early Europe" cluster — and the whole unspoken point of the day is basically to justify that we still exist. The room is a mess: roughly half are medievalists and early modernists who will catch it immediately if I say anything sloppy about periodization, and the other half are undergrads, two alums who fund the thing, and the dean himself, who apparently wants to hear about "interdisciplinary synergies" and definately does not know what the Investiture Controversy was.

I want these remarks to actually take a position on whether the Middle Ages and the Renaissance are real things or just later inventions — not dodge it. And I want that position to do the structural work of the speech: depending on how I answer it, some of the things I'd normally say about why the program matters become dishonest or just empty, and I want the talk to feel that pressure rather than floating above it. Raw material I keep circling: the medievalists' gripe that "Renaissance" is a rebirth story that turns the previous thousand years into an embarrassing mistake; the fact that "Middle Ages" began life as a filler label for the gap between the ancients and whoever happened to be doing the naming; the continuity crowd who say the Scientific Revolution wasn't a revolution so much as a slow drift, and that calling it a revolution smuggles in a whole politics-flavored theory of sudden rupture; the lumpers-versus-splitters thing, where the real question is whether periods are just convenient boxes we impose or whether something in the sources genuinely shifts around 1500-ish; and the uncomftorable point that the periodization did institutional work — it built the chairs, the journals, the funding lines — so if I stand up and say the categories are our inventions, I'm sawing off the branch the program is sitting on.

Please write the remarks — six or seven minutes spoken, so it has to sound like a person talking, not an essay read aloud — and make it land for both halves of the room. Don't let it curdle into a lecture, and don't let it dissolve into a mush of "well, it's complicated."
