A friend of mine who has been reading a great deal of libertarian political philosophy has an argument he deploys at every dinner party, and I have begun to suspect it proves far more than he intends. (1) Theft is taking what belongs to another person without that person's consent, under threat of force. (2) A person comes to own a thing by mixing his labor with it or by voluntary exchange with its previous owner. (3) Taxation takes part of what a person owns without his consent, under threat of fine or imprisonment. (4) Therefore taxation is theft. (5) Stolen goods remain stolen however many innocent hands they pass through; a title acquired from a thief is no title at all. (6) Nearly every existing title to land and resources traces back to an original appropriation — enclosure, conquest, settlement — that shut other people out of what they had been using, without their consent. (7) So by (5) and (6), nearly every existing title is already stolen, the taxes levied on it amount to redistributing stolen goods, and no present owner has standing to complain of theft. He insists that (7) is a reductio of my reading rather than of his argument, and that (1) through (4) stand on their own. I would like to keep (1) and (4) if I can, and I cannot tell whether the trouble lies in his original steps or only in my extension of them. Which step should I give up, why did it look secure, and what does each available repair cost me — in particular, can the historical claim in (6) be had without a premise about who owned the earth in the first place, and if it cannot, what is left of (2)?
