I teach an upper-level seminar on Kant's ethics, and the final exam is a single essay question. I want the question on the murderer at the door to be unanswerable by the usual recital — that Kant is an absolutist, that this is the price of absolutism, that any sensible Kantian would amend the rule. The crux I want tested is the scope of the prohibition: whether it governs the content of what is said, or only declarations addressed to someone who has a standing entitlement to the speaker's sincerity. The question should hold that interpretive issue apart from the further question of what else the speaker may do — stay silent, evade, or answer truly in the expectation of being misread — and it should force students to commit to a reading, to say what each reading has to concede to the other, and to say whether on either reading the case is a genuine objection to Kant or dissolves into a question about standing rather than about truthfulness. Please write the question, a model answer of roughly 700 words, and a marking scheme out of 25 that names the specific moves an A answer must make and the specific errors that should cap a student at a B.
