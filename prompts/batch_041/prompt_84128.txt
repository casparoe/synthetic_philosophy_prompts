I run a small advisory practice that prepares executives for high-stakes negotiations — union contracts, supplier standoffs, regulatory settlements — and I want to replace the credibility memo my analysts write for each client, which is currently mostly intuition dressed up as analysis, with a procedure they can run in about an hour. The job is to take any commitment the client proposes to make or to rely on — a threat to walk, a promise to hold a line, a red line, a take-it-or-leave-it — and return a verdict on whether the other side will treat it as binding. Ground it in epistemic game theory rather than folk negotiating wisdom, and keep it usable by analysts with undergraduate economics and no game theory; general counsel signs anything we put in writing, so the procedure must not smuggle in a conclusion about legality. It should make us check, in order: whether following through would be rational for the client at the moment of truth, and whether the other side can see that we can see that they see it; whether the commitment is merely public or actually common knowledge, with a rule about what kind of act creates common knowledge and why one more round of confirmation does not; whether the client's board or membership must ratify, so that the commitment has to be common knowledge in two rooms rather than one; and where the procedure stops and tells the client not to say the thing at all. Run it on two cases: a client that threatens to drop its sole supplier unless it cuts prices by eight percent, and a client that has already promised its members, in a recorded all-hands, that pensions are off the table ahead of a ratification vote. Tell me which case the procedure decides against the client's wishes, mark the steps that are judgment rather than procedure, and say where it will misfire — especially when the other side is not the forward-looking optimizer the theory assumes.
