I'm a science-and-ideas journalist — I write a column about the small machinery of everyday morality — and I'm currently 30,000 feet up with no wifi, trying to finish a piece about why we bother keeping pointless promises: the ones where nobody's relying on me, nothing hangs on it, and yet bailing still makes me a heel. A friend who did a PhD in social ontology got me into this, and she handed me an argument that promises never actually bind anyone. I've been carrying it around for a month because every step looks fine to me and the conclusion is obviously wrong. Here it is, with her coinages kept: (1) No normative free lunch — an act can put you under a new obligation only if there's already a norm in force saying that acts of that kind do. (2) A norm is in force for me only if I'm already bound to comply with it: either I consented to it, or something non-promissory already obliges me to go along. (And the consent can't itself be a promise, or we spin in a circle.) (3) So either the norm that lets promises bind is one I'm antecedently bound by for non-promissory reasons, or it isn't binding on me at all. (4) If it isn't, no promise ever binds. (5) If it is, then when I keep a promise, what actually binds me is that prior non-promissory obligation — don't disappoint reliance, do your part in a useful practice, whatever it turns out to be — and the promise itself is just a switch that turns a pre-existing duty on. (6) And a switch isn't a source, so a promise can never bind me to anything I wasn't already obligated to do anyway. (7) But gratuitous promises do bind: if I promise to meet you at that terrible cafe at 8am, no one's relying on me, no practice is at stake, I get nothing out of it, and yet blowing it off makes me a jerk. (8) So something in here is false.
