I'm in a reading group that has spent the term on the question of what life is, and we keep circling back to a passage in Aristotle's On the Soul, Book II, chapter 1, that I can follow sentence by sentence but not as a whole. The passage says that "by life we mean self-nutrition and growth (with its correlative decay)"; it concludes that "the soul is the first grade of actuality of a natural body having life potentially in it"; it uses the example of an axe or an eye, saying that if the relevant capacity were removed the thing "would have ceased to be an axe, except in name"; it insists that "We must not understand by that which is 'potentially capable of living' what has lost the soul it had, but only what still retains it; but seeds and fruits are bodies which possess the qualification"; and it ends by admitting that "we have no light on the problem whether the soul may not be the actuality of its body in the sense in which the sailor is the actuality of the ship." Our group's disagreement is not about the Greek so much as about what work these images are doing.

Please help me understand the passage, with attention to three things in particular. First, what exactly is the function of the axe/eye analogy — is it meant to establish that life is an essential property of the thing that has it, or only that the word "organ" is relative to a capacity? Second, why does Aristotle add the caveat about seeds and fruits, and how does that caveat sit with his insistence that a body which has lost its soul is not alive even in name? Third, is the closing sailor-and-ship remark a genuine concession that the soul-body relation might be merely extrinsic, as a sailor is to a ship, or is it a rhetorical way of marking an unresolved question that the hylomorphic account is supposed to answer? A friend in the group also wants to know whether the "first grade of actuality" language commits Aristotle to a graded or scalar notion of life, which would matter for how we treat viruses, prions, and a self-sustaining autocatalytic chemical network in a flow reactor — cases where the boundary between living and non-living seems to be a matter of degree rather than kind. I'd like your answer to say how confident you are in each interpretive claim you make, and to single out the one point you are least sure about.
