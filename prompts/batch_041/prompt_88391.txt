I write a column on science and ideas, and I'm drafting a piece on what becomes of consequentialism once you take seriously that the downstream effects of our actions are largely untraceable. I've ended up defending a position I want you to stress-test. It has two parts. The first is an asymmetry thesis: our ignorance about consequences is not uniform — the near-term effects of an intervention can often be identified with reasonable confidence, whereas its effects a century out are not merely uncertain but unstructured, ramifying through a causal web we have no grip on. The second is a decision rule that follows from it, which I have been calling the legibility rule: where the far future is opaque, prefer interventions whose consequences stay traceable and attributable — paying for cataract surgeries rather than arguing for a shift in an institutional norm — and treat long-run expected-value optimization as unwarranted confidence rather than ambition.

Take the role of a philosopher at home in decision theory, welfare economics, and long-run ethics: sympathetic to consequentialism, allergic to hand-waving. Begin with a few lines stating the strongest version of my position, so I can see you have read it charitably, then tell me what would count against it, ranked by how much it should move me. I want the objections that bite: the case that the asymmetry is an artifact of what we can measure rather than a feature of the world; cases where the legibility rule and the asymmetry thesis pull apart; the possibility that cluelessness is symmetric and so undercuts my own near-term prescriptions; and what a defender should concede as a real cost rather than a bullet to bite. Separate what would be decisive from what would be merely awkward. Finally, say honestly whether the position has been qualified into unfalsifiability — say, by retreating to interventions claimed to be robustly positive — and whether that is a defect in a view of this kind or simply the price of doing ethics under uncertainty. Where possible, note what my position predicts that longtermism and ordinary near-term philanthropy do not.
