I'm a backend engineer at a mid-sized software company, and for the past six years I have also been the volunteer treasurer of a small nonprofit that runs an after-school mentoring program in my city. In three weeks the organization holds its annual donor dinner, and the board has asked me to give the appeal: the six-to-eight minute speech after dessert that is supposed to send people home having written a check. I have given it twice, both times the conventional way, with an outcome statistic, one child's story, a graph, and an ask. I do not think I can give that speech again, and I would like you to write the one I can give.

The difficulty is that I have slowly become convinced that I have no idea what the long-run consequences of our work are, and that this is not a gap that more measurement would close. I write software that runs experiments for a living, so I am not sentimental about this. When we report that ninety percent of the children in the program improved their reading scores, we mean that we compared them to a comparison group we assembled ourselves, over one school year, on one test. Past that, the effects fan out: a child is a little more confident, a teacher is a little more patient, a family moves, a job is taken or not taken. A girl in our 2013 cohort is now, as far as I know, a nursing student, and I cannot tell you whether that has anything to do with us; another child from the same cohort is in prison. If someone asked me whether the organization has, on balance, made the world better or worse since we started, I would have to say that I do not know, and I am not confident the question has an answer anyone could give. I also notice that two things feel different to me even though I suspect they should not: not knowing the far-off effects, and the possibility that there is no fact of the matter about the total, because the effects are scattered across so many people and times that the total is a fiction. I would like the speech to be honest about both, if they really are two things. And I keep noticing that I am just as ignorant about what would have happened had we never existed, so my ignorance is not by itself an argument for shutting the place down; I want the speech to make that point without it functioning as a debating trick that lets me off the hook.

What I cannot do is stand at the podium and say that we know this works, which is roughly what the board's draft of my remarks has me saying. What I also cannot do is stand there and suggest it may all be pointless, because the room will contain staff and volunteers who need the evening to mean something, donors who came for the satisfaction of a measurable win, my parents, who wrote the organization's first check, and one board member who taught philosophy for thirty years and will hear every place where I fudge. I need a speech that does not overclaim, does not deflate the room, and does not turn into a lecture, and that still ends with a request for money I can make with a straight face. I have a half-formed thought that the thing I am least clueless about is the practice of giving rather than any particular effect of it, but I do not know how to say that without sounding evasive.

Please write the speech, at roughly a thousand words. Do not name any philosophers or cite any sources; I could not defend a citation if a donor asked me about it, and I do not want the speech to sound assembled from reading. If one of the constraints above turns out to be unsatisfiable, say so in a short note after the speech rather than quietly dropping it.
