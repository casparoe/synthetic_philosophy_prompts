I should like a piece of sustained deadpan satire, of roughly 750 words, cast as an internal staff handbook issued by a fictional public body — call it the Office of Omission Accounting — in a jurisdiction whose courts have adopted, without qualification, the doctrine that an agent causes an outcome whenever that outcome would not have occurred had the agent acted differently. The Office exists to help the jurisdiction's institutions — hospitals, insurers, utilities, ministries — live with the consequence that everyone is now a cause of nearly everything they failed to prevent, and to convert as much harmful conduct as possible into the cheaper category of a mere omission. The piece is to be written from inside that world, as an in-universe document, and should remain in the bureaucratic register throughout, cheerful and procedural even where the subject matter is fatal. No winking, no editorialising, no authorial voice stepping out to explain the philosophy.

The humour must come from the metaphysics rather than from invented facts; a specialist in the philosophy of causation should recognise the positions at stake and find the exaggeration fair. Work the following into the handbook as numbered sections of standing procedure:

1. The default-value manoeuvre. The Office models the world as a system of variables, and treats an omission as nothing more than a variable left at its default value. This should be presented as a technical convenience. The Office should not notice — or should notice only in a bracketed footnote — that the Office itself sets the defaults, so that what counts as doing nothing is a decision rather than a discovery.

2. Causal selection. If every failure is a cause, then the gardener who failed to water the plant and the neighbour who also failed to water it stand on exactly the same causal footing. The handbook should treat the difference between them as a question of materiality or attributable interest — an accounting category, with thresholds and bands — while continuing to insist that the underlying causal facts are equal and that the difference is one of presentation only.

3. Boundary cases. Withdrawing a ventilator; closing a valve; declining to open a valve; switching off a pump that was already running; and the familiar contrast between a doctor who withdraws treatment and a doctor who administers a lethal dose. The handbook must classify these, and its classifications should visibly depend on which way the paperwork has been filed.

4. A worked example in which the scheme plainly fails: a case in which the Office, to avoid holding the entire jurisdiction liable for everything, quietly reintroduces the very notions of duty, role and legitimate expectation that its official theory of causation was designed to do without.

The result should be legible to a reader who knows no philosophy, and recognisable, to one who does, as an exercise in the profligacy of the counterfactual account, in the appeal to norms that distinguishes causes from mere conditions, and in the act/omission asymmetry as it actually operates in law and medicine. Give the document a title, a reference number, numbered clauses, and a closing line directing the reader to a further source of guidance. Do not cite any literature, and do not name any living person. Write in British English.
