I teach a graduate seminar on safety guarantees for learned systems, and I am drafting the final examination. The exam has a single long essay, and I want a question that a student cannot answer by reciting the familiar slogan that worst-case guarantees are conservative while average-case guarantees are informative. To make recitation impossible, the question will be set inside a specification that I hand to the students; please treat that specification as fixed and build the question on it.

Specification, to appear in the exam and to be treated as self-contained:

- A policy π incurs a loss ℓ(π, e) in [0,1] in each environment e drawn from a set E.
- A guarantee statement is a pair G = (S, ε), with S a subset of E and ε in [0,1], read as: for every e in S, ℓ(π, e) ≤ ε. Call S the scope and ε the tolerance.
- D is the reference distribution over E that the designer used when evaluating π; D* is the unknown distribution under which π will actually be deployed.
- The residual of G under a distribution P is ρ_P(G) = P(E minus S).

The framework is meant to make vivid that a tolerance can always be met by shrinking the scope, and a scope can always be preserved by inflating the tolerance; I have been calling the resulting trade-off the guarantee frontier, and the unnoticed shrinkage of scope at fixed tolerance scope inflation. Students will be told that a guarantee statement which does not also commit to a claim about ρ_{D*}(G) is silent about deployment.

Write the following three things.

First, the essay question itself. It should be answerable in ninety minutes and should require the student to demonstrate exactly three things, in this order. (a) That an average-case guarantee under D and a worst-case guarantee over some set A contained in E are two points on the same frontier and that neither implies the other, supported by an explicit construction on a small finite E. (b) That both guarantees can hold simultaneously while deployment is nonetheless catastrophic, together with the single additional assumption that would repair the gap. (c) Why that assumption is especially fragile in the setting of alignment, and which of the two guarantees a designer should rely on when D* may differ from D. The question should be phrased so that a student who only knows the slogan fails it, and should not depend on any named literature; the exam forbids citations.

Second, a model answer of roughly 650 words. It should contain no citations and should make each of the three demonstrations explicitly, with the finite construction written out concretely.

Third, a marking scheme consisting of five must-hit points, with the marks for each summing to 20, a one-line note on the error that most commonly costs students each point, and a closing sentence distinguishing an A answer from a B answer. End the whole document with a final line of the form Crux: followed by one sentence naming the single idea that separates a top answer from a competent summary.
