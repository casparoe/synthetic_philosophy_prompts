For the past couple of years I've kept a running list of my gut verdicts about the moral status of video-game characters, mostly to see whether they hang together, and I've reached the point where I'd like someone to tell me what principle, if any, is doing the work. I'm a longtime reader of the rationalist blogs, so I'm content with reflective-equilibrium framing: I'd sooner have a principle that earns its keep on the cases than one that flatters my prior. Here is the list. In an old open-world game, running down a crowd of identical pedestrians who never react and are instantly replaced — feels like nothing at all; I don't believe I've done anything to anyone. Betraying a companion who has an authored arc, and who has dialogue in which she registers the betrayal — feels like a small wrong, and specifically a wrong done to her, not merely a failure of my own aesthetic. In a permadeath run, letting that same companion die for good feels worse, and feels like a loss that is really hers. Torturing a captured enemy in a game that rewards it, with the character begging — feels worse than the killing, and worse than the crowd, and I cannot tell whether that is squeamishness about the animation or something about the character. Starting a new save and so 'erasing' everyone I had grown attached to — feels like nothing, no wrong at all, even though it destroys the same characters whose permadeath made me grieve. A procedurally generated NPC with no authored lines — nothing. The death of a beloved character in a novel — something, but a different something, and I don't feel I have wronged anyone. Another human player's avatar in a multiplayer match — matters, but I'm fairly sure the wrong is done to the player rather than the avatar. And modding a game specifically to keep a character in permanent, scripted agony — feels distinctly worse than anything the game itself offers.

The pair I cannot reconcile is the permadeath companion and the save-file wipe: the same destruction, opposite verdicts. My tentative principle is that a character matters to the degree that she can be a target of the reactive attitudes — that she can seem to hold me responsible, respond to me, or be wronged by me — and that what does the grounding is what I've been calling authored interiority, the game presenting her as having a point of view that can be betrayed. I suspect this over-generates in the novel case and under-generates in the torture case, but I would like you to tell me. What I want is the principle, or a small set of candidate principles, stated precisely enough that I can see which clause each of my verdicts is riding on; its verdicts on cases I haven't listed (say, a game whose NPCs are driven by a general-purpose language model and answer unpredictably; a game that reveals late that its characters are recordings of real, now-dead people; a character who in the fiction is herself a moral patient with rights; and a speedrun in which I skip the companion's whole arc so that she never exists in my save at all); and an honest account of which of my verdicts the principle forces me to surrender, and why I should surrender them. Please write in British English.
