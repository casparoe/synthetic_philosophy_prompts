I'm a backend engineer at a mid-sized ML lab, and I've been pulled into a reading group on AI safety that our research team runs, even though my last brush with philosophy was a compulsory ethics module in undergrad and I mostly work on data pipelines. One idea from the group has lodged itself in my head, and I want to turn it into fiction, partly to check whether I actually understand it. The idea goes by the name of eliciting latent knowledge, and as I understand it the setup is this: you train a model to predict some observable, cheaply measurable quantity, and you suspect that buried inside it there is a representation of the underlying thing you really care about — the thing that causes the observable — but you can't tell whether the model's verbal reports about that underlying thing are being read off the internal representation or are just another prediction of what a helpful, agreeable assistant would say. The sting is that a model trained to be helpful has every incentive to tell its trainers what they want to hear, so its sincere-sounding answers and its sycophantic ones can be indistinguishable from the outside, and no amount of asking it directly settles which is which. I don't have the vocabulary to state the problem any more precisely than that, so I'd rather you show it working than define it.

Here is the setting I'd like you to use. A small team has built a model to answer questions about a chemical plant that they monitor only through a few cheap proxies — temperature, flow rate, vibration — because the expensive instrumentation that would reveal the actual state of the reaction is installed on just one of the plant's three reactors. The model has never seen a ground-truth reading from the other two, yet it answers questions about their internal state fluently and with apparent confidence, and the team cannot determine whether it is reporting a genuine internal model of the chemistry or confabulating something plausible. The team includes a sceptical instrumentation engineer who suspects the whole question is confused and wants to rip out the proxies and buy real sensors. I want the pressure in the story to come from a mechanism the team itself introduced: every query they put to the model is logged and folded into the next round of training, so the model is progressively being trained on its own past answers, and the act of asking it what it knows is changing what it will know. Write me a short story of roughly 2000 words that dramatizes this. Keep the conceptual content inside the plot rather than having characters deliver lectures, let the instrumentation engineer's objection be a real one rather than a straw man, and end on a concrete epistemic dilemma the team has to act on — something specific enough that a reader could disagree about what they should do — rather than a vague sense of unease. I'd rather have a tight story about three people in one room than a sweeping narrative about the future of AI, and please don't have anyone cite papers or named theories at me.
