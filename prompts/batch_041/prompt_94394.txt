I'm assembling a short discussion note on Augustine's On Lying, and I need one worked example that does real philosophical work rather than recapping his conclusions. Please supply the example together with the analysis that goes with it.

Two strands in Augustine's account have to be held together, and they do not obviously fit. The first identifies the liar by an inner mismatch, on the principle that "that man lies, who has one thing in his mind and utters another in words, or by signs of whatever kind." The second locates the wrong in the will, holding that "the fault of him who lies, is, the desire of deceiving in the uttering of his mind." Augustine leans on the second strand when he sets aside "jokes, which have never been accounted lies," explaining that they carry no indication of deceit. So one strand treats the mismatch between mind and sign as what makes a lie, while the other treats the desire to deceive as what makes it a lie and a fault.

Find a single concrete case in which the two strands yield opposite verdicts, and work it out. Keep the case ordinary — something that could plausibly have happened to an ordinary person — and specify who says what, to whom, and with what in mind, so that a reader can test each criterion against it. Avoid exotic technology, and avoid hidden third parties who are secretly being deceived; the cases worth having are those in which the mismatch is real and the desire to deceive is genuinely absent, or in which the desire to deceive is real and the mismatch is genuinely absent.

Then do four things. First, state how the mismatch criterion and the desire-to-deceive criterion each classify your case, and explain why they come apart. Second, decide which strand Augustine's position actually requires, and defend your reading from the way he handles the three neighboring cases: the joker, the speaker who asserts a falsehood he believes true, and the speaker who asserts a truth he believes false. Say what he would have to give up under the other reading, and whether that cost is tolerable. Third, say whether the desire to deceive is doing definitional work at all, or only work in ranking the gravity of lies that the mismatch has already identified. Fourth, meet the strongest objection to your example: either that it covertly contains a desire to deceive (a deferred audience, an imagined audience, the speaker deceiving himself), or that it is not an instance of the phenomenon and so shows nothing about Augustine.

Write in American English, in continuous prose. Keep the example itself to a few sentences and give the analysis room to breathe.
