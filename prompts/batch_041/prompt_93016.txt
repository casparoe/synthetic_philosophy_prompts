Yesterday evening I refereed a long argument between two friends of mine about a third acquaintance who has a small preference cycle: she would pay a couple of pounds to swap her ticket for the play for a ticket for the concert, another couple to swap the concert ticket for the cinema, and another couple to swap the cinema ticket back for the play. Neither of my friends is a philosopher by trade, but they went at it for two hours and neither gave ground. A maintained that the cycle itself is irrational, because anyone who has one can be turned into a money pump: start her with the play ticket, let her make the three trades she says she prefers, and she ends up with the play ticket again, several pounds poorer. A set of preferences that reliably walks you in a circle and leaves you worse off, he said, is irrational by definition, and no cleverness about how she plays it changes that. B replied that the cycle, considered simply as a ranking of the three tickets, is perfectly coherent, and that nothing forces her to trade: if she sees where the sequence leads, she can decline the first swap, keep her money, and lose nothing. A said that this only relocates the problem — at each step she prefers the next ticket to the one in her hand, so refusing is declining an improvement she herself acknowledges; an agent who will not take what she prefers when it is offered is self-undermining, so either she is pumped or she is akratic, and the cycle is to blame either way. B answered that this proves too much, since someone with a perfectly consistent, acyclic ranking can also be pumped if he is tired, tempted, or impatient at the wrong moments, and that a person who binds himself in advance — refusing to keep biscuits in the house because he knows he will eat them — is not irrational for sticking to the plan rather than re-evaluating at every step; what the pump really shows, he said, is a fault in a series of choices, not in the ranking behind them. A's last word was that binding yourself is itself just another preference, and if it can outweigh a preference for a better ticket then it must be a preference for something other than the better ticket — which means her supposed ranking was never a ranking at all. They asked me to settle it and I could not. Please adjudicate move by move: say who is right about which claim, and be explicit about the several distinct questions that are being run together here — whether an intransitive ranking is irrational in itself, whether the irrationality (if any) attaches to the choices rather than to the ranking, and whether a chooser who plans ahead or binds himself escapes the pump or merely exhibits a different defect. Tell me what both of them are missing and who is closer to right overall. Please answer in British English.
