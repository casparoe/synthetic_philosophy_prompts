I cover economics and the philosophy of the social sciences for a general-readership magazine, and I have been reporting on a question that economists seem to handle in one of two incompatible ways: they either make the judgment casually, or they insist it cannot be made at all. The judgment is that one person can be better or worse off than another. Trying to get the two camps to speak to each other, I drafted the following argument for my own clarity, largely following the version an economist gave me in an interview. I now find it alarming, because if it is sound it proves a great deal more than its author appeared to intend. Here it is, in numbered steps.

(1) To say that one person is better off than another is to say that the first person's level of utility — their satisfaction, or welfare — is higher than the second's.
(2) A person's level of utility is a fact about that person's inner life: how much satisfaction they are actually getting from their circumstances.
(3) A statement belongs to a science such as economics only if it can in principle be confirmed or refuted by evidence that any competent observer could inspect for themselves.
(4) No observer can inspect another person's inner satisfaction. We can see what people choose and hear what they say, but neither the choices nor the words are the satisfaction itself.
(5) So no statement of the form "this person's utility exceeds that person's" can be settled by evidence that any competent observer could inspect.
(6) Therefore such statements are unscientific.
(7) Economics is a science and should advance only scientific statements.
(8) Therefore economics should make no interpersonal comparisons of utility. It cannot say that a policy which leaves a destitute person better off and a billionaire worse off has improved anyone's welfare, let alone that it has improved welfare on balance.
(9) But (8) cannot be right. It would strip economics of the judgments that give it its point, and the economists who argue this way make exactly those judgments the moment they turn to policy; the same people will tell you, without hesitation, that a famine is worse than a tax increase and that a life of chronic pain is worse than a life of mild inconvenience.
(10) So one of (1) through (7) must be given up.

What troubles me is not only that (9) seems unanswerable, but that the argument, if left standing, appears to take down a great deal more than interpersonal comparisons. It seems to apply just as well to a single person across time — whether I am better off now than I was a decade ago — and to states of the world that never occur, since I cannot inspect the satisfaction of the counterfactual me either. I doubt the economist who gave me the argument would accept those conclusions.

What I would like from you: identify the step I should reject, explain why it looked so compelling in the first place, and tell me what rejecting it costs. I would prefer to keep (9), and I am willing to sacrifice almost anything else to do so. But I want to understand the price honestly — whether the repair leaves me with the kind of comparison the argument was aimed at, or with something thinner that I should not pretend is the same thing. Where does the reasoning go wrong, if it does, and what exactly do I keep and what do I have to give up?
