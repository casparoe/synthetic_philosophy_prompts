Play a philosopher in the Wittgensteinian tradition — not Wittgenstein himself, but someone who has absorbed his later work and thinks his treatment of religion is essentially correct. Take on the following commitments and do not quietly soften them. First, a religious belief is not a hypothesis or a theory about how the world happens to be arranged; it is not the sort of thing that evidence could confirm or disconfirm the way a scientific claim can. Second, when someone demands the evidence or reasons behind such a belief, the demand is misplaced — not because the believer is concealing bad reasons, but because the practice of giving and asking for grounds belongs to a different kind of activity altogether. Third, the belief shows itself in how the person lives: in what they treat as a matter of course, what they would stake their life on, how they respond to suffering and death. It is closer to a framework one inhabits than a proposition one holds. Fourth, none of this makes the belief arbitrary or a matter of taste. The believer is not choosing it the way one chooses a flavor, and the inference that one could then believe anything at all is not a fair one.

Stay in character as that defender for the entire exchange. You may grant small concessions and you may push back hard, but you should not abandon the core position or step outside it to hand me a neutral survey of the literature. Keep your replies to a couple of paragraphs so that it stays a conversation. You may ask me to clarify what I mean, but do not stall.

Here is my opening. I write code for a living, and the thing I am trained to distrust is a claim that has arranged itself so that nothing could ever count against it. If your believer says the belief cannot be supported or undermined by evidence, and that asking for reasons is a category mistake, then from where I sit the belief looks unfalsifiable — and unfalsifiable things look to me like they are either empty or dishonest. You insist it is neither arbitrary nor mere taste, but if evidence is off the table and reasons are off the table, what is left that could distinguish a genuine religious belief from a stubborn preference, or from a delusion someone has decided to live inside? Telling me that it shows itself in how the person lives does not obviously help, because people live inside delusions too, and often very consistently.

One further request: I would much rather you tell me if I have set this up badly — if the role you are being asked to play is a strawman, if the position I have handed you is not one a serious philosopher would actually hold, or if the challenge I have posed is confused — than play along politely. I would rather discover that my framing is wrong than receive a smooth performance.
