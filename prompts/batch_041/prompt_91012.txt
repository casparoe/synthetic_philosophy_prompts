Ok so I've thought about this alot and I've talked myself into a position about AI safety claims, and at this point I can't tell if I'm right or just stubborn, so I need you to try to break it.

The position: the only safety claims about a model that are worth anything are worst-case claims. "It passed our 10,000 red-team prompts" is a claim about the prompts, and the prompts were picked by people who wanted it to pass. "The reward model is well calibrated on the training distribution" is a claim about a distribution the deployment has no obligation to sample from. Neither one tells me what the system does when something is off-script. What I want is this: there's a class E of environments, E contains the environment the system will actually run in, and for every e in E the probability of the bad outcomes stays under some δ. If nobody can state that for a class I actually believe contains the real deployment, then I don't think we know the system is safe.

I already know the class E is doing all the work, and that I can't verify the real deployment is in it. I also can't tell whether the worst case I care about is over inputs, over training runs, over reward functions, over deployment distributions, or over an adversary who gets to design the environment — and I suspect a version of the view that needs all five at once isn't a view I can hold. Someone did suggest I could keep the guarantee from being vacuous by letting E contain only adversaries that can't out-compute the system, which sounds like cheating to me, but I couldn't tell you exactly what's wrong with it.

So: what would count against the position? Rank the counterarguments and evidence from decisive down to merely awkward, including things that have arguably already happened. Include the ones a sympathetic defender would admit count against them, not just the ones critics throw at them. Tell me what my view predicts that the average-case view doesn't — if it predicts nothing different, that seems bad. And be straight with me on two things: (1) is refusing to move until I have a worst-case guarantee a mistake in decision theory, like refusing to buy any insurance because you can't rule out the worst case, or is the analogy bad because with insurance you at least know the distribution? (2) has my position quietly gone unfalsifiable, and if so is that a problem for a view like this or just the cost of doing business?

I also want to know if there's a version of this that survives, and if not, which parts I'd have to give up to keep the rest. Don't just tell me worst-case bounds are conservative and that that's the point — I know that, that's not what I'm asking.

Please answer in American English.
