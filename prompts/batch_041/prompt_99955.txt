I'm a PhD student in political theory and the appointed academic member of the campaign-finance oversight board in the mid-sized city where I'm writing my dissertation. At next month's meeting the board has to rule on a complaint that will fix our rules for years: in the six weeks before a referendum on a downtown rezoning, a committee called Neighbors for a Livable Downtown spent roughly $180,000 on mailers and targeted digital ads that never use the words "vote no" or "defeat" but name the rezoning and say it will raise taxes and hand the waterfront to a single developer. Nearly all of the money came from three donors, one of whom owns commercial property that would compete with the proposed development, and the committee paid a registered lobbying firm to run the campaign — the same firm whose principals lobby the council on the rezoning. Our ordinance caps contributions to ballot committees at $500 per person and requires donor disclosure, but exempts issue advocacy, and it directs the board to adopt a rule covering communications that are campaign advocacy in substance even when they avoid explicit electoral language.

Please draft the written opinion I'll circulate before the vote. It should give a clear recommendation — dismiss the complaint, order disclosure only, or treat the spending as a contribution subject to the cap, with a remedy — and should state the test the board should adopt for distinguishing campaign advocacy from issue advocacy precisely enough that staff can apply it to the next complaint. It should engage the strongest objection to whatever course you recommend: that the only interest our city can legitimately invoke is preventing quid pro quo corruption, that ballot-measure spending involves no candidate to corrupt, and that any rule broad enough to reach these mailers will also reach genuine advocacy and chill it. Our staff report adds a further worry worth addressing: cap this spending and the money likely reappears in a nonprofit vehicle that discloses nothing, so an unenforceable rule may be worse than none. Take a position; don't hand the board a menu.
