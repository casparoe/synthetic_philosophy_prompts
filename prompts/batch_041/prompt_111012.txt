A colleague of mine, a dean who must decide whether to keep a philosophy appointment in a faculty being restructured around measurable research output, has a favourite argument, and it takes the form of a family portrait. Philosophy, he says, is the parent of the sciences. Physics began as natural philosophy; psychology, logic, linguistics, and large parts of economics began as chapters of philosophy and then left home. What makes a child a success is that it grows up, acquires methods of its own (measurement, experiment, formalisation), and no longer needs the parent. His conclusion is that philosophy's only demonstrable achievements are these departures, and that the discipline's own record is a record of questions not settled but handed on. The residue still in the house is, by construction, whatever could not be made into a science; that, he says, is what a philosophy department now is. He supports this with a diagnostic of his own: in physics, rival positions on a question are winnowed until one survives, whereas in philosophy the positions accumulate, so that the stock of live options never shrinks and nothing ever dies of natural causes.

I want to know how much of this the analogy can actually carry. Please set out the correspondence explicitly — what plays the role of parent, child, maturation, leaving home, and the house — and then say where the mapping breaks: which features of real families the picture quietly imports that a discipline and its history do not have, which of the disanalogies matter for the conclusion he wants and which are idle. In particular, tell me whether his conclusion, that philosophy has no results of its own but only departures, survives once the disanalogies are on the table, or whether the analogy has been doing the work all along. I have also heard it suggested that philosophy progresses more as a legal system does, accumulating doctrine and technique without ever converging on a verdict; say whether that picture fares better than his. Keep it concrete: use cases of questions that were answered, dissolved, abandoned, or handed back from the sciences to philosophy.
