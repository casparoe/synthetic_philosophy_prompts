ok so im in this ethics of influence seminar and we've been on nudging for like five weeks now and the class has split hard into two camps and at this point everyone just talks past each other. camp 1 is the libertarian-paternalist side: nudges are usually fine, they help people get what they already want, nobody's options are actually taken away, and defaults/framing are unavoidable anyway so "no nudge" isnt really on the menu. camp 2 is the autonomy/manipulation side: nudging works by bypassing or hijacking the persons own reasoning, so it disrespects them even when it helps, and "you could have opted out" doesnt fix that.

our prof keeps pushing a distinction she sort of made up and its become the flashpoint. she says we should separate nudges that substitute for the target's reasoning (the nudge does the deciding, e.g. a default that the person would never have picked on reflection) from nudges that supplement it (they feed the persons own deliberation, like a salient warning or a side-by-side comparison). her claim is that only the substituters are objectionable. half the class thinks thats obviously right, the other half thinks the line is fake or cant be drawn in practice.

for my term paper i want to write the joint statement that the two camps could actually both sign. so: (1) a numbered list of claims both sides accept about nudging, autonomy, welfare, and what even counts as a nudge; (2) the disagreements stated precisely, each one in wording that the side holding it would accept as fair; (3) the real cruxes -- i suspect one is whether the substitute/supplement line survives, and another is whether the harm (if there is one) is to autonomy or to welfare -- and for each crux, a concrete case or argument that both sides would agree bears on it, plus what each side predicts about that case, written down in advance. if part of the fighting turns out to be just about words, say which part and why. dont give me a reading list or citations, just the document.
