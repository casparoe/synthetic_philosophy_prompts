Construct a one-shot decision problem, realistic and with ordinary stakes -- someone choosing between two jobs, two medical treatments, or two savings plans -- in which the option that maximizes the expected value of the outcome is not the option that maximizes its geometric expectation, the criterion behind the Kelly rule. The outcomes should be a single quantity the person values linearly (income, years of life, or the like), each option should have a genuinely uncertain outcome, and nothing should depend on repetition or compounding. Then do the check in three steps. First, state both recommendations and show the arithmetic that separates them. Second, re-describe the same two options with the outcome variable shifted by a constant, so that the identical outcomes are reported against a different baseline, and show that the geometric recommendation reverses while the expected-value recommendation does not. Third, say what that reversal establishes: which feature of the geometric criterion does the work, whether the criterion picks out any ranking at all before a zero point for the quantity has been fixed, and what becomes of it when one branch of the outcome sits at or below the new baseline. Close with the strongest reply available to someone who thinks agents should maximize geometric expectation -- including whether they may simply identify the agent's utility with the logarithm of the quantity -- and state what they would have to claim about the quantity at issue for their criterion to yield a determinate ranking.
