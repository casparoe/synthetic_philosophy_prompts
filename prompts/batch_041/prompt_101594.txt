Ok so I'm putting together a handout for my grad seminar on moral responsibility and I want to do something different this year. Instead of marching through the usual suspects in order — Strawson, then the Frankfurt cases, then the manipulation stuff — I want to start from the other end and just ask: what does a theory of moral responsibility actually have to do to earn its keep? So write me the desiderata list.

I want the phenomena and cases any such theory has to get right, each with the case behind it. Off the top of my head these should include: the difference between excusing someone (they didn't know, they were coerced) and exempting them (they're a small child, they're not a fit target of these attitudes at all) — the theory has to tell me why that distinction is real and not just a feeling. Cases of manipulation, where someone's history seems to gut their responsibility even though their current psychology looks fine. Psychopathy and severe mental illness. Ordinary cases where you are responsible for something but it would be petty to actually blame you. Maybe moral luck, though I'm not sure that belongs on the same list, so tell me if it doesn't.

And the distinctions it can't collapse: being responsible vs being held responsible vs being blameworthy; the participant attitude vs what Strawson called the objective one; whether responsibility is a fact about the agent or about our practices of holding each other to account.

Also tell me which of these requirements are just parochial — one camp's wishlist dressed up as a constraint — and which ones we should recieve as genuinely non-negotiable. And the part I really want: is there a pair of these that can't be had together? My hunch is that the demand that responsibility be grounded in facts about the agent's psychology (some kind of reasons-responsiveness) fights with the Strawsonian line that responsibility just is the aptness of these attitudes, but you tell me where the real pinch is, if anywhere. If the list turns out to be internally inconsistent, that's a result, not a failure.

No need to score the contenders in any detail — a sentence each on how the Strawsonians, the reasons-responsiveness crowd, and the Scanlonian contractualist types fare against the list is plenty. Keep the weight on the requirements themselves, that's where the thinking is.
