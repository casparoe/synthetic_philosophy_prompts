I work as a software engineer and have no formal training in philosophy — the closest thing I have to a background in it is a decade of arguing about design tradeoffs — but for the past several months I have sat in on a fortnightly reading group that keeps returning to the same dispute, and I would like to commission the document the two principals should have written months ago instead of arguing. The subject is the paradox of deontology: the appearance of tension in a view that grounds its prohibitions in respect for persons yet forbids killing one person to prevent five killings, and so declines to minimize the very thing it claims is at stake. The first of the two holds that certain constraints are absolute — she always reaches for the case of killing one innocent person to save several others — and that the paradox is an artifact of assuming that the only thing that could justify a constraint is its tendency to reduce violations; on her view, once the constraint is understood as expressing each person's standing as someone whose life is not a resource to be allocated, the question of how many lives would be saved never gets a grip. The second holds that constraints are real and not properly understood aggregatively, but that they must give way past some threshold of catastrophe, and that the absolutist position is precisely what the paradox exposes as unstable, since a rule that refuses to trade one life against any number of lives cannot be an expression of respect for persons if it licenses an unbounded quantity of preventable death.

What I want from you is the joint statement these two could actually sign after a good-faith adversarial collaboration. Begin with a numbered section of claims both accept — about what a constraint is protecting, about what a justification of a constraint would have to do, about what the paradox does and does not establish, and about what counts as violating a constraint as opposed to merely failing to prevent harm — and please strike any agreed claim that is only a platitude; I would rather have four substantial ones than twelve empty ones. Then a disagreements table: for each remaining difference, a statement of each side's position written in wording that side would accept as its own, followed by the crux or sub-question that separates them, the argument or case that both sides agree bears on that crux, and each side's prediction about it recorded in advance. I am especially interested in whether part of their disagreement is merely verbal — in particular whether they mean the same thing by absolute, and whether one of them means never outweighed by any consideration at all while the other means not outweighed by ordinary aggregative considerations — and if so I would like that said plainly rather than left as a residual disagreement.

As a non-philosopher I can only guess at where the real cruxes lie, so treat the following as hypotheses to test rather than a list to fill in: whether a justification for a constraint must ultimately be impersonal or agent-neutral, and whether the paradox simply assumes that it must; whether what a constraint protects is a person's standing rather than a quantity of harm, so that aggregation is a category error rather than a standing temptation; whether a threshold can be stated without arbitrariness, and whether arbitrariness would be fatal or merely the ordinary condition of moral knowledge; whether the distinction between doing and allowing does independent work here, since the paradox looks different when the alternative to killing one is letting many die than when it is killing many; and whether inviolability is a binary status or admits of degrees. For the cases on which both sides should record predictions, I would suggest escalating numbers (one against five, one against fifty, one against a million), a probabilistic threat such as a small chance of an enormous disaster, a case in which the alternative to killing one is that someone else kills five, and a case in which the one is a willing volunteer who consents to be killed — the last because I suspect it sorts the two positions more sharply than the standard examples do. Where you can, say for each crux what each side would count as evidence against its own view, and whether anything either side can name would actually move the other. Finally, please do not build the statement around named philosophers or their texts; I cannot check attributions, and I would rather the document stand on the arguments than on who is supposed to have made them.
