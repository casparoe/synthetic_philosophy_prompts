I'm finishing a philosophy of psychiatry seminar on Ian Hacking's looping effects, and rather than end my final paper with a summary I'd like to end it with a poem. Please write a pantoum — the form in which the second and fourth lines of each quatrain return as the first and third of the next, and the closing quatrain folds back onto the opening lines — spoken by a woman who has just been given a newly proposed diagnosis, a category the manual defines as a disorder of compulsive self-documentation, the sense that an experience has not really happened until it has been posted. I want the loop to be carried by the form rather than asserted: let the two refrains be, on one side, the clinician's description of the category and, on the other, the woman's own account of herself, and let the repetitions of the pantoum gradually erode the difference between them — the clinical line picking up her idiom, hers picking up his — so that the final quatrain, where the refrains meet, reads in neither voice cleanly. Someone who has read Hacking should recognize the mechanism at work — a classification that reshapes the people it classifies, who then change in ways that send the classification back to be rewritten — and not merely hear the phrase; someone who hasn't should still find it a good poem. Keep it under about twenty-four lines. Begin with the poem itself, with no preface or commentary.
