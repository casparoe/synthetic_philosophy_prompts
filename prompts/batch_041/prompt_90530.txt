I've been working through Grice's account of conversation — the cooperative principle, the four maxims, the distinction between what a speaker means and what their words literally say — and I've found that the textbook illustrations have gone stale for me. The reference letter that damns with faint praise, the stranded driver who mentions that he's out of petrol, the dinner guest who says the soup is "interesting": I can recite the analysis before I finish reading the example, which makes me suspect I'm pattern-matching rather than actually understanding the machinery. I'd like to see it do real work in a setting it wasn't built for.

So please write me a short story. Set it at a mid-sized insurance firm, Meridian Assurance, that has rolled out an internal messaging system called the Maxim Meter, which appends to every employee message a readout of the maxim it most plausibly flouts and the implicature it most plausibly carries. The protagonist, Priya, is a claims adjuster who has quietly become expert at writing notes that leave the Meter with nothing to flag, and the plot turns on one note she deliberately writes to implicate something she cannot safely say outright. The story needs to stage three distinct things: first, a moment where a hearer has to reconstruct what Priya meant by asking what she could have been trying to accomplish in saying what she said, so the implicature is visibly calculable from the maxims plus the context; second, a moment where she later withdraws or cancels the implicature, revealing that it was never part of what her words literally said; and third, a moment where the system's literal reading and a human's pragmatic reading of the same sentence diverge in a way that actually costs someone something. The emotional stakes can be as small as a damaged friendship or a missed promotion — I don't need melodrama — but the pragmatic details should be load-bearing rather than decorative. Assume I already know the standard vocabulary, so you can use terms like flouting, cancellability, generalized implicature, and what is said without pausing to define them; the story itself, though, should make each of those visible in the action rather than naming it in the narration.

Give the response in two titled sections. First, "The Story," roughly 900 to 1,300 words. Second, "Commentary," roughly 300 to 400 words, explaining which implicature in the story is particularized rather than generalized and why, exactly where the cancellability surfaces, and one respect in which the story exposes Grice's picture as too tidy — a point where speaker meaning and sentence meaning come apart in a way the maxims alone don't anticipate.
