I volunteer as a steward for my county land trust. I have no training in ecology or in philosophy — I run a small hardware store — but I have walked our properties for nine years and I have been reading on my own about a question I cannot get the board to take seriously: whether making an ecosystem healthy makes the animals living in it better or worse off. At its March meeting the board votes on the largest project in our history, the re-wetting of the Harrow Fen tract, a 380-acre peatland that was ditched and drained a century ago and is currently leased to a row-crop farmer. Staff recommend full restoration: block the ditches, raise the water table, restore the seasonal pools, and let the wetland reassemble itself. Their assessment scores the project as an overwhelming gain on our species-richness index and on ecological integrity, which our policy defines as resemblance to a pre-1900 baseline.

What bothers me is what that index actually counts. Species richness, and every other measure we use, treats a mayfly and a heron as one unit apiece, and treats a life that ends in a few days, often by starvation or by being eaten while still alive, as equivalent to a life that ends at fifteen years. My reading — and I know I am an amateur — suggests that the wetland we propose to create would hold an enormous number of very short lives, while the drained cropland we would replace, though poorer in species, supports far fewer animals. If many of those short lives contain more suffering than contentment, our flagship project might be a large increase in suffering presented to the public as a large increase in good. I do not know whether that is true. I do know that our assessment never asks.

Please draft the written opinion I will circulate to the board before the vote. It should recommend a concrete course of action — proceed, proceed with conditions, defer for a specific study, or reject — and if it recommends proceeding in any form, it should give the exact wording of the motion I should move and a schedule of dates tied to the March meeting. It should engage the strongest version of what I expect to hear from the other trustees: that a land trust's business is the persistence of species and the integrity of ecosystems, not the inner experience of individual animals, and that ranking lives by how pleasant we judge them to be would betray both our science and our donors. I would also like it to work through, as concretely as the evidence allows: whether reducing the number of wild animals is ever a legitimate aim for an organization like ours; how much weight to give the possibility that insects and other small creatures feel something, given that we cannot settle the question and that the numbers at stake are enormous if they do; whether the right comparison is between the restored wetland and an empty field or between the restored wetland and the particular animals the cropland supports now, including the ones killed by the re-wetting itself; and whether some form of restoration would create less of the suffering that worries me — re-wetting the fen while omitting the seasonal pools that reliably become death traps for juveniles, and without restocking fish — and whether choosing that form would be a defensible exercise of our discretion or a departure from the science we claim to follow. Write it as a formal opinion addressed to the board, in plain language, for readers who have not read what I have read, and close by stating plainly what the trust would be committing itself to and what it would be admitting it cannot know. Please do not include citations or a reading list; the board will not read them and I cannot supply them reliably.
