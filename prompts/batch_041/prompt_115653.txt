I run a small reading group out of the library (six of us, mostly retired, plus one teenager) and we just finished the MIU / MU part of the Hofstadter book. Our last session is next week and I want to hand out one essay question for people to write up at home, because half the group won't open their mouth unless they've had time to think first. Trouble is I'm not a logician, I just read alot, so I need you to write the question, a model answer I can actually check against, and a marking scheme so I don't end up grading by vibes.

Here's what I'll staple to the question so nobody has to remember the rules. The only string you start with is MI. Then four moves, any of them, any number of times: (1) if a string ends in I you can stick a U on the end, so MI becomes MIU; (2) if a string starts with M you can copy everything after the M and paste it onto the end, so MIU becomes MIUIU; (3) any III can be swapped for a U; (4) any UU can be deleted. The puzzle is to get to MU.

What I want the question to be about is the jootsing part, the jumping out of the system. Everybody in the group already knows the answer is no, MU can't be got, and most of them can even say why (something about counting the I's). I don't want the exam to be about that, that's the easy half. What I want is this. The book says you have to step outside the system to prove it, and I've never been satisfied by that. Outside of what, exactly, and where do you land? If somebody hands me a bigger system that has, as an axiom, MU is not a theorem, have they proved anything, or have they just written down the answer they wanted? And if it turns out you've only moved into a bigger system, does inside and outside even mean anything here, or is jootsing just a fancy name for changing the subject?

So: one essay question built around that, and write it so that a student who recites the I-count invariant and stops gets about half credit, not full. Model answer around 600 words. Marking scheme out of 20, naming the specific moves a top answer has to make and what each one is worth, plus a short list of the wrong turns I should expect — I've got one guy in the group who is definitely going to say this is just Gödel's theorem, and I want to know whether to nod or not.
