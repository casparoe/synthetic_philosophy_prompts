i'm a novelist, about two-thirds through a book where a handful of people each hold a button that ends something irreversable, and i keep coming back to Kubrick's Dr. Strangelove as the model, so i want to get my reading of it straight before i keep stealing from it.

the lens i've been thinking with is the unilateralist's curse: when any one of many agents can take an action alone, and they all have somewhat different estimates of whether the action is worth taking, the action tends to get taken by whoever rates it highest, so the group outcome is worse than if a single person had decided for everyone. the fixes people usually propose are either to funnel the decision through one decision-maker, or to get everyone to defer to what the rest would do.

read the film through that and tell me who the unilateralists actually are. is it Ripper, who uses a standing protocol to launch without the President? the Soviets, building a doomsday machine on their own and keeping it quiet until it's too late for anyone to react? the President, who has the authority but not the ability to recall the planes? i definately want the honest verdict on whether the curse is really the engine of the film or whether i'm pattern-matching.

two seperate things make me suspicious. first, the curse is a story about people disagreeing about values, how good the action would be, but the film's people mostly disagree about facts and about each other's intentions, and they aren't weighing anything, they're panicking. does that difference matter, or is it the same structure in different clothes? second, the fix of one decision-maker at the top is more or less what the film shows failing, and failing for a reason the concept doesn't seem to have a name for: the unilateralists sit on different rungs of the ladder, so a careful man at the top doesn't stop the man in the cockpit, or the men who built the machine years earlier and walked away. if the remedy assumes the agents are peers, what does the film say about that assumption?

then tell me where the film pushes back on the reading. the doomsday machine is the awkward case, because it isn't an action anybody weighs, it's a device that removes the decision entirely, which looks like the opposite disease, too few deciders rather than too many. and the war room is mostly men trying to coordinate on a clock, which might be a plain coordination problem wearing the curse's costume.

last thing, since this is going into my book: the ending already has them arguing about the gap between the mineshafts, the whole unilateralist structure rebuilt out of the ruins, which reads to me like the film saying the thing can't be fixed, only postponed. do you buy that? and is there a fix the film does dramatize, the two leaders working the problem together on the phone, that the concept would call useless but the film treats as tragic because it almost worked? point me at the scenes you'd use, and tell me what the film knows about this that the concept doesn't. no reading list or citations needed, i've read enough of this stuff to be confused on my own.
