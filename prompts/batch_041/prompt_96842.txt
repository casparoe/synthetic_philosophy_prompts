Two of my housemates have been going at each other all week over a thought experiment one of them brought home from a decision theory reading group, and I've been made referee because I was dumb enough to say it sounded interesting.

The case, as best I can reconstruct it: a being they call Omega tells you up front that it has already flipped a fair coin and already formed a prediction about what you'd do. If the coin came up heads, Omega will knock on your door and ask you for $100. If it came up tails, Omega will give you $10,000 -- but only if it predicted you'd hand over the $100 in the heads case. It says it makes these predictions by simulating your way of deciding, and that its simulations have matched what people actually did about seven times out of ten. Then Omega knocks and asks for the $100.

Sam says pay. His argument is that what you're really choosing is a rule -- 'when Omega asks, pay' versus 'when Omega asks, refuse' -- and the paying rule wins: half the time you're out $100, and half the time you collect $10,000 whenever Omega's simulation of you said pay, which for someone running the paying rule it does 70% of the time, so .35 x $10,000 - .5 x $100 = $3,450, against .15 x $10,000 = $1,500 for the refusing rule. He says the coin and the prediction aren't independent, because the prediction is a prediction of the very rule you're running.

Rae says refuse. By the time Omega is at your door you already know the coin came up heads, which is why you're being asked at all, so the $100 buys you nothing whatsoever. She says Sam's sum quietly pays you a reward in a branch you're not in and can't get into, and that you can't buy your way into it with a cheque in this branch. If the link between Omega's prediction and your choice is causal, it's already fixed and paying now can't move it; if it isn't causal, she says, it's just magic, and you're not obliged to be moved by magic.

Sam: the link isn't causal, it's logical -- Omega modelled your decision procedure and ran it, so your procedure is the common cause of both the prediction and what you do. The real choice isn't 'pay or refuse,' it's 'be the sort of thing that pays or the sort that refuses,' and only the second gets scored. He adds that her reasoning would also tell you not to put the $100 in the envelope in the version where you commit before you ever learn how the coin landed, which he says nobody believes.

Rae: the envelope case is different, and that's the whole point -- there you don't know how the coin fell, so paying is just a bet at good odds and she'd take it. Here you do know. She says all Sam has shown is that it would have been better to have been a different sort of person, which isn't a reason for her to hand over $100 now, and that 'be the sort of thing that pays' is a slogan, not an argument. She also says his rule only looks good because it averages over a branch his own rule has already ruled out.

Sam: that's what a rule is for. And Rae's principle -- only the branch you're actually in counts -- would mean nothing is ever worth doing for the sake of a branch you might have been in, which he thinks is obviously false. Rae says that's a slogan too, and that she never said the past doesn't matter, only that a coin that already landed isn't something her choice can reach back and change.

Sam keeps saying it's a known puzzle and the answer's settled; Rae keeps saying a settled answer from a blog isn't an argument. Neither will budge and I honestly cant call it. Can you go through it round by round -- who's right on which move, and what's each of them missing? And one thing they both keep asserting that I don't believe: they say the size of the amounts is irrelevant and it's all about the structure of the case. That can't be right, can it?
