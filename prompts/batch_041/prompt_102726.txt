I am preparing a seminar on precommitment and deterrence, and I want to use Stanley Kubrick's Dr. Strangelove as the centerpiece. Read the film through the idea of a commitment race: two or more parties, unable to trust each other's future conduct, try to secure their position by irrevocably binding themselves to a course of action before the other can do the same, so that the race itself alters what each can rationally do. The Soviet doomsday machine is the obvious instance, but I want a reading that treats it as more than a satirical prop. Does the film dramatize a genuine commitment race, or does it stage a commitment failure -- a device that cannot deter because its existence and trigger are kept secret, and because the men who built it cannot control the circumstances of its use? Where does the film capture the logic of precommitment, and where does it simplify or distort it? Attend to the difference between a threat that is credible because it is automatic and one that is credible because it is public and understood, and to the way the film connects the race to build doomsday devices with the ordinary arms race and the imagined mineshaft gap. I am especially interested in the ending: does the film suggest that commitment races are self-defeating, or that they remain rational until someone makes a mistake, and does that distinction matter for how we should think about real precommitment problems? Finally, say what the film contributes to the philosophical discussion that a dry example cannot -- what it reveals about the psychology, bureaucracy, and absurdity of trying to bind one's future self, and where the honest critic has to concede that the film is not doing game theory at all. Keep the reading faithful in both directions: do not reduce the film to an illustration, and do not distort the idea to fit the film.
