For years now I've been following the argument over whether 'conspiracy theory' names a genuine epistemic kind, and it has the shape of a permanent stalemate. One camp holds that there is a distinctive failure at work — a self-sealing style of explanation that reads disconfirming evidence as further confirmation, infers secret coordination from mere coincidence, and expands to absorb any anomaly — so that the class as such warrants a standing epistemic discount. The other camp denies that any such class exists: real conspiracies happen, plenty of conspiracy claims are true and well evidenced, the alleged defects are ordinary bad reasoning found across every kind of belief, and the phrase does most of its work as a rhetorical demotion — so claims have to be assessed one at a time, on their merits. Both sides concede the same individual cases and profess the same norms (proportion belief to evidence, don't make your theory unfalsifiable); the most recent round is about whether applying the label is itself a fallacy of relevance; and nobody has moved. My hypothesis is that the generalist and the particularist are not disagreeing about epistemic norms but about two different objects — a pattern of explanation versus a social practice of labeling — and that the generalist thesis, once the quantifier and the reference class are written out, turns into an empirical claim about base rates that neither side has tried to specify or estimate. Diagnose this: a verbal dispute, two questions under one heading, a hidden empirical question, or a genuine disagreement about what a class-level epistemic criticism would even have to show? And given the diagnosis, what would count as progress — a distinction, a quantification, a piece of empirical work — or is the question ill-posed?
