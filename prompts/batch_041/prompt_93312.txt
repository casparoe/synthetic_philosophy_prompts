I'm a science-and-ideas reporter and I'm putting together a two-page proposal for a nine-month ideas fellowship, and I've got a half-formed idea I need turned into something a panel will actualy fund. I've been sitting in on citizens' assemblies for a couple of years — the Irish and French ones, a handful of municipal mini-publics — and the debate about sortition is stuck on the same two rails: it gets sold either as descriptive representation (a room that looks like the country) or as deliberation (ordinary people, given time and a decent briefing, can out-argue the parliament). Fine, but neither of those is whats distinctive about the institution, and here's the bit I want to poke at. The lottery is only the first step. After the draw comes the invitation, the non-response, the screening call, the stipend, the briefing pack, the facilitator — and the people who end up in the room are the ones who said yes to a strange letter from a government office, which is itself a filter, and probably one that tracks trust in institutions. So: does the authority of a sortitioned body attach to the randomness of the draw at all? Concretely — would a body assembled by an entirely non-random process, but still made up of people with no political career, no faction and no relationship to each other (say, the first N people who answered a public notice and could clear their calendars), be any less legitimate? If it wouldn't, randomness is basically decorative and I want to know what's carrying the weight instead. If it would, I need a story about what a lottery buys that no other filter can — there's no one to lobby before the draw, or no one can claim to have earned their seat — and that's a much better story than either side is telling.

Turn that into the proposal: state the question so a panel can tell what would settle it; say why the lottocracy crowd and the deliberative-democracy crowd both leave it open (they're answering "is sortition good", not "is the randomness doing any work"); lay out what I'd do month by month — shadow the selection process and not just the plenary sessions, try to get the invite/response/dropout numbers so I can see who the pipeline sheds, interview the dropouts, who nobody ever interviews — and then say what a result looks like and what failure looks like. I also keep getting distracted by a second thing: assemblies need staff, facilitators and returning alumni, so is a new professional class forming one floor down from the career politicians sortition was supposed to replace? Tell me whether that's worth folding in as a sub-question or whether it blows the scope. And don't give me a reading list — I'll do the refs myself, I just need the argument and the plan.
