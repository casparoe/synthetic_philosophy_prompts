I'm writing a section for a paper (ok, a very long blog post that's turning into a paper) on AI reliance and the erosion of understanding, and I keep needing a case I can't build myself. Every version I try either begs the question or collapses into "guy blindly trusts a calculator", which nobody finds interesting. So: build me a thought experiment to spec. It's for the section where I argue the standard reliabilist reply doesn't defuse the worry, so the case has to be one a reliabilist can't wave off as bad methodology.

What it has to do: the person ends up with a true belief that counts as knowledge on a plain reliabilist story -- the process they used is more reliable than what they'd have managed alone, it's not luck, and the reliability is real in their environment rather than a fluke of the one case. And yet they're worse off on another dimension, namely understanding. So: reliability up, understanding down, in teh same episode, without cheating on either side. The hard direction is that it's easy to make someone loose understanding by making them careless; I need the loss to survive the person doing everything a sensible person would do.

Constraints: realistic and contemporary -- a workplace, a hospital, a newsroom, a lab, something a reader could plausibly have lived through. No sci-fi, no evil AI, no implants, no matrix. One paragraph for the scenario itself. The tool has to be ordinary (autocomplete, a triage model, a search summarizer) and genuinely good, not a trap. And I don't want the case resting on the person being lazy, rushed, or under institutional pressure to defer -- those are separate problems and they let the reliabilist off the hook. No stipulating the verdicts; the case has to generate them.

Here's the thing I actually want it built around, and it's a bit stipulative: call a belief *borrowed* just in case the agent would drop it or flip it the moment the tool's output changed, with nothing else in their evidence moving. A borrowed belief is one that's entirely on loan from the machine, however true it is. I want a borrowed belief that is still knowledge by the reliabilist's lights. That combination is the crux.

Then do the checks, in this order:

1. State the belief, the process that produced it, and why the reliabilist says knowledge -- say what makes the process reliable here, don't just assert it.
2. Say what's missing on the understanding side, and be specific about what the person can't do, not just "they don't get it". Give me the counterfactual question they can't answer or the inference they can't make.
3. Run a control case: same person, same tool, same task, understanding preserved. Then name the single variable that differs, because if more than one thing differs I've learned nothing.
4. Say which feature is doing the real work, what happens if I remove it, and how a reliabilist would attack the case -- my coauthor is a reliabilist and I need to know where he'll push.

Keep it tight. End with exactly one line, in this form: \answer{WORKS: <the feature doing the work, ten words max>} -- or \answer{FAILS: <why>} if the specs turn out not to be jointly satisfiable, which is a real possibility and I'd rather know.
