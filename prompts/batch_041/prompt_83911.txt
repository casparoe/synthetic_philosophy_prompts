Next month I'm giving a paper at a small value theory workshop where I defend a version of hedonism about well-being, and I need to prep for the Q&A. My line: the heterogeneity problem (the fact that the pleasure of a cold drink, of finally finishing a proof, of a backrub, of hearing good news seem to share nothing in common) dosen't kill hedonism, because it runs together three different questions. Separate pleasure as a phenomenal kind, pleasure as an evaluative kind (the thing that makes a life go well), and pleasure as a motivational kind (the thing you're drawn toward), and the heterogeneity objection only really bites on the first — while hedonism as a theory of welfare only ever needed one of the other two. I think thats the move, but I'm honestly not sure which of the three I can help myself to without the whole thing collapsing into either triviality or a redefinition of the view.

The room: a value theory reading group, maybe a dozen people. Two committed attitudinal hedonists who think pleasure is just a matter of taking something to be enjoyable, three desire-satisfactionists who'd rather we stopped talking about pleasure at all, one objective list person, a philosopher of mind who works on affect and thinks pleasure is a cluster concept we should stop reifying, and — the one that worries me — a psychologist who studies liking versus wanting and will tell me the empirical work quietly gave up on unity decades ago.

Draft me a Q&A prep sheet: the eight or so objections I'll actually get from these people rather than the generic ones, ordered by how likely and how damaging they are, each phrased in the vocabulary that particular person would use, with a two-minute reply and an honest note on where the reply is soft. Tell me which ones I should just concede up front, what I shouldn't concede even under pressure, and what the one question is that I'm hoping nobody asks.
