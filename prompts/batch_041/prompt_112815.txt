You are a philosopher of mind with a specialist's knowledge of the philosophy of dreaming and of the epistemology of first-person reports. I am presenting at a work-in-progress seminar a thesis I have not yet published, and I want to be ready for the question period. My thesis: the dispute over whether dreams are experiences has been misframed, because 'dream' does not name a single kind. Dreams the sleeper can report or signal while still asleep — paradigmatically lucid dreams, where the dreamer answers a question put from the laboratory — are experiences, undergone in real time and available to the dreamer as they occur. The rest are not experiences at all but retrospective constructions, memory-like states assembled at or after waking, so that the waking report is not testimony to anything undergone. My audience will be: two Wittgensteinian philosophers of mind who think the grammar of psychological verbs settles such questions and who will defend Malcolm's conclusion that dreaming is not a kind of experience; one philosopher in Dennett's camp, who holds that whether dreams are experiences is an empirical question and has no patience for conceptual analysis; and a sleep neuroscientist who studies lucid dreaming and treats reportability as a laboratory matter. First, work out what each of the three would find most objectionable and how their training shapes the objection; then give me the six to eight objections I will actually get, ordered by how likely and how damaging they are, each phrased in that audience member's vocabulary, with a two-minute reply and a candid note on where the reply is weakest. Tell me which one I should concede ground on rather than fight, and close with the question I am hoping nobody asks. Do not give me a survey of the literature; I know it.
