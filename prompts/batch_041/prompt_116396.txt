I sit on the research-governance committee of a hospital trust, and something about our pain-measurement practice has begun to bother me. The trust requires nurses to record a 0–10 self-report score at fixed intervals and titrates analgesia to that score; the regulator has accepted the same scores as the primary endpoint for a new analgesic; and the neuroscience we rely on — our own unit's, and what we cite in the licence application — explains the patient's saying that it hurts entirely in terms of nociceptive traffic, attention and expectation, treating the felt hurt itself as caused but not causing, a by-product that does no work in bringing about the utterance. What does this practice presuppose about the causal and epistemic role of phenomenal states? Does it require that the experience cause, or at least figure in the explanation of, the very report that is used to measure it, or can the practice be reconstructed so that a view on which experiences are causally inert comes out consistent with it — and if so, how is it that our reports nonetheless track the experiences they are about, rather than merely tracking the neural goings-on? Please say what would remain of the claim that we are measuring the experience rather than a correlate of it, what would have to change in the clinic and in the licence application if we took causal inertness seriously, and distinguish throughout what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with, flagging where it is philosophically underdetermined.
