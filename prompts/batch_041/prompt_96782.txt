I cover technology and ideas for a weekly magazine, and for the past two years I have served as one of the lay members on the ethics advisory committee of a mid-sized public pension fund. On Thursday the committee votes on a proposed amendment to our investment policy, and the committee is split five to four, with my vote the swing. The amendment would create a narrow exception to our human-rights screening rules for what its sponsors call developmental-authoritarian states: countries with a record of sustained economic growth and public order but also with restricted speech, assembly, independent courts, and political competition. The test case before us is a country that combines features commonly associated with Singapore and China: it has lifted several hundred million people out of poverty in two generations, maintains high reported public satisfaction with the government, and has largely eliminated everyday violent crime, while also jailing journalists, censoring the internet, and refusing to allow a meaningful opposition. The sponsors argue that prosperity and order are real goods, that the people in question appear to prefer them to the chaos and poverty of the alternatives, and that a pension fund has no business imposing one contested conception of political legitimacy on a sovereign state. The opponents argue that this is a bargain with the devil: that performance legitimacy is either a contradiction in terms or a license for any regime that delivers growth, that it ignores the people who dissent, and that it will be used to whitewash abuses. I have to vote, and I want a memo I can circulate that engages the philosophical issue rather than merely restating the fund's fiduciary duties.

Please act as a political philosopher with expertise in comparative political theory, authoritarian governance, and the ethics of institutional investment. Before writing, brainstorm in a scratchpad: identify the strongest version of the performance-legitimacy argument, the strongest objections (including the baseline problem, the counterfactual problem, the consent problem, the measurement problem, the transition problem, and the moral-hazard problem), and state which considerations you find decisive. Then draft the memo itself, addressed to the ethics advisory committee. It should open with a one-sentence bottom line recommending a concrete course of action -- adopt the amendment, reject it, or adopt it with specific conditions -- and then give the reasoning. It should treat the losing side at its strongest rather than as a straw man, and it should be explicit about what would have to be true for the other side to be right. Do not include citations, reading lists, or references to specific authors or works; I want the argument, not the bibliography. The memo should be no more than 900 words, and it should be written in a formal, polished register suitable for a committee that includes lawyers, economists, and one philosopher.
