A colleague of mine works in the Jain tradition of anekāntavāda, the doctrine that reality is many-sided and that a claim can be true from one standpoint and false from another. She keeps a casebook of the disputes her students bring her, and she rules on each in one of two ways: either the dispute is many-sided, so that both claims hold, each with the qualifier syāt ("in some respect"), and the bare conjunction of the two cannot be asserted in one breath; or it is one-sided, so that one of the claims is rejected outright, with no qualifier at all. Her rulings so far:

1. "A pot is one thing" against "a pot is many things" — many-sided.
2. "A seed is a tree" against "a seed is not a tree" — many-sided.
3. "A man whose memories have all been erased is the same person as before" against "he is a different person" — many-sided.
4. "The word for the sun and the sun are one thing, so the word warms the room" against "the word does not warm the room" — one-sided; the first claim is rejected outright, since its holder has taken a word for a thing.
5. "It is permissible to kill one innocent person to save five" against "it is not" — one-sided; the permissive claim is rejected outright.

The next dispute on her desk: two students argue about a bystander who watched a child drown in a shallow pond when he could have pulled her out at no risk to himself. One says that he killed her; the other says that he did not kill her, he merely failed to save her. Predict her ruling, but I care less about the verdict than about the rule she is running. Give me at least two readings of her record that fit all five rulings equally well, say what each predicts about this dispute, say which reading you would bet on and why, and name the further case that would best tell the readings apart.
