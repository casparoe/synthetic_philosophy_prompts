I have drafted a short essay on unilateral action and what is often called the unilateralist's curse, and I would like your comments before I revise it. I am more interested in whether the argument holds up than in whether it reads well, though I will take either. The essay is below; my questions follow it.

---

**Two Curses and a Licence**

I want to argue that what is usually called the unilateralist's curse is really two different failures that have been run together, and that once they are separated, the standard remedy for the curse turns out to be right in one case and backwards in the other.

The curse, as usually stated, runs like this. Suppose many agents can each take some action on their own; the action is worth taking only if the evidence for it clears some threshold; and the evidence is spread thinly across the agents, none of whom sees the whole picture. Then the action is taken far more often than the group's combined evidence warrants, because it takes only one agent to act, while it would take something like a consensus to justify acting. The usual illustration is an irreversible intervention — say, one actor releasing something into the atmosphere that everyone else would have to live with. The remedy often proposed is a norm of conformity of roughly this shape: act only if a substantial share of those who know what you know would also act.

I think this conflates two cases. In the first, which I will call the curse of plenty, action is individually available, jointly unjustified, and wasteful when duplicated; the failure is over-action. In the second, the curse of scarcity, the action is worth taking and it takes only one agent to take it, but every agent reasons that another will step forward, and so no one does; the failure is under-action. The structural feature the two share — that one agent suffices — is what makes the first a curse and the second a trap. But the remedies point in opposite directions: the conformity norm is designed to suppress action in the first case, and it would only deepen the failure in the second.

What actually distinguishes them, I claim, is not the number of agents or the strength of the evidence but the shape of the payoff in the number of actors. If the value of the action is first-mover — one act captures the benefit, and duplicates are waste or harm — you get the curse of plenty. If the value is threshold-like — the benefit is realised only once enough agents act, or the harm averted only once someone does — you get the curse of scarcity. Same availability, opposite failure.

The second thing the usual statement misses is that acting is rarely private. An act is usually observed, and being observed is itself a signal: others update on the fact that someone with a different slice of the evidence judged the threshold met. So a unilateral act has an informational side-effect that can run either way. It can crowd out independent judgment, as when the first lab to publish an alarming result makes the others defer and destroys the very aggregation that would have disciplined it; or it can license action in others, as when a single public refusal reveals that the cost of refusing is lower than everyone assumed. I will call the second effect the scout's licence: sometimes the reason to act is not that your own evidence suffices, but that your acting will make it common knowledge that acting is possible, and the group needs a first mover more than it needs your private judgment.

Once the informational side-effect is on the table, the conformity norm looks unstable. If acts are observable, conformity among the first movers is exactly what produces a cascade, and a cascade is not aggregation; it is the appearance of it. The norm may still be right for unobservable acts, where no side-effect exists to consider. But for observable ones, the right question is not whether a substantial share of your peers would act on your evidence, but whether you would still act once you subtract the extra acts your act will provoke. That is a much higher bar in the curse-of-plenty cases and a much lower one in the curse-of-scarcity cases, which is what you would want.

The case I cannot fit: quiet replication. Three labs independently hold weak, mutually confirming evidence of a hazard. Each knows the others are working on it but not what they hold. No single lab's evidence clears the threshold; the conjunction would. Acting is observable only as a collective outcome, not as individual moves. My account seems to say each should act, since the informational side-effect is nil and the value is threshold-like; but I also think the honest answer is that each should wait, because the very fact that no one has moved yet is evidence, and treating your own move as information for others corrupts that. I do not know whether this is a third curse or a mistake in my framework.

---

Here is what I would like from you. First, is the distinction between the curse of plenty and the curse of scarcity a real distinction or a verbal one — does anything follow from it that would not follow from the standard single-curse statement? Second, does the scout's licence hold up as an independent idea, or does it collapse into the ordinary observation that an agent's evidence includes facts about what others will do? Third, is the quiet-replication case a genuine third phenomenon, or does it show that my treatment of the informational side-effect is too crude to be repaired? I would also welcome a note on any place where I have quietly assumed something about how agents aggregate evidence that does not hold in general.

For any claims you make in your own voice, say how confident you are in them, and single out the one point you are least sure about.
