I am putting together the closing remarks for a two-day workshop marking the retirement of a colleague who has worked on Fitch's paradox of knowability for the better part of thirty years. The room is split roughly in half: there are modal logicians and epistemologists who know the proof and its literature inside out, and there are graduate students and family members who have heard the phrase "the knowability paradox" at dinner for a decade and could not tell you what it is. I have about ten minutes, and I want the speech to be, first and last, warm -- this is an occasion for affection, not a seminar -- but I do not want it to be philosophy-free or generic. The paradox has to do real structural work in the speech: it should shape what I can and cannot say about the honoree's career, about what it means to leave a problem unsolved, and about why a person would spend a life on something that never resolves. It should not be mere decoration.

Please write the speech. The room includes people who cannot be assumed to know the argument, so at least part of the speech has to make the paradox intelligible to them in plain prose, while the specialists must not be able to catch me misstating the result -- if I get the logic or the shape of the proof wrong in front of them, the whole occasion collapses. So the paradox needs to be explained accurately but without jargon, and I need you to be careful not to overclaim on its behalf, and not to attribute the result to anyone by name unless you are sure of the attribution. If a technical detail would help -- say, the difference between an omniscient being and a principle that every truth is knowable, or the sort of sentence that can be true without anyone being in a position to know it, or why it matters whether the underlying logic is classical or intuitionist -- then use it, but only if you can keep it honest, and be explicit in the speech itself about which parts of the paradox are settled and which are still contested. I would rather the closing image be the open question than a false resolution.

A few more constraints. Do not put words in the honoree's mouth: no invented quotations from them, and no claims about what they privately concluded about the paradox. Their decades of work can be described in general terms as having mapped the space of available responses and shown which ones fail, rather than as having solved or abandoned the problem. The speech should land as genuine warmth for a mixed audience, with the paradox functioning throughout as a way of talking about the value of work that does not end -- and it should end in a toast, not in a lecture. The tone should be polished enough to be read aloud at a formal dinner and plain enough that nobody feels excluded.
