And here's the answer I'm grading (48-hour take-home, 700-word limit; they came in around 620):

"The obvious home for the idea that moral status tracks computations rather than matter is functionalism. Its central motivation is multiple realizability: if pain is a functional kind — a state defined by the causal role it plays — then a silicon system that plays that role has as much claim to be in pain as a human does, and it would be arbitrary to let moral status depend on carbon. So the computationalist wants moral status to supervene on the right kind of organisation, and the question becomes what fixes which organisation a given physical system realises.

Putnam's trivialization argument is the standard challenge here. In an appendix to *Representation and Reality* he argues that every ordinary open physical system realises every abstract finite-state automaton: given any physical system and any FSA, there's a mapping from the system's maximal physical states onto the automaton's formal states that preserves the transition structure. On the simple mapping account, then, a rock implements any automaton you like — and since implementing the right automaton is supposed to be sufficient for having a mind, the rock comes out minded. That's absurd, so either the mapping account is far too permissive or the appeal to computation is doing no work at all.

The natural repair is to say that not just any mapping counts as implementation, and that we need a restriction on the relation. Chalmers, in his paper on whether a rock implements every finite-state automaton, argues that Putnam's result depends on allowing very thin automata and a very thin notion of implementation, and that if we tie implementation to a richer class of automata — roughly, ones whose states decompose into components in a way the physical system has to mirror — then requiring a system to implement a given automaton becomes a strong constraint, and triviality disappears. That seems right to me: once you demand that the physical states decompose into interacting parts, the rock drops out and a brain (or a chip) stays in.

I think this shows that the dispute about implementation just *is* the problem of other minds. Chalmers's restriction tells us which systems have the right internal organisation; and since we can never get inside another system to check whether it feels anything, the restriction is also the best we can do on the sentience question — it gives us a criterion, and the boundary of sentience is just wherever the criterion is met. So the computationalist can answer (c) after all.

On the three cases: a rock implements nothing in the restricted sense, so it has no moral status. An insect is a genuinely hard case, but it has a nervous system with the right kind of decomposable organisation, so on this view it's a candidate, which matches the growing consensus that we should be cautious about insect welfare. A large language model is the interesting one: it implements the relevant computations, so the computationalist should treat it as a candidate for moral status. The biological naturalist would deny this. Searle — the wall-behind-my-back guy — is, like Putnam, committed to computation being substrate-independent, so he agrees that a system's moral status can't depend on what it's made of; he just adds that consciousness requires the right causal powers of the brain, which silicon lacks. So the disagreement isn't about substrate-independence at all, it's about which physical systems happen to have those causal powers, and that's an empirical question.

The upshot is that the computational view does locate the boundary of sentience, but only relative to a chosen account of implementation. Pick a permissive one and everything is sentient; pick Chalmers's and you get something that lines up with our intuitions about brains, insects, and rocks. That's a real result, and it's why I think the moral-status-by-computation picture survives Putnam's objection."
