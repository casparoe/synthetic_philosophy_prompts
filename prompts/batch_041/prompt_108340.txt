Act as a philosopher of social science who also knows what it's like to work on a copy desk. Think this through carefully before you write, and start by brainstorming the distinctions you'll need, because the ask is to build me something usable, not an essay.

I edit the ideas section of a magazine, and we keep printing sentences like 'the Japanese prefer consensus' or 'Brazilians are loose about time' — sometimes a whole essay rests on one, sometimes it's just color in a travel piece. I don't want the blanket ban half my colleagues argue for ('never generalize about a people') and I don't want the free-for-all we have now. So give me a procedure an editor can run on a sentence before it goes to press: the questions in order, what counts as a good enough answer to each, where it stops and the sentence gets cut or rewritten, and where it's a judgment call a person just has to make. Constraints: it has to fit on one side of paper, work for a copy editor with no theory background, and anything it can't settle comes to me.

Two things I'd like it to use, if they're real and not just my invention: a distinction between a sentence that means 'lots of them are like this' and one that means 'this is what it is to be one of them', and some way of handling the fact that 'the Danes are trusting' can be roughly true of a population and still useless or unfair as a claim about the particular Dane you're describing. It should also say something about headlines, and about generalizing about the powerful (a government, an industry, a billionaire class) versus the powerless, because I suspect those two shouldn't be treated the same.

Then run it on three cases: 'Israelis are direct'; 'the Chinese are good at long-term planning', in a piece about industrial policy; and 'Newfoundlanders would give you the shirt off their backs', which is from a writer about her own family and which I'd like to survive — so tell me honestly if it doesn't. Finish by saying what the procedure cant do.
