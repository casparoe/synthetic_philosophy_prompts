I chair the committee that writes the death-determination policy at a transplant hospital that recovers roughly forty organs a year, and I need to turn our two abstract standards into something a night-shift intensivist who has never read the philosophy can actually run at three in the morning. Our policy says that death is either the permanent cessation of circulatory and respiratory function or the irreversible cessation of all functions of the entire brain, including the brainstem, and it commits us to the dead-donor rule: we do nothing that causes a patient's death, and we recover no organ until the donor is dead.

Two things make this harder than it looks, and I want the procedure to handle them rather than bracket them. First, 'permanent' and 'irreversible' come apart. A heart that has stopped is not dead if we are still willing and able to restart it; on our standard, circulatory death is declared only once the team has decided not to resuscitate and a fixed interval of asystole has passed. The moment of death is therefore fixed by a decision about treatment rather than by a fact about the body, and I want the procedure to say so out loud and to name who is entitled to make that decision. Second, some of our teams now want to use normothermic regional perfusion: after death is declared, they restart circulation to the organs they mean to recover while blocking flow to the brain. If circulation to the body resumes, in what sense was its cessation permanent? I have heard both that this is a straightforward extension of our standard and that it quietly abandons the dead-donor rule, and I need the procedure to say which.

Please write the procedure: the questions the intensivist asks, in order; what counts as a sufficient answer to each; where it stops with a no; where it hands the decision to a person because the question is one of values rather than facts; and who must sign off on what, and when. Then run it on two cases: a patient with no brainstem reflexes whose family will not consent to the confirmatory test, and a donor after circulatory death whose team proposes normothermic regional perfusion. Include at least one case in which the procedure returns an answer this committee will not like, and say plainly what the procedure cannot settle.
