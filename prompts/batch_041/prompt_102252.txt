I spent forty years doing bench science before I wandered into philosophy, so bear with me if this is naive. Before I read one more account of inductive inference, I want the spec first: what does a theory in this area actually have to deliver to count as adequate? The full list — the cases it has to get right (the grue business, Hempel's raven and the white shoe, why a thousand measurements count for more than one, that sort of thing), the distinctions it can't just collapse (describing how we actually infer versus justifying it, projectible versus non-projectible predicates, one-shot versus repeated contexts), and the results it has to stay consistent with — I'm thinking especially of the no-free-lunch theorems, which as I understand them say no inference rule beats every other across all problems, so any account claiming to be universally better is already in trouble. For each requirement, tell me why it's a requirement. Mark which ones are non-negotiable and which are just preferences of one camp. And here's the part I most want: tell me whether some of these can't all hold at once. I have a hunch that demanding the theory not assume what it's trying to prove, while also demanding it say some inductions are objectively better than others, is exactly where these things go to die. If one requirement does most of the work in killing off the popular candidates, say which. And please seperate for me what's actually settled among people who know this area from what's genuinely contested — I don't want a contested position dressed up as the consensus.
