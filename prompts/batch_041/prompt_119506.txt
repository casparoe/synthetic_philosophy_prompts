Last week a colleague brought a case to lunch and it split the table. A fifty-eight-year-old man with a rare cancer is deciding whether to take a drug that is expensive and hard to tolerate. In the trial that won the drug its approval, 34% of patients responded. A subgroup analysis of the same trial — men over fifty-five whose tumours carry a particular biomarker — found that eight of thirteen patients responded, a little under two in three. The man has the biomarker. The argument began over what number, if any, he should be given when he asks how likely he is to respond.

Iris said the subgroup figure, or as close to it as the data will support. The class that matters, she said, is the one that matches him on the features that actually drive the response, and the biomarker is precisely the feature the drug's mechanism turns on; to average him into a class containing patients whose tumours lack the target is not caution but a category error, since the resulting number is a frequency in a population he does not belong to. Paul said the trial figure, or something nearer to it. The subgroup was not pre-specified, he pointed out; it was found by looking, and if you look through enough subgroups some will look impressive by chance alone. Eight of thirteen is a handful of patients. A number that would not replicate is not a better estimate of anything.

Iris replied that this is an argument about the reliability of an estimate, not about which class is the right one, and that it cuts both ways, since 34% is also an estimate from a finite sample. If the biomarker really marks the mechanism, the trial average is a biased estimate of his chance, and being unbiased about a class he is not in is worth nothing to him. She added that the ideal class would be narrower still — his genotype, his prior treatments, the particular history of his tumour — and that if you keep narrowing you reach the class with a single member, where the answer is either zero or one. That, she said, is a sign that what we are really after is the chance fixed by his own physiology, not a frequency in a class we happen to have counted.

Paul answered that if the chance is fixed by his physiology then it is zero or one and nobody can know it, and no trial figure estimates it; the numbers we compute are frequencies in classes, and a class whose members we cannot observe is not one we can reason from. He added that the class which would be exactly right — patients who respond — is defined by the outcome, so an inference drawn from it would be circular. The classes available to us are the ones we can pick out without already knowing the outcome, and among those we must trade relevance against reliability.

Iris: then the trade is not free-floating. The principle is that you condition on the factor that makes the others irrelevant, and the biomarker, she claimed, screens off everything else. Declining to condition on a factor merely because the sample is small does not make you careful; it swaps one error for another. And the trawling objection proves less than Paul thinks, since he is content to condition on age and sex, which are subgroups too.

Paul: the difference is that age and sex were chosen before anyone saw the data, and pre-specification is what keeps the choice of class independent of the outcome. Besides, the question the man actually faces is not what his chance is but what to do given our uncertainty about it, and the object that matters for that is the distribution over the chance given everything we know — a distribution dragged back toward the trial average by the fact, as he put it, that most subgroup findings do not replicate.

Neither would move, and I was asked to referee and could not. Please adjudicate the exchange point by point — who is right at each step, not merely who wins overall — say what both of them are missing, and tell me whether there is, in the end, a fact of the matter about which class is the right one. If you have to name a winner, name one.
