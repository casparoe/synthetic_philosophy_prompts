There is a position about wild animals that I have gradually come to hold, and I want to find out what it risks. It has three parts. First, the suffering of wild animals is morally considerable in itself: it counts, and it counts for its own sake, not because it threatens ecosystems, species, or human interests. Second, most wild animals are what ecologists call r-strategists — they produce enormous numbers of offspring, the overwhelming majority of which die young, often slowly, and only a small fraction survive — and on any plausible accounting the aggregate lifetime suffering in such a population is very large, plausibly exceeding the aggregate lifetime wellbeing. Third, because of that arithmetic, the most promising lever on wild-animal suffering is not rescuing individuals but reducing how many animals are brought into existence in the first place, through fertility control, habitat modification, or some other means of lowering the population of high-fecundity species, on the grounds that animals who never exist cannot suffer.

Tell me what would count against this. Sort the defeaters by how decisive they would be. Which are empirical — about what r-strategist lives are actually like, about whether the interventions work, about ecological side effects that swamp the intended benefit? Which are conceptual or ethical — about whether the existence-comparison in the third part is coherent, about whether preventing an animal from existing can wrong it, about whether the view licenses the deliberate shrinking or elimination of species? And which are internal — does the third part actually follow from the first two, or is it smuggled in, so that someone could accept the first two and reject the third? Say what the view predicts that a rescue-the-individual approach does not, and what observation or result would genuinely embarrass it rather than merely complicate it. Tell me what a sympathetic holder of the view would themselves concede counts against it, as distinct from what only its critics would say. Finally, be honest about whether the position has been insulated from refutation — by the fallback that any intervention's downstream effects are too uncertain to evaluate, or by retreating to the very long run — and whether that insulation is a defect or a reasonable response to real uncertainty. I am most persuaded by arguments that turn on what the animals themselves undergo and least persuaded by appeals to the value of species or ecosystems, but I would like to know whether that ranking is itself a liability.
