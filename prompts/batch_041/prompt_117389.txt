I am a doctoral candidate in philosophy of mind and cognitive science, and I am writing the prospectus chapter that has to fix the explanatory target before I can adjudicate between theories of self-deception. I need the desiderata any adequate theory of self-deception must meet. I am not asking whether self-deception exists; I am asking what a theory of it must accomplish, so that the possibility question falls out of the list rather than being assumed at the start. Please do not give me a literature review, reading list, or citations. I want the specification itself, in a form I can use to evaluate candidates. I want the list to function as a decision procedure: a candidate theory that fails a non-negotiable requirement is out; one that fails a negotiable requirement incurs a specific cost.

Set out the requirements as a numbered list. For each requirement, give the case or puzzle that motivates it, say what the requirement commits a theory to or rules out, and mark it as either non-negotiable or as a commitment of a particular theoretical camp. Use and supplement the following test cases; I want the list to be answerable to them rather than generated in the abstract.

1. The classical paradox. If self-deception is intentional deception, the same agent must both know and not know the relevant truth, and must both intend to produce a belief in herself and remain unaware that she is doing so.

2. A diachronic case. Over months, a person gradually stops attending to evidence against a cherished belief, until she sincerely avows the belief. There is no moment at which she decides to deceive herself.

3. A behavioral case. A person sincerely avows not-P, but her conduct tracks P more reliably than her avowals do. It is unclear whether the belief is unconscious, whether the avowal is insincere, or whether the behavior is driven by something other than belief.

4. A strategic-ignorance case. A person avoids information because she knows it would change her mind, and later reports being glad she did not look. Is this self-deception, weakness of will, or a distinct phenomenon?

5. A collective case. A group maintains a shared false belief through norms of silence and selective attention, without any individual meeting the conditions for deceiving herself. Does the theory need to extend to groups, or should it treat this as a different phenomenon?

6. A case of deliberate non-closure. A person adopts a standing policy of not asking certain questions and would not describe herself as believing either P or not-P; she simply keeps the question closed. What must the theory say about this, and does it fall inside or outside the target phenomenon?

Then do the following.

First, separate the requirements that follow from the concept of deception itself from those that follow from a particular theory of belief, intention, or consciousness. Second, identify the requirements that are in tension, and say whether any pair is jointly unsatisfiable. I am especially interested in whether the intentionality condition can be squared with the sincerity condition, and whether any theory can meet both a first-person authority constraint and an evidence-tracking constraint. Third, identify the single requirement that does the most to exclude otherwise attractive theories, and explain why.

Then briefly assess three families against the list: divided-mind or partitioning accounts; deflationary accounts that drop intentionality; and motivational accounts that treat self-deception as a species of motivated belief formation. Keep this assessment subordinate to the criteria.

Finally, state what the theory must say about the difference between doxastic self-deception and mere avowal, and about the difference between self-deception and neighboring phenomena: wishful thinking, weakness of will, adaptive preference, denial, and indifference to truth. Close by saying which requirement you would give up first if forced, and what that concession would cost.
