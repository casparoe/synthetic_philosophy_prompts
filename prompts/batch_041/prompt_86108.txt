I am a novelist, and in three weeks I have to take a single ninety-minute session in a philosophy department's graduate methodology seminar. The colleague who arranged it suspects that novelists understand something about how hypotheticals do their work, and has billed the session as "Intuition Pumps: How Thought Experiments Persuade and Mislead". I shall have a dozen students, most of them second- and third-year doctoral candidates working in ethics or philosophy of mind, two of them from the writing programme. There is a whiteboard, and there is no set reading; I intend to keep it that way. I want a plan for the teaching, not an explanation of the subject.

There is one idea I want them to leave with. A thought experiment does its persuasive work in the gap between what it states and what it leaves the audience to supply; its power and its liability are the same property, because the audience finishes the case, and finishes it with whatever it brought along. I am also sure of one staging requirement: at some point the same case must be made to yield two opposite verdicts inside the room. Tell me where in the sequence that belongs and what I am to do with the wreckage.

I have a working distinction of my own, borrowed from the way scenes work in fiction, and I would like you to test it rather than adopt it. Some cases persuade by recognition: they are built so that the audience recognises a structure it already reasons with, and the verdict arrives feeling like a memory. Others persuade by defamiliarisation: they remove a feature we had not noticed we were relying on, and the verdict arrives as a shock. I suspect the first kind ages badly and the second kind travels worse, which may be why the trolley family has gone stale on us. If the distinction is a dud, say so plainly and put something better in its place.

One further notion from craft that I think may be doing quiet work here: every hypothetical has a detail that carries the whole thing, and if you change that detail the verdict moves. I should like the students to leave able to find that detail for themselves in a case they thought they had understood.

Take the role of a philosopher of science with a novelist's eye. Think carefully before you commit; I would rather be corrected than humoured. Brainstorm two or three candidate cases before you choose one. Assume that I am competent in the material but not a specialist, and do not send me a reading list or cite scholarship at me: everything has to run from the whiteboard and from what the students already carry in their heads. Assume, too, that they can each name half a dozen stock cases and are tired of them. If you need to fix an assumption about my students, state it in a single sentence first.

Set out the plan under the following headings, in this order, at roughly these lengths.

"On my distinction" (about 200 words): whether recognition and defamiliarisation survive contact with a real seminar, what should replace the pair if not, and which of the two the session should lean on.

"The order of ideas" (about 350 words): the sequence in which the components must arrive, what depends on what, and why that order rather than the obvious one; mark the places where I should stop talking and let them talk.

"The carrying case" (about 300 words): the one case to build the session around, why it can bear the weight, what it must have and must not have, how I should tell it, and how I should modify or pair it if it cannot carry ninety minutes alone.

"The misconception map" (about 450 words): each misconception they will bring or form, the point in the sequence where it surfaces, and the sentence with which I head it off; note which ones I should let run for a while before correcting.

"The check at the end" (about 150 words): one question or task that shows whether the idea has landed rather than whether they enjoyed themselves, together with what a good and a poor answer to it would look like.

"What I leave out, and how I say so" (about 150 words): what belongs to a longer course rather than to ninety minutes, and the form of words in which I decline it.
