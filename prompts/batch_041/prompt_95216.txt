I'm trying to work out what I actually think about AI tools and understanding, and my gut is all over the place. Here's the thing I keep circling: when does leaning on a model cost you your understanding of something, versus just being normal dependence on other people and instruments? I've got a pile of verdicts and I can't tell if they add up to one principle or if I'm just inconsistent.

1. I use the calculator app for long division. Nothing lost, obviously.
2. I ask a model why the sky is blue, it gives me Rayleigh scattering, I ask a few follow-ups, and a week later I can explain why sunsets go red and why the sky is deeper blue up at altitude. I'd say I understand it now. I couldn't have worked out any of that myself.
3. Same content, but a colleague explains it to me over coffee. Same verdict.
4. I have a model write the regression code for my paper. I can't read the code. I run it and report the numbers. I don't understand that analysis, and I'm uneasy about it.
5. Same code, but I sit with the model line by line until I can explain every line to a student and notice when it picked the wrong test. I still couldn't write it from scratch. That one's fine, I think.
6. A med student uses a diagnostic model and nails the top diagnosis every time, but if the model were wrong he'd never catch it. I think he understands less than the resident who's slower and only gets it right two out of three times on her own.
7. I proved something hard in grad school. I couldn't reproduce the proof now, but I trust my own old notes and I still cite the result. I know it's true. I don't think I understand it anymore.
8. My physicist friend can't do the spectroscopy in her own paper. A chemist colleague runs it, she can't check it, and she still says the result is hers and I agree she understands it. But if she'd gotten the exact same spectroscopy from a model, I feel like I'd say she doesn't understand her result. I can't defend that asymmetry at all.
9. Someone asks a model whether eating meat is wrong, gets an argument, adopts the conclusion, can repeat the argument to others, but folds the second a counterargument shows up. I don't think she really holds that view.
10. Someone raised in a religious tradition holds a moral view they've never argued for and can't defend against objections. That one I think is fine. That's just how moral education works.
11. A radiologist who used to read scans well now just rubber-stamps the model. I think she's already lost something, even on the scans she'd still have gotten right.

My tentative stab at a principle is: you understand a thing when you can do something with it — answer new questions, tell when it's wrong, explain it to someone else — and it doesn't matter where the stuff came from. But 8 and 9 and 10 seem to show I don't actually believe that second half, and I can't tell if that's a real insight or just bias against the machine.

So: what principle, or what small set of competing principles, actually fits these? State them precisely. For each clause, tell me which of my cases forces it. Then tell me what the best candidate predicts for two cases I haven't listed: (a) a whole hospital where triage is done by a model and no human there can explain the triage policy — does the hospital understand its own triage? and (b) someone who learns a proof entirely from a model, can fill in every step and answer any question about it, but would never in a million years have found the key idea. And be honest with me about which of my verdicts the principle can't accommodate and which one I should probably just give up. I'm fine with being told I'm wrong about one of them, I'd just rather know which.

Please write it in American English.
