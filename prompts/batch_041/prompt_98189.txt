In a recent seminar on divine providence, a fellow participant advanced the following argument against Molinism. I find the conclusion uncongenial, but I also suspect the argument proves too much, since its central premise seems to sweep in truths that no one thinks depend on God's will. I would like a diagnosis: which step should be rejected, why did it look plausible, and what does rejecting it cost? The argument is this.

(1) Molinism holds that, prior to any divine decree, God possesses middle knowledge: he knows true counterfactuals of creaturely freedom. Molinists distinguish this from God's natural knowledge of necessary truths and his free knowledge of what he will actualize. An example of middle knowledge would be: if Peter were placed in circumstances C, he would freely deny Christ.

(2) If such a counterfactual is true prior to any divine decree, then its truth is not grounded in God's will.

(3) But no truth can be independent of God's will. Since God is the creator and sustainer of all things, whatever is true must be true because God wills it or at least permits it.

(4) Therefore Molinism is false.

The problem is that (3), taken at face value, applies equally to necessary truths: that 2 + 2 = 4, that nothing is both red and not red, that if a is identical to b then whatever is true of a is true of b. If those truths are true because God wills them, then logic and mathematics are products of divine volition, which many theists regard as a cost too high to pay. So either the argument is unsound or the Molinist has a way of distinguishing counterfactuals of creaturely freedom from necessary truths without giving up divine sovereignty.

Which premise is the weakest, and why? For each of the two or three candidates you regard as live, explain what the premise initially seemed to secure and what rejecting it would cost. What exactly must a Molinist say about the grounding of counterfactuals of freedom, and does that response leave divine sovereignty intact? If I must choose, I would rather surrender (3) than (1), but I want to know what I am giving up.
