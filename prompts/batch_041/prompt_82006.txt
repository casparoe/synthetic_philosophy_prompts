I am applying for a six-month research fellowship and need a two-page proposal; I would rather have you draft it than stare at the blank page myself. Here is the idea I have been carrying around, in the state in which I have been carrying it.

The debate about fanaticism concerning the far future is usually conducted as an argument about the shape of a value distribution: astronomical stakes, minute probabilities, and whether any decision rule can license pursuing the former at the price of the latter. What I have not seen anyone ask is what the simulation hypothesis does to that argument, and the more I think about it the less I believe the answer is that it does nothing. The hypothesis, on the familiar trilemma according to which either civilizations like ours almost always die out before reaching a posthuman stage, or posthuman civilizations almost never run simulations of beings like us, or we are almost certainly simulated ourselves, appears to bear on the debate through two quite different channels. There is a quantity channel: if simulated minds count and simulations are numerous, the number of future minds at stake, and so the value in play, is multiplied rather than diminished. And there is what I have been calling a warrant channel: if most observers are simulated, our observations are drawn from a reference class dominated by simulated observers, and our evidence about the base-level civilization, including how much future there is to influence, is correspondingly degraded. The first channel looks like an amplifier, the second like a dampener. I do not know how they net out, and I suspect the interesting result is that they do not net out at all, because they act on different parts of the argument.

What I want is a proposal containing, first, a precise statement of the question, sharp enough to have a determinate answer. I suggest framing it as whether the expected value of a canonical far-future intervention, measured against a present-day welfare intervention, rises or falls once simulation credence is introduced, and under which assumptions. Second, why the existing positions leave this open, stated in terms of the two channels rather than by surveying the literature. Third, the method: a small formal model with the credence and the expected number of simulations as parameters, the channels as separate terms, a derivation of the net direction, and a sensitivity analysis identifying which premises the verdict turns on. Fourth, what a result would look like, and what would count as failure. On that last point I want the proposal to be candid about the obvious trap: if the warrant channel turns out to be nothing more than the generic pressure that any exotic sceptical scenario places on our probability estimates, then the simulation argument adds nothing here that a dozen other scenarios would not, and the honest deliverable is a short deflationary paper saying so. The proposal should treat that as a live outcome and say what would distinguish it from a failure to find an answer.

Scope the project for six months of my time and a single paper of about eight thousand words, with no empirical component. Do not include citations or a reading list; I will supply those.
