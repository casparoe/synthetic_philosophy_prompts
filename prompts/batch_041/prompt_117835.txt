I write the Ideas column for a science and technology monthly, and I am assembling an interactive flowchart for a long piece on the problem of logical omniscience: why an agent who is rational in the logician's sense turns out to believe every tautology, everything entailed by what she believes, and everything logically equivalent to it, while actual thinkers manifestly do not. The flowchart should double as a self-sorting quiz, so that readers follow the branches and land on the position they actually hold rather than the one they would name if asked.

Take as the trunk a premise I assume everyone shares: a competent reasoner can believe P, believe that if P then Q, and not believe Q, without any irrationality. Every internal node should then be a question a reader can answer without already knowing where it leads — a concrete case or a commitment, never a label. I want the following distinctions to appear as branch nodes, though you should decide their order and whether each is a genuine fork: whether the objects of belief are sentences, mental representations, or propositions; whether the four omniscience properties come as a package or can be prised apart — belief in every logical truth, closure under entailment, the indistinguishability of equivalent contents, and the impossibility of believing a contradiction without believing everything; whether the remedy is semantic, giving the agent finer contents to represent, or computational, granting her the right contents but bounding what she can do with them; whether deductive closure is a norm that actual believers violate or an idealization on the order of frictionless planes; whether a single notion of belief survives, or must be split into explicit and implicit belief, belief and acceptance, or dispositional and occurrent belief; and whether an agent may hold inconsistent beliefs.

Build in these diagnostic cases: the geometry student who knows the axioms and does not yet see the theorem; the person who asserts each of a long list of claims and, asked about their conjunction, denies it; someone who believes a disjunction without believing either disjunct; a system that will affirm a fact when asked directly but never draws on it in a chain of reasoning; and the probabilist's version of the problem, in which a credence function assigns probability one to every logical truth and equal probability to logically equivalent claims by construction.

Aim for eight to eleven internal nodes and six to nine leaves, at least one of which must be the position that there is no defect here at all. Each leaf should carry the name of the position where a settled one exists, or 'unnamed' where none does, plus one sentence on the commitment that the view's own defenders would find most awkward. Mark the combinations of answers that no position occupies, and say briefly whether each is unoccupied because it is incoherent, merely unattractive, or simply unexplored; flag any two leaves your tree cannot separate. The tree should be built from the logical space — questions first, positions located in it afterward — rather than from a tour of the familiar positions in the literature. No citations or reading lists: the piece runs without them, and I would rather the tree be argued than sourced.
