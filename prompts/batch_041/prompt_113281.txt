I'm working through Rousseau's Letter to d'Alembert on the Theater for a seminar on eighteenth-century moral philosophy, and I've hit a passage in the preface that I can't get into focus. Here it is: "This is certainly the most agreeable and seductive picture that could be offered us, but is, at the same time, the most dangerous advice that could be given us. At least, such is my sentiment, and my reasons are in this Writing." The picture in question is d'Alembert's proposal that Geneva admit a theater company, with the actors regulated and respected, so that the city might enjoy the refinements of drama without the corruption that attaches to the stage elsewhere. What puzzles me is the structure of Rousseau's judgment. He grants that the picture is agreeable and seductive—indeed he spends pages making it vivid—and then calls it the most dangerous advice possible. I can follow the general shape of his reply: that the theater works differently in a small republic than in a large monarchy, that it flatters the passions it claims to purify, and that it would give the Genevans a taste for display and idleness they can't afford. But I don't understand why he insists on the seductiveness as part of the danger, rather than treating it as a merely rhetorical foil. Is the seductiveness itself the problem—i.e., is the very attractiveness of the proposal evidence of its corrupting power? Or is he making a subtler point about the way the theater substitutes an image of virtue for virtue itself?

I'd like a close reading of this passage that explains what work the phrase about the agreeable and seductive picture is doing in his argument, and how it connects to his larger claims about amour-propre, the difference between the theater's effects in Paris and in Geneva, and the role of women in the audience. Please don't give me a summary of the whole Letter; I want to understand this specific move. I'm also curious whether the passage is best read as a warning about the theater's content or about the social form of theatrical spectatorship—whether the danger lies in what is staged or in the fact of a public assembled to watch and be watched. If you can, say something about how Rousseau's own rhetoric in the preface—his willingness to make the opposing case so attractive—relates to his argument that the theater is dangerous precisely because it makes vice attractive.
