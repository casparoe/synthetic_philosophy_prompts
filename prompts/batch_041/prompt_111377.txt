My friend and I spent most of last night arguing about whether compromise between people with different values is best modeled as a trade. Her line was that whenever two people with incompatible commitments can each get more of what they care about by conceding something they care about less, refusing to compromise is just leaving money on the table; her sharpest point was that calling two values incommensurable usually means only that the exchange rate is hard to find, which is a claim about our information rather than about the values themselves. My line was that a moral commitment isn't the kind of thing that has a price at which you'd sell it, and that a trade can leave both parties better off while quietly ruining someone who wasn't at the table. I didn't answer her cleanly, and I've been turning it over all day.

Rather than keep arguing, I'd like to write something. Please draft a deadpan parody of a client-facing prospectus from a boutique firm that brokers moral trades between parties with incompatible value systems — the sort of document a small advisory shop would send a prospective client, compliance-polished and faintly evangelical, roughly 700 to 800 words. It should never wink at the reader.

Make the firm's machinery concrete, so that the comedy comes from the actual structure of the position rather than from generic corporate jargon. For instance: it quotes two-way prices in a unit of moral value that it declines to define; it charges a spread that widens with the number of third parties affected by the deal but not party to it; it keeps a list of blocked goods that may not be traded, while the compliance annex defines that list so narrowly that almost nothing lands on it; it sells structured products to clients who can't locate a direct counterparty; and its testimonials come from clients who are entirely at peace with the trade and describe, in passing, something they can no longer bring themselves to do. Invent whatever further instruments, clauses, and departments you need.

The piece should be readable by someone who has never opened a book of philosophy, but a reader who has thought seriously about commensurability, the Pareto criterion, and the moral standing of non-parties should feel that the jokes are aimed at the real joints of the position — above all at the assumption that a mutually agreeable exchange is therefore a gain, and at what it takes for something to be the kind of thing that can be traded at all.

Be committed: choose one satirical target and press it consistently to the end instead of hedging into even-handedness, and don't let a character step outside the document to explain the joke. After the piece, add a note of about 150 words saying which claims you exaggerated and which reading of the underlying debate you leaned on, so that I can see where I'm being unfair to my friend.
