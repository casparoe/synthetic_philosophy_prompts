I am preparing a short seminar on whether the principle of indifference can be rescued, and I have grown tired of the stock examples — the chord of a circle, the cube factory, the broken stick. What I want from you is a new case, built to order, that does the work those examples are supposed to do while escaping the dismissal that is always levelled at them. That dismissal runs as follows: the classical examples cheat, because they instruct us to choose a point at random, or to draw a chord at random, without fixing the randomising mechanism, so of course different mechanisms deliver different answers; indifference has not been refuted, only misapplied. Your case must therefore be one in which the physical procedure is specified exhaustively — a device, a process, a routine that a workshop, a laboratory or a fairground could actually run, with the source of the randomness fully pinned down — and in which that single fully specified procedure nonetheless admits two or more descriptions, each of which indifference would treat as a partition into equiprobable cases, and which yield materially different answers to one and the same well-posed physical question about the outcome. The two answers should differ by a margin large enough to be visible in a few hundred trials, so that the divergence cannot be waved away as an artefact of idealised precision; if the divergence does depend on idealisation, say so plainly and tell me whether it survives discretisation of the device.

Please honour the following constraints. The competing descriptions must be equally natural: say what you mean by natural, and defend the claim that neither one requires a contrived, informationally unavailable or question-begging step, since if one description is plainly the right one the case proves nothing. The setup must not be a relabelling of the chord problem, and should be one that no textbook, so far as you can tell, has already used. Then do the analytical work. For each description, state the partition it induces, the answer it yields, and why indifference, applied to that description, is defensible on its own terms; where the descriptions differ in more than one respect, tell me which respects are inert. Then say whether any independent consideration — a symmetry of the physical setup, a consistency requirement binding different levels of description, a limiting-frequency argument, a demand that the answer be invariant under the transformations that leave the setup unchanged — selects one answer, and give me the argument rather than the verdict. If no such consideration is available in your case, that is the more interesting outcome, and I want the argument for its unavailability, not an assertion.

Finish with the honest reckoning. Which single feature of the case is doing the work? What happens when that feature is removed — does the case collapse into something benign, or into one of the classical examples? Which answer would an actual frequency experiment on your device vindicate, and does that vindication settle the philosophical question or merely show that the procedure, though fully specified, was never fully described by either partition? And how should a defender of indifference attack the case — what would they have to say to show that the principle was misapplied rather than refuted, and would that reply, if accepted, leave indifference with any substantive content at all?
