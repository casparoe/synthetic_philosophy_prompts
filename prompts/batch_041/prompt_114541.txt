I have been reading the rationalist blogs and forums for a long time — long enough to have watched the coherent-extrapolated-volition proposal go from a slogan to something people actually argue about, and long enough to have absorbed the standard objections to it. I have come around to the position anyway. Stripped down, it is the claim that the right thing to aim a superintelligence at, and more grandly the right thing to aim our own politics at, is neither what anyone currently says they want nor a fixed list of goods, but what human preferences would converge on if the people holding them were better informed, more internally consistent, more reflective, and more the people they would want to become. I do not need this sold to me or dismissed. I need to know where it is exposed.

Give me a defeat map. Treat it as an adversarial exercise: what would have to be true for coherent extrapolation to be the wrong target, and how much damage would each of those things do if it were true? Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, and mark, for each, whether a committed defender of the view would have to concede it or could deflect it. Spend your effort on the structural points rather than the familiar ones. In particular:

1. Order of operations. Extrapolating each person's preferences and then aggregating need not yield the same result as aggregating and then extrapolating. If the two come apart, which is the view committed to, and can the choice be justified from inside the theory, or does it smuggle in an extra commitment?

2. Self-reference. People have preferences about how their own preferences should be idealized — someone may prefer, coherently, not to be made more coherent in a particular respect. If the extrapolation procedure is itself something the extrapolated agent could reject, does the view have a stable fixed point, or can idealization cycle? What follows if it can?

3. Non-convergence. Suppose idealized preferences do not in fact converge, either across people or across different idealization paths for a single person. Is that a refutation, or only evidence that the view needs a tie-break it cannot supply from its own resources?

4. Laundering and the self. If idealization changes the content of the preference a person regards as most central to who they are, is the result still their preference? What would a defender say, and is that answer principled or ad hoc?

5. Construction. If the psychology suggests that preferences are substantially constructed in the act of being elicited rather than waiting to be discovered, does that undermine the idea that there is a fact about what the extrapolation would yield — and how strong is that evidence, honestly?

6. Boundary. Who is inside the extrapolation set, and does the view have a principled way to draw that line rather than a convenient one?

7. Convergence on something monstrous. Could an idealized agent converge on ends we would find repellent? If so, does that count against the view as a normative ideal, or only against its use as an alignment target?

Then tell me what the view stakes itself on: what should we expect to observe if it is right that its rivals would not predict, and which of the defeaters above, if any, do you think are already satisfied? Close with an honest verdict on whether the view has been qualified into unfalsifiability, and whether that is a genuine problem for a view of this kind or a misplaced demand.
