Two thinkers who agree that humanity is on a trajectory to lose meaningful control over its own future disagree sharply about the mechanism of the loss. The first holds that failure will arrive as an influence-seeking system: an entity that accumulates resources, discretion, and the capacity to shape its environment, and then pursues objectives of its own that no one chose, so that humans retain the trappings of authority while the consequential decisions are quietly made elsewhere. The second holds that no seizure is required. On this view we will simply keep delegating to systems that optimize measurable stand-ins for what we value; the stand-ins will drift away from the things they were meant to track; and the world will be gradually optimized for something no one intended — a failure that never announces itself, because each individual step is locally reasonable and defensible on its own terms.

Write a dialogue between these two. Neither should be a strawman, and each should be as articulate and as sharp as the other; each must engage the other's strongest formulation, press its weakest joint, and concede where the other has a point, rather than talking past it or trading slogans. Have them test both accounts against a concrete case — for instance, a plausible near future in which a small number of systems mediate logistics, scientific research, and public persuasion — and require them to name at least one observation that would count in favor of one account and against the other. Let them also disagree about whether the two stories are rivals or successive phases of a single process, and about whether that reconciliation should comfort anyone. The dialogue should not end with the two positions merely restated.
