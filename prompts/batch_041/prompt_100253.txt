I have been working through Harsanyi's aggregation theorem in a reading group, and a friend there has put together the argument below. He says it shows that any social welfare framework built on expected utility collapses into classical utilitarianism, and he treats the conclusion as a reductio of our reluctance to accept it. I cannot accept the conclusion, but I also cannot find the break in the chain, and I would like you to locate it.

The argument, as he wrote it:

(1) Every individual's preferences over risky prospects satisfy the expected-utility axioms, so each individual i can be represented by a utility function u_i, unique up to a positive affine transformation.
(2) There is a social betterness relation over risky prospects that satisfies those same axioms, so it can be represented by a utility function W, unique up to a positive affine transformation.
(3) The social relation satisfies the Pareto principle: whenever every individual weakly prefers prospect L to prospect M, W(L) ≥ W(M).
(4) Before aggregating, we fix each u_i on a single common scale: a stipulated zero point, a stipulated unit, and a reference lottery that everyone is stipulated to evaluate alike. This is meant to make interpersonal comparisons of both levels and differences of utility available.
(5) By Harsanyi's aggregation theorem, (1) through (4) entail that W is a positive weighted sum of the individual utilities, W = Σ a_i u_i with each a_i > 0.
(6) Since no person's welfare counts for more than any other's, the weights must be equal, so W = Σ u_i. This is classical utilitarianism.
(7) Three consequences follow. (a) A world in which everyone's utility is equal is not intrinsically better than a world with the same total distributed with gross inequality; equality matters only through diminishing marginal utility. (b) If killing one innocent person would raise the total by even a little, the total requires it. (c) Any distribution of utility can be justified, provided it maximizes the total.
(8) These consequences are unacceptable.
(9) So one of (1) through (6) is false.

I am attached to (1), (2), and (3) and would give them up last; I care much less about the claim that the social ranking is a plain sum. My friend replies that (6) is nothing more than the moral equality of persons, and that whoever rejects it is saying that some people count for more than others. I do not need an introduction to utilitarianism or a survey of social choice theory.
