I have no academic training in philosophy and have never read Frege in the original, only summaries and a few translated essays, but I keep returning to a question I think I have settled for myself, and I would like it tested.

My position is this. The diary Frege kept in his final years, which became public only decades after his death and which contains antisemitic and anti-democratic remarks along with a hope for some strong authoritarian leader, has no bearing on the philosophical and logical work. It does not make his doctrines about sense, reference, concepts, or the foundations of arithmetic any less likely to be true; it does not change how the arguments should be read; and it does not tell us what he meant by the technical notions. That a man held ugly political views is one fact about him; whether a proof is valid is another. Where it might matter is only in the history of influence and in how we choose to present the work.

What I want is the case against this. Concretely: what would have to turn out to be true for the diary to bear on the philosophy, and not merely on the man? I can imagine several kinds of bearing and I want them kept apart — bearing on the truth of the doctrines, bearing on their correct interpretation, bearing on what the doctrines are really doing (so that a charitable reading is no longer available), and bearing on whether we should keep treating the work as authoritative. For each kind, tell me what evidence or argument would defeat my position, and rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward.

A few things I have wondered about, and I would like you to say whether they are real defeaters or just atmospherics. First, Frege insisted that concepts must have sharp boundaries and treated vagueness as a defect; is there a case that this ideal of logical tidiness is itself a kind of politics, so that the diary is continuous with the philosophy rather than incidental to it? Second, his own view seems to be that thoughts and truths are independent of any thinker, inhabiting a realm of their own; if that is right, then no fact about his character could bear on them, which would make my position not a methodological preference but a consequence of the doctrine itself. Is that genuine support, or is it exactly the kind of self-insulation that should make me suspicious? Third, I have read that in his late writings he turned to diagnosing the political and social life of his time as riddled with logical confusion; if logic was for him something to be applied to a nation's thinking, does that collapse the separation I want?

What would move me most is a case where noticing the politics changes what a passage means, rather than merely coloring how I feel about it. And I would like the answer to say plainly how confident it is in what it claims, and to single out the one point it is least sure about.
