I recently spent an evening arguing with a close friend about Aumann's agreement theorem, and we could not settle what the theorem is actually supposed to show. We both accept the formal result: if two agents share a prior and their posterior probabilities for some event are common knowledge, then those posteriors must be identical. What divided us was its significance. My friend insisted that the theorem places a real constraint on rational disagreement—that when two people who share a prior and are both rational persist in disagreeing, something must have gone wrong, either in their rationality or in the assumption that their priors really are the same. I argued that the theorem is far less useful as a diagnosis of real disagreement, because it requires common knowledge of the posteriors and not merely of the fact that each has a posterior, and because the agents must also have common knowledge of the information structures, signal distributions, and higher-order beliefs that generated those posteriors. In an actual argument, those are precisely the things we are uncertain about. By the end we were circling each other.

Please turn that argument into a dialogue of roughly 900 to 1200 words. The dialogue should be between two characters who have just had a disagreement of this kind and who are now trying to reconstruct what, if anything, Aumann's theorem implies about it. Both characters should be intellectually serious and neither should be a strawman: the defender should not simply chant that all disagreement is irrational, and the critic should not simply dismiss the theorem as irrelevant. The defender should press the idea that common priors plus common knowledge of posteriors leave no room for rational disagreement, so any apparent counterexample must involve a failure of one of the assumptions. The critic should press the distinction between common knowledge of posterior beliefs and common knowledge of the entire information structure, and should ask whether the very fact of their disagreement, once it becomes common knowledge, should itself be treated as a new signal that both agents must conditionalise on. Let them work through a concrete example—perhaps two forecasters who have seen different private reports and announced different probabilities for the same event—and let them examine whether that example satisfies the theorem's assumptions, whether it evades them, or whether it shows the theorem applying but not doing the work the defender wants. The dialogue should contain genuine back-and-forth: each character should respond to the other's actual points, and they should end with a sharper formulation of their disagreement rather than a resolution by slogan. Do not have either character cite papers, authors, dates, or reading lists; the argument should be self-contained and should reason from the ideas themselves.
