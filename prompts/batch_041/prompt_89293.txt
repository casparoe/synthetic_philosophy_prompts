I write fiction, and I am some way into a novel whose central section turns on the cold fusion affair of 1989. I have no interest in adjudicating the physics; what holds me is the shape of the collective belief — how a roomful of serious people came to accept something, and then, within weeks, came to be certain it had never been there at all. I would like the intellectual spine of that section to be an honest analysis rather than a novelist's impression. So: read the episode through the theory of information cascades and rational herding, and then tell me, with equal care, where the theory is the wrong instrument.

Treat the following as given, and do not spend words establishing it. In the spring of 1989, Martin Fleischmann, an electrochemist of standing, and Stanley Pons, at the University of Utah, reported that electrolysis of heavy water at a palladium electrode produced heat in excess of anything chemistry could account for, together with traces of what looked like nuclear products. The claim reached the public at a university press conference in late March, before any paper had appeared. A parallel group under Steven Jones at Brigham Young, measuring neutron flux, had been sharing technique with them and had agreed to publish simultaneously; the university administration, anxious about priority, pushed its men to speak first. Through April, laboratories in several countries announced confirmations — a heat result in Texas, a neutron result in Georgia, an excess-heat report at Stanford — and some of these were withdrawn within days. By the end of April the newspapers had declared the matter closed, and on 1 May 1989, at a special session of the American Physical Society in Baltimore, speaker after speaker reported failure, to the evident satisfaction of the room. The Department of Energy declined to fund the work, and the small community that kept at it did so for decades under other names. Two years earlier, high-temperature superconductivity had taught physicists that an effect no theory predicts can nonetheless be real.

I want the analysis to run in both directions and to be disciplined about the mapping. First: who are the players, and are they a single population at all? The model of a cascade needs agents who share a prior, each holding a private signal, choosing in an observable order from a common action set, and rationally discounting their own signal once the run of prior choices looks long enough. Enumerate the distinct populations here — the electrochemists, the nuclear physicists, the funding bodies, the university administrators, the journalists, the investors — and say whether we are looking at one cascade or several loosely coupled ones, and whether the ordering of moves was exogenous or, as I suspect, manufactured on the spot by the press conference. Second: what counts as a private signal, and what counts as an action? A replication attempt is both at once, and its result is a public good that no one has a private incentive to produce; say what that does to the model's assumption that signals are independent and that actions reveal information. Third: the payoff. The standard setup presumes a common value, so that watching others is informative. Here priority, patents, tenure and credit were in play, which introduces a private-value component and should, by the theory's own logic, make the herd less informative rather than more. Work through what the theory still explains — the fragility of the run, the smallness of the accident that starts it, the way a cascade breaks rather than erodes — and what it misdescribes: the reversals, the strength of the theoretical prior against room-temperature fusion, and the fact that a failed replication is evidence of a different kind from a failed bet. Where the theory has nothing to say, say so plainly rather than forcing it to speak.

Two constraints. Do not supply citations, reading lists or attributions; I want the argument, not the apparatus. And do not narrate the history back to me — I have the history. What I want is the case analysis: the model applied until it strains, and an account of what this episode shows about the model that its usual examples do not.
