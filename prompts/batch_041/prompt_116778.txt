I sit on the AI oversight committee of a mid-sized hospital network, and we are currently deciding whether to sign off on a sepsis-prediction tool that flags deteriorating patients for a rapid-response team. Most of my last month has gone into arguing with colleagues about what happens the first time it misses someone and no one can say who is answerable for the death. That argument sent me into the literature on responsibility gaps for autonomous systems, and it happens that one of the philosophers I have been reading is giving a talk at our affiliated university's ethics seminar next month. I have been asked to moderate the Q&A, and afterwards to record a longer conversation with him for a small technology-ethics podcast I co-host. Take the role of a philosopher of technology with a strong background in the theory of moral responsibility — blameworthiness, accountability, tracing, and corporate and collective responsibility — and think carefully before you write. Begin by laying out for yourself where the weakest joints in his position are, and only then draft the questions.

His position, as I understand it, is roughly this. The "responsibility gap" is usually presented as a metaphysical problem: an autonomous system behaves in ways its designers did not foresee and could not fully control, so no one satisfies the conditions for being blameworthy, and the outcome goes unowned. He thinks that diagnosis is an artifact of insisting on locating responsibility at the moment of action, and he wants to relocate it to the level of what he calls practice design — the setting of objectives, thresholds, escalation rules, oversight budgets, and the decision to deploy at all. On his view responsibility attaches primarily to whoever fixed those parameters, and the failure to anticipate some particular case is no excuse, because the parameters were chosen under conditions in which the range of failure modes was, in principle, surveyable. He also holds that the gap argument is often deployed strategically, to push toward prohibition rather than toward accountability, and that the move from no one is answerable to no one may deploy this slides by far too quickly. What I want out of the Q&A is pressure on that relocation, not a restatement of it.

Draft me eight to ten interview questions, each with a follow-up keyed to the answer he is most likely to give, ordered as I would actually ask them, with a one-line note for each saying what the question is designed to expose. I need at least one question on the many-hands problem — how his account keeps responsibility from dissolving across the dozens of engineers, clinicians, and product managers whose micro-decisions jointly produce the system's behaviour — and at least one on what becomes of his view when a failure mode is not merely unforeseen but arguably unforeseeable in principle, since the system's learned representations are opaque even to the people who trained it. I also want a question on the operator as blame absorber: in practice, when a tool like ours fails, the person at the bedside is the one who answers for it, and I want him to say whether that is a distortion of his view or a consequence of it. One question should press on whether relocating responsibility to practice design collapses moral responsibility into something like strict liability or a regulatory duty, and if it does, whether ethics is doing any work that the regulator could not do alone. Make the follow-ups genuinely responsive rather than generic: if he retreats to collective responsibility, the follow-up should press on whether a collective can really be the bearer of blame or whether that is a way of changing the subject. Finish with a question that forces him to name a case his framework handles badly, and a follow-up asking what he would revise in light of it.
