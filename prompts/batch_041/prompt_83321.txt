I'm a high school student doing an independent study project on the metaphysics of persistence, and I've reached the point where I need to design my own case rather than lean on the famous ones. I'd like you to design one for me.

The two views I'm contrasting: endurantism, on which an object that persists is wholly present at each moment of its existence, so that the very same object is there at every moment; and perdurantism, on which an object that persists is stretched out through time, so that what is located at any one moment is only a temporal part of it, and the whole object is never present at a single moment.

Here is the specification the case has to meet.

Agreement at the surface: endurantism and perdurantism must both say that one and the same object is involved throughout the scenario, and both must say that it persists. The disagreement has to be finer-grained than the everyday question of whether it survived. I want it to be a disagreement about what is literally located at a moment, or about what the subject of the change is.

It has to be an ordinary situation: a household object, a plant, an animal, a building, a person doing something mundane. No teleportation, duplication, fission or fusion, brain transplants, time travel, or exotic physics.

Exclusions, because these are the cases everyone already uses: no gradual replacement of parts (nothing Ship-of-Theseus-shaped), no statue and lump of clay coinciding, and not the textbook case where a single intrinsic property simply swaps (bent to straight, red to green). I want a case that is not the one a student would find in the first chapter of any introduction.

Asymmetry: perdurantism should come out worse. Taking its own commitments at face value — objects have temporal parts, and what is located at a time is a part — it should end up denying, or heavily re-describing, a claim that we would ordinarily regard as plainly true, while endurantism can accept that same claim without re-description. Crucially, the awkwardness must not come from a caricature of perdurantism: it must not depend on perdurantists being unable to relate parts to one another, or on their treating moments as isolated objects.

The scenario itself should be one paragraph, and it must not stipulate the verdicts. Whatever each view says has to follow from the view, not from the way the scene is set up.

After the scenario, please do the following, in this order.

1. Give each view's verdict in its own terms, and explain the reasoning that produces it.
2. Name the single feature of the case that is doing the work — the one thing that, if altered, would make the disagreement vanish or flip.
3. Say what happens when that feature is removed or reversed: does the case collapse into the textbook one, or into agreement between the views?
4. Explain how a committed perdurantist would attack the case, and whether their reply costs them anything they should mind.
5. Tell me honestly whether the case satisfies every specification above. If some specification forced you into a weak or artificial case, say which one and why, rather than quietly working around it.

The case is for a short argumentative paper in which I want to claim that endurantism is the more sensible default for ordinary cases. So the case has to earn its verdicts from the theories rather than borrow them from the setup. Please don't cite sources or name any philosophers; I have no way to check references, and I want the reasoning to stand on its own.
