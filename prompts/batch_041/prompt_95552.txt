**Spinoza's Democracy Isn't Ours, and That's the Interesting Part**

Everyone files Spinoza under proto-liberal: the TTP is a plea for free thought, he calls democracy the most natural form of state, so he's a democrat and we get to claim him. I think that's backwards in a way that matters. Take seriously his equation of right with power -- a thing's right just is what it can do, and a commonwealth's right is its power to make people act -- and the defense of democracy stops being a defense of popular rule as a value. It becomes a claim about which constitution actually holds together, given what people are: creatures of passion who can nonetheless be led as though by a single mind when they share hope and fear.

Three things fall out, and I'm not sure I want all of them. First, there's no independent yardstick left. If right is power, then "the people have a right to X" only ever means they have the power to get it, and a state that has the power has the right, full stop. That sounds like it makes his theory useless for exactly the thing we want a theory of democracy for -- criticizing a government that's dismantling democracy while still mustering enough power to stay in office. Second, his freedom is metaphysical before it's political: acting from the necessity of one's own nature rather than from external causes. So the freedom of the TTP isn't a protected sphere of non-interference, it's the conditions under which reason can operate -- and he defends free philosophizing on state-interest grounds (you can't command thought anyway, and silencing opinion turns subjects into liars and weakens the commonwealth), not because speech is sacred. Relatedly, he hands the sovereign authority over public religion, and in the one chapter he ever devotes to the subject he rules women out of holding political power. Third, he says peace isn't the mere absence of war but a virtue or disposition, which I read as cutting against treating him as caring about stability as such.

The part I'm least sure of: I want to say all this makes him weirdly useful for thinking about democratic backsliding, because it forces the question of what "the people" even is. His multitude only becomes something like a people when it's united; so a state could keep its formal power while the multitude that sustained it has already dissolved. Is that a real reading or am I being cute?

So what I'd like from you: whether the right-equals-power move is right and whether the "can't criticize anything" conclusion actually follows from it, or whether I've skipped a step where something like legitimacy does come back in; whether the metaphysical reading of freedom is defensible or anachronistic; whether the backsliding bit is a real reading or just cute; and in general whether this is interesting or just a familiar line in a new outfit. And if any of my textual claims are wrong, flag them, since I'm working from memory.
