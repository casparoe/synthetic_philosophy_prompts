I do communications for a small wetland-restoration charity — reeds, waders, a new boardwalk, the usual donor newsletter — and I've been handed next year's annual report to draft. For eighteen months now the board has been arguing over whether we should adopt "welfare accounting" in our reporting, and I want to write the report as though we already have: donor-facing register, glossy and sincere, no winking, letting the numbers do the damage. About 800 words, laid out like a real annual report — a chair's letter, a handful of impact figures, a section on the new marsh, a discreet box on the predator-management pilot, a dissenting footnote from one trustee, a "looking ahead." The target is a practice rather than a person: the unexamined "more nature is good" that restoration work runs on, and the way a serious moral thought about animals gets converted into a reporting standard. The comedy should come from the collision between the register (volunteers, reed planting, a commemorative bench) and the arithmetic (estimated hatchlings, estimated mortalities, net welfare balance, confidence intervals the size of the wetland); the most chilling sentences should be the blandest. Fill it with the actual substance of the wild-animal-welfare debate rather than with jokes about vegans: the demographic point that the most numerous animals are r-strategists whose young die in vast numbers and usually slowly, from starvation, exposure, disease, parasites and predators; the population-ethics knot that follows — whether adding animals to a marsh adds welfare or only adds suffering, and whether a life likely to go badly counts as a harm to the one who lives it; the disagreement among people who take the problem seriously about whether predation is itself a target and whether we have any business intervening in systems we don't understand; the epistemic embarrassment that the animals we'd be counting by the billion are the ones we're least sure can suffer; and the institutional fact that our donors paid for reeds, not for arithmetic on how the newts will die. Don't let it become a tract in either direction: the framework has to take its own hits — the measurement pretensions, the fact that nobody can say whether the goal is fewer animals or happier ones, the footnote where the trustee asks what this does to the donor base.

After the piece, add a separate short note (150 words or so, not in the report's voice) on where the satire takes liberties with the ideas or leans on a contested reading — if the mortality picture, the predator position, or the population-ethics claim are things people in this debate actually dispute, say which and how. And if you think the brief is confused — if a welfare-accounting annual report is a contradiction that can only come out preachy or smug, or if I've built it so that one side cannot lose — tell me so plainly instead of writing it anyway. Begin with the report itself: no preface, no framing, no restating of what I asked for.
