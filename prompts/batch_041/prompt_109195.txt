Okay so I spent about forty years as a glacial geologist — ice cores, moraines, varves, the whole business — trying to reconstruct what the climate was doing tens of thousands of years ago. And the thing about all that evidence is that none of it was trying to tell me anything. A moraine doesnt lie, but it doesnt answer questions either; you have to already know how to read it. Now I'm retired, I've gotten weirdly into philosophy, and lately I've been reading a lot about the ancient world — Herodotus, Thucydides, and then modern people arguing about how you actually know any of it happened. And it strikes me the historians have the opposite problem from me. Most of their evidence is stuff somebody made on purpose to say something. A chronicle wants to be beleived.

So here's a distinction I've been chewing on, tell me if its real or if I'm just reinventing a wheel: some evidence is a relic — it survives as a byproduct, a garbage dump, a tax ledger, an ice core — and some evidence is testimony, it survives because somebody meant to tell you something. My instinct, from the old job, is that when the two disagree you should trust the relic. The testimony has an agenda; the relic just is.

What I want from you: give me an example of a conceptual issue that this instinct runs into. I don't mean the obvious one where the writer was lying. I mean a case where reading a source as a bare relic — ignoring what it says, using only the fact that it exists and what it's made of and how it got there — gets you into trouble, or where the relic-reading and the testimony-reading pull in different directions and there's no clean rule for which one wins. Something with real teeth, the kind of thing that would eat up an entire seminar. And bonus points if you can tell me whether my relic-first instinct is actually a defensible epistemic principle or just a bad habit I picked up from forty years of working with things that can't talk back.
