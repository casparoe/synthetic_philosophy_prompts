I'm giving a WIP talk to my department in about two weeks and I want my Q&A prepped before I get up there. The paper in a nutshell: everyone in the blame literature says the hypocrite lacks the standing to blame. I think that's two claims wearing one coat. One is that the hypocrite's blame can't actually make a demand on you -- call it the claim reading. The other is that the hypocrite's blame is itself a bad thing, an added insult, a second wrong on top of the first -- the credit reading. My line is that the claim reading is false and the credit reading is true, and that once you seperate them, hypocrisy comes out as a wrong added to the scene rather than a defeater of the blame that's already there. The hypocrite's blame still lands as blame; what we're objecting to is the nerve of it. Hypocrisy makes blame rude, not void.

The case I lean on: a colleague who has never once answered my emails and has blown off every deadline we share sits me down to explain that professionalism requires prompt replies. I want to say his blame is fully in order and that he is also doing something worse than nothing.

The room: twelve or so people, mostly responsibility-and-blame people in the Strawson-Scanlon line, so they'll hear blame as a way of relating to a person rather than as an act that can succeed or fail -- that's going to shape how they come at me. Two of them will say I've just relabeled standing as warrant. One is a threshold purist who thinks standing is all or nothing and will say my degrees-of-standing talk is incoherent. Two grad students work on the moral psychology of blame and will hit me with what people actually do when a hypocrite blames them; I don't want to be flat-footed there, since I won't get to say "that's a different question" twice in one hour. Nobody in the room works on speech acts, so I can't hide in that jargon.
