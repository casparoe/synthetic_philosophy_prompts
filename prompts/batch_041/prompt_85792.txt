I'm running a 75-minute session for our law-and-philosophy undergrad society (about 15 people, a few have done some political philosophy, none have done any logic) on whether a law can change the rules for changing the law. Don't explain the topic to me — plan how I should explain it.

The hook is a game I'll hand out at the start: a stripped-down Nomic. Eight numbered rules; two are marked entrenched, so they can only be changed by a unanimous vote, the other six by simple majority; on your turn you propose an amendment to any rule, including the entrenchment rule and including the rule that says how proposals get voted on; every adopted amendment scores the proposer a point; first to ten points wins. Dont lean on the official Nomic rule set or on anything you half-remember about it — the version above is the one that exists for this session.

What I want from you:

- The order the ideas have to come in, and what depends on what. I have a hunch that "a rule about rules" has to be on the table before the paradox can even be stated properly, but tell me if that's wrong and what else has to come first. Also say where the game itself sits in the sequence — before the framing, during it, or after.

- The one example that should carry the whole session, and what it has to have (small enough to run in your head, no authority outside itself, a reason to want to amend) and what it must not have. - One question to ask at the end that tells me they've got the structure of the problem rather than just enjoyed the game.

- What I should openly say I'm not covering at this level.
