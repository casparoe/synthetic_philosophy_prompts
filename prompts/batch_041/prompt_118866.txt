ok so my reading group is doing kant next month and somehow i've ended up with the universalizability thing, and i want to open with a poem rather than a summary, because the summary always turns into the empty-formalism fight and then we never get past it. heres what i'm after.

a poem, under 30 lines, no rhymes needed, first person, someone talking to themselves — not an essay in verse. it has two halves, one maxim each. one line has to keep coming back and it should be the universalizing move itself: something like "if everyone did that" or "what if we all did it too," your phrasing, but it has to be the same line every time and it has to land differently each time it returns.

half one runs a maxim that dies the moment you imagine everyone doing it. not "dies" as in it becomes a bad world, but as in the practice it leans on can't survive being made general, so the thing the speaker was doing becomes unthinkable and the point of it evaporates. half two runs a maxim that sails through that same move with no contradiction at all, nothing collapses — but the world it describes is one nobody could actually want to live in, and the poem needs to make that read as a different kind of failure, not the same failure in a new costume. the two failure modes are the whole reason i want this as a poem instead of a paragraph: if the form can't tell them apart, it isn't doing anything.

dont use the promise example for half one, everyone knows that one and it'll just get recited. pick something from ordinary life now. and both maxims should be things the speaker is actually doing, so it reads as a person catching themselves rather than a person lecturing a room. last stanza brings the repeated line back one more time and lets both failures sit in it at once.

then after the poem, a short note: separate whats settled among people who actually work on this from what's genuinely contested. i know there's a live fight about whether the two kinds of contradiction are really two kinds or whether the second one is doing any independent work, and about whether the test gets the answers kant wanted or just launders ordinary moral judgment. if the poem only holds together because you're taking a contested line on one of those, say so, dont dress it up as the standard reading. i'd rather have a poem with an honest footnote than a poem that quietly cheats.
