I am a software engineer with no formal philosophy background, and I would like you to restate the free will debate in the vocabulary I actually use, then tell me what does not survive the translation.

The vocabulary I want you to translate into is the one I think in at work: deterministic programs and their inputs; seeds and sources of randomness; closed versus open systems; functions mapping inputs to outputs; interrupts, signals, and preemption; watchdogs, failsafes, and override logic; sandboxes and capability permissions; dependency injection; closed-loop control and feedback; version control and the attribution of changes to authors; postmortems and accountability processes. I am fluent in all of that, including the parts about attribution and blame in a codebase. I am not fluent in modal logic, possible worlds, or any of the formal apparatus philosophers use for counterfactuals, so please do not use those as the target vocabulary; if something needs them, say so in the residue report instead.

Take the main positions as they are usually drawn — hard determinism, libertarian incompatibilism, and compatibilism — and restate each one as faithfully as you can in the vocabulary above, one at a time. I want the translation to be honest: do not quietly assume the compatibilist's conclusions and then present them as neutral translation, and do not let the software framing do argumentative work that the original position would not endorse.

Then give me the residue report, which is the part I care about most. Specifically:

1. The claim that an agent could have done otherwise: I am tempted to read this as counterfactual sensitivity of a deterministic program to its inputs — same code, different input, different output. Does that capture what the incompatibilist means, or does it substitute a different question? If it substitutes, what exactly was lost?

2. The class of cases where a hidden mechanism would have forced the agent's hand but never actually had to intervene, so the agent acted on its own reasons and is still held responsible. That maps, I think, onto a watchdog or override process that never fires. Tell me what the translation gets right and what it flattens — in particular, whether anything in the original case depends on the mechanism being hidden or on the agent not knowing about it, which my vocabulary would treat as just an information asymmetry.

3. The manipulation scenario, where someone's decisions have been shaped from the outside since before they could object. In my vocabulary this is close to a system whose behavior was specified by a designer, which describes essentially every program I have ever worked on. Does the manipulation argument therefore lose all its force when translated, or does the translation reveal that the argument's force came from a contrast that does not exist in my vocabulary?

4. Responsibility and blame. In a codebase, blame is an attribution practice: we assign it by convention, we revise it when the postmortem changes, and no one thinks we are discovering a fact about the universe. Does translating moral responsibility into that show that the compatibilist's notion is a social artifact, or does it show that my vocabulary is too thin to distinguish causal authorship from accountability? I genuinely cannot tell which.

Finally, tell me where the residue indicates a defect in my vocabulary rather than a defect in the philosophy. If some part of the debate has no counterpart because my concepts cannot express the distinction being drawn, say which concept I am missing and what it would have to do. If instead the residue is a demand that no program and no person could satisfy — for instance, an agent being the ultimate author of its own source — then say that plainly, and say whether the philosophers treat that demand as a real requirement or as a reductio.
