So I'm running the last session of a decision-theory reading group and I want a handout, not another essay. Topic is the backward induction paradox — the centipede game, and the unraveling argument in the finitely repeated prisoner's dilemma — and the room is going to be full of people who agree about the game trees and disagree about absolutely everything downstream of them. What I need is a decision tree that sorts them.

Start from the thing everyone in the room accepts: in these games there's a unique subgame-perfect equilibrium, it gets found by reasoning backwards from the last node, and it tells the first player to stop or defect straight away (in the centipede, on the very first move; in the finitely repeated PD, in round one and every round after). Then branch on the commitments that actually separate people. Every interior node should be a question a reader can answer without already knowing which position it leads to — phrase them as cases or claims, not as labels. The splits I care about:

- whether the conclusion is a claim about the model or about rational agents: does it follow from the description of the game plus the assumption of rationality, or only from a particular solution concept being imposed on that description?
- whether two real players can have common knowledge of each other's rationality, and whether the regress that generates is well-founded or self-undermining
- whether the money payoffs exhaust the players' preferences — do other-regarding or process tastes (a taste for cooperating, or for not being a sucker) get to enter the description?
- whether rationality attaches to acts or to plans — if I've decided at move one to cooperate later, am I irrational for reneging at the last node where reneging pays?
- whether the unit that reasons is the individual or the pair
- whether the small-chance-of-error argument (any real player might tremble, and once they might, cooperating early can be optimal) is a refutation of backward induction or a change of subject
- and whether a position is predicting what people will do or prescribing what's rational — alot of the literature slides between these and I want them seperate on the tree
