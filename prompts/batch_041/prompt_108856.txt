I cover science and ideas for a metropolitan daily, and I once spent a year reporting on why people keep paying for things they have already lost. On Saturday I give the closing toast at the retirement dinner of the editor who hired me. Her family will be at two tables near the front and will know none of the shop stories.

The story the room tells about her is that she surrendered. Twenty-two years ago she and a reporter at a rival paper were working the same long investigation; the two papers spent four years and, by the accountants' estimate, close to two million dollars trying to beat each other to a story that finally ran in both places on the same morning and changed nothing. In the third year she asked to be taken off it. Half the newsroom has never forgiven her; the other half has never quite explained her.

What I believe is true is a dollar auction: two bidders, a dollar bill, the highest bidder pays and takes it, the second-highest pays and gets nothing, and the bidding runs past a dollar because neither can afford to be the one who stops. Escalation is rational from inside the game and ruinous from outside it, so the only good move is never to enter, and by the time you notice you are in it you have already lost whatever you put in. Her third year was that moment. What the room wants from me is a redemption story — that she saw it first, that quitting was the win. What I believe is narrower and less consoling: by then she was already the second-highest bidder, and stopping recovered nothing; it only stopped the bleeding.

I want the toast to turn on the difference between a commitment and a sunk cost, because that is the distinction she kept and the newsroom never did. A commitment is worth holding because holding it changes what other people can count on; a sunk cost is worth dropping because the only thing keeping you in is what you have already paid. She held the first for two years and dropped the second in the third, and the same decision looks like fidelity from one side of that line and folly from the other.

Two further things. The dinner has a custom — four speakers, each topping the last with a wilder story about her, and I am the fourth. I do not want to top anyone; I want to end the sequence, and I want the room to feel the trap it is in before I let it out. And she once stood up in the middle of the paper's charity dinner, where the auctioneer runs the same game with the proceeds going to the food bank, so that stopping looks like stinginess, and pledged the amount of the standing bid if everyone would put their paddles down. Nobody has ever settled whether that was generosity or sabotage.

Write the toast I should give: five to seven minutes, meant to be spoken aloud, no game-theoretic vocabulary beyond that single image, and nothing in it that her sister would have to take on faith.
