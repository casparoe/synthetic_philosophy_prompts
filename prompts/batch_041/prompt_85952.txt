ok, i'm TAing for a political philosophy course and i've got alot of midterms to get through. one of the questions was this:

"Rawls argues that parties in the original position would choose his two principles of justice, including the difference principle, by a maximin argument. Harsanyi, starting from a similar veil-of-ignorance setup, argues that the parties would instead choose a principle of average utility. Explain how Rawls's argument for the difference principle is supposed to work, and explain the main point of disagreement with Harsanyi. Does Rawls's argument require the parties to be risk-averse? Be explicit about (a) what the parties know and don't know behind the veil, (b) what motivates them, and (c) why Rawls thinks maximin is the appropriate rule for their choice."

here's the student's answer:

"Rawls's original position is a hypothetical choice situation where the parties agree on principles of justice for the basic structure of society. To make the choice fair, Rawls puts them behind a veil of ignorance. They don't know their place in society, their class or social status, their natural talents, or their conception of the good. They do know general facts about society and human psychology, and they know they'll have some conception of the good and will want primary goods—rights, liberties, opportunities, income, and wealth—as means to pursue it. Since they don't know who they'll be, they can't tailor principles to their own advantage, so whatever they agree to will be fair.

Rawls argues the parties would choose two principles: first, equal basic liberties; second, fair equality of opportunity plus the difference principle, which says social and economic inequalities are just only if they benefit the least advantaged. The first principle has priority, so basic liberties can't be traded for economic gains.

The reasoning behind the difference principle is maximin. Because the parties are impartial and concerned above all with the worst-off, they rank alternatives by their worst possible outcome and pick the one whose worst outcome is best. Utilitarianism might produce higher total or average welfare, but it could also leave some people very badly off, and the parties won't take that chance. So they choose the arrangement that makes the least advantaged as well off as possible.

Harsanyi also uses a veil of ignorance, but he argues the parties would assign equal probability to being anyone and then maximize expected utility. That gives average utility, not the difference principle. So the disagreement is really about what attitude to risk the parties have. Rawls assumes they're extremely risk-averse, so they refuse to gamble on a utilitarian arrangement that might leave them at the bottom. Harsanyi assumes they're risk-neutral and willing to maximize expected utility. Rawls thinks the risk-averse choice is rational because the worst outcome under utilitarianism—being in a hopeless underclass—is something no one could accept, and because the parties can't estimate the probabilities of ending up in different positions. So the difference principle is the maximin solution, and the veil is what makes it fair."
