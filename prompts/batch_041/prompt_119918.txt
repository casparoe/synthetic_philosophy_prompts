I am the teaching assistant for an undergraduate course on philosophy of history and the social sciences. One of the midterm questions, worth 10 points, read as follows:

Question 3. Some writers hold that the Nazi seizure of power and the Holocaust are best explained by structural conditions — economic collapse, the paralysis of the Weimar party system, the settlement of 1919, the imperatives of a mass industrial war economy — and that something much like the same outcome would have occurred even if Hitler had never existed. Others reply that this counterfactual is unknowable and that the thesis assumes what it needs to prove. Assess the structural thesis. In your answer, (a) say what kind of claim it is, (b) say what would have to be true for it to be correct, and (c) say what it shows, if anything, about the "great man" view of history.

One student wrote:

(a) The thesis is a counterfactual claim about robustness, not a claim about the actual causal chain. It says that across a wide range of nearby worlds in which Hitler is absent or marginal, an authoritarian and expansionist regime still emerges. That is stronger than saying the outcome was overdetermined here: overdetermination is a claim about how many sufficient causes were operating in the actual world, whereas robustness is a claim about what survives an intervention on one of them, and you can have the first without the second. So the structuralist owes us a mechanism, not just a list of background conditions.

(b) For the thesis to be correct, the structural conditions would have to be sufficient for the outcome on their own, and the counterfactual would have to be specified without smuggling Hitler's agency into the background: you cannot hold fixed the party's organizational structure, the political idiom of the early 1930s, and the timing of rearmament, and then conclude that Hitler made no difference, since those things were partly his doing. Set that aside, though, and the structuralist's core point stands. It is at least arguable that Hitler was necessary for the regime, in the sense that no one else could have assembled that coalition, and necessity is not causation: a cause must be sufficient for its effect, and no single person is ever sufficient for an outcome of that scale. So the fact that no Hitler means no Nazi Germany tells us nothing about what explains it.

(c) What the debate really shows is that the two positions are not rivals at all. Marx already saw this: for him history is the record of economic and social forces working themselves out, and individual actors are only the mouthpieces of those forces. The great man view is therefore not so much false as incomplete, and the disagreement is one about emphasis rather than substance.

Please give this answer a grade out of 10 and write the comments I should return to the student. Organize the comments by part, saying what earns credit and what does not, and explain why any claim you mark down is wrong rather than merely naming the error. Assume the student is able to follow a technical point.
