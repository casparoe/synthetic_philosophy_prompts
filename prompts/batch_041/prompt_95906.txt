I'm an undergraduate in a seminar on ethics and public policy, and I've been asked to open next week's session on price controls — minimum wages and rent control — with a map of where the positions in the debate actually lie. Please build me a taxonomy in the form of a branching tree: each branch labelled with the question that splits it, each leaf carrying the name of a position (or "unnamed", if nobody defends it) plus one sentence on that position's most awkward commitment. It needs to fit on a one-page handout, so keep each label to a sentence.

Build it from the logical space rather than from a list of names: begin with the questions people can genuinely disagree about, work out which combinations of answers they permit, and only then locate the named positions within the space you've constructed. Two things matter to me especially.

First, every branch question has to be answerable by someone who doesn't already know the labels — concrete cases and yes/no claims, never "are you a welfarist?". I want these on the tree, though you may add others:
- Does the position treat the unregulated competitive price as the moral baseline, or as one institutional arrangement among others?
- Is there anything wrong with a "gouging" price — bottled water after a flood, a flat in a tight market — and if so, is the wrong the exploitation of urgent need, the departure from what desert or need would require, or nothing beyond the efficiency loss?
- When a ceiling binds, the good is still allocated — by queues, lotteries, landlord or employer discretion, personal connections. Does the position treat that rationing mechanism as part of the policy under assessment, or as a neutral implementation detail?
- Does the verdict change when the employer side of the market is monopsonistic, or when the buyer's alternative is homelessness rather than merely a worse flat?
- Is the policy assessed against the status quo, or against a whole institutional scheme (taxes, transfers, supply rules) of which the ceiling is one part?
- Does the position count the tenant's or worker's loss of mobility — the frozen tenant who no longer shops around, the worker who no longer searches — as a harm of the same kind as the money saved, or as a harm of a different kind?

Second, keep labour markets and housing markets separate enough to be diagnostic: a view that is welfarist about minimum wages and rights-based about rent control should land on an identifiable leaf, and if your tree cannot place such a person, say so rather than papering over it.

Please test the tree on the cases I'd actually put to the class: a binding minimum wage in a one-employer town; a city-wide rent ceiling; a ceiling that fixes sitting tenants' rents but lets the landlord name any rent once a unit turns over; and an emergency freeze on a necessity after a disaster. Note any combination of answers that turns out to be unoccupied, and diagnose it — incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. Aim for around twelve leaves. No citations, reading lists, or references to particular books or authors: use the standard label where a position has one and a descriptive one where it doesn't.

End with a single line in exactly this form: \answer{N leaves; unseparated: <pairs, or none>}
