I have been asked to referee a chapter for a handbook of value theory, and I need to decide how much of its treatment of the heterogeneity problem I can let stand. The author is a competent philosopher writing for a general readership, and the paragraph in question runs as follows:

"Hedonism is the doctrine that pleasure is the only thing good in itself. Its most serious difficulty is the one that has come to be called the heterogeneity problem. If we attend honestly to our own experience, we find that the pleasure of a hot bath, the pleasure of a good argument, and the pleasure of hearing that a friend has recovered from an illness have nothing in common: no single feeling runs through them the way a single feeling of warmth runs through all warm things. A hedonist cannot treat this as a merely verbal difficulty, because his theory requires that these experiences be good in the same way and be comparable in amount; the theory tells us to maximize pleasure, and we can maximize only what can be added up. Bentham took that requirement seriously and treated pleasure as one measurable quantity. Mill, seeing that the pleasures of a human being are of a different order from those of a pig, introduced the distinction between higher and lower pleasures, and so conceded that pleasures differ in kind, surrendering the common currency the theory needs. Most philosophers since have followed him. Since the word 'pleasure' does not name any single kind of mental state, it is at best a heading for a list; and a theory whose central term has no content can be neither stated nor defended. The usual retreat — that all pleasures share a 'hedonic tone' — only renames the problem. What survives is the observation that we seek pleasure and shun pain, which is true but trivial: it tells us nothing about what is worth seeking."

My instinct is that the paragraph is sound on the phenomenology and too quick in what it concludes from it, but I cannot yet say where the line should be drawn, and I would rather not submit a report that merely asserts that instinct. What I need from you is an audit I can act on.
