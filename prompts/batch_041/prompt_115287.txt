I'm the clinical ethicist on call at a big teaching hospital, and one of the jobs I inherited is being the person they page when a death determination gets contested — the family won't accept it, a nurse on the unit thinks the team is rushing, or the organ procurement coordinator is standing in the hallway and everyone can feel the clock. It happens three or four times a month, and the consults are usually run by whichever fellow is covering for me, someone with a nursing or social work background and no philosophy training at all. So I need a procedure that person can actually run in about an hour and then defend in writing to the medical director, who has to sign the note. Every time we try to write it down we end up in the same fight: is death the loss of the whole brain, the brainstem, the capacity for consciousness, or something else, and does it even matter whether we're talking about what death *is* versus what state counts as satisfying it versus what we actually check at the bedside. I keep telling the group those are three different levels of question and we keep anwsering all three at once. And I'll admit the thing that makes me nervous about the whole-brain story is that there are documented cases of people who met the criteria and whose bodies were kept going on machines for years afterwards, which suggests a body can remain some kind of integrated system long after we've said the person is gone.

Two more things I want the procedure to get right, because I suspect they're the whole ballgame. First, there's a difference between a state being irreversible — nobody could bring this person back with any technology that exists — and a state being permanent, meaning we're not going to try, and as I understand it the donation-after-circulatory-death protocols, where the heart has stopped and everyone stands there counting for two or three minutes, are resting on the second of those and not the first, because if somebody started compressions right then they might very well get a rhythm back. I want the procedure to say out loud which of the two it's using at each step instead of sliding between them, and I'd also like it to notice that some of these deaths look more like a process and some more like a single event. Second, we can't lose sight of the dead donor rule — you don't take organs from someone who isn't dead — so whatever the procedure says about the borderline has downstream consequences for real people on a waiting list, and I don't want it pretending otherwise.

So please write me that procedure. The questions to ask in what order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where it stops with a flat no, and where it has to hand the call to a human being because the remaining question is a value choice and not a fact question — and be honest with me about which of the steps are judgment calls dressed up as procedure. Then run it out loud on three cases: a patient who meets the whole-brain criteria and is on a ventilator, whose family objects on religious grounds and wants the ventilator kept on; a donation-after-circulatory-death case where the heart has stopped and the team wants to declare after two minutes; and a patient with no evidence of cortical function at all but an intact brainstem, who breathes on their own, where I'll confess I think we ought to be able to call that death and I suspect the procedure wont let me. Write the whole thing as continuous prose, no headings, no bullets, no bold, no tables — our working group documents have to be readable as prose and honestly I think it forces the joins to show. And at the end tell me what would change your mind: which argument or consideration would make you draw the procedure differently, and where you think your own reasoning is most likely to be wrong.
