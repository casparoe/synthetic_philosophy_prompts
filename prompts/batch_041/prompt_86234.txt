In a reading group on decision theory, a participant keeps invoking the line “intention is not a prediction” to dismiss any account that treats an agent’s intention as a forecast of her own future action. The case under discussion is the toxin puzzle: an agent is offered a large sum of money if, by tonight, she intends to drink a mild but thoroughly unpleasant toxin tomorrow; the money is paid once the intention is formed, and after that she is free to change her mind. Both she and everyone else know that once the money is paid she will have no reason to drink and will not drink. The participant says that the agent can intend to drink the toxin even though she knows she will not, and concludes that intention cannot be a kind of prediction. I suspect the slogan is being made to carry more argumentative weight than it can bear.

Please lay out the distinct precise claims that “intention is not a prediction” could be making in this context, from the nearly trivial to the radical. Treat the slogan as a piece of language rather than as a thesis to defend. For each reading, state the thesis as exactly as you can, say whether it is analytic or substantive, whether it is defensible, and whether it would actually support the participant’s conclusion. Include readings on which the slogan is true but irrelevant to the puzzle, readings on which it is false, and readings on which it is a claim about practical rationality rather than about the psychology or metaphysics of intending. I am especially interested in the difference between (a) intention not being identical with a prediction, (b) intention not requiring a predictive belief, (c) intention being compatible with the belief that one will not act, and (d) intention being a commitment that can outlive the reasons for forming it.

Finally, say which reading is most likely the one people mean when they use the line in this debate, and which reading a defender of a predictive or belief-based account should accept. Separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who work on intention; do not present a contested reading as the consensus. No citations or reading list are needed.
