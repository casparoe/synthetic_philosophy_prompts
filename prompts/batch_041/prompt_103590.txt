I am a teaching assistant for a second-year undergraduate course on population ethics, and I am working through the final exam. The last unit was on the procreation asymmetry — the claim that coming into existence can be a harm while failing to come into existence cannot be, and that coming into existence can be a benefit while failing to come into existence is not a harm even though a benefit is thereby missed. In class we worked through the standard formulation, distinguished the asymmetry from the total view, and spent a session on the symmetry objection and on the question whether non-existence can be assigned a welfare level at all. I would like a careful second opinion on one student's answer before I return the exam. A colleague skimmed the pile and thought this one was solid, and I would rather not be unfair in either direction.

The exam question was: "Consider the procreation asymmetry, i.e. the claim that coming into existence can be a harm while failing to come into existence cannot be, and that coming into existence can be a benefit while failing to come into existence is not a harm even though a benefit is thereby missed. (a) State the asymmetry precisely, contrasting for a given possible life what is true of the existence case and what is true of the non-existence case. (b) Explain how the asymmetry differs from the total view, and why a proponent of the total view is committed to rejecting it. (c) State the symmetry objection and give the best reply available to a defender of the asymmetry. In your reply, say whether non-existence can be assigned a welfare level."

The student wrote: "(a) The asymmetry is about how the two outcomes are evaluated, not about which is worse. In the existence case two things are true: whatever suffering the life contains is bad, and whatever happiness it contains is good. In the non-existence case there is no one to undergo anything, and yet the absence of suffering still counts in the favourable direction, whereas the absence of the happiness does not count against. The two absences are treated differently: we can be glad that no one suffers, but we need not regret that no one is happy. So the asymmetry is not the claim that existence is worse than non-existence; it is the claim that the reasons on the two sides fail to mirror one another. (b) The total view instead aggregates the good and the bad and treats a life that is never lived as the loss of the happiness it would have contained. Where the total view records a cost for the happy person who is never created, the asymmetry records nothing, since there is no one for whom the happiness is missing. That is why a totalist must reject the asymmetry: the missed good life is precisely the item the asymmetry refuses to count. (c) The symmetry objection says that if it is good to create a happy person it must be bad not to, and if it is not bad to omit a happy person it cannot be good to create one, so the asymmetry cannot have it both ways. My reply is that non-existence is a state of zero welfare, a neutral baseline against which a life can be measured: a life below zero makes existence a harm, a life above zero makes existence a benefit, and the absence of a life is simply neither, so there is nothing to regret. This also gives us the antinatalist conclusion, since the asymmetry shows that coming into existence is a harm and non-existence is not, and it follows that procreation is wrong."

Please assign a grade out of ten, together with the letter equivalent, and then give me a point-by-point assessment that justifies it. I am not after a general impression: tell me which of the student's claims are correct and earn credit, and which are mistaken, conflated, or do not follow from what precedes them, explaining in each case what the correct treatment would be and roughly how many points the problem should cost. I am particularly interested in whether the answer's parts hang together — whether what the student offers as a reply in (c) actually supports the asymmetry as stated in (a), and whether the closing move in (c) is licensed by anything the student has established. Grade the answer against the framing used in the course, not against your own verdict on the asymmetry itself; if the student takes a contested position, that is acceptable so long as the argument offered for it is sound.
