I'm giving the commencement address at a small liberal arts college next month. I did my PhD in logic, and the one idea I actually want to leave them with is the Löbian obstacle to self-trusting agents — the fact that a consistent formal system can't just adopt the schema that says if it proves P then P, for every P, because Löb's theorem turns that into a proof of everything; so an agent that wants to trust its own reasoning has to be careful about exactly how it does it. The audiance is a few hundred graduating seniors and their families; most have never heard of any of this, and I don't want to give them a lecture. I want a commencement address that makes the Löbian obstacle do real structural work — not just a clever aside. Maybe the address keeps telling them to trust themselves and keeps running into the fact that self-trust can't be a theorem; or it finds a way to be sincere about trust without proving it. One more thing: a mentor of mine had a late idea she called the modesty constraint — an agent can't prove its own reliability, so it has to act on something like unearned faith in its own reasoning, and that's what trust actually is. I'd like that idea to shape the address, especially the ending, which should land for the families without being false for the few philosphy majors in the crowd. No citations, no reading lists. Give it as exactly five numbered beats, each one a sentence or two, with no intro and no outro.
