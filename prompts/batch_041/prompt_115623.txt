A friend and I spent most of a long drive last week arguing about the dark forest hypothesis — the idea that the galaxy looks empty because any civilization that becomes detectable is eventually destroyed by someone who was listening. Her line was that it is the most economical answer to the Fermi silence; mine was that it is a Hobbesian anthropology dressed up as cosmology, and that the whole thing turns on a few assumptions about detection, strike costs, and the intentions of strangers that nobody ever states out loud. We got nowhere, and I have decided to stop arguing and run it instead as a session for my class. Please design the activity.

The setting: a 90-minute session for an interdisciplinary seminar on philosophy of science and science fiction, 18–24 participants, one room, no computers. What I want them to have felt by the end, rather than been told: that the dark forest is not a claim about how many civilizations are hostile, but about a payoff structure in which even a civilization that assigns a very low probability to meeting a predator can have a dominant reason to hide or to strike first — and, just as importantly, how brittle that result is, since it seems to rest on a handful of assumptions that each look contestable the moment you name them. They should arrive at the equilibrium themselves; do not name the hypothesis, or anything resembling it, until the debrief.

I want a runnable activity rather than a lecture plan: rules, materials, roles, a payoff scheme that needs no real money, a timeline for the 90 minutes, and a debrief. Some constraints on the mechanics, because I have a specific set of assumptions I want the game to encode and then expose:

- Give each civilization a position on a shared galaxy map, a technology level that advances one step per era, and the choice each round among expanding, listening, broadcasting, and launching a strike at a location it knows. Write orders simultaneously. Make strikes take several eras to arrive, and let a strike fail if the target's technology has advanced far enough in the interim — the "use it or lose it" window, where waiting makes an attack pointless and attacking early is the only attack that works.
- No civilization can be perfectly silent: passive listening should give a small chance per round of picking up leakage from nearby civilizations, so that hiding is a matter of degree, and deliberate broadcasting is a gamble taken for a reason. I want the distinction between accidental leakage and deliberate signaling to be live in the play, not explained afterwards.
- Deal one or two participants, secretly, a role that strikes at anything it detects with no strategy at all. Nobody should know how many such roles exist, or whether any exist. Part of what I want the group to feel is that the equilibrium does not require a galaxy of predators — one unknown hostile is enough to make broadcasting a bad bet.
- Plan a second, shorter run in which exactly one rule changes. Offer the facilitator a menu: a costly beacon that verifiably forfeits expansion for two eras, so a peace signal becomes credible; a defensive technology that can intercept incoming strikes; or a win condition based on survival rather than on territory held, which quietly tests whether the result depends on the assumption that everyone is expansionist.

The debrief should map particular moments in the play onto the analysis: the first broadcast and the first strike; the moment someone realized a strike launched now might be futile by the time it lands; the reveal of the hidden role, if it was dealt; and the first run's win condition, which I want flagged as an assumption the participants were playing under without noticing. It should also distinguish the dark forest from a Prisoner's Dilemma and from an ordinary coordination failure: here mutual non-aggression is not a stable outcome that someone defects from, but a strategy that is dominated, because there is no channel for communication that does not itself disclose your position, and no way to make a commitment credible. Tell me which of the three variants actually dissolves the forest and which merely relocates the problem.

Two more things. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle it — I already expect an inert board with everyone hiding for forty minutes, at least one person who broadcasts for the fun of it and then feels singled out when they are destroyed, and side deals whispered across the table that the real scenario would forbid. And be honest about what the activity cannot reproduce: that we have a sample size of one and no payoff matrix can model that evidential situation; that real interstellar conflict would be dominated by light-speed delays and archaeology rather than simultaneity, so the game makes the dilemma feel far more immediate and far more robust than the cosmology permits; and that my participants already know the trope, which is itself an artifact of running this in a room full of science-fiction readers.
