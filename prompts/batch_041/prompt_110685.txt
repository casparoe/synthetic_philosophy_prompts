I am a doctoral candidate in formal epistemology, and I have been trying to get a precise grip on a conceptual difficulty in the theory of Jeffrey conditionalization that I keep encountering and that the textbook presentations seem to slide past. I would like you to explain it to me from the ground up, with attention to the details rather than the standard summary.

The difficulty is this. Jeffrey's rule is standardly introduced as the generalization of strict conditionalization that licenses updating on evidence that is merely uncertain: where conditionalization demands that the agent become certain of some proposition, probability kinematics demands only that the agent's credences in the cells of some partition move to new values, with the conditional probabilities of everything else given those cells held rigid. The posterior is then delivered by mixing. What I cannot see is how the rule itself fixes the partition to which it is applied. The posterior is defined relative to a partition and a vector of new cell-probabilities, and the prescription is to keep the within-cell conditional probabilities fixed; but any coarsening or refinement of a given partition is also a partition, and holding the cells of a finer partition rigid imposes strictly more constraints than holding the cells of a coarser one rigid. It looks, then, as though one and the same evidential situation — one and the same shift in the agent's credences over the propositions she cares about — can be fed into the rule under different partitions and emerge with different posteriors. If that is right, the rule is either incomplete, in that it needs the partition supplied from outside, or it is not well-defined.

Please explain:

(1) exactly where the partition enters the rule, and why rigidity with respect to a finer partition is a genuinely stronger constraint than rigidity with respect to a coarser one — ideally by constructing a small, concrete example in which a coarse and a refined partition encode the same evidentially relevant shift in the probability of some proposition, yet yield different posteriors for a hypothesis of interest;

(2) whether the standard rejoinder — that the partition is simply the set of alternative possible outcomes of the evidence-gathering episode, so that the evidence itself determines the partition — is a substantive answer or a restatement of the problem, and what would have to be true of an agent's epistemic situation for the partition to be genuinely forced rather than chosen;

(3) how the difficulty bears on the usual claim that Jeffrey conditionalization improves on strict conditionalization precisely because it accommodates uncertain evidence, given that strict conditionalization at least has no partition to select;

(4) whether the trouble is best described as a defect of the rule, as an incompleteness in the way the rule is usually stated, or as an artifact of asking the rule to do something it was never meant to do — namely, to tell the agent what her evidence is, as opposed to how to respond once her evidence has been characterized.

I am especially interested in the last point, because I suspect that much of the disagreement here turns on an ambiguity about whether the new cell-probabilities belong to the input of the update or to its output. I would like you to separate those two readings and say which one, if either, renders the rule well-defined, and to flag any place where a single term is being used in two different ways across the discussion. Please give me the argument rather than a reading list; I am not looking for citations.
