I'm a postdoc who somehow became the de facto methods person for our lab — twelve of us, psych and behavioral-econ crossover — and the deal is that anyone about to write up a surprising result brings me the data and the draft, and I get about ninety minutes to say whether I think it's real before it goes out to a journal. It's a couple of times a month, the stakes are someone's first-author paper or a whole thesis chapter, so I can't just say 'looks fine, ship it' and I also can't be the guy who kills everything that walks in. What I keep reaching for is Occam's razor, and I know it's been formalized — Solomonoff's universal prior, Kolmogorov complexity, minimum description length, the whole picture where a shorter hypothesis gets more prior mass — but everytime I try to actually use it I get stuck on the translation. Description length in what language? Do I count free parameters, or bits, or how many plots I'd need to explain the thing to a smart undergrad? And the bit I can't shake: K is only pinned down up to an additive constant, which is harmless if you're taking limits over infinite data, except I have n=58. So please write me a procedure I can run in those ninety minutes: the questions in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where it just stops and says no, and where it has to hand the decision back to me as taste or values rather than fact. Where a distinction that matters in the formal theory has to be dropped to make a checklist, say so and say what it costs me.

Run it on two things from last term. First: a student found that people who watched a ten-minute nature video scored higher on a 'willingness to cooperate' scale than people who watched a news clip — big effect, n=58, the whole write-up describes it in a single sentence, no mechanism, and I happen to know she tried three other video conditions first. Second: a different project where the analysis is a hierarchical model with a couple dozen parameters and a long story about the one subgroup where the effect shows up, and honestly it does fit held-out data better than the simple version. I want to know whether my instinct to be more suspicious of the complicated one is anything better than instinct. Make sure at least one worked case comes out against what I'd like — either in favor of the ugly model or against the pretty one; if the procedure can only ever land on 'too complicated, don't believe it' then it isn't doing what the theory says it should and I'd rather you tell me that outright.

One more thing, and it's half-baked. I've been wondering whether the right move is to charge the description length in units of 'how many analyses could have been run' — if she tried four video conditions and reported the best, the hypothesis I'm actually evaluating isn't 'nature videos help' but 'one of four videos helps', and that description is longer by roughly the log of four. Is that a legitimate reading of the formal picture, or am I just reinventing the garden of forking paths with extra steps? Related confusion: I've read that with enough data the prior washes out and Solomonoff induction converges no matter where you start, which makes me think the razor mostly does its work in small samples — but I've also seen it said that the complexity penalty grows as you collect more data. Both can't be the headline, so sort that out for me, and tell me what it implies about which cases I should be most suspicious of. Throughout: separate what's actually settled in this literature — theorems, provable equivalences — from what's contested or is just my lab's folklore, don't hand me a contested position dressed up as the consensus, and flag the steps where you're least confident. I'd rather have a procedure with honest holes than a tidy one that's quietly wrong.
