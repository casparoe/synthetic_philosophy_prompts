I'm in a graduate seminar on the philosophy of the social sciences, and last week we read David Lewis's Convention (1969). I followed the coordination-problem apparatus well enough, but the definition at the center of the book kept sliding out from under me, so I've been more dependent than I'd like on other people's summaries of it. On the walk home from the seminar, a friend of mine who works in game theory gave me his account of the book, and it was so crisp that I've been repeating it ever since; I'd like to check it before it settles into my understanding. What he said, more or less, was this: 'Lewis's basic idea is that a convention is any regularity of behavior that a group shares — queueing, shaking hands, dressing for weddings are all conventions in his sense. What makes it a convention rather than a coincidence is that everyone knows that everyone else does it, and that's enough to keep it going: I do it because I expect you to, you do it because you expect me to, and so we lock in. Since the regularity could have been otherwise — since there was some other thing everyone could have done instead that would have worked just as well — conventions are arbitrary, and that arbitrariness is the heart of the theory. In effect, conventions are solutions to prisoner's dilemmas, and Lewis's deeper contribution was to show that norms can evolve without anyone ever agreeing to them, because the practices that coordinate us better get copied and spread while the others die out.' His conclusion, delivered with some relish, is that Lewis's theory is really a debunking theory of norms: once you see conventions as arbitrary solutions to coordination problems, you see that a great deal of what we experience as morally binding is just a habit that happened to stick, and that any convention could be swapped for its alternative without any real loss.

I have a nagging sense that this is a competent summary with one or two things quietly distorted, but I can't tell whether my unease is intellectual or just discomfort with where it lands. Could you go through it clause by clause and tell me what Lewis would accept as fair, what is a harmless popularization, and what is a genuine misreading — including whether 'arbitrary' is being used in Lewis's technical sense or in its everyday one? I'd especially like to know whether the move from 'conventions are arbitrary' to 'conventions carry no authority, and moral norms are just conventions that happened to stick' is licensed by anything in the book or is an overreach a careful reader would reject, and whether the evolutionary story about norms is a fair rendering of what Lewis actually offers. No reading list, please — I just want to see where the summary does justice to the view and where it doesn't.
