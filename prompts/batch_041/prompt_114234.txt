I am the instructor for an advanced undergraduate course in metaphysics and the philosophy of action. I have just finished marking a short-answer exam and would like a second, independent assessment of one student's answer. The question and the student's response are reproduced below.

Exam question:

Causation by omission is often introduced with cases in which an agent does nothing and a bad outcome follows. Suppose we adopt the following account, which I will call the normative-baseline account. An omission O counts as a cause of an outcome E when (i) O consists in the absence of some action A; (ii) if A had occurred, E would not have occurred; and (iii) A was required of the agent by a norm—legal, moral, or role-based—that applied to the agent in the circumstances. On this account, the norm does not merely make it appropriate to blame the agent; it fixes the contrast against which the counterfactual is evaluated. The omission is a cause relative to the baseline of the action that was expected, not relative to every alternative in which the outcome would have been different.

Consider the following case. A lifeguard, L, is on duty at a beach and sees a swimmer, S, drowning. L does not enter the water, and S drowns. S's mother, M, is on the beach; she is not a lifeguard and does not enter the water either. A shark is absent from the water throughout. Using the normative-baseline account, explain why L's omission is a cause of S's death while M's omission and the shark's absence are not—or, if you prefer, why they are causes only in a weaker or derivative sense. Then identify one serious objection to the account and explain how a defender of the account might reply. Finally, say whether the account treats omissions as real causes or as merely explanatory placeholders, and justify your answer.

Student's answer:

The normative-baseline account improves on a simple counterfactual account by adding a normative condition. A simple counterfactual account says that an event c is a cause of an event e just in case, if c had not occurred, e would not have occurred. This makes too many omissions causes: the absence of a shark, the mother's failure to swim out, and the lifeguard's failure all satisfy the counterfactual condition, since if any of them had been different, S would not have drowned. The normative-baseline account avoids this by requiring that the omitted action was required by a norm.

In the case, L is a lifeguard on duty. There is a role-based norm requiring L to attempt a rescue. If L had entered the water, S would not have drowned (we may assume L could have saved S). So L's omission is a cause of S's death. M is S's mother, but she is not a lifeguard and has no role-based duty to rescue. A general moral norm might apply to her, but the account as stated requires a norm that applied to M in the circumstances, and the case does not establish one. So M's omission is not a cause on this account. The shark's absence is not a cause because absences cannot enter into causal relations; only positive events can. This is why the account is superior to the simple counterfactual account.

One serious objection is that the account makes causation depend on moral luck. Whether L's omission is a cause depends on whether there happens to be a norm in force, and norms can vary across societies and circumstances. A defender might reply that the relevant norms are objective and knowable in advance, so L could have known that her omission would be a cause. Another objection is that the account cannot handle cases of overdetermination. Suppose two lifeguards are on duty and both fail to rescue S. Neither omission is a but-for cause, because if either had acted, S would have survived. The normative-baseline account would have to say that neither caused S's death, which is implausible.

On the whole, the account treats omissions as real causes. It says that the omission itself satisfies a counterfactual condition and a normative condition, and that is what it is for something to be a cause. The account does not merely explain why we blame L; it identifies L's omission as part of the causal structure of the world. The norm is not just a reason to select one cause from many; it is what makes the omission a cause in the first place.
