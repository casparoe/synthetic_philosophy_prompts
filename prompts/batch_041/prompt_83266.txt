My friend Rosa and I are in a political theory reading group that has drifted way past where it was supposed to go, and she's spent the last month building an arguement about the Condorcet jury theorem. Last night she wrote it out and I couldn't find the hole. I'm pretty sure there is one, because I don't want to end up where she ends up.

Her argument, cleaned up:

(1) The jury theorem: if each voter is even slightly more likely than not to get some yes/no question right, and their votes are independent of one another given the truth, then the probability the majority is right increases with the number of voters and tends to 1. Mirror image: if each voter is slightly less likely than not to be right, the probability the majority is right decreases with the number of voters and tends to 0. So a large group is an amplifier — it magnifies whatever individual competence is there, it can't create any.

(2) Real mass electorates get things wrong at an appreciable rate. Not controversial things — outcomes that large numbers of the voters themselves would call a mistake afterwards, or would reverse if you asked them again a few years later.

(3) Now combine. By (1), in an electorate of a million or more, a wrong majority is possible only if individual competence is below one half. If competence were above a half, majority accuracy at that size would already be essentially 1.

(4) And if competence is below a half, then by (1) the majority is worse than one randomly chosen voter, and it gets worse the bigger the electorate gets. So mass democracy would be epistemically worse than a coin flip, and worse than a small committee drawn from the same population.

(5) So anyone who accepts the theorem and accepts (2) is committed to saying mass democracy is epistemically worse than random.

(6) That's absurd, so something has to go.

I tried two escapes and she closed both. I said maybe our electorates aren't big enough for the convergence to have bitten — she says do the arithmetic, at a million voters you'd need average competence within a fraction of a percent of a coin flip for majorities to be wrong more than rarely, and nobody believes that about any real electorate. I said the independence premise is unrealistic — she says fine, allow any correlation you like, it doesn't rescue anything: all correlation can do is keep the group stuck around the members' own accuracy, it can't turn a crowd of individually sub-coin-flip voters into a reliable majority.

I'd like to keep (1); it's a theorem, I've seen the proof, it's just binomial arithmetic. What I can't accept is (5). So which step is broken, and what does the repair cost? If the answer is that (1) has to be weakened, tell me exactly which of its premises I'm giving up and what's left of the theorem without it. And I'd also like to know whether (5) is really as absurd as it sounds, or whether it's a bullet somebody could bite.
