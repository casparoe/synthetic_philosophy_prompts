I am a retired experimental physicist — thirty years in condensed-matter research — who has spent the last decade reading philosophy of mind and now teaches a small upper-level seminar for science students called Mind, Matter, and the Limits of Explanation. I am drafting the final examination and need one essay question on the knowledge argument, together with a model answer and a marking scheme. The question must not be answerable by reciting the standard Mary's-room setup and the usual replies — the ability hypothesis (she gains an ability rather than knowledge of a fact), the acquaintance reply (she gains acquaintance with a property), and the phenomenal-concept strategy (she gains a new concept of a fact she already knew). It should force students to locate the crux: whether the knowledge argument turns on a claim about what facts there are or only about what concepts or abilities Mary has, and whether the physicalist's reply can be given without either conceding a new fact or collapsing into the claim that Mary could have known what it is like all along.

To keep the question from becoming a recitation, centre it on a variant I have been turning over, which I call Mary's Ledger. In the standard case, Mary knows all the physical facts about color vision and then sees red for the first time. In Mary's Ledger, her physical knowledge is complete, and in addition she has been given a complete physical-functional specification of the phenomenal concept she will acquire when she first sees red — the concept she would express by saying what red is like. The specification states that concept's possession conditions, its inferential role, and its causal connections to her other concepts; Mary has memorized it; and she has been told, truly, that the specification is complete, i.e., that there is no further physical fact about the concept that it omits. She has never seen red. On leaving the room, she sees red. Does she learn anything?

Please write:

1. The essay question as it would appear on the exam. It should direct students to (a) say whether Mary gains a new fact, a new concept, or a new ability; (b) state what the physicalist must claim about the physical explicability of the phenomenal concept's content; and (c) assess whether the knowledge argument, so modified, threatens physicalism or merely records a fact about concepts.

2. A model answer of about 700 words.

3. A marking scheme for 20 marks, built around five must-hit points, with a one-line note for each on how students typically get it wrong.

The exam is 90 minutes; students may not use notes or texts. Do not include citations, bibliographies, or reading lists — the question, model answer, and marking scheme must stand on their own without appeal to any named author or work.
