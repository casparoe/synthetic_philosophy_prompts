I am a first-year PhD student in an evolutionary biology lab, and I keep losing an argument that I suspect I ought to be winning. I would like you to tell me whether the reasoning is sound, and if it is not, exactly where it fails.

The argument, as my cohort-mate puts it: the units-of-selection debate was settled decades ago and survives only because people enjoy relitigating it. Genes are the only things that are copied and handed down, and selection just is differential copying; therefore the gene is the unit of selection. Anything that looks like selection "for the good of the group" can be rewritten, term for term, as selection on genes that happen to find copies of themselves in their neighbours — same dynamics, same predictions, no empirical difference — and the reverse rewriting works as well. So group selection and gene-level selection are not rival hypotheses about the world but two bookkeepings of a single process, and asking whether selection "really" acts at the level of the gene, the individual, or the group is asking a question with no content.

I find this persuasive, and I would like to be able to defend it. But two things nag at me. First, if the two descriptions are genuinely equivalent, why does it feel as though they are answering different questions — one about whose fitness is being maximised, the other about what is being copied and transmitted? Second, why do field biologists still argue about group selection — about whether between-group variation is strong enough to matter, about whether some traits are adaptations of groups — if the whole thing were dissolved by a bit of algebra? Someone also told me the crux is whether a group's fitness is more than the sum of its members' fitnesses, whether there is an "emergent" component, but I cannot see how that is anything other than a fact about which decomposition we happened to choose.

So: is the debate a verbal dispute once we notice the equivalence, or is there a substantive question left over? Take a definite position and argue for it. Do not give me a survey or a list of who says what; I want your verdict and your reasons. If I am half right, tell me which half, and tell me what I should say to my cohort-mate when he makes the argument again.
