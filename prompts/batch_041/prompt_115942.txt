I am a high-school student and I run the philosophy club at my school. Next Thursday I have the whole forty-five-minute meeting, and I want to spend it on superrationality — the term, as I understand it, comes from Douglas Hofstadter. My audience is about a dozen students between fifteen and seventeen. They met the Prisoner's Dilemma once in economics and can say what a dominant strategy is, but they know no decision theory at all, and I have nothing but a whiteboard.

Please do not explain superrationality to me; I have read enough to have the idea roughly in hand. Plan how I should explain it. Tell me the order in which the pieces have to be introduced and what depends on what; which single example should carry the session, and what that example must have and must lack; the misconceptions they will bring or form — my guesses are that it is just the Golden Rule, that it requires the other player to be literally a copy of you or some mechanism forcing your two choices together, and that it is really about playing the game repeatedly — and the exact point in the sequence where each one should be headed off; one question I can ask at the end that would show the idea has landed rather than that they can repeat my sentences back; and what I should openly refuse to cover at this level.

I want the session built around a live demonstration I have designed, and I want your verdict on whether it should carry the lesson or whether it plants the very misconception I am trying to avoid. Each student writes C or D on a card; the payoffs are the ordinary one-shot Prisoner's Dilemma. They are then told that they have been paired with a student in another room who answered the same logic quiz in the same way, that in each pair only one card will be read and entered as both players' move, and that in their pair it is their own card — while their partner has been told exactly the same thing. In the last ten minutes I want to re-run it with a relatedness dial: only a stated fraction of the room is reasoning this way, and we turn that fraction up and down. Tell me what that exercise actually shows and where in the sequence it belongs.

Give me one definite plan rather than a menu, choosing wherever a choice has to be made — including whether to mention Newcomb's problem at all — and giving your reasons. Flag any place where the plan presents a contested reading as settled; I gather superrationality is not orthodox, and I would rather know where I am oversimplifying than have a student find out for me.
