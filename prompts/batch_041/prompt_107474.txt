Two friends in my discussion group spent a long evening arguing over whether "Moloch" names a real phenomenon or is a poetic relabeling of things we already had names for, and neither would concede. I'd like you to referee.

The background is the essay that gave the term its current currency: it uses "Moloch" for situations in which every participant acts sensibly, no one wills the outcome, yet the outcome is bad and it sticks. Dana's position was that this is just the multipolar trap, or the tragedy of the commons wearing a demon's mask, and that the personification does positive harm, because it invites people to hunt for a villain or to chant about coordination instead of specifying the mechanism. Ravi's position was that this misses the point: the name is deliberately the name of a non-agent, marking the *absence* of any coordinating will, and what it captures that "tragedy of the commons" does not is the ratchet — the way the dynamic moves in one direction and cannot be reversed by any single participant.

Dana: "But a ratchet is just a subgame-perfect equilibrium of a repeated game. You've shown me the mechanism is game theory. You haven't shown the name adds a thing."

Ravi: "Then write the game down. In the cases that matter — arms races, racing to the bottom on safety, everyone standing up to see the concert — the players don't know the terminal state, and each step is locally sensible while the sum is a place no one chose. A Nash equilibrium is a static object; this is a process with no author."

Dana: "Processes with no author are markets. You're describing emergent order and treating the fact that you can give it a demon's name as if that were analysis. And your examples all happen to be outcomes you dislike — so tell me the test. Is Moloch just 'emergent stuff I don't like'?"

Ravi: "The test is stability against individual defection. If any one player stops, that player is worse off, so no one stops, and the system sits there. That's the signature."

At that point I offered a case to break the tie and it only made things worse. The case: a linguistic community in which everyone follows the same grammatical conventions. No one chose the conventions, no one can unilaterally defect to a private idiolect without being worse off, and the arrangement is perfectly stable — yet no one would call it a disaster. Dana said this proves Ravi's test over-generates, since it certifies as Molochian a benign, indeed valuable, equilibrium. Ravi answered that the difference is that in the grammar case there is no feasible alternative that everyone would prefer, whereas in a genuine trap there is. Dana replied that this reduces to "a stable equilibrium that everyone would rather trade for a feasible alternative" — which is the textbook definition of a coordination failure — so we are exactly where we started, with the name adding nothing.

Please adjudicate round by round: for each move, say whether it is mistaken, defensible, or right, and why. Then say who has the better of the overall dispute, and what both of them are missing about what the concept is doing.
