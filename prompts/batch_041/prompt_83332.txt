I am a software engineer with no formal training in philosophy, and next week I am presenting a short paper at a graduate epistemology reading group I have been sitting in on for the past year. The paper takes aim at Fitch's paradox of knowability. I set the proof up the way the group does: suppose some truth p that nobody knows, so that the conjunction of p with nobody-knows-p is itself a truth; if every truth is knowable, then it is possible that someone knows that conjunction; knowledge is factive and distributes over conjunction, so it is possible that someone knows p and yet nobody knows p, which is absurd; therefore no truth goes unknown, and the knowability principle is dead. My contention is that the derivation works only because knowability is treated as a modal operator on whole propositions, and that if knowability is instead a relation between a proposition and a particular knower, the proof fails at the conjunction step. Prepare me for the question period. The audience is mostly analytic epistemologists, two of whom work on modal logic and formal epistemology and will know the derivation cold, one intuitionist logician who will want to know exactly which inference rule I am proposing to give up, and one person who thinks the whole paradox is a triviality about blindspots. Give me the objections in the order I will actually get them, phrased the way this audience would phrase them and ranked by how much damage they do, each with a two-minute reply and a note on where that reply is soft, what I should concede up front, and the one question I am hoping nobody asks.
