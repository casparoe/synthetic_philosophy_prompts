I write a column on science and ideas, and I am at work on an essay about the recent revival of akrasia as a topic shared by philosophy and behavioral economics. A behavioral economist I interviewed offered a tidy reduction: weakness of will, he said, is simply hyperbolic discounting by another name. On his account, when we procrastinate, break diets, or abandon exercise, we are not experiencing a failure of will at all; we are reversing our preferences because the immediate reward looms larger than the future one, and the remedy is whatever flattens the discount curve or binds the future self—Odysseus at the mast, automatic enrollment in savings plans, pre-commitment apps, and the like. I find the reduction attractive, and I am inclined to accept it. A philosopher friend insists on distinguishing synchronic akrasia, where one acts against one's present all-things-considered judgment, from diachronic preference reversal, where one's later preferences differ from one's earlier ones, but I am inclined to think the distinction is merely verbal, since both are cases of the near outweighing the far. If the reduction is correct, then the ancient problem of akrasia is really a special case of intertemporal choice, and the language of weakness, will, and practical judgment is at best a picturesque way of describing a steep discount rate.

What I would like help with is the following. If akrasia just is hyperbolic discounting, how should we understand cases in which someone acts against their own settled judgment even though no future reward is at stake—for example, deciding not to check the phone during a conversation and then checking it, not because the phone has suddenly become more valuable but because the intention seems to dissolve in the moment? And if the reduction is right, why do philosophers continue to treat akrasia as a problem about the relation between judgment and action, rather than as a problem about time preference? I am less interested in a literature review than in a conceptual map: does the reduction survive these cases, or is it a category mistake, and if it is a mistake, what exactly distinguishes the preference reversals studied in intertemporal choice experiments from the action-against-better-judgment that the tradition calls akrasia? I would also like to know what each diagnosis implies about remedies, since commitment devices seem tailored to the discounting story while they seem beside the point for the phone case.
