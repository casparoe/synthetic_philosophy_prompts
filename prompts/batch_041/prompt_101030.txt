I am a counsellor in a four-person practice, and for several years now we have built much of our work with anxious and over-committed clients around what we have always called the Stoic dichotomy of control. Last night I had a long argument about it with a friend who wrote her dissertation on Hellenistic philosophy, and I came away unsettled. Her complaint was not the familiar one, that Stoicism is quietist or that it counsels indifference to injustice. It was that the doctrine we teach is a modern invention, a product of the self-help revival rather than a report of anything Epictetus held, and that the error is not merely historical. On her account the Stoic division is not between what I can and cannot control, nor even between what I can and cannot influence, but between what is and is not attributable to me as an agent, and that class is far smaller than we pretend: my judgements, my impulses and desires of a certain kind, the use I make of my impressions — not my health, not my reputation, not whether my marriage survives, not the outcome of any project I begin. She says the giveaway is that when you ask the popular question, whether I can control this, the answer for almost everything that troubles my clients is no, and the advice then collapses either into fatalism or into a covert promise that managing your attitude will manage your life. What the Stoics actually built, she claims, is a set of three disciplines — over desire, over action, over assent — together with the practice of undertaking anything with a reservation and a doctrine on which health, money and reputation are preferred but not good; and she says none of that survives translation into the advice to focus on what one can control. I am not certain she is right, and I would like to know where she is overstating the case. But what I actually need is something else.

I want a procedure my colleagues and I can run in a fifty-minute session with a client who arrives with a tangle of grievances, fears and decisions, one that keeps whatever the Stoic account really says rather than the version we have been retailing. It should be a sequence of questions to ask in order, with some indication of what would count as a sufficient answer to each, an explicit statement of where the procedure stops with a no, and a clear marking of the points where it must hand the matter back to the client because the question is one of values rather than of fact. It has to be usable by two junior colleagues whose only exposure to any of this is the popular books, and it has to survive our clinical supervisor, who must sign off on anything we adopt and who is allergic to frameworks that present contestable judgements as neutral steps. Then run it on three clients: a woman in her sixties whose adult son is dependent on heroin and who wants to know how to stop him; a man newly diagnosed with a degenerative illness who is furious at his own body and wants that fury taken away; and a woman being worn down by a manager who belittles her in front of the team and who cannot decide whether to report him. One of those three should come out in a way I will not enjoy hearing, and I would like to be told plainly which one that is and why the procedure gives that answer. Finally, tell me what the procedure cannot do and where it is most likely to misfire on a client — where the honest thing is to admit that the theory has been stretched past what it will bear. Please write the whole thing as continuous prose, with no headings, no bullet points, no bold text and no tables, the way you would set it out in a long letter to a colleague who has to use it next week.
