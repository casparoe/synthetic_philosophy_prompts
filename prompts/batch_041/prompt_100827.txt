For the past several years, since retiring from a career in atmospheric physics, I have been reading philosophy of history in a state of productive bewilderment, and I keep returning to a question I am not equipped to settle: whether the course of human affairs has a direction, and in particular whether it tends toward justice. I notice that the standard image for that claim — the arc that bends toward justice — borrows a word from my old trade without, so far as I can tell, paying for it. I would like a poem that puts the word under pressure.

Please write a poem of roughly thirty-five to forty-five lines, in whatever meter you find natural (blank verse or loosely rhymed quatrains would serve), spoken in the voice of a retired scientist of my sort — someone who trusts instruments more than intuitions and is faintly embarrassed to be moved by a metaphor. Let the rainbow, the optical bow, be the governing image for the arc of history, and let the physics and the moral claim interrogate each other rather than merely sit side by side.

Several features of the phenomenon should be used, and used accurately, because the whole poem depends on their being right: that a bow is in fact a circle, of which we see only an arc because the earth cuts off the rest; that its centre is the antisolar point, which is to say the place where the shadow of the observer's own head would fall, so that every bow is centred on nothing but the spot from which one happens to be looking; that it is nowhere — no one can stand at its foot, and it withdraws as you walk toward it; that the secondary bow repeats the first with the order of its colours reversed, and that between the two lies a darker interval, Alexander's band, where the light cancels; and that all of it follows from sunlight being turned back at a fixed angle by drops of water, a fact that has no preference among outcomes and no memory of what it has illuminated.

I would like the poem built in two mirrored halves, the second half returning through the images of the first in reverse order, the way the secondary bow reverses the first. Let the opening half entertain the providential reading — that the direction is real, that the far end lands somewhere, that the curve is the visible portion of a whole — and let the closing half admit the naturalist's suspicions: contingency, entropy, the absence of any end in view. Do not resolve the tension; I would rather the poem end in the middle of it, somewhere inside Alexander's band. If you can manage it, let the final line return to the first, so that the arc the reader has just traversed turns out to be a closed circle — a formal closure that answers nothing.

The poem will be read aloud at the end of a reading group I attend, so it should stand on its own for listeners who know the philosophical debates — about whether history has a telos, whether progress is a fact or a story we tell about facts — and for those who do not. Do not name philosophers or cite works; the ideas should be present as weather rather than as citations. A reader who knows the physics should find nothing to wince at, and a reader who does not should still find it a good poem.
