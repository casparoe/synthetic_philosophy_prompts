I'm in a graduate seminar on Bayesian confirmation theory, and we keep circling back to the problem of old evidence. The stock illustration: a theory such as general relativity is taken to be strongly supported by the anomalous precession of Mercury's perihelion, yet astronomers had measured that anomaly decades before the theory existed, so the evidence already sits at probability one in the agent's credence function, and conditionalizing on it cannot raise the theory's probability at all. Half the room treats this as a fatal defect in Bayesianism; the other half treats it as an artifact to be patched with a counterfactual maneuver — assess the boost from the standpoint of an agent who did not yet know the evidence — and then moves on. I want to argue for a third position, and I'd like you to build the strongest possible case for it.

My claim is that the puzzle has been mislocated. It is not fundamentally about time (how long the evidence has been known) nor about certainty (the awkwardness of probability one). It is about description: a body of evidence is never confirmed as a bare fact but always as something described a particular way, and the characteristic thing a new theory does is to furnish a new description of already-known data — it redescribes the anomaly as a specific quantitative signature, as an instance of a symmetry, as the predicted output of a mechanism. I want to argue that once descriptions are taken seriously, old-evidence confirmation is just ordinary confirmation: the theory is confirming the evidence under the new description, and the agent was not antecedently certain of the proposition so described. Please make this argument as rigorous and as strong as it can be made, rather than gestural or hedged.

Concretely, I want the following addressed. First, state the position precisely, including what "description" must mean for it not to collapse into a trivial relabeling. Second, explain why the move is not simply cheating: the natural objection is that the old and the new description pick out one and the same proposition, so a logically omniscient agent's probabilities must coincide and any apparent boost is an illusion generated by an impoverished algebra. Third, say what must be given up — logical omniscience, the description-neutrality of the evidence set, or something else — and whether the price is acceptable. Fourth, handle the case in which the theory's new description is itself the only reason to think the redescription is apt, so that the theory threatens to confirm itself. Fifth, close with the strongest surviving objection and your reply. I care more about the argument's internal coherence and its treatment of the self-confirmation worry than about any historical case, so reason abstractly where that helps; there is no need to survey the literature or cite anyone.
