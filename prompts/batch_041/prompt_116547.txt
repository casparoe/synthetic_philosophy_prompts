I sit on the graduate medical education committee at a large teaching hospital, and at Thursday's meeting we vote on whether to replace our end-of-rotation assessments with a vendor package that three of our residency programs have been piloting. Under the pilot, each resident receives a percentile score on eight behavioural competencies — situational awareness, communication under pressure, escalation judgement, and so on — computed by comparing that resident against the other residents in their cohort rather than against a stated standard, refreshed monthly on a dashboard that automatically flags anyone who falls below the twenty-fifth percentile twice in consecutive months for a targeted development conversation with their program director. Our present system is criteria-referenced: an attending either documents that a resident has met a stated standard or does not, and the narrative comments go to the clinical competency committee for review. The vendor's pitch is that percentile ranking is objective, that it surfaces struggling residents months earlier than narrative review does, and that the flags are non-punitive because nothing is entered in a resident's file. My own instinct is that something has gone wrong here that the usual worries about inter-rater reliability and test validity do not quite capture, and a colleague who did graduate work in philosophy before going to medical school insists the interesting part is precisely the shift from measuring residents against a standard to measuring them against one another, and that this shift is what Foucault is getting at when he talks about normalization — with, on her reading, everything else in his book on the prison being downstream of it. I half believe her and half suspect she is using a large vocabulary for a small administrative change.

Please draft the written opinion I will circulate before the vote. It should take a clear position — adopt as proposed, reject outright, or adopt with specific modifications — and recommend a concrete course of action with a timeline the committee could actually vote on, covering both the ranking engine and the automated flags. What I most need from it is a serious engagement with the philosophical machinery rather than a gesture at surveillance: what an account of disciplinary power actually claims about the relations among continuous observation, judgement against a norm, and formal examination of the individual; why it matters on that account that these techniques are productive rather than merely repressive, and what follows for a committee that finds itself asking whether being made comparable to one's peers is a harm; and, importantly, what such an account does and does not license as a criticism, given that it is offered as a description of how institutions work rather than as a moral verdict on them. Give the strongest case for adopting the package a fair hearing, including the uncomfortable possibility that our existing narrative system already does the same work under a friendlier name and simply conceals it better, and the possibility that my colleague's framework, being descriptive, cannot by itself tell this committee how to vote.

A few constraints. My background is in epidemiology and I will be defending this document in a room of surgeons who have no patience for abstraction, so keep the prose plain and let any technical vocabulary earn its place. Do not give me citations, references, or a reading list; I do not need page numbers, I need the argument, and nothing should appear in quotation marks as though it were lifted from a text. The opinion should run to roughly a page and a half, end with the recommendation stated in a form that can be moved and seconded, and be honest about what would have to be true for me to be wrong.
