Our county health department's emergency operations board meets Thursday morning, and as the board's staff philosopher I am responsible for circulating the decision memo by Wednesday evening. The case concerns Saturday's deployment of our single mobile vaccination unit during a measles outbreak. The unit can go to the Northside clinic or the Southside clinic. Our standing rule, adopted after the last outbreak, is to send the unit to whichever clinic has the higher projected case count at the Thursday briefing; this week the projection favors Northside by a wide margin.

The complication is a direct-action group that has blockaded our vaccination sites three times in the past two years. They have never obtained a leak from inside the department. Instead they publish a model of our decision criteria — the standing rule, the threshold at which we override it, the weight we give to staff availability and to elected officials' preferences — and they use that model to announce, days in advance, which site they will blockade. Their announced target has matched our actual deployment in every prior instance. Their current public prediction is that we will deploy to Northside and that they will blockade Northside. Our own after-action review confirms that their model predicted our choices even in the two instances when the board deliberately leaned the other way, because the model treats our deliberation as an input and predicts the outcome of our attempts to deviate. The model is not clairvoyant and has no independent access to our final deployment order; it predicts from our criteria and our deliberations.

Two board members have circulated notes. The first argues that the choice of clinic does not cause the blockade: the group's announcement is already made, and our vans cannot push their people off the sidewalk. On this view we should follow the case-count rule, deploy to Northside, and treat the blockade as a separate operational problem. The second argues that we should deploy to Southside precisely because the group predicts Northside: if we go where they do not expect us, the vaccination effort succeeds and the outbreak is contained. The chair wants a memo that decides.

Please draft that memo. It should recommend a concrete course of action, with a timeframe, for Saturday's deployment and for any change to our decision procedure for future deployments; give the reasoning in a form the two board members can respond to, including the strongest version of the position you reject; and address the structural question their notes raise, namely whether the group's prediction should enter the decision at all and, if so, how. Do not rest the memo on the bare formulas that correlation is not causation or that we should maximize expected utility. Assume the board is intelligent but untrained in decision theory, and that the group's model is as accurate as the after-action review says.
