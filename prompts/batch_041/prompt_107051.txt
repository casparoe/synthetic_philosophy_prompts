I'm taking a philosophical logic course and for our unit on epistemic logic I have to write a short essay on the problem of logical omniscience. I've sketched an argument below and I'd like comments on it — specifically whether the central move actually does any work or whether I'm just relabeling the problem, whether I've misdescribed any of the standard positions, and which part is weakest. I care much more about being told the main idea collapses than about polish. Here's the draft:

The problem is usually stated this way: on the standard possible-worlds semantics for knowledge, an agent knows everything that follows from what she knows, and knows every logical truth, because the set of worlds compatible with what she knows is closed under entailment. Since nobody actually knows all the consequences of what they know, the model is wrong about us. The standard fixes are either to add "impossible worlds" so that logically equivalent sentences can still differ in what they rule out, or to add an "awareness" parameter that screens out propositions the agent isn't even in a position to consider.

My idea is that both fixes treat the symptom. The hidden assumption is that "knows" takes a proposition as its object and nothing else. If instead knowing is more like having a sentence written down in a ledger you can consult, entailment doesn't automatically add lines: the ledger gets a new entry only when you actually do the derivation. A logician and a child could have the same axioms "in there," but only one of them has run the proof, so only one has the line.

The obvious objection is that this makes knowledge too syntactic and too arbitrary — two people could know exactly the same facts, and one would know the consequences and the other wouldn't, purely because of how the entries happen to be written. I want to say that's not a bug, since a ledger entry is only useful if you can read it, and the way it's written affects what you can do with it. But I can't tell whether that's a real answer or just me restating the failure of closure as a feature. There's also a case I don't know how to handle: a mathematician who knows the axioms, and knows in the abstract that the theorem follows, but has never "seen" the derivation. Do they know the theorem on my view, or not? I feel like I need a principled line there and I definately don't have one.

So: is the ledger picture a genuine alternative, or a restatement? And if it is genuine, does it force me to give up something I should want to keep (like the idea that knowledge is factive, or that it's closed under obvious entailments at least)?
