I have drafted the essay below for a graduate seminar on the philosophy of history. What I want from you is a critical response rather than encouragement. Do not send me to further reading and do not worry about citing sources; assume the argument has to stand on its own terms, and engage with the text in front of you. Where I attribute positions to Ranke, Carr, or Hayden White, treat those as broad characterizations of well-known stances and feel free to correct me if the characterization itself is off.

Two Objectivities

It is often said that Ranke's aspiration to relate the past as it actually was and the skepticism of Carr and Hayden White cannot both be right — that one must choose between a naive realism about the past and a corrosive constructivism about it. I want to argue that the standoff is an artifact of treating objectivity as a single property when it is at least two.

Call the first event-objectivity. This is the claim that the events a historian recounts — that a battle was lost, a treaty signed, a child carried off by fever — occurred, and occurred with roughly the causal structure the historian attributes to them, whether or not any historian ever narrated them. Event-objectivity seems to me both true and largely uninteresting. It is a claim about the past, not about history writing, and it leaves untouched every question about what the historian actually does.

Call the second description-objectivity. This is the claim that the description under which the past is recounted — which events are selected as salient, where the narrative begins and ends, which actors are treated as agents and which as conditions, whether the sequence is emplotted as tragedy, farce, or progress — is itself fixed by the past rather than by the historian's interests, values, and rhetorical situation. Description-objectivity is false. A chronicle with no selection, no emphasis, and no principle of relevance would not be a neutral history; it would be no history at all. Ranke's formula, read as a demand for description-objectivity, is therefore incoherent.

If this is right, then Carr and White have established something true but narrower than their rhetoric suggests. What they show is that the historian's description is not dictated by the past; what they sometimes imply is that there is nothing left for the past to dictate. The second claim does not follow from the first, and it is the overreach, not the sober thesis, that makes the position look like a license for relativism.

To show that the distinction leaves something for objectivity to be, I propose the following criterion, which I will call the recognition test. An account is description-objective to the degree that the agents whose conduct it narrates could, on reading it, recognize their own reasons, constraints, and uncertainties in it — not endorse it, not enjoy it, but find in it the situation as it appeared from the inside. Objectivity then becomes a property of the fit between a narrative and the practical standpoint of those it describes, rather than a correspondence between a narrative and a past that has no standpoint.

The criterion is admittedly thin, and I can see three pressures on it. It privileges the articulate over the inarticulate, since only some agents can report a standpoint at all. It seems to collapse when the agents are systematically self-deceived about their own reasons, since the historian may have to override the very self-understanding the test consults. And it risks circularity, since deciding which self-descriptions to credit looks like exactly the interpretive judgment the test was meant to discipline.

I would like your comments on four things. First, is the distinction between event-objectivity and description-objectivity a real one, or does it collapse — for instance, if every candidate event is already an event-under-a-description, in which case my first category is empty and the second is doing all the work? Second, does the recognition test earn its keep, or does it smuggle the historian's own normative judgments back in under the guise of what the agents would recognize? Third, suppose a constructivist presses the deeper point that the boundary between event and fact is itself constructed; can my first category survive that, and if it can, does it still do the work I assign it? Fourth, tell me the single strongest objection to the essay and what I would have to concede to accommodate it — and, if you think the whole piece is a restatement of the older interpretive or re-enactment tradition dressed in new vocabulary, say so plainly and show me where the restatement occurs.
