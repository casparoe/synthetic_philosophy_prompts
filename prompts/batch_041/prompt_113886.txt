I'm a third-year philosophy student, and I also work two shifts a week as a healthcare assistant on a ward for people with advanced dementia. Much of the job is deciding whether someone is in pain: they can't tell me, they can't reliably use a body map, and the nurse in charge signs off on any analgesia I ask for. I've been reading the problem of other minds for a course and want it turned into something I can run in the middle of a shift. The accounts I've met so far are these: the argument from my own case by analogy; the view that another's experiences are a hypothesis posited because it explains their behaviour better than any non-mental rival; and the view that pain-behaviour isn't evidence from which I infer a hidden state but part of what being in pain consists in, so there's no gap to cross. None of them tells me what to do when the behaviour is ambiguous, when it might be distress of a non-painful kind, or when the resident is on something that suppresses the very behaviour I'd be reading. Please write a decision procedure that keeps whatever survives translation from these accounts and drops what doesn't: the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer at each step, where it stops and tells me not to act, where it stops and tells me to act anyway, and the point at which it hands the decision to the nurse in charge because what remains is a question about acceptable risk rather than about what the resident is feeling. Then run it on three cases: (1) a resident who grimaces and cries out when moved but is cheerful the rest of the time; (2) a resident whose moaning reliably begins when one particular carer comes on shift and stops when that carer leaves; (3) a resident who is drowsy and still after a medication review, so that almost no behaviour is available either way. At least one case should come out as one where the procedure makes me do something I don't want to do. Format the answer as: "What the accounts license" (a sentence or two each, ~100 words total); "The procedure" (numbered steps in the order I'd run them, 500-700 words, with the stop rules and the hand-off marked); "Three worked cases" (~200 words each); "Where this misfires" (failure modes, the answer I won't like, and which questions are conceptual rather than evidential, ~200 words); and "What it cannot do" (three or four sentences). Write it so a colleague with no philosophy could follow it, and don't hedge every step into uselessness.
