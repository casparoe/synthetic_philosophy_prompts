Question 3. Buchak's risk-weighted expected utility (REU) theory is often presented as a rival to expected utility (EU) theory that vindicates the rationality of risk-averse preferences. Set out the formal structure of the theory, explain how it differs from expected utility theory, and assess whether it succeeds in showing that the Allais preferences are rationally permissible.

Student answer:

Expected utility theory evaluates a gamble by summing the utilities of its possible outcomes, each weighted by the probability of that outcome. Buchak's risk-weighted expected utility theory keeps the utilities but changes the weights. Order the outcomes of a gamble from worst to best, o_1, ..., o_n. The risk-weighted expected utility of the gamble is

REU(O) = o_1 + Σ_{i=2}^{n} r(Pr(O ≥ o_i))(o_i − o_{i−1}),

where r is the agent's risk function. The risk function is increasing, with r(0) = 0 and r(1) = 1. It represents the agent's attitude to risk. If r(p) < p for intermediate probabilities, the agent is risk-averse: she gives less weight to the chance of doing better than the worst outcome than an expected utility maximizer would. If r(p) > p, she is risk-seeking. When r is the identity function, the formula collapses to ordinary expected utility, so expected utility maximization is the special case of REU maximization in which the agent is risk-neutral.

The main motivation is the Allais paradox. In the standard version, most people prefer a sure moderate prize to a gamble with a slightly higher expected value, but reverse this preference when both options are made chancy in the same way. These preferences violate the independence axiom of expected utility theory, so no expected utility maximizer can have them. Buchak's theory is meant to show that they are nonetheless rationally permissible: an agent whose risk function weights chances in the rank-dependent way will have exactly the Allais preferences, and there is nothing irrational about that.

The risk function is best understood as a kind of subjective probability weighting: a risk-averse agent, in effect, assigns a lower degree of belief to the better outcomes, and r encodes this deflation. That is how the theory accommodates the Allais pattern without tampering with the agent's utilities.

It is worth adding that, because setting r to the identity recovers expected utility, any REU maximizer can be redescribed as an expected utility maximizer with a suitably adjusted utility function. So the two theories do not really disagree about which choices are rational; they are two ways of representing the same underlying preferences, and the contribution of REU theory is mainly to give a more psychologically realistic vocabulary for talking about risk.
