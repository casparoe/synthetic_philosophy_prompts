I'm building a little case-study collection on the symbol grounding problem and I want you to do the analysis, not a survey of the literature. Put on your philosopher-of-cognitive-science hat — someone who knows Harnad's framing of grounding but also actually knows the history of AI, warts and all. Think it through before you answer, maybe brainstorm a bit first, then give me the real thing.

The case: Japan's Fifth Generation Computer Systems project. MITI launched it in 1982 as a ten-year push, set up ICOT to run it, and the bet was on logic programming plus massively parallel inference machines — a machine that would reason over enormous knowledge bases at hundreds of millions of logical inferences per second, "knowledge information processing" was the phrase they liked. It produced genuinely good work on concurrent logic programming and then died a commerical death; nobody got the thinking machine.

The lens: the symbol grounding problem as Harnad laid it out in his 1990 paper. The tokens of a symbol system are distinguished only by their shapes and defined only in terms of other tokens, so the whole thing rides on chasing definitions around a dictionary forever unless the symbols bottom out in something non-symbolic — representations the system itself can pick referents out with, sensorimotor categories it can discriminate and act on. Meaning has to be intrinsic to the system, not parasitic on the meanings sitting in our heads.

What I want: run the case through the lens in both directions. Who or what in that project was supposed to supply the grounding, and what did the symbols actually bottom out in? Then be honest about what the grounding problem explains here and what it doesn't. My suspicion is that two failures get conflated in the usual telling — one being the real dictionary-go-round, symbols with no referents anywhere in the system, and the other being the boring old knowledge-acquisition problem, hand-coded commonsense facts defined in terms of other hand-coded facts, in a system that couldn't tell when a rule applied. Which one actually sank it, if either? Or was it neither, and the post-mortem is really about hardware and evaluation criteria and a ten-year deadline?

Then the other direction: what does the episode do to the theory? Confirm it, complicate it, or expose it as a diagnosis that can't be falsified? Bear in mind we now have systems trained purely on text, no sensorimotor grounding at all, that hold up conversations — that's awkward for the standard story, so say what it does to yours. Be specific about whether FGCS is evidence for the theory or just an unhelpful anecdote, and don't hedge your way out of the question.
