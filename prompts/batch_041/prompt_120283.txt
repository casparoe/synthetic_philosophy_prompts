I'm in a philosphy of value course and I keep getting stuck on something that came up in a discussion about climate policy and nuclear war. Almost everyone I know, including people who aren't religious at all, treats human extinction as a catastrophe that's much worse than the same number of deaths spread out over time. If a plague killed eight billion people but a few thousand survived and rebuilt, that seems horrible; if everyone died and no one was left, people say it's a different kind of horror, not just more deaths. The same thing shows up in institutions: governments fund asteroid defense and AI safety, UNESCO preserves cultural heritage, people care about whether there will be anyone around in a thousand years, and even secular arguments for climate action often say the worst thing isn't the death toll now but that it could end the human story. My friend says this is all just concern for future people who would otherwise exist, plus maybe grief for the dead, and that once you subtract those there's nothing left to explain. I'm not so sure.

I recently heard about an idea from Samuel Scheffler, something like an afterlife conjecture. The thought experiment is: imagine you're told you'll die in thirty days, versus you're told you'll die in thirty days but then, thirty days after your death, all human life will end. Most people find the second scenario much more devastating, even though in both cases they're dead and won't experience the aftermath. Scheffler's suggestion is that we value the continuation of human life after us as a background condition for our own projects and values to be meaningful, not just because we care about particular future people, but because a future after us is part of what makes our present pursuits worth pursuing. I might be mangling it, but that's the gist. So here's what I want to ask: what philosophical commitments does the widespread practice of treating extinction as a special, non-aggregative catastrophe actually presuppose? Does it presuppose that humanity's continued existence has value in itself, independent of any valuer? Or does it presuppose something like Scheffler's conjecture, that the value of our current projects depends on there being a human future? Can it be fully explained by ordinary concern for future people plus the badness of death, or does it require a value that isn't reducible to welfare? And are those two presuppositions, value in itself versus Scheffler-style dependence, really different, or do they collapse into each other?

Also, if the practice is underdetermined between those views, say so. What I really want is a reconstruction of the implicit theory, what it rules out, whether the different parts of the practice (existential risk funding, heritage preservation, secular climate rhetoric) presuppose consistent things, and what would have to change in the practice if we replaced the Scheffler-style view with a straightforward person-affecting or total-welfare view. Please don't just give me a list of books or articles; I'm trying to understand the commitments, not build a bibliography.
