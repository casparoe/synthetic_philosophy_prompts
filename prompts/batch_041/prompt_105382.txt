My old college friend is coming to stay with me for a week — he teaches constitutional law back in the States, sharp as anything, but he's the kind of American who says "separation of church and state" the way other people say "the law of gravity," like it's just how reality is arranged — and he's announced that he wants me to finally explain to him how the European church-state thing actually works, because he genuinely can't get his head around the fact that a modern liberal democracy would have the government collect a tax on behalf of the churches. I've got maybe ninety minutes with him over a couple of evenings and I'd rather plan it than improvise. So don't give me the explanation itself; give me a teaching plan for it. Assume he's smart and knows US establishment-clause doctrine backwards, and that he has never seriously entertained any arrangement other than disestablishment — that's his entire mental furniture. What I need is the order the pieces have to go in and why (what has to be in place before the very idea of a church tax stops looking like a category error to him), and which single example should carry the whole thing.

My instinct is that it should be the German church tax — the Kirchensteuer, a surcharge of roughly eight or nine per cent of your income-tax bill, which the ordinary state tax office collects alongside your taxes and hands to whichever church you happen to be registered with, and which you can only get out of by formally deregistering your religion at a government office, something the church reads as your leaving it for good. Tell me whether that's the right carrying example or not, and if not, why not — I considered French laïcité and the Church of England instead and I suspect both are worse for different reasons. What does my chosen example have that lets it do the work, and what does it lack (I worry it makes the whole thing look like a German peculiarity rather than an instance of a general model)? I also want him to come away seeing that "established" isn't one dial but several seperable questions — does the state have an official church, does it recognise religious bodies as public institutions, does it collect their money, does it teach their religion in schools, does it pay their clergy — and that the US answers no to most of those but definately not all of them.

Then the misconceptions. Here are the ones I expect him to walk in with or form along the way: that a church tax is basically a state subsidy of religion; that disestablishment equals secularisation, so the States ought to be the least religious country in the West when it's plainly one of the most; that "secularism" names one thing, so French laïcité, German cooperation and American neutrality are just accents of the same position; and that quitting the church is like cancelling a gym membership. Tell me which of those are real traps and which I'm imagining, which others I've missed, and exactly where in the sequence I should head each one off so it doesn't get baked in. Finally, one question I can put to him at the end that would actually show whether the model has landed rather than whether he can recite facts back — ideally something where he has to classify a case he hasn't heard of — and what I should deliberately leave out at this level and say I'm leaving out.
