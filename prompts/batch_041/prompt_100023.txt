I'm a phil PhD and I'm running a 90-minute session for our decision-theory reading group — about 12 people, mostly grad students who are fine with basic utility theory but have never had to actually do anything with a money pump. I want a two-station activity that makes the pump feel like a live threat instead of a cute reductio, and that walks them into the diachronic version without me lecturing first.

Station 1, the swap shop: I elicit each person's pairwise choice among three small gambles — a sure 3 chips, 80% of 4 chips, and a 10% shot at 35 — one pair at a time on slips, never showing all three at once, so ordinary risk aversion on the middle pair plus risk seeking on the long shot tends to hand most people a loop (they say A>B, B>C, and then C>A). Then I reveal the three cards and that their own choices close a circle, and a shopkeeper starts offering to swap them around the loop for a 1-chip fee, again and again, for as long as they keep saying yes. Station 2, now-or-later: pairs play agent and bank, the agent picks a plan (5 chips now, or 8 chips in three rounds), the bank then offers to buy them out of the later payout for a fee, and we run a variant where the agent can pay up front to make the commitment unbreakable. Chips redeem for raffle entries or first pick of the snack table, no real money.

Give me the rules, the materials, the payoff sheet, the run-sheet/timeline, the roles (I'll be the shopkeeper in round 1 and a bank in round 2), and a debrief that maps each station onto its argument — the synchronic pump as a case against intransitive preference and the diachronic one against time-inconsistent preference — and then turns on them: does being pumpable actually show irrationality, or does the argument quietly need the agent to be naive (a sophisticated chooser just refuses to keep trading), or to be obliged to accept every offered improvement? I want them to end up fighting about whether binding yourself to your earlier plan is the rational move or a failure to respect your later self, and whether the unit of rationality is the person or the time-slice. I expect a good chunk of them won't cycle at all and most of the rest will spot the loop and refuse once its revealed — which is kind of the point but also guts the demo, so tell me how to handle that and what else will predictably go wrong the first time. And be honest about what the activity can't reproduce; I'm guessing it can't actually give anyone a genuinely time-inconsistent preference, so I'll have to supply that empirical claim myself.
