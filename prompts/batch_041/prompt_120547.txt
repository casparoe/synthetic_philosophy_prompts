I teach a mid-level philosophy of mind course and I also sit on a small working group with a psychiatry colleague who's running a trial on whether a new compound changes the felt character of depression — they use introspective reports as a primary outcome, and last week in our meeting I made the mistake of bringing up Dennett's coffee tasters. You probably know the case: two tasters, Chase and Sanborn, who've each drifted over the years. One says the coffee tastes the same as ever and that he's the one who changed; the other says the coffee itself now tastes different. Both are sincere, both behave the same way at the counter, and the point is that we can't say which of them is right about whether the taste-quality changed — no possible evidence seems to settle it. Which, if you take it seriously, is bad news for the idea that there's a private, ineffable, intrinsic, immediately-apprehended property in there doing explanatory work. Dennett's own satirical dictionary glosses the verb "to quine" as "To deny resolutely the existence or importance of something real or significant," and that's roughly the move: not that experience isn't real, but that the qualia posit specifically is confused enough that we should stop appealing to it.

So here's what I want, and please don't give me the whole history of the debate or a reading list — I need to know what actually follows. What are the most important implications of taking that critique seriously for a project like my colleague's? Specifically: what can and can't introspective reports be evidence of, is her outcome measure measuring anything, how would she redesign the study if she took this on board, and what gets lost if she drops felt-quality talk entirely? Then the other direction: which alleged implications are overblown or just don't follow. I keep hearing that this undermines all first-person methods, or that it means pain patients are never authoritative about their own pain, and both strike me as too quick — tell me whether I'm right about that and why.

And the part I actually care about: if the qualia posit is incoherent, what does that do to everything else we lean on it for — the hard problem, the moral status of animals and maybe of AI systems, the role of experience in utilitarian ethics, the whole "what it's like" vocabulary in consciousness science? Rank those implications by how much they'd really change someone's practice, defend the ranking, and tell me what would move one up or down. I've been going back and forth on this alot and I want to know where the weight actually lands, not just that the questions are interesting.
