Two people in my reading group got into a fight last week over epiphenomenalism — the view that experiences are caused by brain processes but don't themselves cause anything, so your arm goes up because of the neurons, never because you wanted it to. I was just sitting there, I didn't say anything, and now they want a ruling. Can you go through their exchange move by move, say which moves are good and which are bad, then say who's more right overall and what both of them missed? Please don't just split the difference.

Sam went first. He said the view is self-undermining. If experiences cause nothing, then his belief that he's in pain right now, and his saying so out loud, are produced entirely by brain states. The pain itself contributes nothing to the report. But then the report would come out exactly the same if there were no pain at all — same brain state, same words. So the only evidence any of us could ever have that experiences exist is evidence that is, by the theory's own lights, not evidence about experiences. The theory cuts off the branch it's sitting on.

Ravi said thats way too fast. The report isn't supposed to be caused by the experience, it's supposed to be a reliable indicator of it — the way a printout of a weather model isn't caused by the storm but is still good evidence about the storm, because both trace back to the same conditions. Same deal with the brain state: it causes the pain and it causes the report, so the report tracks the pain perfectly well. Reliability doesn't require causation. Nothing self-undermining there.

Sam then tried a case. Imagine a drug that leaves the pain-processing in the brain completely intact — same neurons firing, same words coming out, the patient says 'ow, that stings' — but the felt sting is just gone. Nothing it's like. Ravi's story says the report is doing its job: it's reliably tracking the brain state. And yet the report is flatly wrong about the experience. So it was never tracking the experience in the first place, only the brain state, and the reliability story is dead. Ravi said no, that's just a case where the report is false, and my view predicts exactly that; all it shows is that reports can come apart from experiences, which I never denied. In the normal case the correlation holds and the inference goes through.

Sam came back: then when you introspect and think 'I'm having an experience right now', you can't rule out that you're in the drug case, because by your own account that thought is produced by the brain state alone. So you're never in a position to know you have experiences at all. Ravi said that's just the general skeptical problem — you can't rule out the brain in a vat either, and nobody thinks that refutes perception. Sam said it's not the same, because in the ordinary case you're allowed to say the experience itself is part of why you believe in it, and epiphenomenalism is the one view that takes that away.

They've been at this for a week. Adjudicate: rate each move from clearly mistaken to clearly right, and tell me who's got the better of it overall.
