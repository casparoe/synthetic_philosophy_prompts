I have been arguing with a colleague about William James's "The Will to Believe" and W. K. Clifford's "The Ethics of Belief," and he has produced an argument that I cannot refute and cannot bring myself to accept. I would like a diagnosis: which step fails, why it looked compelling, and what it costs to give it up.

He calls it the self-defeat argument, and writes it out as follows.

(1) Clifford's principle: it is wrong, in every time and place and for every person, to believe anything on insufficient evidence. Belief is to wait upon evidence; where the evidence falls short, the honest course is suspense of judgment.
(2) That principle is itself something one holds. If it is a belief, it falls under its own jurisdiction; if it is a rule for forming beliefs rather than a belief, it is still the sort of thing one must be warranted in holding. Either way, it may be held only if the evidence for it suffices.
(3) What evidence is there for it? It cannot be proved from self-evident premises, and it cannot be assumed without begging the question against its rivals. The only support available is a comparison of practices: that adopting evidentialism rather than a more permissive policy leads to better inquiry, fewer errors and more truths over the long run.
(4) But a principle defended by showing that adopting it has better consequences for the practice of belief is a principle accepted on pragmatic grounds.
(5) Pragmatic grounds are precisely what the principle forbids. To hold a belief because holding it serves some purpose of yours is to hold it for reasons that have nothing to do with its truth, and that is the credulity the principle exists to forbid.
(6) So evidentialism can be held only on grounds that evidentialism forbids. It refutes itself, and the only norms of belief left standing are the permissive ones, James's included.

I would rather keep (1) and (2) than either of the later steps. My colleague is confident the fault is in (2) — he says a rule is not a belief and does not answer to itself — which strikes me as too easy. For my part, I cannot tell whether the trouble lies in (3)'s claim about what evidence is available, in the move from (3) to (4), or in (6)'s either/or.

Tell me where the argument goes wrong, and then price the repair: what has to be given up, and by whom. I want the repair tested against the cases James cares most about, the ones in which the belief is part of what makes the thing believed true, as when a friendship becomes warm because both parties trust it, or a venture succeeds because those involved believe it will. If the evidentialist cannot hold his line there, say what he has to pay, and whether some weaker version of the self-defeat argument survives the repair anyway.

Lead with your verdict in the first sentence — name the step you reject and your reason for rejecting it in one line — and then give the argument for it. No preamble, and do not restate my question.
