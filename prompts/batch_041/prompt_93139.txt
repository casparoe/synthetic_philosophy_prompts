Two decision theorists are arguing about what the common prior assumption is actually doing in the theory of games with incomplete information. The first treats it as a substantive thesis about rational agents: if two agents are equally well informed, any difference in their beliefs must trace back to a difference in information, so the assumption is either an empirical claim about how beliefs are formed or a normative constraint on rational belief, and the persistent disagreements we observe are evidence against it. The second treats it as a bookkeeping device: what the assumption really buys is the ability to represent a correlated equilibrium as an equilibrium of a game in which each player privately observes a mediator's signal, together with an ex ante standpoint from which an analyst can compute expected welfare. Drop it, this second theorist says, and agents remain perfectly rational; the model merely admits a larger space of belief hierarchies, and talk of one agent having more information than another stops being well defined.

Write a dialogue in which each tries to talk the other out of their position. Anchor the exchange in a concrete case: two analysts who have seen all the same data, who each know the other to be a careful Bayesian, and who nonetheless disagree openly and persistently about whether a particular coin is biased. Both characters should be equally competent, neither should be a strawman, each should respond to the other's actual arguments rather than restating their own, and at least one of them should refine or partially concede a position under pressure. Have them press each other on at least three of the following: whether the theorem that agents who share a prior cannot disagree once their beliefs are common knowledge is a substantive finding about disagreement or merely an unpacking of the definition of conditional probability; whether the no-trade result, according to which traders who share a prior and hold an already efficient allocation cannot trade with one another at any price, is a reductio of the assumption or an artifact of the framework; whether the assumption is needed for welfare analysis rather than for the existence of equilibrium; and what becomes of the assumption when the two agents do not even share a state space, so that there is no single proposition over which their priors could coincide.
