I'm running the opening session of a reading group on metaontology, and I want to use it to teach one idea properly rather than survey the field. The audience is mostly graduate students in linguistics and computer science — people with sharp intuitions about how words work and almost no exposure to ontology — and the idea I want them to leave with is what it is for a dispute to be merely verbal. Please plan the session rather than explain the topic to me. I need the order in which the pieces have to arrive and why, because I suspect the group will want to begin with examples and only later discover that the phrase does no work until they already have two prior distinctions: that a word's meaning is a convention that can quietly diverge between two speakers who believe they are using it the same way, and that a single disagreement can be simultaneously about the world and about the language we are describing it with. Tell me what has to be in place before the question can even be posed, and where in the sequence each prior idea has to land.

I had planned to carry the whole session on one case: whether a virus counts as alive. My sense is that it works because the disagreement is genuine — researchers who study viruses really do disagree, and the disagreement shapes how they classify and handle things — while also being plainly entangled with what the word 'alive' is supposed to mean, so that neither side can simply stipulate the other out of the way. Tell me whether that case can bear the weight or whether it quietly fails, and what a replacement would have to have and lack. (I considered opening with whether Pluto is a planet, but I suspect it teaches the wrong lesson, since there the matter was settled by an explicit decision to redefine the term; tell me if I am wrong to set it aside.) Then map the misconceptions for me: I expect the triviality reading ('merely verbal, so no one is really disagreeing'), its mirror image ('if defining our terms makes the dispute evaporate, it was verbal all along'), the conflation of a dispute about a word with a dispute over whether to use that word, and a slide into relativism ('if each side is right relative to its own meaning, there is no fact of the matter'). For each, tell me where in the sequence it will form and exactly where I should head it off. Finish with one question I can put to the group that would reveal whether they have grasped the distinction rather than merely acquired the vocabulary, and tell me what I should openly refuse to cover at this level.
