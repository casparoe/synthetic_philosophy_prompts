I run a small reading group that's doing the Hart-Fuller debate this term, and I want a decision tree I can hand out so people figure out which position they actually hold instead of just announcing "I'm a positivist" or "I'm a natural lawyer" and then defending whatever the label usually means. Can you write it?

Some constraints, because the obvious version of this is useless:

Start the tree from a commitment everyone in the room already shares, so we don't waste nodes on it: we all accept that whether a norm is law is at least partly a matter of social fact — it was enacted by whoever counts as enacting things, or accepted by the officials who apply it, or something along those lines. Nobody in the group thinks law is just whatever is morally correct. So the interesting question is the next one: where, if anywhere, does moral merit enter — into whether something is law at all, into whether it's binding on us, into what it actually says, or nowhere.

Every node has to be a question someone can answer without knowing which leaf it leads to. Phrase them as cases or claims, not as labels. So not "are you an inclusive positivist?" but something like: imagine a legal system whose officials, when they argue about what the law is, routinely appeal to what justice requires — does that make justice part of the test for law in that system, or is it just a fact about how those particular officials happen to reason? And one case that should definately be in there: a post-war court facing someone who informed on a neighbour under a monstrous but perfectly properly enacted statute from a regime that has since collapsed. What is defeated when we say the statute was monstrous — its validity, the obligation to obey it, or nothing legal at all?

Build the tree from the logical space first: work out the combinations of answers the nodes allow, and only then locate the actual positions in that space. If a combination is empty, say so and diagnose it briefly — incoherent, unattractive, or just unexplored. Don't reverse the order and start from a list of names.

Each leaf should state the view in one sentence, then give the label (or "unnamed" if nobody defends it) and one sentence on that view's most awkward commitment. I want leaves for at least these: injustice never affects validity, it just means the law isn't binding; the rule of recognition in a given system can itself contain moral criteria; the moral work happens entirely in interpretation, so validity stays social but the content of the law doesn't; extreme injustice strips validity; a thoroughly unjust system isn't law in the focal or central sense rather than being invalid law; and legality comes in degrees.

Flag any pair of leaves the tree genuinely can't seperate. I suspect at least one pair will collapse — the extreme-injustice-removes-validity leaf and the degrees-of-legality leaf look dangerously close, and there may be two flavours of the rule-of-recognition leaf that the nodes can't pull apart. If so, say so rather than papering over it.

Keep it to six to eight nodes, and pitch it at people who've read Hart and Fuller but aren't specialists.

Last thing: be upfront about how confident you are in the way you've drawn the tree, and tell me the single point you're least sure of — I'd much rather have a flagged uncertainty than a confident-looking tree with a soft spot I can't see.
