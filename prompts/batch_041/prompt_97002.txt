I spent most of Saturday evening arguing with a friend who teaches history of science, and I would like to turn the argument into something my colleagues can use. His position was that whenever we write that a superseded theory was closer to the truth than the theory it replaced — phlogiston closer than the four elements, Newtonian mechanics closer than Aristotelian — we are either saying something so weak that nobody could object, or smuggling in a ranking we could not defend if pressed. He had the formal literature on his side: Popper's proposal to define closeness to truth by comparing a theory's true consequences with its false ones; the result, established by Tichý and Miller in the 1970s, that this comparison cannot do the work Popper wanted, since between two false theories any excess of true consequences on one side brings an excess of false consequences along with it, so the two comparisons can never point in opposite directions; and the further objection that the ranking can be reversed by re-describing the same facts in a different vocabulary — replacing the original predicates with truth-functional compounds of them, or permuting their extensions — so that what counts as closer depends on the language rather than on the world. I conceded the technical points and then discovered I could not say what we should do instead.

I am the series editor for a history-of-science textbook, and I have to make this call repeatedly. For each episode we cover, my authors want a sentence ranking the rival theories, and I am the one who approves or strikes it. The decisions arise several times a month, they are made by people with no formal training in semantics, and they end up in print for a decade. Please write me a decision procedure my editors can actually run on a proposed ranking.

It should be a short ordered list of questions, with what counts as a sufficient answer at each step, ending either in a licensed ranking, a licensed weaker claim (give me the weaker wording to use), or a referral to me because the question has become one of values rather than evidence. It must not pretend to compute a content-based measure of the kind the impossibility result rules out; say what it does instead, and say whether that substitute is a measure of closeness to truth at all or a proxy for something else, such as predictive accuracy or explanatory unification — my authors conflate these, and the procedure should force the distinction. It must confront the language-dependence objection rather than ignore it: either require the ranking to survive a re-description of the episode in a different but equally natural vocabulary, or declare the ranking relative to a fixed vocabulary and mark that openly as a limitation. It has to work when no numbers are available, which is most of the time.

Then run it on three cases: the replacement of miasma theory by germ theory; the replacement of Ptolemaic astronomy by Copernican, where the predictive ledger in the first decades is not obviously favourable to the winner; and one case of your choosing in which the procedure refuses to certify the ranking my authors would prefer. Say in each case which step is doing the work. Close with what the procedure cannot do, and with the honest reply to my friend: whether the sentence we print is a claim about the world or a claim about our vocabulary.
