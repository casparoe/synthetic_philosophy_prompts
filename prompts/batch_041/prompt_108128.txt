Write me a joke about Molyneux's problem. It must be a real joke, with a setup and a punchline, and its humour must turn on the substance of the problem: a reader who has never heard of it should find the joke baffling rather than funny, and a reader who knows it well should recognise the point as one of the actual cruxes.

Most retellings skip the detail I want you to build on. The man born blind is not a stranger to cubes and spheres; he has handled them, learned to tell them apart by touch, and can already say which is which before the operation. So the test does not ask whether he can see shape. It asks whether the idea of a cube that arrives through the eye is the same idea he already has through the hand, and whether the word he has used all his life for the thing in his palm picks out the same thing that the word picks out for us. Let the joke live in that gap: at the moment of the test, in the naming, and in the possibility that the interesting question is which of the two is being examined, the man or the concept.

Keep it clean, and short enough to tell aloud; the blindness is the occasion of the joke, not the joke itself. Do not settle the matter by reporting what experiments with newly sighted patients have found, and do not let the punchline be merely that philosophers ask odd questions. You may use the fact that any real test hands him the object while he is looking at it, so the two senses are never as cleanly separated as the question pretends.

Reply with exactly five numbered jokes, each no longer than two sentences, with no introduction, no commentary between them, and no closing remarks.
