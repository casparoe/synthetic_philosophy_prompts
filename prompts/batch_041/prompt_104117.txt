You are a philosopher of language with a deep command of Grice's pragmatics and, unusually for a philosopher, a working knowledge of communication-protocol design and information theory. I am a network protocol engineer: I think in layers, frames, payloads and headers, out-of-band signaling, side channels, error correction, and quality-of-service constraints. I have no formal training in the philosophy of language, and I would like you to restate the core of Grice's account of meaning and implicature in my vocabulary as faithfully as you can — and then give me a candid residue report.

Before you begin the translation, spend a short while thinking through, for yourself, what Grice is actually claiming at each step and which of his distinctions carry explanatory weight as opposed to being terminological scaffolding. Then produce the restatement. Do not let my vocabulary quietly dictate the shape of his claims: where his point resists my terms, say so rather than forcing a fit.

Cover at least the following, and render each in my idiom:

1. The distinction between natural and non-natural meaning — the difference between something indicating a state of affairs the way smoke indicates fire, and a speaker meaning something by an utterance.
2. Grice's analysis of speaker meaning in terms of reflexive intentions: roughly, that a speaker means something by an utterance when she intends to produce an effect in her audience partly by means of the audience's recognition of that very intention.
3. The Cooperative Principle and the maxims of Quantity, Quality, Relation, and Manner.
4. The distinction between what is said and what is conversationally implicated, together with the properties standardly used to identify conversational implicature — cancellability, non-detachability, and calculability.
5. The difference between conversational and conventional implicature.

For each, tell me what plays the role of a frame, a header, a payload, an out-of-band channel, a side channel, a protocol invariant, a QoS constraint, a retransmission or error-correcting mechanism, and the difference between a specification and a particular implementation.

Then the residue report. For every item, say whether it maps cleanly, maps only with distortion, or has no counterpart in my vocabulary at all. I am especially suspicious of three places where I expect the translation to leak, and I want you to address each directly:

- Reflexive intention. My notion of a sender's intent is causal and one-level: the sender wants a state to obtain, and the encoding is a means to it. Is there anything in protocol design that genuinely requires the nested structure — intending that the audience's recognition of the intention do the work — or does the existence of a shared protocol make the nesting idle? If my vocabulary can only express the one-level version, is that a limitation or a clarification?
- Cancellability. The nearest thing I have is deniability: steganography, or a receiver who cannot tell whether a message came from the sender or from noise. Is that the same phenomenon as an implicature being cancellable without contradiction, or a different one wearing similar clothes?
- Normativity. Any maxims I obey are constitutive rules of a protocol — violate them and the protocol simply fails. Grice's maxims are defeasible expectations that a cooperative participant may rationally flout while remaining cooperative. Does my vocabulary have any way to express the second notion, or can it only represent rule-following and rule-breaking?

Close with a verdict: is the residue a defect of my framework, something protocol engineering is missing, or is it evidence that Grice's distinctions are less substantive than the philosophy suggests? Where you think a better translation exists than the one you gave — a different engineering concept that would carry more of the original — name it and explain what it buys.
