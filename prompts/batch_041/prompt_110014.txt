I am a third-year philosophy student serving as an undergraduate assistant for an introductory ethics course, and I have been asked to run the fifty-minute discussion section for the unit on promising. I do not want the topic explained to me; I want a teaching plan. The students will arrive from the lecture able to recite that promises generate obligations and that breaking them is wrong, but they have almost certainly never asked what makes a promise bind in the first place, and I have one hour in which to make that question feel like a real one rather than a philosopher's contrivance. Lay out the explanatory arc: what has to be established before the question even becomes pressing, the order in which the pieces must come and why each depends on the one before it, and the precise moment where the session turns from promises being things we already take seriously to there being something here that needs explaining at all. I want a single carrying case for the whole session, and I want you to specify what that case must have and what it must lack — my own instinct is a promise made to someone who cannot be harmed by its breach and who will never learn whether it was kept, on the grounds that it isolates the bindingness from any effect on the promisee, but tell me whether that is the right workhorse or what would be better. Say as well why the obvious classroom cases — the borrowed book, the lunch date, the friend waiting at the airport — are poor choices for this particular job even though they are perfectly good examples of promises.

Then map the misconceptions in the order they will actually surface, together with the point in the sequence where each should be headed off: that promises bind because breaking them damages trust or disappoints people; that a promise is nothing more than a strongly stated intention; that promising is a species of consenting, so that any voluntary commitment binds; that a release from the promisee dissolves the obligation instantly and completely; that the obligation is always conditional on the other party holding up their end; and any further error you predict, including what I suspect is the deepest one, namely that the students will conflate "are promises always binding?" with "what makes a promise binding at all?" Finally, give me one closing question I can put to the room that will reveal whether they have understood the question rather than merely learned to repeat an answer, and a short list of what I should openly refuse to resolve in fifty minutes — stating plainly that I am setting it aside — so that the session does not collapse into my defending a position on the metaphysics of obligation. It is the last section before the midterm and attendance will be thin, so the plan should work even if half the room has not done the reading.
