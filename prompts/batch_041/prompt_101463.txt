I lead the quantitative risk group at a small reinsurance firm, and we have adopted a convention: every probability we report must be tagged with the interpretation it is meant to carry — frequentist (a relative frequency in a reference class we can name), subjective (a specified analyst's coherent degree of belief), or propensity (a disposition of the physical process itself) — and two tagged numbers may never be pooled, averaged, or chained into one when their tags differ. Give me the most important implications of that convention for our practice, not a restatement of the textbook differences among the three views. What must change when we pool several analysts' judgments, when we calibrate our forecasts against our historical loss record, when we update on a single unprecedented event, and when we defend a number to a regulator who will ask what it is supposed to mean? For each implication, identify which interpretation it turns on, why it follows from the convention rather than from general good practice, and the strongest objection together with who would raise it; and say where the convention itself is most likely to break down.

Return this as a JSON object with a top-level "implications" array whose objects have the keys "implication" (one sentence), "stage" (one of pooling, calibration, updating, communication), "interpretation" (one of frequentist, subjective, propensity, all), "reasoning" (why it follows), "practical_change" (what we must do differently), and "dissent" (the strongest objection and its source); plus top-level fields "most_fragile_point" (a short paragraph) and "keep_if_forced_to_drop_the_rest" (one implication, with a sentence of justification).
