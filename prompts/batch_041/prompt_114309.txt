I have spent the past couple of years reading about why elected officials seem so disconnected from the people they represent, and I have come to believe the problem is less any particular politician than the career structure itself — the fact that holding office has become a long-term job with its own incentives, its own professional class, and its own way of filtering who reaches the top. I have no formal training in politics or political science, so I have been reasoning this through on my own, and I have ended up with a list of reforms that mixes the familiar (term limits, sortition) with a few combinations I have not seen anyone actually propose. I would like you to rate them.

Please treat the goal as concrete: how much each reform would, over roughly a decade, close the gap between what the legislature actually does and what a randomly chosen cross-section of adult citizens would want it to do. For each reform, give me a score from 0 to 1 for how much it would close that gap, and a separate 0-to-1 confidence score for how much I should trust the first number. I want the two kept apart, because I suspect some of the ideas I find most appealing are also the ones I am least sure about.

Here is my list:

1. A hard single-term limit: one term, then permanently barred from any elected office.
2. A fully sortition-based lower house: the whole chamber filled by stratified lottery, one fixed term, with the same legislative powers the elected house has now.
3. An advisory-only citizens' assembly: it produces recommendations that the elected legislature must debate publicly, but is not bound by them.
4. Term limits plus a cooling-off period: two terms maximum, then the person must sit out one full term before running again.
5. Lottery-restricted candidacy: ordinary elections continue, but a lottery first narrows each district's field down to a random handful of candidates, who then campaign as usual.
6. A compulsory-service model: every adult is in the pool, service is paid at the local median wage, and refusing to serve is penalized the way refusing jury duty is.
7. Total rotation: the entire chamber is replaced by a fresh lottery every session, and nobody may serve twice.
8. Competence-gated sortition: members are drawn by lottery, but must pass an exam before taking the seat.
9. Recall-by-petition sortition: members are drawn by lottery, but can be removed mid-term by a citizen petition followed by a vote.

For each reform, please answer in a table with exactly these columns:

- Reform: the short name from my list above.
- Gap_closure: a number from 0 to 1, as described.
- Confidence: a number from 0 to 1, as described.
- Main_channel: whether it works mainly by changing who gets in ("selection"), by changing how people behave once they are in ("incentives"), or "both" — pick one of those three words.
- Strongest_point: one sentence on the best argument for it.
- Failure_mode: one sentence on the most likely way it backfires or gets gamed.
- Tier: one of S, A, B, C, or D, grouping it with the others that perform most similarly.

After the table, add three short sections: (a) the tiers listed from best to worst with the reforms in each; (b) any reforms in my list that you think are effectively the same mechanism wearing different clothes, and why; (c) any reform where your confidence score is low enough that you think I should not act on it regardless of how high the gap-closure score is.
