I'm putting together a prospectus for a one-year masters thesis in philosophy and I've got a half-baked idea I want you to turn into a real proposal. The area is far-future ethics -- the view that the biggest deal in the universe is getting a large, good, long-lasting future civilization going, and that we should be willing to swallow enormous present costs for a small bump in the chance of securing it. People push back on this with fanaticism worries: if the far future is astronomically valuable, then almost any sacrifice now is justified by a tiny probability of getting it, and the view seems to eat everything else.

My idea is to bring in the simulation argument, but not in the usual way. Normally it's used either to mess with whether the world is real or to spin out weird decision puzzles. What I want to ask is whether it should dampen the fanaticism. Rough thought: the far-future case rests on us having real causal reach into a huge future. But if there's a non-negligible chance we're in a simulation, then a lot of that reach is into a simulated future some simulator could just switch off, and our influence on the real future is maybe zero. So maybe the simulation argument is a deflator -- it knocks the expected value of the far future down enough to take the edge off.

But then I keep worrying it cuts the other way. If simulations are cheap and simulators run lots of them, then maybe most futures are simulated, which could multiply rather than divide our expected impact -- more futures, not fewer, and maybe our actions are inputs the simulators actually care about. And theres a nastier version: the number of simulations that exist might itself depend on how the far future goes, so the probability we're simulated isn't independent of the very thing we're trying to influence. That entangles the two arguments and I genuinely don't know if it helps or hurts the fanatic.

So write me the proposal. State the central question so it has a determinate answer -- something like whether taking the simulation argument seriously reduces the expected value of far-future-oriented action, and whether any reduction is big enough to matter for the fanaticism worry. Say why the existing treatments leave this open (my hunch: people either use the simulation argument for reality/decision puzzles, or they discuss fanaticism with no mention of simulation, and nobody has put the two together as a question about the structure of expected value). Give me a method. This is armchair stuff but I want it rigorous, so say what formalism I'd use, what the key variables are, how I'd avoid just stipulating a probability of being simulated, and how I'd check that the argument doesn't beg the question. Say what a real result looks like (I think it's a taxonomy: under what assumptions the argument dampens, when it amplifies, when it's neutral) and what would count as the project failing. And be honest about scope for a year -- what's the version I can actually finish, and what part should I flag as beyond it.

Don't bother with citations, I'll add those myself. Refer to positions and arguments by what they say, not by who said them.
