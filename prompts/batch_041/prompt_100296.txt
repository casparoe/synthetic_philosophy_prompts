I'm setting the final exam for an upper-level seminar on rationality and decision theory, and I want one essay question on Parfit's hitchhiker, worth 25 marks, that cannot be passed by reciting the usual moral that it can be rational to have a disposition to do what you will have no reason to do. To force real analysis, I want the question built around a variant of the case rather than the case itself. In the original, the driver stops only for a traveler she can tell would pay. In my variant, the driver's rule is cruder: she stops only for travelers who do not flinch when she names her price. Flinching is, as it happens, a reliable sign of someone who will not pay, but it is a behavioral reflex that a traveler can sometimes, with effort and concentration, suppress.

I want the question to make students decide whether this variant preserves the original conclusion, and, if it does not, to state exactly what the original argument presupposes about the signal the driver is responding to. It should also require them to characterize the relation between the driver's choice and the traveler's choice, and to say whether rational irrationality names a genuine capacity or merely a favorable causal arrangement.

Please write (a) the essay question, (b) a model answer of roughly 600 words, and (c) a marking scheme out of 25 that separates an A from a B by naming the specific moves each must contain and the specific mistakes that should cost marks.
