I am assembling the opening session of a graduate seminar that puts philosophers and machine-learning researchers in the same room, and I want to give the students a single sheet of paper — a specification — before they spend a term reading claims that a system is 'safe' or 'aligned' or that a training method 'guarantees' something. Please write that specification for me. Frame it as the requirements any adequate account of alignment assurance must satisfy: take the thing being assessed to be a claim of the form 'procedure P, or the system S that it produces, will behave acceptably in deployment', and set out what such a claim must deliver in order to be usable by someone who has to decide something — which cases it must get right, which distinctions it must not collapse, which results from learning theory or from the practice of evaluation it cannot contradict. For each requirement, give the consideration that motivates it, and say plainly which requirements are genuine and which are the parochial preferences of one research camp dressed up as necessities. I am most interested in the quantifier structure of such claims: what exactly is being quantified over — inputs, environments, policies, training runs, the selection procedure that picks the deployed model — in what order, and whether the guarantee attaches to the process or to the finished artefact; a claim whose quantifiers are left implicit is, in my experience, usually doing two incompatible jobs at once.

Several considerations I should like to see addressed explicitly, because they are where I expect the list to bite. First, non-vacuity: a criterion that no system can fail, or that no system can satisfy, gives no guidance, and I suspect the honest formulation has to say something about which systems the criterion is meant to discriminate among. Second, what I have taken to calling the decision window — a guarantee is worth having only if it is available before the commitment it is meant to inform becomes irreversible, so I want the requirements to say something about when an assurance claim must be in hand, not merely whether it is true. Third, the degeneracy problem: the most conservative systems satisfy almost any worst-case criterion, so a safety requirement with no accompanying floor on capability is satisfied by a system that refuses to do anything, and I want to know whether that is a separate desideratum or a constraint internal to the others. Fourth, the fact that the object of assessment is typically produced by an optimiser that can be sensitive to how it will be assessed, which makes any measure estimated on a distribution the system can influence suspect. And fifth, whether guarantees are required to compose, so that assurances about parts transfer to systems built out of them, and what becomes of the list if they do not.

Finally, and this is the part I care about most: tell me which requirements in the list cannot be jointly satisfied, and what that impossibility is a result about — I would far rather have a short list with one genuine tension exposed than a long list of platitudes. Say which single requirement does the most to exclude candidate accounts. Then, briefly, how three broad families fare against the specification: approaches that seek formal worst-case certification of behaviour, approaches that offer distributional or average-case guarantees estimated by empirical evaluation, and approaches that place the constraint on the training process rather than on the resulting behaviour. I want no bibliography, no reading list and no citations, since I am asking you to reason rather than to point. Please write in British English, tell me how confident you are in each of your main claims, and single out the one point about which you are least sure.
