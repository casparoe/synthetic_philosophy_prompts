Over the past year I've been learning to look at paintings with a group of people who have done it seriously for decades, and I've gradually realized that nearly every aesthetic opinion I hold — that a certain late Rothko is sublime, that a local sculptor's work is merely clever — I took over from them rather than arrived at by looking. I no longer know whether I know these things or am only echoing them, and I'd rather have a number than a tour of the debate.

Give me your credence in the following claim, stated as precisely as I can manage: a belief about a work's aesthetic merit that is held entirely on the basis of another person's testimony, with no first-hand experience of the work, cannot be an aesthetic judgment at all — it is a belief of the wrong kind rather than merely an unjustified one — and no degree of reliability in the testifier can change that. Then decompose the number, with a rough probability and a one-line justification for each term: the probability that aesthetic properties are essentially acquaintance-dependent, so that no propositional substitute can carry them; the probability, conditional on that, that the impossibility is a matter of kind rather than of justification; the probability that thin aesthetic predicates (beautiful, good) and thick ones (garish, delicate) come apart here, and how much that split should move the total; and the probability that "aesthetic judgment" names a normative achievement rather than a psychological state, which would make the claim either trivially true or trivially false depending on which way it lands. Add any term you think I've left out, combine them, and tell me which one the final figure is most sensitive to and what you would have to learn to move it by more than 0.1. Please answer in American English.
