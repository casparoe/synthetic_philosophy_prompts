I'm preparing a session for a historiography seminar that uses film to teach the difference between the Great Man theory of history and structural explanation, and I want to build the whole session around Quentin Tarantino's Inglourious Basterds. The trouble is that the film stages the counterfactual so nakedly — the Basterds and Shosanna kill Hitler, Goebbels, and the assembled Nazi high command in a Paris cinema in 1944 — that it is easy either to say Tarantino is simply indulging a Great Man fantasy or to say he is doing something more interesting, and I want a reading that actually settles the question rather than gesturing at it. So: read the film through the debate over whether a figure like Hitler is an irreplaceable cause or the replaceable occupant of a structural slot. Where does the film's fantasy of decapitation presuppose the Great Man view — the idea that removing the man removes the regime? Where does it undercut that view, whether in its fairy-tale framing, in its relish for individual agency (Landa's cold opportunism, Shosanna's personal revenge, Aldo Raine's scalping), or in the sheer absurdity of the operation itself? I want both the film and the historiography taken seriously, so please do not reduce either to a slogan: say what the film's structure actually commits it to, and be honest about the scenes where it resists the reading you are giving it.

A framing I keep circling and would like you to engage directly: the counterfactual question of what would have happened had Hitler been killed is really two different questions wearing one coat. There is a weak version, in which Hitler is a symptom — the structural preconditions (the settlement at Versailles, the Depression, the paramilitary milieu, the collapse of the Weimar parties) would have thrown up some analogous movement and some analogous leader, so the assassination changes little. And there is a strong version, in which the specific man is load-bearing — his particular obsessions, timing, and rhetorical gifts are what made the catastrophe take the shape it did, so removing him changes everything. The long-running dispute among historians over whether the Holocaust was driven by Hitler's personal will or by the slow radicalization of a bureaucratic machine is, I take it, a version of the same fork. My suspicion is that Tarantino's climax can only function as drama because it silently assumes the strong version — otherwise the bullets are pointless — and that this is exactly the assumption a structuralist would deny. Test that suspicion: is the film doing Great Man history by default, or can the same climax be read as a structuralist fable, even a satire on the idea that shooting the right people could ever have stopped anything? End by saying which reading the film, taken as a whole, better supports, and what it adds to the debate that a straightforwardly argued essay could not.
