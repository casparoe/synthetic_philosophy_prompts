Here's the gist of the essay. (It's rough, treat it as notes toward an argument.)

The usual move is to treat looping effects as a problem for the objectivity of psychiatric classification: we classify people, they internalize the classification, they change, and so the classification ends up tracking something it partly manufactured. Hacking's own examples — the successive waves of multiple personality / dissociative identity, the shifting profile of homosexuality before and after declassification, the child-abuse-survivor scripts of the 80s and 90s — are all cases where the label, the clinical template, and the patient population co-evolved.

I want to argue that this framing buries the interesting variable. What matters isn't *whether* a classification loops; almost all of them loop to some degree. What matters is the gain and the delay of the loop, and in particular whether the cultural script attached to the label is running ahead of, in step with, or behind the diagnostic criteria.

So I want two ideal types:

- **Cold loops**: the label changes behaviour without much changing self-understanding. Someone fills in a screening questionnaire, scores above threshold, starts monitoring their mood, fills in the same questionnaire at follow-up, and reports more symptoms because they've learned the items. The change is real, but it's mostly a reporting/attention artifact plus a mild behavioural adjustment. Think PHQ-9-style instruments, sleep apps, ADHD self-screening.
- **Hot loops**: the label changes the person's self-narrative, and the narrative feeds back into which symptoms get experienced and reported. Hacking's DID cases are hot; so is a lot of the current discourse around trauma, neurodivergence, and burnout.

Claim 1: most of the "is this a real disorder or a social construction?" heat is actually about hot loops, but most of the empirical evidence we have about looping is generated in cold-loop settings (questionnaires, screening tools, structured diagnostic interviews). So the debate is running on the wrong evidence.

Claim 2: what gets called concept creep — a term's extension widening so that milder cases start to count — is really just a loose hot loop where the script expands faster than the criteria. Once you separate the two, concept creep stops looking like a moral panic and starts looking like a predictable dynamical consequence.

Claim 3 (the one I'm least sure of): what stabilizes a cold loop is that the script is *thin* — few instructions about how to be the kind of person the label names. What destabilizes a hot loop is that the script is *thick* and narratively rewarding: it explains your suffering, hands you a community, assigns you an antagonist. On that view the DID epidemics and the recovered-memory business weren't caused by bad clinicians alone; they were a thick, rewarding script meeting a classificatory slot with no stable criteria. And the fix isn't better criteria, it's thinner scripts.
