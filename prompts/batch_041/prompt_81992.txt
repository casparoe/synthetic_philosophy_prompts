Last night I spent nearly three hours arguing with a friend about a case so simple that we were both embarrassed to be stuck on it: I can save one person on one side or two people on the other, and I cannot save all three. He maintains that the numbers do not count. I maintain that they obviously do. We got nowhere, and below I have tried to state his replies as forcefully as he made them rather than as I would prefer to remember them.

(1) He began from the claim that each person's interest in being saved is equally weighty, and that equal claims do not add up. Two equal claims, he said, are not a larger claim than one; there is no fact of the matter that makes the pair's claim heavier than the lone person's. Since he must choose, and has no reason to prefer either side, he would flip a fair coin — not because fairness requires a coin, but because he has no reason, and the coin is a way of not pretending that he does.

(2) I conceded the arithmetic and tried to turn it against him. A fair coin does give everyone the same chance: one in two for the lone person, and one in two for each of the pair. But look at what that chance buys. Half the time, two people die when only one needed to. If a rescue service faces this same choice a hundred times over the years, flipping coins saves about a hundred and fifty people where always saving the pair saves two hundred. Fifty people are in the ground who need not have been, and no one can tell them that the coin had a reason.

(3) He said that fifty is a fact about an aggregate, and that aggregate facts are precisely what he denies give anyone a reason to act. Across my hundred cases, every person faced the same chance as everyone else situated as they were; no one was subordinated to anyone. If I want to name the person his procedure wrongs, I cannot. My rule, by contrast, wrongs the lone person every single time, and wrongs him for no reason except that there happened to be more of them.

(4) I said I can name them: the fifty. And by his own standard the lone person is not wronged, since that standard denies that claims add. On his view, when the coin comes up for the pair, the lone person's equal claim is overridden just as surely as it is under my rule — only half as often. He has not avoided overriding anyone's claim; he has spread the overriding around.

(5) He answered that spreading it is exactly what equal respect requires when no claim outranks another. My rule concentrates the loss on whoever happens to be standing alone; his distributes it, and a coin is the purest way to distribute it without pretending to a reason.

(6) I offered a case meant to break the procedure. Suppose it is one person against a hundred. His coin still gives the lone person a one-in-two chance: half the time he lets a hundred people die in order to honor a device that favors nobody. He cannot say the lone person's chance should shrink as the other side grows without conceding that numbers count. But a rule on which one person's rescue is a coin flip against a hundred deaths seems to have lost contact with what is at stake.

(7) He said the numbers are exactly what he refuses to weigh, so he accepts the consequence. If he lets the hundred weigh, he has already conceded that the many are heavier, which is the thesis he denies. That the consequence is uncomfortable is not an argument; it is the same discomfort I feel at every step, and he does not treat his discomfort as a premise.

(8) That is where we stopped, and where he says I lost the thread. I told him we are not disagreeing about arithmetic at all: he thinks an outcome can be worse without giving anyone a reason to act, and I think that if two deaths are worse than one, and I can bring about the better outcome without overriding anyone's claim more than the alternative does, then I have a reason to. He said I was sliding between a fact about value and a fact about claims.

Please take the exchange as it stands and adjudicate it move by move — for each of the eight, say whether it is sound, unsound, or defensible but costly, and why. Then say who is more nearly right overall, and what both of us are missing, including whether there is any position that keeps the coin while resisting the hundred-person case, and whether the appeal to equal chances is doing independent work or is just aggregation in a different currency.
