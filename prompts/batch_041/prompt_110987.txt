I'm a philosophy student writing a term paper on the knowledge argument, and I need help with the part of it that has actually defeated me. In my seminar we spent nearly a month on Mary and the black-and-white room and finished the unit exactly where we began: the same case, the same replies, the same two-sentence summaries of one another's positions, and no one's confidence visibly moved. The argument is easy to state — Mary knows every physical fact about color vision, and when she first sees red she learns something, so the physical facts do not exhaust the facts — and I can recite the standard replies in my sleep. What I cannot do is say why the exchange never terminates.

Here is how I would put the standoff. The physicalist offers one of two things: that Mary gains an ability or an acquaintance rather than a proposition, or that she gains a new way of representing a fact she already knew, so that nothing about the world has been added. The anti-physicalist replies that the first is a relabeling that concedes the point in different words, and that the second owes us an account of that new way of representing — a phenomenal concept — which does not itself help itself to the very experience it is meant to explain. The physicalist then objects that the original inference runs from "she could not have deduced it" to "it is not physical," and that this step needs a principle connecting what is deducible to what is identical, which is precisely what is in dispute. Each side's decisive objection looks question-begging from the other side, and I suspect that is not an accident.

My hypothesis is that the impasse is not about Mary, or about color, but about two prior questions: what a complete description of the world must contain, and what stands in need of explanation. The anti-physicalist treats an unclosable conceptual gap as itself a fact requiring explanation, while the physicalist treats the gap as a fact about our representational equipment that is fully explained by pointing to the equipment. If that is right, the disagreement is really about a standard of explanation, and no further facts about color vision will touch it.

I want a diagnosis rather than a survey, and I want it tested rather than merely asserted. Is my hypothesis correct, or is the real work being done elsewhere — by a disagreement in the epistemology of modality about whether deducibility or conceivability tracks possibility at all; by a semantic disagreement about whether knowing what something is like even reports propositional knowledge, which would make part of the dispute verbal; or by two different questions sharing one name, namely whether the argument refutes physicalism and whether it refutes the claim that the complete physical truth a priori entails everything else? I have also been trying to see the whole thing through the old distinction between knowledge by acquaintance and knowledge by description, and I cannot tell whether the two sides are placing Mary's new knowledge on opposite sides of that line or whether they mean different things by the line itself.

Tell me what kind of disagreement this is, using what each side actually says, and tell me what my hypothesis predicts: if it is right, would the impasse survive replacing color vision with a sense that no human possesses — and if it would not survive, what does that show? Given your diagnosis, say what would count as progress (a distinction, a formal result, a change of question) and whether the impasse is a sign that the question is ill-posed or merely that it is hard. I don't want a reading list or citations; I want the diagnosis and the reasoning behind it.
