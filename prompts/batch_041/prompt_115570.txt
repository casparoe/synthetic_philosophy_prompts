I am a philosopher of biology preparing a paper for a small workshop on explanation in evolutionary theory, and I want to spend it on a puzzle I have not been able to shake. The argument over adaptationism that Gould and Lewontin opened with their 1979 critique in the Proceedings of the Royal Society B, known ever since as the spandrels paper, is now more than forty years old, and it has a strange shape. The position that was attacked, the view that each trait should be assumed to be an adaptation until shown otherwise and that the biologist's task is to reconstruct the selective history of traits one at a time, is one that few now admit to holding, and the complaint that the paper attacked a caricature is itself one of the standing objections to it. Yet adaptationist reasoning remains the default in practice, and the critiques of it are still being written. When the critique appeared, a common reaction among working biologists was that it had a healthy effect and forced the field to demand better evidence, offered in the same breath as the insistence that adaptation is still the core of biological thinking. The pluralists, for their part, grant that selection is a major force while maintaining that constraints, byproducts, and drift are systematically underdescribed and that the field's explanatory habits have not really changed. Both sides can look back on the same forty years and claim vindication. I am not asking you to decide who is right about organisms. I am asking you to diagnose the disagreement: what kind of dispute is this, and is there anything that would move it?

Please engage with the following, which is where I think the interest lies.

1. The founding example may be misdescribed. The San Marco surfaces that serve as the model of a byproduct are, strictly, pendentives, the curved masonry surfaces that carry the transition from a square bay to a circular dome, and their number and shape follow from that geometry. Critics have pressed the further point that alternative structural solutions, corbels or squinches, would have served the engineering purpose equally well, so that the choice of pendentives was itself a design decision rather than a forced consequence, and they conclude that the flagship illustration of the byproduct idea is in fact a case of deliberate design. Gould's reply was to widen the term: a spandrel can be any leftover space, and pendentives are leftover spaces in that sense. Ask whether the argument against adaptationism depends on the example or merely uses it as an intuition pump, and whether it survives if the architectural facts are as the critics say.

2. The concept may lack truth conditions. Whether a trait is classified as a byproduct or as an adaptation appears to depend on how the trait is individuated and which ancestral condition is held fixed; redescribe either and the classification can flip. Gould himself insisted that historical origin and current utility must be separated, and he later introduced a distinct term, exaptation, for features co-opted for a use they were not built for, which suggests he took the distinction to concern history rather than present function. Consider whether the spandrel concept can be given conditions precise enough to test, or whether it functions as a placeholder for the absence of a functional explanation.

3. The term has traveled. Outside evolutionary biology the word is now applied to features, religious belief being the example I have in mind, whose byproduct status is typically asserted rather than demonstrated. Decide whether this migration reveals something about the original concept, that it is easy to apply and hard to confirm, or is merely a separate error.

4. The critique can be read at three strengths: as a demand that adaptationist hypotheses be rigorously tested before acceptance; as a demand for pluralism, that nonselectionist explanations be given equal standing; and as the stronger claim that nonselectionist explanations are to be preferred. These have different evidential requirements, and the literature slides among them.

My hypothesis, offered as a hypothesis rather than a conclusion, is that the debate looks stalled because it is several disagreements fused under one word: an empirical disagreement about how much of organismal form selection actually explains, which is in principle settleable but not by the evidence either side tends to offer; a methodological disagreement about defaults and the burden of proof; a disagreement about what a satisfying explanation must do, whether a selection story explains a trait or merely relabels it; and a sociological overlay from the fights over sociobiology and human nature, which gave the dispute stakes that outran its content. If that is roughly right, much of the apparent impasse is an artifact of treating four questions as one.

What I want from you is a diagnosis, not a survey. For each distinguishable strand of the dispute, say whether it is verbal, empirical, methodological or normative, or a clash of starting intuitions that neither side can argue for; where you call a strand empirical, say what observations would settle it and whether they are available. Give a verdict on the founding example: does the critique stand or fall with the architectural facts? Say what would count as progress for each strand, which single move, if any, would move more than one of them, and whether any such move is likely. Answer directly whether the debate should be expected to resolve, and why or why not; if you think part of it is verbal, say what each side is actually tracking.

Anchor the diagnosis in at least one concrete contested trait. The masculinized genitalia of female spotted hyenas is a candidate that has been used on both sides of this dispute; use it, or choose another case you can describe accurately, and say what facts about the case would be needed to classify it either way. Do not assume the reader knows your example; describe it.

Aim for roughly 3,500 words of prose, with the diagnosis as the spine and the conceptual work in the foreground. Do not include a bibliography or citation apparatus; refer to positions and arguments rather than to authors and dates, and I will supply the references. I have read the literature, so do not summarize it. What I need is the diagnosis.
