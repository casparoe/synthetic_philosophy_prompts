I'm a retired geochemist — thirty-odd years of isotopes, most of it tedious — and since I stopped going into the lab I've been running a small monthly reading group that's turned into a low-key podcast, basically philosophy for people who spent their lives measuring things. Next month's guest is a philosopher who holds the old-fashioned line about war: that once two armies are actually fighting, the soldiers on each side are moral equals, the one fighting for an unjust cause being no more a murderer than the one shooting back at him, and that the justice or injustice of the cause doesn't by itself take away anyone's license to kill. She's sharp and she's been defending this against the revisionist crowd for alot of years, so I don't want to hand her a list of soft questions. I'd like nine or ten that actually bite, and under each one a follow-up keyed to the answer she's most likely to give, because we'll be going back and forth live and I need to know where to go when she gives me the standard reply.

Here's where I want the pressure put. At least one question on the difference between her saying the unjust combatant's act is *permitted* and saying it's merely *excused* — she tends to deal with hard cases by saying the revisionists confuse liability with culpability, and I want to know whether a blameless man doing something he wasn't permitted to do is really enough to get the equality she wants, or whether she has quietly switched to the weaker claim. One on liability specifically: does she think the soldier on the just side is permitted to kill the soldier on the other side even though that other soldier has done nothing to forfeit anything, and if so, what makes them equal rather than merely symmetric in the wrong way? One on whether the whole thing is a moral truth or a working convention — that sorting soldiers by the justice of their cause would be a humanitarian catastrophe, so we agree to treat them as equal, which is a different claim from their *being* equal. And one on the soldier who genuinely can't know, the conscript, the duped, the ones fed lies — whether that makes the morality of the act depend on how lucky you were about your information. That one bothers me, because in the lab we always said the person who signs off owns the result even when the protocol came from above, and I'd like to see what she does with that.

Then I'd like at least one on the awkward symmetry: the usual argument is that combatants are in the same boat, each a threat to the other, so we can't sort them — fine, but civilians are in a boat too, and nobody thinks that makes it open season on them, so what stops the argument from proving too much? One on whether a just side that starts deliberately hitting cities changes anything about the moral status of the men sent to stop it, and whether her equality claim survives that case. And one on what she would actually say to a soldier who comes to believe his war was unjust — does she tell him his kills were murders, or does she have a way of telling him they weren't, and is that way consistent with everything she said earlier? Please don't cite anything at me or review the literature, she'll know all of it anyway; I just need questions I can really ask, each with the follow-up ready.
