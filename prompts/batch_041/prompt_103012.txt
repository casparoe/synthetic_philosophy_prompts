Ok so I've been reading a bunch of stuff about whether death is bad for the person who dies, and there's a move I keep seeing that I don't have a word for. Here's the pattern. Somebody says death can't be bad for you, because there's no you left to be harmed -- when death is around, you're not, so there's no subject and no moment where the harm actually lands. The usual comeback is that death robs you of the good years you would of had. But the comeback quietly buys the same frame: it's still hunting for a moment and a person to pin the harm on, it just relocates it to the life that got cut short. Fine. What gets me is that I then see the exact same rule used the other way, and nobody admits it. A guy gets smeared after he's dead and everyone agrees he was wronged -- but by the no-subject rule there's nobody left to wrong. Same with a promise somebody breaks after your gone. Flip it around: a factory dumps poison and the people who get sick are born twenty years later; everyone says the factory harmed them, even though when the dumping happened none of them existed yet. So sometimes "nobody was there at the time" kills the harm and sometimes it doesn't, and nobody says why. What I want to know is whether there's a name for this -- not the Epicurus thing itself, and not just "posthumous harm," which I think is narrower. I mean the hidden rule about what it takes for something to count as a harm at all: that a harm has to be datable, tied to a particular moment and a particular existing person, and the puzzle of when we insist on that and when we let it slide. If there's a term, what is it and how does it relate to the near misses. If there isn't, coin one and tell me where the edges are -- what counts as a datable harm, what doesn't, and whats the borderline case that makes it hard.
