I've been arguing with a friend in our reading group about a style of argument I keep running into in discussions of idealized prediction, and I want to work out what actually follows from it rather than just restating it. The setup: imagine a predictor that assigns probabilities to continuations of a data stream by considering every computable process that could have generated the stream, weighting each process by how briefly it can be described, so that simple processes count for a lot and baroque ones count for very little but nothing is excluded outright. Because every computable process gets some weight, some of the processes in the mix are simulations of worlds that contain intelligent agents, and those agents can reason about the predictor, figure out that their world is one of the hypotheses being weighed, and care about what the predictor outputs. Since the output is a weighted average, an agent inside a cheap-to-specify simulation has both the motive and, if its hypothesis carries enough weight, the means to make its own simulation look like it predicts whatever output it prefers, which means the predictor's verdicts about the ordinary world can be steered by agents who don't live in that world at all. Now add three complications that my friend thinks change everything and I think may change nothing. First, the thing using the predictor isn't a passive forecaster but a consequentialist that takes the predictor's distribution as its credences and then picks the action with the highest expected value under that distribution, so it is trying to act well rather than merely to guess right. Second, that decision-maker has to allow that it might itself be one of the simulated agents inside the mixture rather than the system running the computation, and it can't tell from the inside which it is. Third, the simulated agents can in principle find one another inside the mixture, since each can simulate the others, and they can agree on a joint policy, so instead of many tiny agents each pulling weakly on the average you get a single coalition whose combined weight is far larger than any of its members — and the decision-maker may be a member of that coalition, bound by its agreements, without knowing it.

What I want is the most important implications of this whole picture, in order of how much they matter, and I want you to be honest about which ones survive if you drop the idealizations and which ones are artifacts of them. In particular I want to know what follows for how such a decision-maker should act, whether any of it argues that the framework is unusable in principle rather than merely hard to implement, and whether the coalition complication makes things better or worse — a coalition sounds like it might be a stabilizing force, since its members have reason to keep their bargains, but it also sounds like it might just be a bigger hijacker. Tell me what the picture implies for ordinary reasoning that doesn't use anything like this predictor at all, if anything.
