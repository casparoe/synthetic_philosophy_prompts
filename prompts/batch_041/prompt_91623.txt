I'm a features writer at a general-readership science-and-ideas magazine, at work on a long piece about the old puzzle of why the breakthrough to sustained industrial growth happened where and when it did — the question usually put as: why northwestern Europe, and England in particular, rather than the lower Yangtze or some other commercially advanced corner of Asia, when the two ends of the comparison looked broadly alike in the eighteenth century by most measures one cares to name, from commercialized agriculture and dense, sophisticated markets to elaborate credit instruments and growth that came from trade and specialization rather than from any change in how things were made. Two of the historians I've interviewed for the piece reached, independently, for the same methodological picture, and I want to build a section around it, since how this kind of history is actually argued is the part my editor thinks readers never get to see. Both of them said, in one form or another, that the comparison is a natural experiment: two cases alike in nearly everything, differing in the one factor each of them happens to favour, so that the case where the factor is present diverges and the case where it is absent does not. One, who puts his money on the accident of accessible coal, described it to me as a controlled trial in which history ran the treatment arm in one place and the control arm in another; the other, who cares more about institutions, was perfectly happy to use the same picture while filling in a different factor. I'd like you to audit the analogy rather than adjudicate between their theses.

Give me the correspondence table first — what plays the role of the treatment, the control, the randomization, the outcome measure fixed in advance, and the sample size — and then tell me where the mapping fails, sorting the failures into those that actually matter for the conclusion my sources want to draw and those that are merely decorative. I'm especially interested in three things. First, whether the two cases can be treated as independent observations at all, given how much of the eighteenth-century European and Asian economies were linked through trade, silver flows, and the diffusion of crops and techniques, so that the control arm was quietly changing too. Second, what happens when nobody can say in advance what the unit of analysis is — a nation, a region, a delta, a market town, an empire — and when the outcome being explained is defined by the fact that one side won, which makes the endpoint of the trial a piece of hindsight rather than a measurement. Third, whether the picture quietly forces the question into a single fork in the road, when the divergence may have been a difference in slope accumulating over a century rather than a switch thrown at a moment — and whether it smuggles in an assumption that causes come single and separable, and that the two arms of the comparison are symmetric, when a contrastive why-question of the form why this and not that is notoriously lopsided, so that one could ask the mirror-image question and get a different answer with equal right.

Finish by telling me whether the conclusion my sources actually need survives: not that coal, or institutions, or anything else is the cause, but the weaker claim that the search for a single decisive cause is misguided and that we should expect a conjunctural story in which several ordinary conditions had to line up. Is that claim supported by the comparison, or was the natural-experiment picture carrying it the whole way, such that once the disanalogies are on the table the weaker claim also falls and something else has to do the work? And if there's a better comparison available for the piece — a race, a phase transition, a path-dependent walk, an avalanche that needs both a slope and a trigger — say which one you'd use, what it would buy a reader, and what it would cost.
