For the past two years I have served on the board of a small food cooperative — roughly forty member households, each holding a share and a vote on major policies — and we are now deadlocked over a proposal that has come to be known, half-jokingly, as the Well Plan. The co-op's shared well sits under the edge of a single member's plot, and bringing it up to code would require that household to surrender perhaps a third of its garden and to put up with construction noise and truck traffic for about a year. In return, the rest of us would gain reliable water and avoid an assessment that would run each household a few hundred dollars. On any straightforward accounting the aggregate benefit to the other thirty-nine households plainly exceeds what the one household gives up; the figures are not close, and no one disputes them. A board member whose judgment I generally trust keeps framing the matter this way: if all of this were happening to one person — a year of inconvenience now, in exchange for a lifetime of good water — we would not hesitate for a moment, and the co-op is simply a larger version of that single person. I cannot articulate what troubles me about the comparison, though something does. My instinct is that it matters that the cost falls entirely on one household while the benefit is spread across all the others, and that there is no one who will "experience" the group's gain or the group's loss as a group. But I cannot tell whether that instinct registers a genuine moral insight or merely a squeamishness about imposing on a neighbor — especially since the household in question is one of our better-off ones and has said, without much enthusiasm, that it would go along if the board decided.

What I would like from you is not a verdict so much as the underlying structure. Help me get clear on what precisely is supposed to be different between a single person trading a bad year for a good lifetime and a group in which one party has the bad year so that the others may have the good ones: is that difference real and morally load-bearing, or is it an artifact of how we happen to picture the two cases? I am especially puzzled by a related case that seems to cut against my instinct — suppose it is one and the same person throughout, who endures the year of inconvenience first and is herself the one who benefits afterward; that strikes me as obviously unobjectionable, so what would it even mean to insist that the group case is different in kind rather than merely in complexity? If you think my instinct does point at something real, I would also want to know what it implies for the Well Plan itself: is there a version of the proposal — compensating the household, rotating or spreading the burden, requiring its genuine consent, or abandoning the plan altogether — that answers the worry, or does the worry cut against the whole manner in which we have been comparing costs and benefits? Set out the precise complaint as best you can, say whether it survives scrutiny and in what form, and tell me what it would and would not rule out.
