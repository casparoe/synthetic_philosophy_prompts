Write a client-facing white paper of about 800 words for a fictional analytics consultancy, whose name you should invent, built around a service the firm calls 'level selection': the business of deciding at which level of aggregation a client's data should be presented. The underlying phenomenon is the one known as Simpson's paradox, in which an association between two variables reverses direction when the data are pooled or split by a third. The consultancy's clients are the sorts of institutions that arrive with awkward numbers: a hospital trust whose treatment looks worse overall yet better in every severity band; a university whose admissions figures reverse once they are broken down by department; an employer defending an equal-pay claim. Choose one such client and build the paper around it.

The satire should be aimed at the practice rather than at arithmetic: at the habit of selecting the level of analysis that suits the conclusion one wants, and at the further habit of treating a reversal as though it refuted the pooled figure rather than redescribed it. A reader who has never heard of the paradox should be able to follow the piece; a statistician should come away satisfied that the author understands it exactly. Accordingly:

- The paper must contain a worked example with actual, internally consistent numbers that genuinely reverse. Give the table. If you can make the reversal occur at a second level as well, so that the sign flips once between the pooled figures and the departmental ones and again between the departmental and some finer breakdown, do so; that is the part I most want to see.
- Hold the voice flat and confident throughout, in the register of a consultancy that is being paid to be helpful. Never signal that the advice is dubious; never wink at the reader. The comedy should come from recognition rather than from the author's nudging.
- Get the underlying philosophy right, and show it rather than assert it: that the reversal is not an error but a genuine property of the data; that the choice of level is a causal assumption in disguise, turning on whether the stratifying variable is a confounder, a mediator, or something else; that the pooled and the split figures can both be correct answers to different questions; and that what is objectionable is not the partition itself but the post hoc selection of it.
- Include a short 'Ethics Appendix' in which the firm answers the charge of cherry-picking. The answer should sound reasonable and should not be.
- Include three or four numbered recommendations phrased as advice to the client, at least one of which would be defensible if it were not sitting alongside the others.
- Invent everything: the firm, the client, the dataset, any quotations. Do not refer to real studies or real institutions, and do not quote or cite any published source.
- Give the piece a title and headings. About 800 words, in British English.
