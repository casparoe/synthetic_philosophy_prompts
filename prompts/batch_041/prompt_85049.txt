I'm defending a thesis chapter at a political theory workshop in three weeks and I need a Q&A prep sheet. The room is not my crowd: two civic republicans who work on non-domination and think of membership in a self-governing body as the thing that protects you from arbitrary power, a couple of people who've spent years defending the all-subjected principle against the all-affected interests principle, one EU constitutional law person who cares about citizenship doctrine and whether any of this is implementable, and one comparative politics person who thinks the whole boundary problem is philosophy fiat and that real demos boundaries get settled by history and violence. Nobody there needs me to explain what the boundary problem is; I'm the only one who works on it.

My claim is that the demos for any given decision should be set by a rebuttable presumption of inclusion. Start with everyone causally affected by the decision. The currently enfranchised may narrow that set only if they can pass what I'm calling a decision-integrity test -- roughly, that including the excluded group would make the procedure unable to reach a stable outcome, or would let one group hold the rest hostage. Burden of proof sits on exclusion, and every exclusion carries a sunset clause: it has to be re-justified each cycle by whoever wants to keep it. I argue this beats all-affected (indeterminate scope, recursive, ends up giving you the whole world for any decision) and beats all-subjected (under-inclusive once you're dealing with externalities, climate, commuters, downstream users). And I've got a bootstrap answer to the self-application problem: the rule can't be adopted by the demos it defines, so legitimacy has to come from revisability rather than from origin, and the starting point is just whatever demos you inherited historically.

So: what will I actually get from this room? Put the objections in their vocabularies, not mine -- the republicans will say it differently than the all-subjected people will. Order them by how likely they are times how much damage they do, give me a two-minute reply to each, and flag where the reply is soft so I don't walk into it unprepared. Tell me what to concede up front and what to hold. Include the question I'm hoping nobody asks.

And please dont do the thing where you present three possible readings and let me choose. Take a position: tell me straight which objection actually sinks the paper, and give me your verdict on whether the bootstrap move works or whether my rebuttable presumption is just inherited power wearing a procedural hat. If you think the sunset clause is a fig leaf, say so and say why.
