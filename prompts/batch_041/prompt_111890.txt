I've been trying to work out what my country's chemical and drug regulators actually believe, because the pattern doesn't add up to anything I can name.

Start with a new molecule. Say a company wants to sell a new pesticide, a new food additive, a new plasticiser. The default answer is no. They have to hand over the studies, and if the studies don't cover something, the gap counts against them -- the regulator's line is basically that the absence of evidence of harm isn't itself evidence that there's no harm. Some of these rules don't even look at exposure. If the stuff is the kind of thing that can cause cancer, that's enough to sink it, even if the dose anyone would ever get is minuscule; it's banned for what it is rather than for what it does.

Now take the same agency and ask it to pull something off the shelf that's been sold for thirty years. Suddenly the burden is on the regulator. Show me the harm, in humans, at real exposure levels, with the confounders controlled. If the evidence is a mess, the product stays. That same absence-of-evidence point never gets used in this direction.

Third thing. A new drug needs a decade of trials before anyone can take it. But a surgeon can invent a new operation and start doing it next week, and there's no approval process at all -- the profession just polices itself afterwards, and if it goes wrong the defence is that it was the state of the art. Same with a hospital quietly switching on an internal AI triage tool. No pre-market gate for any of it.

Fourth. When the agency does act, the numbers it acts on are weird. A theoretical one-in-a-million lifetime cancer risk from a new additive is treated as a real reason to ban it. But a workplace exposure that measurably kills a few hundred identifiable people a year gets told the epidemiological evidence is contested, and gets another decade of study.

So heres what I want. Not what the guidance documents say they're committed to -- I want the view you'd have to hold for all four of those to hang together. What does the pattern presuppose about what a risk is, about who the default protects, and about the difference between introducing a hazard and tolerating one? I suspect there's a hidden premise about the status quo being the safe place, and if that's right I want to know exactly where its doing work and where the practice could get along fine without it. Which parts of this are strictly required by the underlying view, and which are just institutional habit? And is it even self-consistent -- because acting on the hazard rather than the exposure in one case, and needing measured human harm in another, can't both be the general rule, so is there a principled line between them or is it just two different agencies' cultures colliding inside one building? And if someone replaced the whole thing with a framework that treats inaction as a choice with its own body count, what would actually have to change on the ground?
