I teach a graduate seminar on political trust, and I am writing a paper that turns on a distinction I would rather test against a serious opponent than against my own notes. The distinction is this. When I rely on an institution or an official, three quite different things may be going on. I may merely predict: I expect you to behave in a certain way and attach no normative weight to the expectation, so your failure is a disappointment and nothing more. I may trust: I expect you to act well and I hold you to it, so your failure is a betrayal and I retain standing to complain, to demand an accounting, and to withdraw. Or I may defer: I go along with you without holding you to anything, so your failure is simply bad luck that I had no standing to protest. My suspicion — and it is only a suspicion — is that the calm we admire in high-trust societies is frequently the third of these wearing the name of the second, and that what separates trust from deference is precisely whether the relying party keeps standing.

I want you to play the committed defender of the high-trust attitude: the position that the Scandinavian disposition is not merely the by-product of small, homogeneous, prosperous states but is the more reasonable attitude, and that American suspicion, however bracing, is the worse one. Give the character real resources. The advocate may concede that trust can be betrayed, that a trusting public can be complacent, and that not every suspicion is unreasonable; but they must not concede that the high-trust attitude collapses into deference, nor that suspicion is on balance the more reasonable stance. Let them argue with mechanisms rather than anecdotes — for instance, that suspicion is self-sealing, that surveillance selects for the sort of people willing to be surveilled and crowds out the intrinsic motives of those who serve, that accountability machinery needs a baseline of trust in order to be used rather than weaponized, and that suspicion is more often a symptom of untrustworthy institutions than a cause of trustworthy ones. They should also meet the deference charge head-on, by asking what standing actually consists in and whether the trusting citizen really lacks it.

Constraints: stay in character until I tell you to stop; write one or two substantial paragraphs per turn, in a dry, courteous, unhurried voice, willing to grant a point and then turn it against me; do not step outside the role to give me a balanced survey; do not cite authors, studies, or statistics — I want the argument, not the literature; and if you need a fact about how either system works, ask me for it rather than assuming it.

Here is my opening, in the voice of the suspicion advocate: your calm citizen, I grant, sleeps better. But look at what the calm actually is. If I refrain from checking you because it has never occurred to me to check, and if I have neither the habit nor the appetite for it, then I am not trusting you — I am deferring to you, and I am calling it trust because that sounds warmer. Suspicion is what keeps the citizen a party to the relation rather than a passenger in it: it is the request that will not be dropped, the audit, the reporter who keeps digging. So the American attitude, sour as it is, preserves the very thing you say you prize, a citizen with standing, while your Scandinavian is comfortable and has surrendered the standing. Which of us is really trusting, and which of us has simply stopped looking?
