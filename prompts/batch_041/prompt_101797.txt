I am teaching a seminar that pairs Woolf's Orlando with contemporary work on the metaphysics of social kinds, and I keep colliding with a difficulty I would like you to help me resolve. The novel's central conceit is that Orlando, having lived some thirty years as an Elizabethan nobleman, wakes in Constantinople as a woman and remains, on the biographer's own insistence, the same person: same memories, same unfinished poem, same oak tree, same estate, same handwriting; what changes is her future, and it changes in ways she cannot govern. My students reach immediately for the money analogy. Sex, they say, is like currency — a status conferred by collective acceptance, so that the very same stuff is legal tender in one economy and worthless in the next, a gentleman in one drawing room and a woman in another, with nothing intrinsic altered. I want you to take that analogy seriously and then tell me how far the novel actually sustains it. Bring in the distinction between kinds that are socially constructed because the relevant property is conferred by collective recognition — the Searle-style formula on which a slip of paper counts as money only because a community accepts that it does, and the conferralist view that social properties are bestowed by others' attitudes rather than possessed intrinsically — and kinds that are socially constructed because membership causally shapes the people who occupy them, the way being raised as a woman produces the disciplined, scented, obedient creature the biographer describes, an hour at the hairdressing and an hour at the looking-glass, until Orlando reflects on the voyage home that she will now have to pay in her own person for the desires she indulged as a young man. Money, I take it, is the pure case of the first; gender, on most accounts now on offer, is some unstable mixture of the two; and my question is which mixture the novel dramatizes, and whether Woolf saw the difference more clearly than the philosophers have.

Take the scenes that pull in opposite directions. In one direction: the lawsuits over Orlando's estate that the biographer keeps promising to settle and never does, in which her sex keeps surfacing as a legal problem rather than a personal one; the Archduchess who turns out to be an Archduke, so that the kind ascription was doing all the work and the body underneath was always ambiguous; the gipsy chapters, where an English title and an English estate are simply not currency and no one is impressed by the deed; and the sailors and landladies and shopkeepers who read the same body as a woman's and adjust their conduct accordingly. In the other direction: the novel refuses to let the change be a mere status swap with no remainder, since something is unmistakably lost and something acquired — chastity is their jewel, their centrepiece, which they run mad to protect, and die when ravished of — and the biographer's insistence that identity survives intact sits awkwardly beside the centuries of discipline that follow it. And then there is the spirit of the age, which is not a body of collective belief Orlando could in principle opt out of but an enforcer that arrives with the nineteenth century like weather and makes her put on a ring. I would like a reading that is faithful in both directions: it should say where the money analogy illuminates the novel and where it distorts it, and it should not distort the philosophy to fit the fiction. Which scenes would you assign to a seminar of graduate students who have read Haslanger and Hacking but not Woolf? Does Orlando vindicate the thought that gender is a conferred status rather than a trait, or does it cheat — because what makes a conferred status stick to a body is precisely being raised inside it from birth, which Orlando never was? And if the novel is right that her sex change altered her future while leaving her identity untouched, what does that imply about whether gender and money are constructed in the same way, or whether the analogy to currency is a seductive mistake that a decent metaphysics of social kinds should resist? End by telling me what to say to the student who insists it is all just money, rather than hedging. Continuous prose, please — no headings, no bullet points, no bold, no tables; write it the way you would write a long letter to a colleague.
