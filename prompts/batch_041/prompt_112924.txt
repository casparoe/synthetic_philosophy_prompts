I am the instructor for a second-year undergraduate course in computability and its philosophy, and I am marking the final examination. One of the questions, and one student's answer to it, appear below. Please give me a mark out of 20 and — more useful to me than the number — the comments I will return with the script: a point-by-point assessment of what the answer gets right, what it gets wrong, and, for each thing it gets wrong, exactly which move fails and why. Where the student has asserted something that is a defensible position rather than an error, say so and keep it separate from the genuine errors; I do not want to penalise a student for taking a minority line, but the comments must land on the steps that do not hold up. Do not reward or penalise the prose, and do not give credit for material that merely sounds sophisticated. Note also any place where the answer is internally inconsistent.

The question is worth 20 marks, divided 8 / 7 / 5 across parts (a), (b) and (c). Put the mark first, then the comments keyed to each part.

Question as set:

(a) Distinguish two claims that are frequently run together.

(M): every function computable by an effective procedure, in the ordinary informal sense of that phrase, is computable by a Turing machine.

(P): every function computable by some physically realisable process is computable by a Turing machine.

For each of (M) and (P), say what kind of claim it is — a definition, a theorem, an empirical hypothesis, or something else — what sort of evidence bears on it, and what would count as refuting it.

(b) Assess the following argument. The Turing machine, the lambda calculus, and the class of general recursive functions were devised independently of one another, and they turned out to capture exactly the same class of functions. Moreover, any device assembled from finitely many kinds of components, each occupying one of finitely many states, and changing state in accordance with rules that depend only on the states of components in its immediate neighbourhood, can be simulated step by step by a Turing machine. So the claim that computability is Turing computability is as securely established as a theorem, and only a definitional scruple stands between us and calling it proved.

(c) A researcher proposes a machine that performs its first operation in half a second, its second in a quarter of a second, its third in an eighth of a second, and so on, so that infinitely many operations are completed within the single second that follows the moment it is switched on. By observing whether a given program has halted before that second has elapsed, the machine returns answers to questions that no Turing machine can answer. Does this refute (M), or (P), or neither? Justify your answer.

Student's answer:

Both claims concern the extent of a notion of computability, but they are claims of different kinds, and the difference matters.

(M) relates an informal notion to a formal one. It cannot be a theorem, because a theorem is proved from definitions and axioms, and "effective procedure" is not a defined term; there is nothing from which the equivalence could be derived. Nor, though, is (M) a bare definition, because the informal notion had a life of its own before it was formalised: people had a tolerably firm grip on what it is to follow a rule mechanically, and any proposed formalisation is answerable to that grip. So (M) is best understood as an explication — a precise replacement for a vague notion, answerable to the vague notion it replaces. The evidence for it is the convergence of the independent formalisms: several different attempts to make "effectively computable" precise, arrived at from different directions, all capture the same class of functions. That is not a proof, since it shows only that these formalisms agree with one another, not that they exhaust the informal notion; but it is strong inductive evidence, and no counterexample has ever survived scrutiny.

(P) is a different sort of claim. It is about the physical world, and is therefore empirical. It asserts that nothing in nature will let you compute a function that is not Turing computable. It is refutable in principle: exhibit a physical device that reliably returns the values of a function that no Turing machine computes, and (P) is false. It cannot be refuted by argument.

Since the formalisms agree, the thesis is ultimately a definitional matter rather than a claim about the world; and this carries over to (P) as well. To call a physical process a computation is to say that it can be simulated by a Turing machine, so a non-Turing physical computation is a contradiction in terms rather than a discovery waiting to be made.

The argument in (b) is therefore essentially sound. The convergence of the independent formalisms is the strongest evidence we could have for (M), and the finiteness argument supplies the rest for (P): if every physical system is composed of finitely many kinds of parts, each in one of finitely many states, interacting only with its neighbours, then every physical process is a finite-state process and admits of step-by-step simulation by a Turing machine. That closes the gap between (M) and (P). There is no room in the physical world for a process that outruns Turing machines, because there is no room in the physical world for anything other than finitely many kinds of locally interacting parts.

The machine in (c) does refute the thesis, though not in the way one might expect. It is an effective procedure in the ordinary sense — a definite, rule-governed sequence of operations — that computes a function no Turing machine computes. So the informal notion of effective computability is broader than the formal one, and (M) is false as a statement about all effective procedures.
