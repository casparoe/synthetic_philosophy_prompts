I am a fifth-year PhD student in philosophy, and next week I am responsible for the seventy-five minute session of a cross-departmental reading group — linguists, cognitive scientists, an anthropologist, and two philosophers — on whether disputes in metaphysics can ever be merely verbal, with the metaontological debate over composite objects as the running case. I have read the relevant literature and do not need the idea explained to me; what I need is a plan for teaching it. Specifically: the order in which the components have to be introduced and, for each one, what it depends on and what it would be premature to say before it; the single example that should carry the session, together with an account of what that example must contain and what would disqualify it; the misconceptions this audience will arrive with or form along the way, and the precise point in the sequence at which each should be headed off; one closing question that would tell me whether the distinction has landed rather than merely been memorised; and an explicit list of what I should refuse to cover at this level, said out loud as a refusal, with a reason for each omission.

The components I am currently inclined to run through are these. First, the ordinary and easy distinction between disagreeing about the world and disagreeing about what a word means, of the kind you get with two people each convinced the other is misusing "bank". Second, the much harder claim that a dispute can be caused by language without being settled by consulting a dictionary — that is, that two people may agree on every fact and still disagree about what to say. Third, the metaontological case itself, where one party insists there are only particles arranged chairwise and the other that there are chairs. Fourth, the suggestion that "there is" might be understood differently in different frameworks, so that the two sides need not be contradicting each other at all (this is the idea I have seen labelled quantifier variance; I would rather you teach the content than the label). Fifth, the counter-thought that this is a way of ducking the question of what there really is rather than answering it. And sixth, the reflexive worry that diagnosing a dispute as verbal is itself a move within the dispute rather than a neutral description of it. Two complications are already in play, and I want them built into the plan rather than handled in passing. A linguist in the group has written ahead to say that "verbal" properly means "spoken", that the phrase is therefore a misnomer, and that we should be talking about disputes over words — a complaint which, if I am not careful, will consume the session, and which may itself be a verbal dispute about verbal disputes. And one of the philosophers has announced in advance that if a dispute is verbal then it is unimportant and nobody is wrong, which I take to be the conflation of at least two distinct claims, one about the source of the disagreement and one about its value or its resolution.

As for the carrying example, I keep reaching for the tomato — botanical fruit against culinary vegetable — or for the reclassification of Pluto, and my worry is that both are too easy, in that they teach the group that a verbal dispute is just an ambiguity to be cleared up by disambiguation, which is precisely not the lesson I want. Tell me whether either can be made to carry the session regardless, or whether the load has to go to the chair-and-particles case, and why not the others. I have drafted a check to use at the end: ask them to take a dispute of their own choosing and say, first, whether it would survive both parties agreeing to use a single stipulated term, and second, what either party would have learned had it dissolved. Tell me whether that check is sound, whether it will actually discriminate among the positions I listed above, and whether there is a better closing question. Finally, I intend to cut the formal apparatus of framework-relative quantification, the verificationist history behind the internal/external contrast, and the analytic/synthetic background, announcing each cut and its reason as I go; tell me if any of those three is load-bearing for the specific understanding I am after, and what I should say in its place if so.
