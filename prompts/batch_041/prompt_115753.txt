A colleague and I recently spent an evening trying to referee an argument we had both witnessed, and we got nowhere, so I would like an outside judgment on it.

The argument concerned a third person, whom I will call Theo, who spent an afternoon at a flea market. Theo arrived owning a banjo. He traded the banjo for a mandolin, paying the dealer $5 to close the swap; later he traded the mandolin for a fiddle, paying $5 again; and later still he traded the fiddle back for the banjo, paying $5 a third time. He went home with the banjo he had started with and $15 less. He was not deceived, was not drunk, and regrets none of it; he says he would do it again, because each trade was what he wanted at the moment he made it. His rankings were stable across the afternoon and appear to be complete: he prefers the mandolin to the banjo, the fiddle to the mandolin, and the banjo to the fiddle, and he ranked them that way both before and after.

Two friends of mine, Rosa and Idris, then argued about what, if anything, this shows. Reconstructing from memory:

(1) Rosa said it shows that Theo's preferences are incoherent, and that the proof does not rest on any single bad decision. At every moment, including the last, Theo's own ranking places the endpoint of the course he did not take (banjo plus $15) above the endpoint of the course he did take (banjo alone). A ranking that makes every step of a sequence look better than its alternative while making the sequence as a whole worse than an available alternative is being walked in a circle by its own content, and that is what a money pump demonstrates. She added that no informed or clever adversary is needed: a vending machine with a posted rate of $5 per swap would extract the same $15, and would keep extracting it indefinitely, knowing nothing about Theo except what his own trades revealed.

(2) Idris said this conflates two questions. Whether a choice is rational is a question about the chooser's options, preferences, and information at the moment of choice, and by that standard each of Theo's three trades was strictly better than its alternative, hence rational. What Rosa has shown, he said, is that Theo's preferences are defective, not that Theo chose irrationally, and these are different claims. He compared Theo to a weak chess player: losing to a stronger opponent is not irrationality, and being exploitable by someone who can read your preferences is not irrationality either.

(3) Rosa replied that the chess analogy fails in a way that matters. The chess player's defeat is defined by a standard external to him, namely the rules and the opponent's skill, whereas Theo's loss is defined entirely by his own ranking. The fact that he would circle the machine forever, paying at each turn for a change he then wants, is not a fact about the machine. She also noted that his preferences were stable, so the usual excuse, that he simply wanted different things at different times, is unavailable.

(4) Idris granted the stability point but said it proves less than Rosa thinks. What normally guarantees that locally best choices add up to a globally best plan is the transitivity of preference, so the pump shows at most that transitivity is needed for coherence, which is a claim about the shape of the ranking rather than about the rationality of Theo's conduct. He added that every remedy Rosa might propose (declining the first trade, refusing the third, signing a commitment device) requires Theo to act, at some moment, against what he then most prefers, and that a person who holds that each moment's self should do as it then pleases is not making an elementary error but defending a substantive view about diachronic rationality. The pump, he said, is therefore not the neutral test it pretends to be.

(5) Rosa answered that this makes the remedy sound worse than it is. If avoiding a pointless $15 loss requires Theo to bind himself in advance, that is a defect in Theo, not in the argument. And if his view really is that each moment's self is sovereign, then he has agreed to be treated as a succession of strangers, and cannot complain when one of those strangers profits at his expense.

(6) Idris said the last point cuts both ways. If Theo is a succession of time-slices, then the dealer is not Theo profiting at Theo's expense but one person profiting from another's preferences, which is ordinary market behavior and not a failure of rationality in anyone. Rosa's insistence on treating Theo as a single agent across time is exactly the assumption that needs defending, he said, rather than a premise she may help herself to.

Please adjudicate move by move. For each of the six numbered moves, rate it from clearly mistaken through defensible to clearly right, and justify the rating briefly; distinguish any claim that is true but does not bear on the dispute from one that actually decides something. Then give an overall verdict: who is right about which specific point, whether the disagreement is ultimately about rationality at all or about something else, and what both Rosa and Idris are missing. Do not simply award the argument to one side.
