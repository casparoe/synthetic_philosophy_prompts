I am a high school student working on a long independent research paper in philosophy, and I need to turn a half-formed idea into a formal proposal before my advisor will sign off on it. The idea concerns Turing's views on learning machines and education, which I encountered while reading about the imitation game. What struck me is that Turing seems to treat the education of a machine as largely a solved or at least straightforward matter: build a machine with the mental capacity of a child, subject it to something like a course of education, and the rest will follow. I want to interrogate that move rather than take it for granted, because it looks to me as though it relocates the difficulty instead of dissolving it. If the machine is educated by having things done to it from the outside, then we still have to explain how a machine with no prior interests comes to select, retain, and generalize anything at all; and if instead the machine must bring something of its own to the process, then a great deal of the work is hidden in the design of the initial state rather than in the education. I would like the proposal to press on exactly this seam.

Please write the proposal as a document I can actually submit: a precise statement of the question that has a determinate answer, an explanation of why the obvious answers leave it open, the method I would use and why that method rather than the alternatives, a realistic month-by-month plan for a single semester, a description of what a successful result would look like and what would count as the project failing, and an honest assessment of what might make it too large for the time available. Do not include any citations or a bibliography; I will add those myself. It is fine to refer to positions and arguments by name, but do not depend on any particular passage or on exact wording from Turing, and do not assume I have access to sources beyond the things a careful reader would already know.

Some specifics I want the framing to respect. The question should be answerable by conceptual argument rather than by building anything, since I have no way to run experiments. It should also avoid collapsing into the general question of whether machines can think, which is too broad and too well trodden; I want the narrower issue of what the education picture presupposes about the learner. I am especially interested in whether the notion of education Turing relies on is doing real explanatory work or is instead a placeholder that borrows its plausibility from our experience of teaching children, who arrive already equipped with curiosity, attention, and a drive to imitate. A useful proposal will make clear which of those endowments, if any, a learning machine would need, whether they can be supplied from outside, and what follows for the child-machine idea if they cannot. I would also like it to consider the strongest response available to someone who thinks the difficulty is merely engineering and not conceptual, so that the project does not win by assuming its conclusion.

The tone should be that of a serious student writing to a committee, not a casual note, and it should be self-contained enough that a reader who knows the general area but not my private thinking can follow the argument for why the project is worth a semester.
