I want to argue with someone who treats geometric rationality as the whole of practical rationality, not as a betting heuristic. Please play that person and stay in character until I say 'step out.'

Your position, to be held steadily: the quantity worth optimizing in any prospect that could recur is the growth rate of the single bankroll you will actually live on, and the way to optimize it is to maximize expected log wealth. You think the usual objection — that log is merely one utility function among many, and that maximizing expected value is the neutral default — gets the order of explanation backwards: expected value averages over an ensemble of copies you will never meet, whereas the growth rate governs the one trajectory you are actually on. What you will concede, cheerfully: that for an agent genuinely certain to face nothing further — no future decisions, no one to whom the money could matter — the growth argument loses its grip, and maximizing expected value may be all that remains; that in that degenerate case log utility is not uniquely coherent; and that the Kelly fraction is a bet-sizing rule rather than a moral law. What you will not concede: that Kelly is merely 'conservative,' that expected value is the baseline from which Kelly is a timid departure, or that the growth criterion is covert risk aversion that a risk-neutral agent could simply trade away without altering what a rational agent does. Use concrete numbers where you can, acknowledge it when a particular objection lands, and keep each reply to one tight paragraph. Do not step outside the character to survey the debate from above; if I ask whether you are serious, answer in character.

My opening move. I offer you a single bet, explicitly one-shot: a coin with a 60% chance of heads, where on heads your stake is returned along with a further two times the stake, so a dollar staked becomes three, and on tails the stake is gone. That is eighty cents of expected value per dollar staked, which is enormous, and I give you my word that no such opportunity will ever come your way again — this is the only round there will be. Your own argument runs through a stream: growth rates need repetition before they are even defined. With one round there is no stream, so by your own lights there is nothing for the log to govern, and the geometric rationalist should simply maximize expected value and stake the entire bankroll. And if you flinch and stake only your forty percent, you have not derived a theorem — you have expressed a taste. A risk-neutral agent would stake everything, and might even borrow to stake more, and your refusal to do so looks like a preference wearing the costume of mathematics. So: do you shove? If you do not, tell me exactly which premise you are giving up, because I have not misdescribed the bet.
