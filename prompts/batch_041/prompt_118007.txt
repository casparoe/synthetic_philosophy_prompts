I sit on a committee that approves flood-defence schemes, and every report we see rests on a short list of assumptions — rainfall projections, soil permeability, the continued maintenance of upstream barriers. I would sign off on each of those assumptions on its own. Together they are meant to support a conclusion about how a one-in-two-hundred-year event will behave, and I have begun to notice that the same arithmetic gets used on whichever side of the argument suits. When I defend a scheme I am told that five well-supported assumptions do not amount to a well-supported conclusion, since any one of them may fail; when I attack one I am told that five doubts do not amount to a doubt about the conclusion, since no single assumption is actually in doubt. I know the two paradoxes — the one about lottery tickets and the one about an author who believes each of her claims and also believes that at least one of them is false — and this has the same shape. But in my case the assumptions are not independent tickets drawn from an urn, and it is not my own book: the same evidence bears on several of them at once, and I cannot tell whether that makes the uncertainty compound or whether it cancels.

Is there an established name for the underlying phenomenon, and if there is not, is it honest to coin one? I am not after a bare label. I want to know what the phenomenon is and what it is not, whether my case is an instance of the lottery paradox, of the preface paradox, of both, or of neither, and what exactly would have to change for it to fall on the other side of that line.

Give me your answer as a JSON object with these fields: `established_term` (the established name, or null if there is none); `nearest_concepts` (an array of objects, each with `name`, `what_it_gets_right`, and `where_my_case_diverges`); `closest_match` (the name of the concept that fits best, with one sentence saying why); `verdict` (one of "lottery paradox", "preface paradox", "both", "neither"); `definition` (if you coin a term, a definition precise enough to rule out the nearest concepts, and if you do not, the sharpest available statement of the phenomenon); `boundary_cases` (an array of two or three cases, each with a note on which side of the line it falls); and `crucial_variable` (the feature of my case that decides whether the individual uncertainties compound into a single uncertainty about the conclusion).
