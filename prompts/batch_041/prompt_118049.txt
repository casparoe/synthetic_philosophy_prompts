Eight months ago I was diagnosed with a slowly progressive condition. Nothing dramatic happens at any single moment; the prospect is that each year I can do slightly less than the year before, indefinitely, until I am by any ordinary standard dramatically worse off than I am now. I had braced for grief. What has kept me awake instead is a piece of reasoning I put together out of a book on value theory I read years ago, which appears to show that the decline I am facing is not a difference in value at all.

(1) Any two states of a life are comparable: either one is better than the other or they are equally good. There is no third possibility of their being on a par or incomparable.
(2) A difference that cannot be detected in a single comparison is not a difference in value: if two states cannot be told apart in value when compared head to head, then neither is better than the other.
(3) Take a sequence S0 (me now) through S1000 (me, much worse off), built so that each step from Si to Si+1 is too small to be detected in a single comparison.
(4) By (2) and (3), for every i, Si is not better than Si+1 and Si+1 is not better than Si.
(5) By (1) and (4), for every i, Si and Si+1 are equally good.
(6) Equally good is transitive: if X is equally good as Y and Y is equally good as Z, then X is equally good as Z.
(7) So S0 and S1000 are equally good.
(8) But S1000 is plainly much worse than S0.
(9) One of (1) through (8) is therefore false.

If (7) stands it generalizes: any two states of any life come out equally good, and no choice can be better or worse than its alternative. That is more than I can accept; it would make a mockery of the plain thought that I am losing something. So something above has to go. My difficulty is that every exit looks expensive, and the two people I have tried this on disagree about which is cheapest. One of them teaches decision theory and insists that (6) cannot be the culprit, because transitivity is not an optional extra but part of what a value relation is, so a relation that fails it is not a betterness relation at all; he would have me drop (1) instead and say that adjacent states are neither better nor worse nor equal, a verdict I cannot so much as state without the very comparison I am trying to make. The other says this is only the sorites paradox in disguise and that the usual treatment of vagueness disposes of it, but I cannot see that the trouble lies in a predicate being vague; it seems to lie in the relation itself, and in whether small differences may be summed.

I would rather keep (2) and (8) than give up either, since (2) is what makes the puzzle bite and (8) is simply true of my prognosis. So tell me which line to reject, why it looked unobjectionable, and what I lose by rejecting it. If the answer is (6), I want to know what is left of practical reasoning without transitivity, and whether anything but a technical trick distinguishes the resulting view from nihilism. If the answer is (1), I want to know what the third possibility would amount to and how one is to choose between things that are neither better nor worse nor equal. Price the options for me.
