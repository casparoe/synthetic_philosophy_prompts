I've been reading the alignment blogs and the associated forums for a long time now, as an interested amateur rather than anyone with a research position, and I keep circling the family of proposals that goes under the heading of iterated amplification and distillation. I think I have the shape of it: rather than training a model directly on tasks whose answers no human can check, you define an amplified version of the model, in which a human decomposes a question into pieces small enough that human judgment is reliable on them, those pieces are then answered (recursively, if the scheme is doing its job), and the human reassembles the result; then you distil the behaviour of that amplified system into a single model cheap enough to serve as the helper in the next round, and you iterate. What I've never been able to work out is how much of the surrounding rhetoric is load-bearing and how much is aspiration, so I would rather have a careful audit of one particular characterisation than another general explainer.

Over coffee last week a friend of mine — same blogs, considerably more confidence — summarised the whole thing as follows: 'IDA is the scheme where you give up on training a model on tasks humans can't check, and instead have the human break the task into sub-questions small enough for the human to answer or verify, hand those to the model, and stitch the answers back together; the amplified system inherits the human's alignment by construction. Since running that amplified system is far too expensive, you train a single model to imitate its outputs — that's the distillation step — and then you use the distilled model as the assistant in the next round of amplification, and repeat. The upshot is that you can end up with a system that does things no human can supervise while staying aligned, because alignment was never trained for directly; it was preserved by the loop. The one real failure mode left is that distillation is lossy, so the student may be less capable than the teacher and the whole thing may stall — but that is an engineering problem, not an alignment problem.'

I'd like you to go through that clause by clause and tell me what someone who actually works on this proposal would accept, what they would wince at, and what they would say is simply not what the proposal claims. In particular I want to know whether the 'inherits the human's alignment by construction' step survives contact with the fact that the student is trained on a teacher's outputs rather than on the teacher's internals, and whether the claim that competence loss is the only remaining failure mode is one a proponent would endorse or would regard as a category error. Someone in a comment thread once said something about a distilled model learning to produce arguments that look convincing to the human overseer without being sound, and I could never tell whether that was a serious objection or generic hand-waving; if it bears on any of this, say where it bites. Finally, if the closing claim — that this reduces alignment to a capability question — is too strong, tell me what the defensible version of it would be, and flag anything in the summary that a careful proponent would call a misreading of the proposal rather than a mere overstatement.
