Ok so I'm editing a small anthology of fiction about decision theory and I need one short story on expected value fanaticism. Not the usual thing where a professor explains a thought experiment to a grad student — I want the fanaticism to be *in* the plot, so the reader feels the pull of it instead of being told about it.

Two things I want you to build in, because otherwise you'll just write the standard version everyone has seen a hundred times:

1) No infinities. Infinities are a cop-out — everyone already agrees infinite utilities break everything and you can wave them off as a maths artefact. I want the fanaticism to come out of plain finite numbers. So the story needs at least one bet where a very small chance of a very large but finite good beats a certainty of something that genuinely matters — and the reader should be able to do the arithmetic themselves and see that it really does beat it. No hand-waving.

2) My main character has a *probability floor*. She's not a sceptic about small probabilities in the usual sense. She thinks probability talk below some threshold isn't false, it's meaningless — her reason being that there aren't enough distinguishable states in her own future light cone for a difference that small to ever get cashed out, so a 1-in-10^80 chance isn't a long shot, it's a number with nothing behind it. That floor is what lets her sleep at night. The story is about the day the floor stops doing the job she needs it to do.

Some rules. Don't have anyone say 'expected value' or 'Pascal's mugging' out loud — I want it dramatized, not labeled. Don't end on a lecture or a tidy verdict; I want the reader to close it still unsure whether she was right to do what she did. The anti-fanatic side has to get its best argument in, not a strawman — 'you're insane' doesn't count, and neither does 'but what if everyone did that'. And the enormous good should be something she actually cares about, not just a big number on a screen.

Roughly 1,500-2,000 words. Concrete figures, one real decision, a real thing she gives up. It can be world-historic or it can be tiny and personal, your call, but I want it definately felt either way.
