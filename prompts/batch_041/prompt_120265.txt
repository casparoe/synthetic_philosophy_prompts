I cover science and ideas, and I've been asked to give the opening remarks at a three-day conference on citizens' assemblies — half the room are deliberative-democracy researchers, the other half are citizens who actually sat on an assembly and the civil servants who staffed it. I want the remarks to hang on the Condorcet jury theorem, but I'm not willing to pretend that the assumption doing all the work, the independence of individual errors, survives contact with the very thing we're here to discuss: deliberation is supposed to raise competence and is also, by design, a machine for correlating error, and once even a small shared bias is in play a large jury can converge on the wrong answer with something close to certainty. My remarks have to own that without arriving at the conclusion that the arithmetic was a fairy tale, and they have to land for the practitioners as much as for the theorists, so that the room leaves with a reason to keep going rather than a tutorial. Write the speech, roughly eight minutes, carried by one small invented scene from an assembly floor rather than by any citation. Continuous prose only: no headings, no bullet points, no bold, no tables, just the way one writes a letter.
