I teach a 4th-year seminar in moral and decision theory, 18 students, and the last 75 minutes of term are basically a free slot. I want to use it for the aggregation-of-tiny-harms problem -- the torture-vs-dust-specks thing, where a tiny bad thing times a big enough number is supposed to outweigh one terrible thing, and everyone's gut says no but the gut also says each extra tiny bad thing makes the world worse. I don't want to just talk about it, I want them to run into it with their hands.

So: design me a runnable classroom activity, something where they construct the problem themselves and only realise what they've walked into near the end. I'm thinking of some kind of commission or tribunal that has to make betterness judgements between outcomes, with the number of tiny harms escalating, and some physical or locked-in element so they can't quietly rewrite their earlier answers. I'm open to the exact mechanic -- a floor-line they have to physically arrange, a sealed ledger of commitments, majority verdicts per round, rotating roles, whatever works. Feel free to make it more clever than my sketch.

What I need in the deliverable:

- the full rules, the roles, the materials (assume a projector, a whiteboard, masking tape, and nothing else fancy), and a payoff or commitment scheme that creates real pressure without any actual money changing hands
- a minute-by-minute timeline for the 75 minutes
- the escalation schedule -- how many rounds, how the number of tiny harms grows, and where the pinch points are
- a debrief that maps specific moments in the activity onto the actual structure of the puzzle (ordering, monotonicity, the ceiling, the crossing), and that names the thing only after they've felt it
- what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how I should handle each failure
- and an honest note on what this activity can NOT reproduce -- some feature of the idea I'll have to just supply verbally, since the game can't manufacture it

One thing I care about: in the debrief notes, be careful to separate what's actually settled from what's genuinely contested among people who work on this. The logical squeeze (you can't have each tiny harm strictly adding weight and also a ceiling that no number of them ever crosses) is one thing; whether the right response is to accept aggregation, or to deny the harms add up at all, or to deny the outcomes are even comparable, is a live fight and I don't want it presented to my students as if everyone agrees. Flag the seams.
