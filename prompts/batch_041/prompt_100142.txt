I am a PhD student in philosophy writing a dissertation chapter on the combination problem, and I want a position I have been defending stress-tested rather than restated. The position is this. The combination problem for panpsychism is normally posed as a problem about parts and wholes: how do countless micro-experiences add up to one macro-experience? I think the framing itself is the mistake. On my account, micro-subjects never add up, because no aggregative operation is available to them; they combine by dissolution. Two micro-experiential fields combine into a single subject precisely when each comes to register the other as internal to itself rather than as an external boundary, that is, when the mutual externality that individuated them as two is no longer represented in either field. A macro-subject such as you or me is then what you get when dissolution runs to completion within some system and is blocked at that system's interface with the rest of the world, so that the boundary of a subject is simply wherever dissolution stops. I call this the dissolution account.

What I need from you is a map of what would count against it, sorted by how decisive each defeater would be. Please keep the following distinct. First, the internal objections: does the account even have a coherent notion of a micro-subject prior to dissolution, given that subjects are individuated by represented boundaries, and does it therefore presuppose the very unity it claims to explain? Second, the collapse worry: does dissolutionism quietly reduce to a rival, for instance to neutral monism, to a representationalist or illusionist treatment of the unity of consciousness, or to a priority-monist view on which the cosmos rather than the micro-subject is fundamental? Third, the comparative question: what does the account predict that the standard rival solutions do not, and are any of those predictions actually testable, or does the view merely relabel the explanandum? Fourth, the empirical constraints: which findings from split-brain research, binocular rivalry, anaesthesia, and dissociative phenomena would bear on it, and which of those would be genuinely telling rather than merely awkward.

Finally, tell me what a committed dissolutionist would concede as counting against the view versus what they would dismiss, whether the position has been insulated against refutation by its own qualifications, and whether that insulation is a defect or simply what a metaphysical position of this kind should expect. For my part, I would find it most damaging if you could show either that the account has no empirical content at all or that it cannot describe a case in which dissolution fails without borrowing the very notion of a unified subject it is meant to replace.
