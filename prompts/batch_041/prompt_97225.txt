I retired four years ago after 34 years as a field ecologist (restoration work, mostly grassland and wetland sites), and since then I've been reading my way through philosophy — epistemology and philosophy of science, mainly — which has made me alot more suspicious of tidy methodological rules than I used to be. I never took a statistics course beyond what the job forced on me. Anyways, a friend lent me one of those popular books about how statistics get abused, and the chapter on Simpson's paradox is bothering me. Here's the gist of what the author says, near enough word for word: "Simpson's paradox, which the statistician Edward Simpson discovered in 1951, is what happens when a pattern that holds inside every single subgroup of a dataset flips when you pool the subgroups together. The textbook case is the Berkeley admissions data from the seventies: overall, men were admitted to graduate school at a noticeably higher rate than women, which looked like plain discrimination, but as soon as you split the numbers by department the gap mostly evaporated, because women were applying to the departments that were hardest to get into. The same thing shows up in the kidney-stone data: one treatment beats the other for small stones, and also beats it for large stones, yet loses once you lump both sizes together. What's going on underneath is confounding — a lurking variable is tangled up with both the grouping and the outcome — and that's why you can never get causation out of observational data. The remedy is a rule statisticians have used for a century: adjust for the confounder. So the moral is that an aggregate number isn't merely incomplete, it's actively misleading: when the parts and the whole disagree, the parts are the truth and the whole is an artifact of how the data happened to be sliced up. Really it's just the ecological fallacy wearing the opposite coat, since both are cases of a group-level number lying about the individuals inside it." He then restates his rule about three more times, in bold: never report an aggregate when you can disaggregate, and when the two disagree, go with the disaggregated result.

I'm inclined to believe him, partly because of something that happened to me on the job. We trialed two seeding methods across twelve sites over three seasons — method A (seed plus a mulch mat) versus method B (seed only) — and when we pooled everything A came out clearly ahead, but when I broke it out site by site B was ahead at every single site, which I've never fully understood. My guess is that A got used mostly on the easy sites, the ones with decent soil and reliable rain, because that's where we had the budget to try the expensive thing, and B got the leftovers. So his rule feels right to me. But a younger colleague took the same data and split it by whether the site had burned in the second year — a variable we only recorded after the treatments had already been running for a year — and got a third answer, and now he's citing this book to argue we should report that version. That one smells wrong to me but I can't say why.

Could you go through the passage claim by claim and tell me what's straight, what's the sort of simplification a competent popularizer gets away with, and what a working statistician would wince at or flatly reject — and then tell me whether his bolded rule actually follows from whatever survives? And separately: what should I have told the agency about my two seeding methods, and was my colleague entitled to disaggregate by the burn variable? I don't want citations or further reading, just the audit.
