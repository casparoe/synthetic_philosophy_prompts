I have been trying to settle a narrow question — whether better than is transitive — by writing down my verdicts on a specific series of cases, and the exercise has left me with judgements that will not sit together. I would like you to tell me what principle, or small set of candidate principles, best captures them, what each candidate implies for cases I have not judged, and which of my verdicts the best candidate forces me to abandon.

The cases concern one patient with a fixed, stable condition, and a choice among treatments for it. Each treatment is described by two numbers: the additional months of life it is expected to afford, M, and the severity of its side effects, S, on a scale from 0 (none) to 10 (as bad as the underlying condition). I have been assuming that an extra month is worth more than a single point of side-effect severity, at least over the range below.

My verdicts are these.

1. (M = 7, S = 1) is better than (M = 6, S = 0). One more month is worth a single point of manageable side effects.
2. (M = 8, S = 2) is better than (M = 7, S = 1), and likewise at each step up the chain, through (M = 15, S = 9) better than (M = 14, S = 8), and (M = 16, S = 10) better than (M = 15, S = 9). If a month outweighs a point at the bottom of the scale, it appears to outweigh a point all the way up.
3. (M = 6, S = 0) is better than (M = 16, S = 10). Ten additional months do not justify side effects as bad as the disease itself.
4. (M = 6, S = 0) is better than (M = 11, S = 5), though I hold this less firmly than 1 and 3.

Each of 1 to 4 seems obviously right when I look at it on its own.

Three further things I believe, and hold as firmly as any of the above.

5. At least as good as is transitive. I cannot make sense of a cycle in it: if A is no worse than B and B is no worse than C, then A is no worse than C.
6. For any two of these treatments there is a fact of the matter about which is better; the comparison is not merely a report of my tastes.
7. When I run the same items in the opposite direction, from (M = 16, S = 10) down to (M = 6, S = 0), each successive item strikes me as better than the one before it, so that I end up judging (M = 6, S = 0) better than (M = 7, S = 1), which reverses verdict 1. This survives reflection; it is not a slip. My verdict on the two extreme items does not reverse in this way, however — it is the adjacent comparisons that flip with the direction of presentation.

Taken together, 1, 2, 3 and 5 cannot all be true, and 4 deepens the conflict, since the chain in 2 implies that (M = 11, S = 5) is better than (M = 6, S = 0). My tentative repair is to give up the flat exchange rate: perhaps a month outweighs a point of severity only while severity is low, and beyond some threshold each further month is worth steadily less, so that the later steps in the chain are not improvements at all. This would let me keep 5 and 6, at the price of abandoning verdict 2 for the later steps and perhaps verdict 1 as well.

What I want from you is the following. State the principle, or the competing principles, precisely enough that I could apply them to a new case without further help. For each clause, name the verdict above that motivates it. Tell me what your preferred principle implies for two cases I have not judged: (M = 9, S = 3) against (M = 6, S = 0) considered on their own, and (M = 7, S = 1) against (M = 6, S = 0) considered as a single link within the long chain rather than in isolation. And say honestly which of my seven verdicts it cannot accommodate. If the best you can do is a choice between a complex principle that saves every verdict and a simpler one that overturns a verdict, tell me which you would choose, and why.

Please write in British English.
