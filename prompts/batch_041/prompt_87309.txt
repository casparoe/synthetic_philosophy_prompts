I teach a seminar on classical Chinese thought and have been drafting a paper in which I defend what I take to be the strongest form of the butterfly-dream argument — deliberately not the Cartesian one. Zhuangzi's version, as I reconstruct it, needs no deceiving demon and is not after radical doubt for its own sake; it turns on the ordinary fact that we have nightly first-person experience of being unable to tell, from the inside, that we are dreaming, and it ends by asking whether the self now writing is any more securely placed than the butterfly was. The position I want you to examine is this: my present experience belongs to a kind that has, on countless occasions, been dream experience; I possess no non-circular test that separates the two from the inside; so I am not entitled to treat the manifest image of my present life as knowledge of what I am. Tell me what would count against it. I do not want the stock replies recited back to me — I have heard that dreaming is parasitic on waking, that the word for dreaming is one I learned while awake, and that the hypothesis is unverifiable — but rather a judgement about which of those stock replies are actually decisive and which are merely awkward, and above all which defeaters are specific to the Zhuangzian version rather than to the Cartesian one. I would like you to weigh, among other things: whether the empirical study of dreaming, including lucid dreaming, the ordinary reportability of dreams, and the characteristic cognitive failures of dream experience, bears on the argument at all or answers a different question; whether the passage's apparent interest in the mutual transformation of things, rather than in doubt, means my reading has quietly converted a metaphysical claim into an epistemological one, and what evidence would show that I have done so; and whether anything could in principle tell against the position, or whether it is insulated by its own modesty, since it claims only that I lack entitlement and not that I am now dreaming — and if it is insulated, whether that is a defect or precisely what a position of this shape should be. I will say in advance that what would move me most is a demonstration that my formulation equivocates between two senses of experience being of the same kind, and a clear statement of which sense the argument actually needs.

Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, indicate which ones I as a defender would be obliged to concede and which I would be within my rights to dismiss, and be candid about whether the argument survives as anything more substantial than a mood. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence and then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of my question.
