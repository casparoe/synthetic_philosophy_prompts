Suppose we grant two empirical premises. (1) Farmed animals in typical confinement systems have lives that are, on balance, bad for them. (2) Cultivating crops kills a very large number of wild animals — small mammals, birds, reptiles, invertebrates — through plowing, planting, harvesting, and pesticides. The best estimates of how many are killed per hectare are contested and range over orders of magnitude, but the numbers come out large on any estimate.

Now consider an argument that circulates in the animal welfare literature. A kilo of chicken is implicated in more crop production than a kilo of beef, because chickens eat harvested grain and soy, while cattle — especially grass-fed cattle — mostly eat forage from pasture that is never plowed, planted, or combined. If crop cultivation is where most of the collateral animal death happens, then the chicken eater is implicated in more wild animal deaths than the beef eater, and the case for a plant-based diet is not as clean as it looks.

Assess the argument. What would have to be true for it to go through? What is the strongest objection to it? Take the standpoint of a utilitarian who takes wild animal suffering seriously, and be specific about what the argument has to establish at each step.

heres what the student wrote:

The structure of the argument is fine in the abstract. It takes a per-hectare figure for wild animals killed by agriculture and converts it into a per-unit-of-food figure, and everything turns on that conversion. Two things have to be pinned down first. One is a common currency. Deaths are not obviously the right unit, since a death that cuts off a long life is a bigger loss than one that cuts off a short life, and the amount of suffering behind each death varies a lot across species, so the raw mortality numbers need some weighting before they can be compared. The other is the numbers themselves. My sense is that the estimates are noisy but that recent work is converging on something in the neighborhood of a dozen or so animals per hectare per year, which is at least the right order of magnitude for the argument to bite.

The load-bearing assumption is the pasture one, and I think it survives. Pasture is not cultivated, so the plowing, planting and harvesting that kill field animals do not happen there. Once you grant that the deaths are concentrated in harvested cropland, the chicken-versus-grass-fed-beef comparison is fairly mechanical and it comes out the way the argument says it does. You do have to set feedlot beef aside, since those cattle eat the same grain and soy the chicken does and are implicated in the same crop deaths. Grass-fed is the real case.

Where I would push back is on the suggestion that the argument undercuts plant-based eating. That looks like a non sequitur to me. The comparison is between chicken and beef. What vegans do is beside the point, because the question is what to eat instead of chicken, and the fact that a vegan diet involves less harvesting than a chicken diet does not change what happens when someone swaps chicken for beef.

One last thing. I would resist asking whether the field animals had lives worth living. Being killed is a harm in itself, regardless of the quality of the life that came before it, and building that question in would push us toward the odd conclusion that killing an animal is fine as long as its life was decent. The harm is the death. That is what gives the argument its force for a utilitarian. (The premise that farmed animals have lives that are on balance bad for them is doing a lot of work in the setup, and that is a claim about the quality of a life, not just about whether it ends.)
