I teach a graduate seminar in the philosophy of psychiatry — 14 students, one 90-minute session — and I want an in-class activity that makes them feel the force of Hacking's looping effects: the way classifying people can change the people classified, so that the evidence for the classification shifts under its own feet. The loop has to arise from what the students do, not from anything I announce; no lecture up front, and the term itself should surface only in the debrief. My sketch is a two-round simulation in which one group applies a short behavioral checklist to another, and those who receive a label then act differently because of it, so that the second round's data is already contaminated by the first round's categories — but rebuild the mechanic however you can make the loop tightest. Give me the rules, the materials (write out the checklist and the role cards), a minute-by-minute timeline, and a debrief that maps what happened onto the loop and onto the question of whether such effects undercut a realist view of psychiatric categories. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle it, and be candid about which feature of looping effects the activity cannot reproduce and that I will have to supply myself. In the debrief notes, keep what is settled about looping effects separate from what is genuinely contested among people who work on this, and do not let a contested reading pass as the consensus.
