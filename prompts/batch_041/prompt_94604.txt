I am a fifth-year PhD student in South Asian philosophy, and I have been asked to give the memorial address at a small gathering for my dissertation supervisor, who died this spring. She worked for thirty years on Nyāya epistemology, especially the theory of testimony and sentence understanding, and for the last eight of those years she taught philosophy in a state prison's college program. The room will be about half philosophers who know the Nyāya and Mīmāṃsā literature in detail, and half her students from the prison program together with her family; the second half will have no Sanskrit and no epistemology. She left instructions that a student, not a colleague, should speak, and that the address should not be a survey of her work.

I want an address of about eight minutes that is a genuine memorial and also does philosophical work, in the sense that one idea from her field governs the whole speech rather than being mentioned in it. The idea I want at the center is the Nyāya account of what makes a sentence intelligible. On that account, a string of words yields a unified cognition only when certain conditions are met: ākāṅkṣā, the mutual expectancy by which one word demands another to complete its sense; yogyatā, the fitness or compatibility of the meanings thus brought together; sannidhi, their proximity in time so that they are held together at all; and tātparya, the speaker's intention, which fixes which of the many possible connections was meant. A sentence, on this view, is not self-sufficient; the hearer has to supply what the words leave open. I want the address to treat a life the way Nyāya treats a sentence, as something that becomes intelligible only when the hearer brings expectancy, fitness, proximity, and an estimate of what the speaker meant, and I want the philosophical half of the room to hear that as a claim about understanding while the other half hears it as something true about her.

Two things I need handled rather than avoided. First, the account is a theory of cognition, not a consolation, and I do not want it softened into a metaphor about how we all complete one another; if there is a point where the analogy to a life stops being apt, the address should mark it. Second, Nyāya holds, against the Mīmāṃsā contention that validity is intrinsic to a cognition, that a cognition's validity is established by later confirmation rather than guaranteed from within, which sits awkwardly with a memorial, since a memorial is usually a claim that a life was already, intrinsically, worth something. I want the address to acknowledge that awkwardness and then decide, in the speech itself, what it can honestly claim on her behalf. Use the technical terms at most once each and only where the sentence carries a non-specialist through them; do not attribute to her any position I have not given you, do not quote anyone, and do not invent anecdotes. I will supply the biographical material, so write the address so that it holds together with the facts above and leaves room for me to insert the rest.
