Background so you know what you're working with: I'm a journalist, I cover science and ideas for a general-readership magazine, and once a year the small ideas festival we co-host sticks me with a "hands-on" slot instead of a panel. So I need a thing to actually run, not a talk to give, and I'm definately not going to do the math on stage myself.

The room: about 30 adults, 75 minutes, one session, no computers, four or five seperate tables of six or seven. Mixed crowd — a couple of physicists, one philosopher who'll be smug about it, and a lot of general readers whose last math class was at sixteen.

What I want them to feel in their hands: that pick one at random is not one instruction. It sounds like it names a single fact about the world, and it doesn't — it names a procedure, and different, equally innocent procedures give different long-run answers to the same question.

Here's the thing I want them to trip over. Circle drawn on the table, equilateral triangle inscribed in it, chord drawn at random: is the chord longer than a side of the triangle? Three devices, each of which a person would happily call a chord drawn at random:

- pick two points on the rim (dice, or two people each poking a finger at the rim blindfolded) and draw the chord between them — in the long run about 1/3 of chords come out long enough;
- pick a radius, then a point along that radius, and draw the chord perpendicular to the radius there — about 1/2;
- pick a point inside the disk and draw the chord that has that point as its midpoint — about 1/4.

And it isn't just three: you can build a device that lands on any number you like between 0 and 1, and I'd much rather they discover that than hear me say it. (There's also a fiddly thing where one of those procedures double-counts the diameters — I don't know if that's worth staging, your call.)

Then I want a second round that isn't about circles, so nobody files this under circles are weird. A factory makes cubes whose edges are somewhere between 1 and 3; what's the chance a cube is smaller than 2 on a side? Uniform in edge length gives 1/2, uniform in volume gives 7/26, uniform in face area gives 3/8.

Then the fix round, which is the part I care most about getting right. Somebody in the room will say: just pick the procedure that doesn't depend on irrelevant stuff. So have them test it — a device that can't know where the circle sits on the table, or how big it is, gets forced into one of those three answers, and it's the 1/2 one. That's Ed Jaynes's move from 1973 and it's contested; I want the debrief to be straight with them that it's a choice about which symmetries count as irrelevant, and that for some pick-a-random-X questions there provably is no such choice at all — something about cutting a ball into finitely many pieces and reassembling two balls out of it. I'd like the facilitator to be able to gesture at that without pretending we've demonstrated it.

What I need from you: a run-of-show with timings, a cheap materials list, the betting scheme with chips or tokens (no real money, but it still has to sting a little when you lose), word-for-word instruction cards for each table — worded so that every table's spec reads like an equally innocent way of saying pick a chord at random — the moment where the specs get swapped and read aloud, the debrief that maps each stage of the session onto the idea, an honest list of what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and what to do mid-run when it does, and a plain statement of which part of this an activity like this cannot reproduce and I'll have to say out loud myself. No reading list please — I need a session I can run on a Saturday.
