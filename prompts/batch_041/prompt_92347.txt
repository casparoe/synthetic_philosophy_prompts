I'm a freelance science-and-ideas journalist — long features, mostly for a couple of monthlies, plus the odd essay — and after fifteen years of writing other people's arguments I've got a shot at a six-month mid-career fellowship for hacks who want to do something more conceptually ambitious than a 4,000-word feature. The application wants a two-page research proposal and a short note on the deliverable; the fellowship pays for one substantial piece of work, and I'm thinking an 8,000-word essay for a serious-but-general readership, maybe with a shorter companion piece for a philosophy blog. My half-formed idea is about the utility monster, and what nags at me is that everyone who wheels it out — usually as a one-line refutation of total utilitarianism, delivered with a little smirk — skips the part that actually matters. I want to argue that the monster only bites if you accept a whole package of assumptions about welfare: that it comes in degrees comparable between people, that those comparisons can be summed, and that there's no ceiling on how much of it one being can have. Take away the ceiling and the monster can't swamp the rest of us; take away interpersonal comparability and the thing dissolves; keep all three and you've conceded something almost as contentious as the conclusion you were trying to refute. So the real question isn't "is the utility monster a good argument against utilitarianism" but "which of those assumptions is doing the work, and can any of them be defended independently of the conclusion?" And here's the bit I'm most attached to, possibly because I haven't read enough: I suspect the intuition people are actually having when they recoil from the monster isn't about aggregation at all. It's the thought that no single being's interests can be unboundedly weighty — which sounds like a claim about the structure of moral status, not about sums. If that's right, the monster argument is smuggling in a thesis about status and dressing it up as a counting problem. I know this is next door to the repugnant conclusion and the person-affecting debates, and I'm fairly sure there's a literature on bounded utility functions, but I couldn't tell you who wrote it or whether it says what I think it says, so please don't lean on me getting that right.

Turn this into the proposal. I want a precise statement of the question — one that has a determinate answer, not a shrug — then why the existing positions leave it open, then the method: what I'd actually do month by month, which variants of the argument I'd try to formalise (bounded versus unbounded welfare, ordinal versus cardinal comparison, diminishing marginal value, giving one being's interests lexical priority), which cases I'd test the variants against, and who I'd want to argue with. Then what a result would look like, what would count as my having failed, and an honest assessment of whether six months is enough or whether I'm quietly proposing a book. Don't give me a reading list or citations; I'll fill those in myself, and anyway I'd rather the proposal stand on the argument than on name-dropping. What I do want is for you to seperate what's actually settled among people who work on this from what's a live fight — if the boundedness point is standard and I've just not noticed, say so, and if my "it's really about moral status" hunch is the kind of thing that sounds clever at a dinner party and collapses under five minutes of scrutiny, tell me that too rather than building the proposal around it. I'd rather find out now.
