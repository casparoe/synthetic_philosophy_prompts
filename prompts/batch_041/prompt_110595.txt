I run a small reading group that sits awkwardly between the computer science department and the philosophy department — a few complexity theorists, a few people working on epistemology and the philosophy of mathematics — and we keep having the same argument in different vocabularies about what complexity results actually tell us about the world. The exchange always begins from ground everyone grants: that P, NP, BQP and the rest are precisely defined; that no polynomial-time algorithm for the NP-complete problems is known and that the smart money says none exists; that this is a robust mathematical fact rather than a fact about the current generation of hardware; and that the polynomial/not-polynomial line is drawn with full awareness that an algorithm running in n^1000 is "efficient" only by courtesy, and that the same problem can move across the line if you change how inputs are encoded. What we can never agree on is what follows. One of us thinks NP-hardness is a feature of the problems themselves, the way being even is a feature of the number 4, and that an intractable problem is intractable for God as much as for us; another thinks hardness attaches only to the pair (problem, model of computation), so that swapping in a quantum, analog, or biological device is a change of subject rather than a discovery; a third thinks the P-versus-NP framing is a formal proxy for a normative notion of "feasible" that has no exact extension, so the boundary sits where it is useful rather than where it is true. I would like a handout that turns this into a decision tree I can pass around, so that people can see where they actually part company instead of trading examples past one another.

Please write that tree. Begin from the shared commitment above — the mathematical results accepted, no reading of them yet assumed — and make every branch node a question a reader can answer without already knowing which leaf it leads to. I expect the nodes to be questions about where hardness lives (in the problem, in the pair, in our epistemic situation); whether "feasible" is descriptive or evaluative, and whether that makes the P/NP boundary a discovery or a stipulation; whether the constraints of complexity bind an ideal reasoner or only a finite one, and correspondingly whether "ideal rationality" is a coherent notion at all; whether relativization results — and the later barriers of the same shape, which show that entire families of proof technique cannot separate the classes — are evidence about what is true or only about what we can currently prove; whether the reliance on unproven hardness assumptions in cryptography turns those assumptions into empirical posits about nature, mathematical conjectures, or a kind of resource that is simply mined rather than believed; and whether any of the class distinctions carve nature at the joints or merely mark a useful partition of a continuum.

Aim for five or six branch nodes and between six and eight leaves. Each leaf should carry a name for the position, or be marked "unnamed" where the combination of answers is unoccupied, plus one sentence identifying the commitment that would be most awkward for that position to defend. Build the tree from the logical space first — enumerate the questions and the answer-combinations they permit, then locate the actual positions within that space — and where a combination is empty, say briefly whether the view is incoherent, merely unattractive, or simply unexplored. Where two leaves differ only in emphasis or in what they choose to stress, flag them as a pair the tree cannot genuinely separate rather than manufacturing a distinction to keep them apart; I would rather have seven honest leaves than ten with two indistinguishable pairs. No citations or reading lists, please: the handout has to stand on its own. Assume the reader knows the definitions of P, NP, BQP, uniformity, and polynomial-time reduction, and nothing at all about the philosophy of any of it.
