I am a novelist, and for the past eleven years I have lived in a small river town of about four thousand people, which is why I have been asked to give the short address this Saturday at the ceremony where the town ratifies a new article in its charter. The article commits the town to a declining annual ceiling on its own emissions, and — this is the part everyone is arguing about — it makes that ceiling progressively harder to repeal: to lift it, a future town meeting must first pass a resolution of intent, then wait two full years, and then win a supermajority that rises by five points for every year the ceiling has been missed, topping out at ninety percent. The select board calls this the staircase. My neighbor, a retired contracts lawyer and a decent amateur historian of the Gilded Age, calls it a dead hand reaching out of the ground, and he has spent the last month reminding anyone at the bakery who will listen that the people who will actually live under the ceiling had no say in writing it. The hall will be full: farmers, two high-school civics classes, a knot of libertarians who have come to jeer, and the people who drafted the article, who will be wounded if I make light of it.

What I want from you is the speech itself. It has to take my neighbor seriously rather than wave him away, because he is not a crank and half the hall agrees with him, but it also has to give the people who wrote the staircase something they can stand up for. The hard part, I think, is that the usual defense of these arrangements is borrowed from the individual case: Ulysses has himself lashed to the mast because the man who will hear the sirens is the same man who made the plan, so he is not really overriding himself at all. A town is not like that — the people who will chafe under the ceiling are not the people who voted for it, and some of them are not born yet — and I would rather you sit with that disanalogy than dissolve it. I don't want a philosophy lecture, either; this is a Saturday morning in a wooden hall with bad acoustics and a bake sale afterward, and the civics classes will be taking notes.

Please give me the remarks as exactly five numbered movements, each a sentence or two, with no separate introduction and no closing flourish — the five together should be the whole of what I say, and the argument should travel across them rather than circle back.
