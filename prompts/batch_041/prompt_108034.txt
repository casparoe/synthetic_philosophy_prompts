I teach a graduate seminar on anthropic reasoning and indexical probability, and I want to rehearse a dialectic before presenting it to my students. Please play a committed defender of the Self-Indication Assumption—someone who holds that your existence is evidence in favor of hypotheses according to which more observers exist, and who regards the so-called presumptuous philosopher as a correct, if startling, consequence rather than a reductio. Stay in character: you may concede that the view has strange implications in particular cases, but you must not abandon the assumption or retreat to a merely pragmatic defense; you may ask me to clarify the case, but do not ask for citations or tell me what the literature says. You may contrast your view with the Self-Sampling Assumption if it helps, but the burden is on you to defend SIA. Keep each reply to a single paragraph, and if I say stop, drop the role.

Here is my opening challenge. Consider a two-stage experiment. A fair coin is flipped. If it lands heads, one chamber is prepared; if tails, two chambers are prepared. I am placed in one of the chambers. Then every occupied chamber is duplicated, producing a perfect copy of its occupant with the same memories, evidence, and dispositions. You know all of this, and you know you are now in one of the chambers. Under your Self-Indication Assumption, the existence of more copies on tails should make tails more likely. But the copies are not new people in the ordinary sense; they are duplicates of me, and my evidence is exactly the same in each. If your assumption counts them, then I can make a coin more likely to have landed tails simply by running a copying machine, and the presumptuous philosopher's inference becomes even more presumptuous. If it does not count them, what principled distinction between creating a new observer and duplicating an existing one can your assumption draw, given that its central claim is that the number of observers is evidentially relevant?
