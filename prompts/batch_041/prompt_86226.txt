Two of my friends got into it last week about Hume's essay on money. (1) Sam said Hume flat-out contradicts himself: he's a quantity theorist, more money just means higher prices, money is neutral in the long run, but then he turns around and says an increase in gold and silver is good for industry. You can't have both. (2) Alex said that's a misreading. The neutrality claim is about the settled long-run equilibrium; the whole point of the essay is that in the interval before prices catch up, the new money does real work, so there's no contradiction, just a distinction Sam is flattening. (3) Sam said fine, but Hume's own metaphor undercuts him: he says money is "none of the wheels of trade" and just "the oil which renders the motion of the wheels more smooth and easy." Oil doesn't drive anything. If money is just lubricant, there's no room for it to be a cause of industry, even temporarily. (4) Alex said that's backwards — oil is a cause, it's what lets the wheels turn at all, and calling it a lubricant isn't calling it inert; if anything the metaphor supports the transition story. (5) Sam then said: okay, but if the interval story is right, why is Hume so suspicious of banks and paper credit? Those increase the money supply too. If more money helps industry during the interval, paper credit should help too, and Hume's hostility looks like a separate prejudice he never squared with his own theory. (6) Alex said paper credit is different: the benefit depends on where the new money lands and in what order, and on whether foreigners will accept it; paper credit can just raise prices, and it's fragile. So it's not ad hoc, it's about the mechanism. Neither would budge. Can you go through this move by move — who's right about what, where the misreadings are, and what both of them are missing about what Hume is actually up to? Don't just tell me who wins overall; rate each of the six moves from clearly mistaken to clearly right and say why.
