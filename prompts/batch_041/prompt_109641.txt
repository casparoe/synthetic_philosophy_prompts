I'm teaching Christopher Nolan's Memento in a philosophy of mind seminar and would like a reading of it through the extended mind thesis. Leonard's tattoos, Polaroids, and index cards are usually offered as a textbook Otto's-notebook case, but the film insists on two features the standard case suppresses: the notes are authored, and they are untrustworthy — Leonard writes some of them precisely in order to deceive his future self. Does the Clark and Chalmers framework, with the conditions it attaches to Otto's notebook (roughly, constant availability and automatic endorsement), have the resources to distinguish cognition that is genuinely extended from cognition that is merely delegated or colonized? I would also like the reverse chronology taken seriously as a formal argument: the viewer is forced into Leonard's epistemic position, so the film itself functions as a scaffold that manufactures a temporary anterograde amnesia. Tell me what the film dramatizes that the arguments miss, where the reading overreaches and I should flag it in class, and whether the case motivates a distinction between extending one's memory and outsourcing one's will. Please write in American English.
