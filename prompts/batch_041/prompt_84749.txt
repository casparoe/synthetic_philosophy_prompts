I'm drafting the opening lecture for a first-year historiography seminar and have got stuck on a question I assumed I could answer in a sentence. The seminar is nominally about how historians carve the past into 'the Middle Ages', 'the Renaissance' and 'the Scientific Revolution', and my students have already pushed me past the point where 'these are just convenient labels' will do. One of them suspects the whole vocabulary is a nineteenth-century inheritance; another has noticed that the Middle Ages is the only one of the three whose name is a put-down; a third wants to know whether the Scientific Revolution could in principle be told as a Chinese story, or whether the term is Europe-shaped by construction. The sharpest of them asked the question I can't answer: if someone turned up a working steam engine in a fifteenth-century workshop, would that refute the claim that the Scientific Revolution belongs to the seventeenth century, or would we simply redraw the period? I don't want to adjudicate the three terms yet. I want the specification first.

So: write me the desiderata for an adequate account of historical periodisation. What must any such account deliver — which phenomena it has to accommodate, which distinctions it cannot collapse, which tests it has to survive, which uses of period terms it must license or forbid — with the case or consideration behind each requirement. Please be concrete about the cases: boundary fuzziness and the 'early'/'late' prefixes; the fact that art history, climate history and economic history seem to draw their lines in different places and at different scales; the difference between using a period term descriptively and using it to explain; periods whose inhabitants did and didn't think they were living in them; and the awkward fact that the same scheme gets exported to societies that never had a Middle Ages to be in the middle of. I'd also like you to distinguish the kinds of thing a period term might be — a mere chapter-heading, the name of a real discontinuity, an instrument for comparing two societies, or something else again — and say what each kind owes its user, since I suspect much of the confusion in my seminar comes from people defending one kind while arguing about another. Assume the purpose is a scheme good enough both to organise a survey course and to support explanations that invoke period terms; if those two purposes pull apart, say where.

Finally, flag the requirements you think cannot be jointly satisfied, and say which single requirement does the most to rule out the accounts people actually give. I'll do the comparing of named historians in class, so keep the weight on the criteria and their motivating cases; but if one family of answers survives the list more or less intact, tell me which. Write it in British English, as the handout goes straight to the students.
