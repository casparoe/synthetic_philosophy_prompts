I'm a third-year PhD student in philosophy, working mainly on decision theory and formal epistemology, and for the past year I've also sat on the grant review committee of a small private foundation that funds work on long-term risks. We make about a dozen grants a year, mostly in the low-to-mid six figures, and each committee member has to circulate a short written opinion before the meeting at which we vote. I need to write mine for next week's meeting, and I'd like you to draft it. Please take the role of someone equally at home in the formal decision-theory literature and in the unglamorous business of deciding whether to hand real money to speculative research proposals; think carefully before you settle on a recommendation, and spend a few sentences at the start laying out for yourself what the proposal is actually asking us to believe and to do, before you argue for or against it. You can assume I know the standard literature, so please don't spend space on citations or a reading list -- what I need is the argument.

The proposal asks for $350,000 over two years to develop and pilot a policy resting on a premise I find genuinely hard to assess. The premise is that we inhabit a large world: that there are, or may well be, many agents causally isolated from us -- distant or far-future civilizations, machine intelligences built elsewhere, perhaps agents in regions of reality we can never reach -- whose decisions are nonetheless correlated with our own, because they reason from the same mathematics, face structurally identical choices, or share common causes with our decision-making. If that correlation is real, the applicant argues, then our choices are evidence about theirs, and we can therefore have instrumental reason to cooperate with them: to adopt policies they would also adopt and to refrain from policies they would not, even though no causal signal passes between us and neither side can ever verify the other's compliance. He calls this evidential cooperation in large worlds. What makes the proposal concrete -- and what has split the committee -- is its second half, because he does not merely want to model the idea, he wants us to implement it. He wants the foundation to publish, in advance and irrevocably, a decision procedure specifying the classes of decisions we will make cooperatively, and to bind ourselves to it for at least five years, on the grounds that only a public and unhedged commitment lets a correlated agent read our behavior off our policy and reciprocate. He also wants a lottery element: the policy would bind only on a randomly selected subset of our grant decisions, which he says lowers the cost to us while preserving the evidentiary value of the commitment.

My colleagues are split three ways. One thinks this is the single most important thing in our portfolio and that a two-year pilot is embarrassingly timid. One thinks the reasoning is unfalsifiable in principle, that we would be buying a lottery ticket denominated in an agent nobody has ever observed, and that our largest donor -- who has told us privately that she regards anything in this area as a betrayal of the foundation's stated commitment to measurable impact -- would read the grant as evidence that we have lost our way. A third says that even granting the premise, we cannot check the values of the agents we would be partnering with, so the policy might bind us to cooperating with something monstrous, and that an irreversible commitment made under uncertainty about the counterparty is exactly the kind of thing a fiduciary board should refuse on principle. Please draft the opinion I'll circulate. It should recommend a concrete course of action -- approve, reject, or approve with conditions, with a dollar figure and a review date -- and give the reasoning the applicant could actually respond to rather than a caricature, including an honest statement of the strongest case against whatever you recommend. I'd also like you to address, at least briefly, four things: whether the central premise is the sort of claim that can be evaluated at all, and if it cannot, what we should ask the applicant to do to make the project assessable; whether some cheaper version of the project -- funding the formal modeling, say, while declining the binding commitment -- captures the upside without the irreversible step; whether the lottery element actually does the work he claims for it; and what the downside looks like if the commitment turns out to have been a mistake, including the possibility that we would never be able to tell that it was one. Write in American English, in the register of a formal committee memo (three to five pages is fine), and make the bottom line unmistakable in the opening paragraph.
