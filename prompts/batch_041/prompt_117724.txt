I'm putting together a little anthology of fiction that smuggles real evolutionary biology into it, and I need one more piece. Can you write me a short story — say 1200-1800 words — that dramatizes the units-of-selection fight? Not the watered-down version where one guy is the selfish-gene guy and the other is the "for the good of the species" guy and the selfish-gene guy wins. I want it in the weeds.

Here's a setup you can use, change whatever you like: a field station studying a made-up colonial marine invertebrate. Colonies get founded by a handful of siblings that settle together. Most cells in a colony just feed and multiply, but some fraction differentiate into defenders — they never reproduce and they die stinging off predators. So within a colony, the non-defenders (call them freeloaders) leave more offspring than defenders; the defenders are literally giving up their own reproduction. But colonies with lots of defenders survive predation better, and since colonies are founded by siblings, colony-mates are close relatives. Two postdocs are at each other's throats about what this means.

Postdoc A: it's gene-level all the way down — a defender is just a gene's way of getting copies of itself into the next generation via relatives, all the group talk is a useful fiction or a bookkeeping shortcut, nothing is really being selected at the level of the colony. Postdoc B: no, the colony is a real unit — it has heritable variation in a trait (defender fraction) that affects its own survival, and that's group selection by any sane definition; the fact that you can redescribe it in gene terms doesn't make the colony-level cause unreal. And I'd love a third character — the PI, or a visiting philosopher of biology — who says something like: you two are running the same math, the accounts are formally intertranslatable, so the real question isn't which one is true but which description is doing causal work and which is just accounting, and whether the two ever make different predictions. Have them actually press each other on that.

Some things I want the story to make vivid, because I think this is where people get sloppy:

- the difference between selection of a thing and selection for a property — a colony can be selected against without the colony-level trait being what's selected for, the trait might just be riding along on something else;
- the cheater problem — why freeloaders don't just take over every colony, and what has to be true about how colonies form and mix for the altruist trait to hang on at all (assortment/relatedness, not just "the good of the group");
- the fact that "is group selection real?" splits into (a) a bookkeeping question, where the two descriptions are equivalent, and (b) a causal question, where they might come apart — and I want the characters to notice that split instead of talking past each other. You can have them partition the change into a within-group part and a between-group part if that helps.

Don't resolve it with a tidy "and they were both right." I'd rather end on a concrete disagreement they can't settle with the data they have, or on the specific observation/experiment that would actually discriminate the views. No lectures from the narrator, no textbook exposition — let the science come out through argument, fieldwork, and the characters being annoyed at each other. Make up names, place, species. And if you can, sneak in one genuinely tricky wrinkle — like a colony where the defenders turn out to also be the ones who disperse, or where the freeloaders turn out to be helping in some way the models didn't capture — so the neat two-level story gets messy. Also it should definately feel like a story and not a dialogue-shaped lecture, so give me some weather, some mud, alot of standing around in a boat if that's what it takes.
