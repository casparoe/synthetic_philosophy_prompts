Some friends and I have been argueing for weeks about whether it's wrong to do things that contribute to collective harms — eating meat, taking a flight, voting for a third-party candidate who can't win, buying fast fashion. I've ended up landing on a view I want to stress-test before I actually commit to it. Call it the participation view: an individual act is wrong when it's a constituent part of a collectively harmful practice, and — here's the part that makes people's heads explode — it doesn't matter whether my particular act makes any difference. No threshold crossed, no marginal bump in expected harm, none of that. The wrongness is inherited from the pattern I'm participating in, not from the difference I make. So the standard inefficacy move ("one burger, one vote, one flight changes nothing") isn't even an objection on this view; it's the setup.

What I want from you is a map of what would count against the participation view. Not the generic grumbling — I mean the things that should actualy move me if they went through: cases the view can't describe, commitments it now forces on me that I'd find unacceptable, results showing it quietly collapses into some rival like rule-consequentialism or a Kantian universalizability test, or that it can only survive by piling on qualifiers that leave it with no teeth. Which of these would a sympathetic defender concede as genuinely counting against the view, and which would they wave off? Rank them from fatal to merely awkward, and tell me what the view predicts that its rivals don't. I'm especially curious whether the view needs to say anything at all about the threshold structure of the harm — everyone in this debate seems to assume the harm is either a hard threshold or linear in the number of contributors, and I have a hunch that's a false dichotomy the participation view can just sidestep. Tell me if the hunch is right or if I'm using it to hide from the real problem.

And be honest at the end: is there a real risk this view has been qualified so heavily (coercion caveats, "only when the practice is harmful on net," "only when you'd endorse the pattern from behind a veil," etc.) that it no longer risks anything at all? If so, is that a problem for a view of this kind, or is it just what a decent moral theory looks like once you've cleaned it up? Please write your answer in American English.
