I am assembling the opening section of a paper on the axiology of infinite populations, and before I defend any particular view I want to be explicit about what such a view has to do. Write me the desiderata: the requirements that any adequate theory of value for infinite, or merely unboundedly large, populations must satisfy — each stated as a requirement, each accompanied by the case or result that motivates it, and each tagged as non-negotiable or as a preference of one camp.

Fix the purpose as follows, since adequacy is relative to a use: the theory has to be usable for decisions whose stakes may be unboundedly large, chiefly decisions about the very long-run future, where the number of lives affected may be astronomically large or literally infinite. A theory that returns silence for such decisions, or a verdict only for toy cases, fails on that ground alone. Where you need to make a requirement precise by positing a principle, state the principle and say whether it is itself controversial.

Treat the following as fixed points the theory is responsible for — cases to be handled, not to be argued away.

1. Two worlds, each containing countably many people, one with everyone at level 5 and one with everyone at level 6.
2. A world with countably many people at level 5; the same world with one additional person at level 100; and the same world with one person deleted.
3. A population whose members are indexed 1, 2, 3, … with levels 1, 2, 3, …; and the same population with every level raised by one, or with the whole assignment shifted along the index.
4. A world with countably many people at level 10 together with countably many at level 0, compared with a world of countably many at level 5.
5. Any permutation of levels among the people leaves the value of a world unchanged — and what that implies for theories that read value off an average, a limit of partial averages, or a density.
6. A certain finite good now versus a minute probability of an unboundedly good outcome, and the reverse; the theory must not make finite deliberation either trivial or impossible.
7. A case I have not seen treated and am unsure how to classify: a population indexed by the integers, with no first or last member, and a second population identical except that finitely many people near the middle move from level 0 to level 1. I want to know whether a theory's verdict here is constrained by its verdict on the one-sided cases above, and whether it can answer at all without resting on a cutoff that is itself arbitrary.

What I want from you, in this order. First, the desiderata list, each requirement with the case or result behind it, and each marked as non-negotiable or as something only one camp insists on. Second, and this is the part I care about most: tell me whether some subset of these requirements cannot be jointly satisfied, and if so which ones, deriving the conflict informally rather than gesturing at it. Third, say which single requirement does the most excluding work — which one rules out the largest number of otherwise plausible theories. Then, briefly and last, how three broad families fare against the list: sum-like or totalist orderings; person-affecting or pairwise-comparison views; and limit, average, or finite-approximation views. Keep that last part short — the list is the deliverable, not the scorecard.

Argue from first principles. Do not give me citations, reading lists, or appeals to what the literature is said to establish, and do not lean on names I may not know; I want the requirements and the reasoning, not the bibliography.
