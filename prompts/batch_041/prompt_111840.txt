I chair the board of a small liberal arts college -- 1,900 students, an endowment you'd be polite about. Four years ago we denied a professor tenure, he sued us, and we're still in it. $412,000 in outside counsel so far. Our insurer covers half of defense costs, nothing toward any settlement, and that coverage runs out in June. His latest demand: $150k, a neutral reference, and a look at one page of his file. It expires in nine days. Trial is set for April, our summary judgment motion is still pending, and two years ago we turned down a $48k demand, which his lawyer mentions every time we talk. Meanwhile a donor pledged $250k restricted to legal defense with an understanding, not in writing annoyingly, that it lapses if we settle; one trustee keeps saying we can't let people think we pay to make lawsuits go away; and the faculty senate is watching. There's alot of people who think I should just decide this myself and not put it to a vote.

What's actually eating at me is a whiteboard game our counsel's analyst drew for us. Two players bid for a dollar, ten-cent steps. High bid wins the dollar and pays what they bid. The loser also pays what they bid and gets nothing. She says game theorists have a name for this, I forget it. Then she rewrote it to match us: every bid we make is half-price to us because the insurer picks up the rest, but the walk-away price is full price, and she says that makes the trap deeper rather than shallower, which is probly right. She also says the only real defense is not to enter the auction at all, which is a strange thing to tell a defendant who never got to choose whether to sit down.

I have to circulate a memo before Tuesday's meeting. Give me a concrete recommendation with a deadline and a fallback if the plaintiff won't move, the strongest case for fighting on stated as well as our own lawyer would state it, and an honest account of how much weight the $412k and the earlier refusal deserve. My instinct is none for the money and some for the refusal, since consistency has to count for something, but tell me if that distinction is as flimsy as it might be. And tell me what that game actually shows about a case like ours and what it doesn't -- if our bids are subsidized and the exit isn't, is the insurance driving the decision rather than the merits? If your answer flips when you change that one fact, say so. And if the real lesson is about who let us sit down at the table in the first place, give me the standing rule to adopt for next time, because there will be a next time.
