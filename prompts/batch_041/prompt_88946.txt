I'v been keeping a running list of my gut verdicts about when a thing at one time is the same thing as a thing at another time, and I want to know whether there's one principle behind them or whether I'm just making it up case by case.

A wooden bench my grandfather built, with three of its four legs replaced: same bench. Replace the fourth leg too, so no original wood is left: still the same bench, I'm fairly sure. But a bench someone builds from scratch to look exactly like it, using the rotted original as firewood: not the same bench, even though it has more of the original material in it than the repaired one does.

A person who loses all her memories in an accident: same person, no question. A person changed by decades of age and experience until she and her younger self share almost nothing: same person, though the two wouldn't recognize each other.

A river whose every drop of water has been replaced: same river. A river that's dammed, its old bed dried up, and a new channel cut that carries the same water: I want to say the river moved, but I also feel the old river stopped existing and a new one started.

A company where every employee and every shareholder has turned over: same company. A company that's bought, gutted, its name kept and nothing else: not the same company, or barely.

A song played by a different band, in a different key: same song. A song whose every note has been changed, one at a time, over a century of being passed along by ear: still the same song to the people singing it — and I find I agree with them, though I can't say what makes that different from the gutted company.

A file I edit and save on my laptop: same file. A file I copy to a new drive and delete from the old one: same file, I'd say, even though nothing physical carried over. But two copies that both keep going, both edited from then on: now I don't think either one is *the* file anymore, which is odd, since each one seems to have everything that made the copy the same file in the first place.

Is there a principle that captures these, or close? What does it say about a case I keep flip-flopping on — a person whose brain is divided and each half put into a new body, both waking up claiming to be her? And which of my verdicts, if any, would the principle make me give up? I teach this stuff and I'd like to be able to tell my students what I actually think.
