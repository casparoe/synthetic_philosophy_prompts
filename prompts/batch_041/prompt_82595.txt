I am a novelist at work on a book set in a small town whose council must decide whether to remove a novel from the public library. My protagonist, a political philosopher by training and a novelist by temperament, is asked to draft the charter that will govern the council’s decision. The charter has to respect two commitments she cannot bring herself to abandon. The first is a liberal commitment: certain choices—what one reads, whom one loves, how one worships—are the person’s own, not the community’s to make. The second is a Pareto commitment: if every person prefers one outcome to another, the community should not choose the dispreferred outcome. I have become interested in the result that these two commitments can conflict—usually stated with a minimal liberalism condition, on which each person is decisive over at least one pair of alternatives, and a weak Pareto principle—and I want the novel’s intellectual spine to be her attempt to write a charter that survives the conflict without turning into a lecture.

Below I list eight candidate clauses. Some are standard moves in the literature; some I have invented for the novel. I want you to rank them. For each clause, give two ratings from 0 to 10: first, how much it preserves the liberal commitment that some choices are not subject to collective decision; second, how much it actually dissolves the conflict rather than merely relocating it. Then combine the two ratings into a single overall tier list—S, A, B, C, D—with a sentence or two of justification for each placement. Weight the two dimensions equally unless you think the novel requires otherwise, in which case say why. I am less interested in which clause is true than in which would give my protagonist a coherent public position and which would impose a visible cost on her. Where two clauses are jointly incompatible, say so, and say which one the novel would have to sacrifice. Finally, tell me which clause would make her most sympathetic in a public meeting, and which would make her look most evasive.

1. Domain restriction: the liberal right protects only choices that do not affect anyone else’s preferences; everything else is left to the Pareto principle.
2. Lexicographic priority: rights are side constraints that lexically dominate the Pareto principle; no Pareto improvement may be achieved by violating a right, even if the right-bearer would prefer the violation.
3. Procedural rights: the right is not to an outcome but to a procedure—the right-bearer decides whether the procedure is followed, not what the outcome is.
4. Waiver and alienability: rights can be waived or traded; the conflict arises only when a right-bearer insists on a right that she could have exchanged.
5. Informational basis: replace preference satisfaction with a richer account of freedom or capability, so that the conflict is between freedoms rather than between preferences.
6. Meta-rights: a right to determine the scope of one’s own rights, ranked lexicographically above ordinary rights.
7. Publicity: a clause is acceptable only if it can be publicly justified to the person whose right is overridden, in terms she could not reasonably reject.
8. Separate spheres: the community is divided into jurisdictions, each with its own decision rule, so that no single procedure ever has to rank all the relevant alternatives.
