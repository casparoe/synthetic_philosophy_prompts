I am a software engineer with no formal background in philosophy or economics, and I would like some careful help with a phrase that has started to bother me. In a postmortem for a project that ran badly over budget, a director cut off the discussion with a single line: "Sunk costs are sunk." The clear implication was that the money already spent was gone and therefore irrelevant, so we should decide about the remaining work only on the basis of what it will cost from here and what we expect to get. That sounded like common sense. But since then I have watched the same four words do very different work. A teammate invoked them to argue that we should push on with a feature that keeps failing, on the grounds that the money is spent either way and the only thing that matters is whether continuing from here has positive value. A poker-playing friend used the same reasoning to defend calling a bet he knew was bad, because he was already "pot-committed." And in reading about the dollar auction — the game where a dollar is auctioned and two bidders keep raising each other, each adding a little more to avoid losing what he has already put in, until the bids far exceed a dollar — I noticed that the forward-looking logic the slogan recommends is precisely the logic that seems to keep the bidders going.

What I want is not a definition and not a verdict on whether the director was right. I want the menu of distinct, precise claims that "sunk costs are sunk" could actually be making, laid out so that I can tell them apart in the moment. Somewhere in there, I suspect, are claims as different as: an accounting truism about which numbers appear on which report; a psychological prediction about how people in fact behave; a normative claim about what a rational agent ought to do; a claim about the correct decision procedure even for an agent who knows he will be tempted; and something stronger, that only future consequences matter at all, so that regret, loyalty, fairness to the people who did the earlier work, and the value of being the sort of person who finishes things carry no weight whatsoever. For each reading, please say whether it is trivially true, substantive but defensible, or actually false, and what further assumptions it needs in order to go through.

Two things I especially want you to address. First, which of the readings would actually support the director's use of the phrase to shut down the debate, and which would not — in particular, does the slogan by itself tell you to stop, or does it only tell you to look forward, which can just as easily support continuing? Second, please say how the dollar auction and the broader pattern people call escalation of commitment look under each reading: is the escalating bidder making a mistake, and if so, which reading is the one that diagnoses it? I have a suspicion that the slogan is often doing covert work — smuggling in an assumption that the past is separable from the present in a way that needs to be argued for rather than asserted — and I would like to know whether that suspicion is well founded, and if so, which precise reading makes it true.

Please write your answer in American English, in a form I can read over a lunch break.
