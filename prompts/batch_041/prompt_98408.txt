A friend in my medieval philosophy reading group reconstructed an argument out of Maimonides' treatment of the sacrificial commandments, and I cannot decide whether it is a genuine refutation or an artifact of how he set it up. His reconstruction:

(1) Maimonides explains the sacrificial laws as a concession to circumstance: the Israelites, raised among nations for whom animal sacrifice was the ordinary medium of worship, could not have been moved all at once to a purely inward service; so the Law permitted sacrifice, but confined it to one chosen place, one altar, and the name of the one God, so that the practice would be gradually redirected from idolatry to the worship of God.
(2) On that account, the content of those commandments is fixed by a contingent fact about the habits of the ancient Israelites.
(3) Had the recipients of the Law been a people with no such habits, a perfectly wise legislator would not have needed to command sacrifice, or would have commanded something else instead.
(4) So there are possible circumstances in which the perfect Law would have contained different commandments, or none of these.
(5) But Maimonides also holds that the Law is perfect and that it will never be abrogated or replaced by another.
(6) A law whose content is fixed by contingent facts about its first recipients cannot be the eternally best law for all peoples.
(7) Therefore either the explanation of the sacrifices must be abandoned, or the doctrine of the Law's perfection and permanence must be.

I would most like to keep (1). The account of the sacrifices is the part of the Guide I find most illuminating, and I would rather surrender something else than give it up; but I do not want to hand over (5) cheaply either, and I notice that if a commandment's reason is a historical circumstance, it is hard to say why the commandment does not lapse once the circumstance has passed.

Which step do you reject, and what does rejecting it cost? Name the specific premise, explain why it looked compelling, and price the alternatives. If the trouble lies in (2) or (3), say exactly which notion of a commandment's rationale is being confused with which — the occasion of its promulgation, its continuing justification, the purpose of the legislator, the good of the one who keeps it — and show how the distinction blocks the inference. If the argument is instead valid as it stands, say which of (1) and (5) must be restated, what the restated version can still claim, and what it must give up. In either case, tell me whether the concession reading of the sacrifices survives the repairs in any form, and what that surviving form is.
