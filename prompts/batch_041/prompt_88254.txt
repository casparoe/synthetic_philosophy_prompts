My book group has mutated into a politics argument club and we've spent the last three Thursdays on Scottish independence, then Catalonia, then a long detour into Quebec. One of the guys — a solicitor, of course — keeps saying the whole business is basically a divorce. His line goes: the union was a marriage, marriages end by agreement, and you don't get to walk out of the house with the good furniture while the other person is still sitting in it. That's meant to settle three things at once: that there can be no referendum without Westminster's consent, that an independent Scotland can't just keep the pound, and that an independent Scotland takes its share of the debt. He runs the same routine for Catalonia (the 2017 declaration was someone moving out before the papers were signed) and for Quebec (he says the Clarity Act is just a prenup). Everyone nods along and I'm starting to think the analogy is doing all the work and nobody has checked it.

So could you audit it properly? I want the correspondence laid out — who or what are the spouses, the house, the joint account, the kids, the furniture (oil, fishing rights, the trident base, the currency) — and, more to the point, who the second party actually is when the union is between two peoples rather than two people, since half of Scotland voted to stay and I don't think anyone asked them to sign anything. Then the places it breaks, sorted into the ones that matter for his three conclusions and the ones that are just noise. I've got a nagging suspicion it cuts the other way on the unilateral point, because as far as I know most modern divorce law is no-fault and lets either party leave without the other's blessing, which is the opposite of what he's arguing — but I can't tell if that's a real objection or me being clever at 11pm. And if children don't get a veto over a divorce, what does that do to the unionists-as-children version? If the thing can't carry the weight he's putting on it, tell me what we should be comparing a secession to instead.
