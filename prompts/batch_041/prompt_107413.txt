I am in a reading group on animal minds and moral uncertainty, and a friend has put forward what he calls the scale argument, in the following form. (1) The moral weight of a being is the product of the probability that it is sentient, its welfare range, and the number of experiences it has. (2) We cannot rule out insect sentience, and a probability as low as one in a thousand is still far above zero. (3) There are on the order of 10^18 insects alive at any time, and ordinary human activities affect astronomical numbers of them: a hectare of cropland may contain hundreds of millions, a single drive may kill thousands on the windshield, and a porch light may draw and kill moths by the hundreds. (4) Even if an insect's welfare range is a thousandth of a human's, the expected sum of insect welfare affected by ordinary human choices dwarfs the expected sum of human welfare affected by those same choices. (5) If expected welfare is what matters, then agriculture, housing, travel, and even medical sterilization are morally dominated by their effects on insects. (6) Therefore we are required to give up or radically transform those practices, and any human interest that conflicts with reducing insect suffering has no weight. (7) But that conclusion is absurd: it would make it wrong to save a drowning child if the rescue would drown a thousand ants, and it would license exterminating all insects to prevent their suffering. (8) So one of (1) through (5) must be false.

I would most like to keep (3) and (5), and I do not want to reject (7). I am not sure whether the trouble lies in the aggregation in (4), in the probability assignment in (2), or in the product rule in (1). There is also a further wrinkle: a friend who works on insect neuroanatomy says that many insects have decentralized nervous systems, with local ganglia that may not be integrated into a single global workspace, so it may be indeterminate how many subjects of experience are present in a single body; that would make the counting in (3) and the multiplication in (4) suspect, though I cannot tell whether it rescues the argument or only replaces one problem with another. Tell me which premise or step to reject, explain why it looked compelling, and say what rejecting it costs. I want to know what each candidate rejection would force me to accept elsewhere, and whether the argument can be repaired without either accepting the absurd conclusion or making an ad hoc exception for insects.
