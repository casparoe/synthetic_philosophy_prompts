My reading group burned like an hour on the trolley stuff last week and I ended up refereeing a fight I couldn't settle, so I'm outsourcing the verdict. I've been lurking on rationalist blogs since the old Sequences days, I've read enough LessWrong to know I should be steelmanning both sides, and my instinct says somebody here is equivocating on "intend" — but I definately can't tell who, and I don't want to just hand it to the side I already lean toward. I asked them what their crux was and they both just restated themselves, so that was a dead end.

Two people in the group, Owen and Dana, went at it. Here's the exchange as close as I can reconstruct it (not word-for-word, but the moves are right):

1. Owen: The whole point of double effect is that you can foresee a bad side effect without intending it. In the plain switch case you can see the guy on the side track is going to die, but you don't intend it — you'd pull the lever even if that track were empty — so it's allowed. On the footbridge you intend the big guy's death as your way of stopping the trolley, so it's forbidden. Intention is doing all the work.

2. Dana: Then take the loop case. The side track curves back around and rejoins the main line further down, so if the one guy weren't standing there, the trolley would just loop back and kill the five anyway. Which means the only reason the five get saved is that the trolley hits him and stops. His death isn't a side effect, it's the mechanism. By your own test that's intending it as a means, so double effect says don't pull — but almost nobody thinks you should stand there and let the five die. So your doctrine either forbids something we all think is fine, or intention isn't the thing doing the work.

3. Owen: The loop case is a trick of description. Intending something isn't about which cog in the world's machinery ends up mattering, it's about your plan and your attitude. In the loop case your plan is just "get the trolley off the main line"; the fact that his body happens to be what stops it is the world's business, not your intention. In the footbridge case your plan literally has "use his body to stop the trolley" as a step. That difference survives the loop.

4. Dana: If the plan is whatever you say it is, then double effect can't condemn anything. The terrorist says his plan is "topple the regime" and the civilians were foreseen but unintended; the guy on the bridge says his plan is "stop the trolley" and the big guy dying was just how it went. And be honest — in the loop case, if the one guy hopped off the track at the last second you'd be horrified, because you needed him there. You needed his body. So what you're actually pointing at is using someone as an instrument, or doing something to him, not intention. The intention talk is a costume.

5. Owen: Fine, maybe there's a using element. But it can't be the whole story either. Suppose the big guy turns out to be indestructible — he stops the trolley and walks away. You'd be thrilled. So even on the bridge you didn't need him to die, just to be hit. Meanwhile in the loop case you need him to be there but you never lay a hand on him. So "using him as an instrument" and "doing something to him" come apart, and I don't see which one is supposed to be the thing that makes the bridge wrong.

Dana's last word was something like "then your doctrine is three doctrines in a trenchcoat," and Owen said "or it's one doctrine and you keep redescribing the cases until it cracks," and then we had to give the room back.

What I want: go through it move by move and tell me who's right at each step — not who wins overall, I want the actual scorecard — and then tell me what both of them are missing. In particular: is there a reading of double effect that handles the loop case without just collapsing into a doing/allowing thing? And in move 5, is Owen's indestructible-guy point a real problem for Dana, or is he cheating somehow? Dont feel like you have to be nice to either of them, and please write it in American English.
