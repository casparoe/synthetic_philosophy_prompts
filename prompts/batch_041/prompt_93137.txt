I'm a doctoral student in philosophy writing on the epistemology of conspiracy theories, and for a chapter on how the field's stock examples distort its theories I've been trying to use the day-care ritual-abuse panic of the 1980s as a stress test. The episode is familiar enough in outline: over roughly a decade, first in the United States and then in Britain and elsewhere, a wave of allegations held that children were being sexually abused in organized satanic rituals at preschools and day-care centers; the allegations typically surfaced through recovered-memory therapy and through repeated, highly suggestive interviewing of very young children; a string of prosecutions followed, including the McMartin Preschool case in California, which ran for years and ended without a conviction; and by the early 1990s the panic had collapsed, as law-enforcement and professional investigations found no corroborating evidence and courts began reversing convictions. What interests me is not the historiography but the fit between the episode and our theories of why conspiracy theories take hold, and I keep finding that the case fits none of them cleanly.

I'd like you to run it through three accounts and tell me, for each, what it explains and where it breaks. The first is the errant-data account: a conspiracy theory is best understood as an explanation that earns its grip by accounting for the residual facts the official story leaves dangling, so that the believer is doing something like inference to the best explanation over the leftovers. The second is the self-sealing account: what defines such a theory is not its content but a structural feature — it treats the absence or suppression of evidence as itself confirming the conspiracy, so that no possible observation could disconfirm it. The third is the status account: the theory is attractive because of who holds it and why — the disempowered, the marginalized, or those whose standing or worldview is under threat, the reading sometimes summed up in the slogan that conspiracy theories are for losers. My working hunch is that the third account is the most strained here, since the believers were not obviously the powerless — they included therapists, prosecutors, social workers, and a credulous press — but I would rather be told I'm wrong than have the account patched in order to survive the case.

Finally, I want to know whether the episode is better handled by a framework from outside the conspiracy literature altogether — the sociology-of-deviance notion of a moral panic, in which it is a society rather than a movement that generates the episode — and if so, what the conspiracy-theoretic accounts lose by comparison. Please be disciplined about the mapping: say who counts as the believers, what exactly the alleged conspiracy was, what the "errant data" were supposed to be at each stage, and what would have counted as disconfirming evidence. And where an account is simply silent, say so rather than forcing it to speak.
