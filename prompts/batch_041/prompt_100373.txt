Last night a friend and I argued for hours about a sentence that is supposed to be impossible to utter sincerely: that it is raining and that I do not believe it is raining. I said it was merely a clumsy, self-defeating thing to say, the verbal equivalent of tripping over a step; she said it was not awkwardness at all but contradiction, that the first half already commits me to the very belief the second half disowns, so that the whole is not one bad sentence but two clauses at war. Neither of us could say what the contradiction actually consists in, and I have come to suspect the answer is sitting in a practice we perform constantly without ever inspecting it.

Consider how we ask people what they believe. A pollster asks a sample whether they believe the climate is warming, whether a defendant is guilty, whether their neighbours can be trusted, and the answers are tallied and published as facts about what people believe. A hiring committee, a doctor taking a history, a friend across a dinner table — all do a version of the same thing: they ask, they record, and they treat the answer as the last word. You cannot be corrected about your own belief. If you say you believe it, then you believe it; there is nothing further to check. And if you tell me today that you never really believed it, I will think you lied or changed your mind, not that you were simply mistaken about your own state.

And yet the same answers are treated as claims about a shared world. We explain why people believe false things; we call whole populations deluded; we argue people out of their beliefs and blame them for holding some. We find it perfectly natural to say that someone believes something that is not so. And of ourselves we would say that we believe something and might be wrong, but never that we believe something and that it is false.

So the practice seems to rest on two pictures at once. On one, a statement of belief is a report of an inner state, and beliefs are private possessions each of us can read off and no one else can contradict. On the other, a belief is a commitment about how things are, answerable to evidence and shared with everyone who takes up the same question, and a statement of belief is less a report than a stand taken. The first picture makes belief-statements incorrigible; the second makes them correctable. The oddity my friend and I could not name seems to live exactly where the two pictures meet.

What I want from you is an account of what this practice presupposes. Reconstruct the theory of belief, and of belief-statement, that asking people what they believe — and then holding them to it — only makes sense under. Say which of the two pictures the practice genuinely requires and which it merely happens to sit alongside; I suspect it can run on either, and that the apparent conflict is produced by our sliding between them. Say what the practice rules out: does it presuppose that a person can be mistaken about their own beliefs, or not? That belief is something we choose, or something that befalls us? That the first-person statement of a belief describes a state or expresses a commitment? Then say where the practice is underdetermined, so that several incompatible theories would license exactly the behaviour we observe. Finally, tell me what would have to change if we adopted the second picture wholesale — if every statement of belief were treated as a claim about the world rather than a report about the speaker. Would the polling, the hiring, the diagnosis, the dinner-table question survive intact, or would they have to be rebuilt? And does the incoherence my friend sensed in that sentence survive the reconstruction, or does it dissolve once we see which picture the sentence is trying to hold together?
