My friend and I spent most of last night arguing about whether a society should be in the business of forgetting, and the argument kept circling back to the fact that our own legal order runs two opposite policies at once. It lets people outrun their past: juvenile records are sealed, adult convictions can be expunged after a clean interval, and an individual can demand that old, embarrassing material be delisted from search results. But it also refuses to let the past go: sex-offender registries are permanent and public, and archival and legal-deposit statutes oblige the state to retain essentially everything ever published, with no individual right of removal. The same system therefore treats the past as something a person may lawfully shed and as something no one may shed.

I want the implicit theory reconstructed. What conception of the person and of memory does that combination presuppose? What must it assume about whether a person is constituted by their deeds or can be reborn apart from them, about who owns a life's record, and about whether remembering is a duty or merely a tool? Which rival views does the combination quietly rule out? Separate what the practice strictly requires from what it merely fits well with: which parts need a substantive view about memory, and which are explicable by mundane concerns such as predictive risk, administrability, or cost? Is the pair coherent under a single theory, or is it two theories welded together by history, with the seams showing? And say where the practice is philosophically underdetermined, so that several incompatible theories would license the same rules.

Then tell me what would have to change under either rival pole: the view that a community has an ethical duty to remember, of the sort Margalit defends, or the view that memory is a burden we would often be better off setting down, of the sort Rieff defends. Give me your own committed verdict on which theory the practice actually needs, and defend it rather than laying out the options for me to choose between. Keep the whole answer under 600 words.
