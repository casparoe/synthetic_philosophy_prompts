Odd one for you. I've been covering the free-will wars for the best part of a decade — for my sins — and the strand I keep coming back to is the Dennett–Caruso spat about just deserts: whether we can go on punishing people once we stop believing anyone ever really deserves to suffer for what they did, and what sentencing even is if you swap desert out for the forward-looking stuff (risk, deterrence, reform, restitution). For a satire package I'm putting together, I'd like you to write a parody document: the internal caseworker Q&A from a jurisdiction that actually went and did it. The setup is that the fictional State of Calder passed the Public Safety (Containment) Act ten years ago, formally abolished desert as a ground for detention, and handed the whole business to the Calder Bureau of Preventive Custody, which has been improvising ever since. Give me the Bureau's staff handbook section: numbered Q&As, 600–700 words, deadpan bureaucratic register, no winking, no narrator nudging the reader — the reader should work out what's being sent up from the document itself. The target isn't any individual (keep Caruso and Dennett out of it as characters) but the practice: what actually happens on the ground when you keep the cells, the schedules and the paperwork and just change the vocabulary.

The jokes have to come from the philosophy being done properly, so I definately want it to read as fair to someone who knows the abolitionist case and Dennett's replies, and still make sense to someone who doesn't. These things should surface, worked through in the Bureau's own flat prose: two detainees with identical actuarial risk scores and wildly different culpability, and the form the caseworker has to fill in; a release decision that turns on remorse, where the panel can't say whether it's assessing danger or quietly demanding penance; a victim's family asking for something the statute has no word for; the finance office pointing out that the cheapest way to prevent future harm is no detention at all, and the Bureau's answer to that; and the vocabulary schedule, where the word desert has been struck from the approved terms and the list of replacements keeps growing. Somewhere in there I'd like a leaked marginal note from a staffer of the older school who thinks they've kept the cages and mislaid the reason for them.

It shouldn't resolve into a verdict either way — end where the Bureau's actual practice has ended up, which is somewhere they'd rather not look at too closely. Title it like a real handbook, with the Act's commencement date in the header.
