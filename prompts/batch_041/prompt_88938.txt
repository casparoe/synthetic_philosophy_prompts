I've been argueing with my flatmate about why promises bind. She's got this line that it's all the institution — there's a social practice of promising with rules, and when you say the words you've stepped into the practice, so you're on the hook. I think that's backwards: what puts you on the hook is that you've given the other person an assurance, you've made them expect something they can now reasonably count on, and the whole thing is about them, not about the ritual. We keep going in circles because every example we reach for (lending a book, meeting for lunch, splitting a bill) is one where both stories say the same thing, so neither of us has anything to push against. So I want you to build me cases where the two come apart, in both directions, so I can see whether these are really two different theories of promissory obligation or just two ways of describing the same thing.

Case one: everything on the institutional side is in place — I utter the words, I mean them, the other person hears and takes it up, no coercion, no misunderstanding, the thing promised is perfectly ordinary and permissible — and yet the assurance view says I'm not really bound, or only very weakly. Case two: none of the institutional machinery is in place, nothing that could be called invoking the practice of promising has happened, and yet the assurance view says I owe the other person something and the practice view says I don't. Keep both cases in ordinary life — no deathbeds, no lifeboats, no promises wrung out by villains — and don't let the split come from either side having some implausible caveat tacked on; it has to fall out of what each theory says grounds the obligation in the first place. For each case, spell out what the practice theorist says and why, what the assurance theorist says and why, and name the one feature that's doing the work. Then remove that feature and tell me whether the case collapses back into the boring lunch-date example.

And be honest with me about how well this works: which of the two cases is genuinely load-bearing and which is a cheap trick, and whether the practice theorist can just say that the word 'promise' isn't what matters and that case two is still a promise — if so, tell me what that shows about whether the two views are actually distinct. I'd also like you to say how confident you are in each verdict you hand down, and to flag the single point you're least sure about.
