I'm putting together a reading group on rule-following next month and I want to open with a dialogue instead of a paper. Write it for me.

Two characters, both of them good at this. The first is the skeptic from the reading of Wittgenstein that Kripke made famous — the one where nothing in your past use, your dispositions, or anything going on in your head picks out addition rather than the cooked-up function that agrees with addition everywhere you've ever actually checked and then goes haywire past that. She has taken the skeptical solution rather than the nihilist exit: sure, there's no fact of the matter about what you meant, but "meaning addition" is still in perfectly good order — it's just that what makes it true is what your community is willing to let you get away with, not a fact inside you. Call her the Communitarian.

The second is a straight-answer person: she thinks there really is a fact about what you meant, and that it lives in the individual — your dispositions, your intentions, or some primitive normative state that doesn't reduce to anything else. Call her the Individualist. Neither of them should be a strawman. The Individualist can't just say "obviously I meant plus, everyone knows that," and the Communitarian can't be a relativist who thinks anything goes; she has to hold the line that meaning is a matter of communal sanction while still thinking it's objective enough a business to correct people.

Dont let them just trade the textbook moves back and forth — I've read those. Anchor the whole thing in three cases, and make each character deal with each one instead of ducking:

1. The last speaker. A woman has done arithmetic her whole life inside a community that corrected her when she slipped. She's now the last one left — pick whether that's because everyone else died or because she's been in solitary confinement for decades, and say why you picked it. She has never once added two numbers above 500. Today she has to. Nobody is around to check her. What fixes what she means? And does anything change if she's been alone so long that she can no longer remember a single time anyone corrected her?

2. The rulebook with no readers. A dead civilisation leaves a tablet of rules for a game nobody alive has ever played. There are no communal dispositions left anywhere — just the text and whoever picks it up first. Does the Communitarian have anything to say here, or does she have to appeal to a possible community, or a future one, and is that cheating? Press her on this one. I want to see her either wriggle out of it or go down.

3. The confidently wrong community. Everyone in some community has always applied a rule in a way that's quietly self-contradictory — they'd endorse a principle in one case and deny it in a structurally identical one, forever, and never notice. Does the community's verdict make it so, or can a whole community be wrong about its own rule? If the Individualist says a community can be wrong, make the Communitarian ask where that fact of the matter is supposed to live; if the Communitarian says the community can't be wrong, make the Individualist ask whether that makes her community infallible, which sounds like exactly the thing she was trying to avoid.

Have them respond to each other's actual points rather than talking past each other, and let them go a couple of rounds deep on cases 1 and 3. Then finish with a short paragraph in your own voice — not either character's — saying which of the three cases did the most damage and what the skeptic has to give up in order to survive it.

Maybe 1200 to 1800 words. No citations needed, I just want the arguement.
