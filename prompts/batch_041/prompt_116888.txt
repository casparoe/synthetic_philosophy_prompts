I retired about six years ago, after the better part of four decades sticking electrodes into retinas, and since then I've been trying to teach myself philosphy of mind, which is how I ended up in a reading group with three grad students and one very patient emeritus logician. Last month we did von Neumann's little book on computers and the brain -- the Silliman lectures he was invited to give in the mid fifties and never delivered, because he was already dying, so what we have is really an unfinished manuscript that got published posthumously. I came in expecting to nod along, since my whole working life I'd understood von Neumann to be the man who said the brain is a computer. The standard design of every machine I've ever used is called the von Neumann architecture, and he's the one who wrote the famous draft report on the EDVAC back in 1945 (or was it the ENIAC? I can never keep those two straight) that handed everybody the stored-program idea.

So I assumed that when he turned to the nervous system he was saying: neurons are the hardware, thought is the software, brain equals machine, and that's why simulating one is in principle just an engineering problem. But the book does almost the opposite. He keeps stressing that the components are slow and unreliable, and that the brain somehow gets away with it through sheer numbers and redundancy; that some of what the nervous system does looks analog and some looks digital and he isn't sure how to carve it up; that the language of the brain might turn out to be statistical in a way we don't yet have the mathematics for; and he does one of those back-of-the-envelope estimates of how many bits the thing could hold and then more or less shrugs. There is a great deal of him saying he doesn't know. Half the book is questions.

So here's what's bugging me, and I'd like it walked through slowly. Is the von Neumann architecture of my laptop actually von Neumann's view of the brain, or is it just a name that got stuck onto him for something entirely different? Did he change his mind between the forties and the fifties? And if the brain isn't really a stored-program machine of that kind -- if the memory is mixed up with the processing and the signals aren't clean ones and zeros -- does the whole idea that the mind is a program collapse, or is that a separate question I'm muddling together with the first one? One of the grad students told me the computer/brain comparison was always meant as a heuristic and that nobody serious ever claimed the brain is a von Neumann machine, but then why is he cited constantly, and why did I have to read a book to find out what he actually claimed? I also have a vague memory of a Turing award lecture complaining about the von Neumann bottleneck, which sounds like the phrase being used in a completely different, engineering sort of way, and I don't know what to do with that either. Where did my picture come from, and what should it have been?
