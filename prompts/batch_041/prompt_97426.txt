I am a doctoral student working on the ethics of political membership, and I want to think through a puzzle that the ordinary architecture of the welfare state seems to generate.

Take a typical wealthy liberal democracy. Entitlement to a state pension, and in many systems to unemployment insurance, typically depends on a contribution record: one receives roughly in proportion to what one has paid in. Entitlement to healthcare, schooling and housing assistance typically depends on residence: one receives them by living there. Entitlement to enter the territory, to remain in it and to vote typically depends on citizenship, which may in turn be acquired by birth, by descent or by naturalisation — and those who lack it may be admitted on terms that expressly bar them from most public funds even as they pay tax and use public services. The same set of institutions therefore sorts people by at least three distinct criteria — contribution, residence and membership — and treats the claims of those inside the scheme as vastly stronger than those of equally needy or needier outsiders, while its official philosophy insists that every person's fundamental worth is equal.

I would like the implicit theory reconstructed. Four things in particular.

First, does the pattern commit the state to the proposition that compatriots have special claims which outsiders lack, or can it be accounted for by something thinner — for example, that the state is merely the only agent in a position to act, or that it holds agent-relative permissions to favour its own members? Separate carefully what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits comfortably with.

Second, if special claims are presupposed, which ones? Contribution-based entitlement suggests a reciprocity or insurance rationale; residence-based entitlement suggests a territorial-solidarity rationale; membership-based exclusion suggests a national-community rationale. Are these three rationales mutually consistent, or does the practice draw on whichever is convenient to the case at hand? Identify any point at which the parts presuppose incompatible things — for instance, where long-resident non-citizens are treated as members for the purposes of taxation and service provision but as outsiders for the purposes of political voice.

Third, where is the practice philosophically underdetermined — that is, where would several rival theories, liberal-nationalist and moderate-cosmopolitan alike, license the very same arrangement?

Fourth, what would have to change in the design of these institutions if one adopted a thoroughgoing cosmopolitanism that denied any intrinsic moral significance to the border? Say concretely which features would have to go, which could survive, and which would be left untouched because they never depended on the nationalist premise in the first place.

I am not asking for a survey of the literature, and I do not need citations. I want the implicit theory stated, its components tested for consistency, and a clear verdict on what the practice genuinely requires as against what it merely accommodates.
