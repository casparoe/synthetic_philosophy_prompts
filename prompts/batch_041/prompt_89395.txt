Im on the behavioral design team at a mid-size fintech that sells commitment savings products -- the ones where you pledge to save a fixed amount each month and get penalized if you pull the money back out. About 40 flagged accounts a month land on my desk, each tagged by a support rep as "needs a stronger commitment device," and honestly we've been treating all of them the same way: crank up the penalty, lengthen the lock-up, add more nagging texts. It isn't working, and I think it's because we're lumping together things that philosophy and behavioral econ actually keep apart.

Heres the distinction I keep circling and can't operationalize. Weakness of will in the strict philosophical sense needs a conflict of judgment: the person thinks it's best to do X, right then, and does not-X anyway. But a lot of what we see looks more like the ranking itself flipping as the moment gets close -- at the start of the month she'd rather save than take the trip, two days before the trip she'd rather take the trip, and there's no moment where she's acting against her own verdict. That's not akrasia in the strict sense, it's a reversal in the preference ordering over time, and I suspect the two need different fixes. Then there's a third bucket where there's no endorsement at all -- the goal came from a spouse or a doctor and the client never really signed on -- and a fourth where the person genuinely can't, rather than won't, save that month. We've also got an internal number my colleague calls the "endorsement gap": what a client says they want at sign-up minus what they actually defend when we call them after a lapse. And one pattern I can't explain: when we ask clients to pre-commit on payday itself, lapse rates drop hard; when we ask them to pre-commit two weeks before payday, they don't drop at all. I don't know if that's discounting, salience, or something else, and I'd like the procedure to have a place to put it.

What I want from you: a triage procedure my team can actually run, on paper, in about 15 minutes per account, by support coaches who have no psychology or philosophy training. Give me the questions in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops and says no (no commitment device, decline the account, this isnt ours to fix), where it hands off to a human because the call is a values or a clinical one rather than a design one, and where the four buckets above get told apart with something more than vibes. Be explicit about which steps are judgment calls dressed up as procedure, and say where a coach is likely to get it wrong in the direction of selling the product anyway.

Then run it on three accounts and show me the whole trace, not just the verdict:

1. A woman saving for a house who transfers the balance back out almost every month, right before a trip she's already paid for. She says, unprompted, that she knows she shouldn't, and describes the transfer as "stupid" while doing it.
2. A man referred by his wife to a gambling-blocking version of the product. He signed the form but in every call he says he doesn't have a problem and is only doing it to get her off his back. He keeps the block in place.
3. A gig worker with wildly variable income who misses months not because he spends the money but because some months there isn't any.

I already know I won't like one of these answers, so don't soften it. And tell me plainly what the procedure can't do -- which cases it will misclassify no matter how well it's written, and which part of the underlying theory gets lost when you squeeze it into a form a coach can run.
