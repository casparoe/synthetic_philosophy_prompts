I'm in a reading group that's spent the whole term circling a contrast a freind of mine keeps pushing over dinner: he grew up in Stuttgart and moved to Portland and he says German folk morality is basically about order -- you keep the rule, you don't make an exception for yourself, and the highest thing you can say about a person is that they're dependable, someone you never have to think about twice -- while American folk morality is about conscience, the lone individual answering to what he takes to be right, treating a rule as something you're allowed to break when it's wrong. The German side reads that as self-indulgence; the American side reads the rule-keeper as a coward hiding behind a code. We keep going in circles and I'd rather close the term with a poem than another round of argueing, so I want you to write it.

Roughly 24 to 30 lines, in two movements and a coda. First movement in strict form -- rhymed iambic tetrameter, regular, and don't let the syntax spill across the line breaks -- the rule-keeping voice. No caricature of cold authoritarianism, though: it has to make the real case, that the rule is what spares other people the burden of having to read your character, that predictability is itself a kind of courtesy, that the person improvising from conscience is asking everyone around him to trust his mood. Second movement in loose free verse, the conscience voice, also given its best case: a rule is legible, so you can keep it perfectly and mean nothing by it, whereas conscience can't be faked and can't be checked either -- and somebody has to be able to say no when the rule itself is the crime. Then a coda of four lines back in the strict tetrameter but carrying the second movement's content, so the form crosses over to the other side at the end. I want someone who's read in comparative moral psychology and in the German duty tradition to recognize both positions as their own strongest versions rather than a strawman and a hero, and I want the poem to end up somewhere on the question of which one has the better of it -- the coda is where that should land.

Two things on top of the poem. First, a short note on where the verse takes liberties or leans on a contested reading -- if the whole contrast is a folk invention the empirical work doesn't actually support, say so rather than writing around it. Second, tell me which single line is carrying the most weight and is most likely to be wrong, and what argument or consideration would make you flip which movement the poem's sympathy ends up on.
