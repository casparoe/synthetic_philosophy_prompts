I have been reading rationalist and adjacent blogs and forums for years, and I keep encountering Darwin and Turing paired under a single heading — the strange inversion of reasoning — with the gloss that Darwin shows how the appearance of design can arise without a designer, and Turing shows how the appearance of thought can arise without a thinker. I think I have absorbed the lesson, but I want to check, because I suspect I have drawn a conclusion that the inversions do not actually license.

Here is the conclusion I have drawn. If competence can be produced without comprehension — if a process can reliably do the thing without understanding the thing — then comprehension is doing no explanatory work that competence does not already do, and the question of whether a system genuinely understands is a leftover of folk psychology rather than a factual question. So once a system is competent across the relevant range, there is nothing left to ask; insisting that it still might not really understand is just a refusal to accept the inversion. Yet the same forums are full of people arguing about whether large language models understand, and treating the question as substantive on both sides. Is that not simply a failure to take the inversion seriously?

Two things I would like clarified. First, is my reading of what the inversions establish correct, or have I collapsed something — a claim about the order of explanation, say, into a claim about what exists? Second, if the distinction between competence and comprehension survives, what work is it doing? It seems to me that the very slogan of competence without comprehension already presupposes that the two can come apart, which makes it hard for me to see how the distinction could be empty. So either I am confused about one of the pieces or about how they fit, and I would rather be told which.
