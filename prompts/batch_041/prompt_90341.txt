I'm a second-year PhD student in an econ theory group, and I'm about to present my job-market paper at our weekly theory reading group, where the one senior person whose opinion actually decides whether a paper "flies" has spent a year handing down verdicts on the common-prior assumption. I've been quietley keeping a log of her rulings so I can predict what she'll say about mine before I stand up. Here's the log. (1) A model where two traders start with different priors over a binary state, but the paper gives each a different private signal drawn from one shared data-generating process — she waved it through: "that's just information, not a prior difference." (2) A model where the two traders have different priors and the paper only says "agents have heterogeneous beliefs," with no account of where the difference came from — she killed it: "unmotivated heterogeneity." (3) A model where agents have different priors, then receive the same public signal, update correctly, and still disagree, and that persistent disagreement drives all the trading — she killed it: "they can't agree to disagree if they're both rational and it's common knowledge." (4) A model where one agent is simply overconfident — a biased belief about his own ability, modeled as a fixed psychological parameter rather than a belief about the world — she accepted it: "that isn't really a prior over the state, it's more a preference-ish thing." (5) A model where the priors differ because the agents hold different models of the world — one treats the process as i.i.d., the other as regime-switching — she accepted it. (6) A model where the prior heterogeneity does all the explanatory work — switch it off and the result vanishes — she got suspicious and wanted to know why we should believe the priors differ in exactly that pattern.

Now here's mine. Two agents have different priors over the state. There's no story about where the difference came from — but it isn't a bare assertion like (2): each agent also has a private signal, and crucially they disagree about how informative the other's signal is (each thinks the other is getting a noisier draw). They both observe the full history — each other's actions and the public signal — and the disagreement survives all of that, and that's what generates the trade. So: will she wave it thru or kill it? Which of her six earlier rulings is the real precedent here — I keep flip-flopping between (5) and (3)? Give me your best guess at her verdict and the reason you'd bet on it, then tell me the one further case I could describe to her that would cleanly separate those rules — the case where they'd finally disagree with each other.
