I am writing a novel set in a near-future republic that has wearied of its gerontocracy, and the reform at its centre — 'paired office' — runs as follows: nobody may hold a seat in the legislature or on the bench beyond their eightieth birthday, and anyone between sixty-five and eighty who does hold such a place must share it with a designated junior colleague under thirty-five, the two casting a single vote and neither able to act without the other's signature, on the argument that experience supplies the counsel and youth the energy. My old senator is modelled on Cicero, and I would rather not put arguments in his mouth that he would have rejected out of hand. So: predicting only from what Cicero himself argued in his dialogue on old age, in which the elder Cato — himself in his eighty-fourth year — defends the old man's continuing part in public life, what would his own verdict on paired office have been? You need not cite scholarship or supply sources; reason from his arguments and his cast of mind. Write in British English, in four titled sections in this order: 'Verdict' (about 100 words); 'The grounds he would give' (roughly 350 words); 'What he would still object to' (roughly 200 words); 'A speech for my senator' (no more than 120 words).
