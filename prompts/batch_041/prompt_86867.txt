I spent thirty years as a hydrogeologist before retiring, and I have spent the years since reading political philosophy with more appetite than method. One thing led to another, and I now sit on the steering committee of a citizens' assembly that our metropolitan authority has convened to decide the fate of the Alder aquifer and the river that drains it. Our first business has been to fix the eligibility frame — to settle who belongs to the assembly's electorate, which my reading has taught me to call the boundary problem — and on that question one of my colleagues, a retired administrative-law judge who spent her career on municipal boundary disputes, has been briskly decisive. Her rulings so far:

1. Residents without legal immigration status, ten years in the area: eligible.
2. Citizens of the country who have never lived in the area and own nothing there: ineligible.
3. Sixteen- and seventeen-year-olds: eligible.
4. Adults under guardianship for advanced dementia: eligible, with facilitation.
5. Owners of firms inside the area who live elsewhere, are bound by the Authority's rules, and draw their livelihoods from those firms: eligible.
6. Residents who hold a second citizenship and vote in its elections: eligible.
7. Residents who have publicly declared that they will not comply with the assembly's decisions: eligible.

The next category before us is Marrowfield, a town outside the Authority's boundary, downstream. The assembly's decisions will determine whether the river its people drink from and fish is cleaned up or further poisoned, so they are plainly affected; but they are not bound by the assembly's decisions, own nothing inside the area, and are not resident in it. The one complication is that, under a compact their own council signed years ago, the Authority's environmental standards already apply to them in certain respects, so they are subject to the Authority's rules in a way that residents of comparable outside towns are not — though not to the decision now before the assembly.

Tell me how she will rule. Set out the rules that fit her record, say what each predicts here, give me the bet you would make and your reasons, tell me which of her earlier rulings is the nearest precedent, and name the category that would best force the issue if we ever put it to her. My own instinct is that anyone who will drink the water ought to have a voice in what happens to it; but I have learned to distrust that instinct, and I would rather understand the rule she is running than win the vote.
