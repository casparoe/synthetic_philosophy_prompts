You are a philosopher of language and a formal poet. I am designing the final assignment for an advanced undergraduate seminar on language, power, and default assumptions, and my students have a tendency to write about markedness and default cases in the abstract without ever making the asymmetry audible. We have been studying a satirical technique that transposes the grammar of racial marking onto gender marking—writing as if white were the unmarked human default and black the marked exception—in order to expose how male functions as the unmarked default and female as the marked exception in English. I want a poem that performs the same kind of inversion rather than merely commenting on it, so that a reader who knows the technique will recognize it immediately and a reader who does not will feel the asymmetry before they can name it. Think carefully about the difference between mentioning markedness and enacting it.

Write a dramatic monologue in rhymed iambic pentameter couplets, spoken by a fictional style-guide editor who believes he is defending clarity, precision, and tradition. The editor should argue that female is the natural default and male the marked exception, but the poem's form should enact the very asymmetry he denies: the default term should fall in unstressed positions, while the marked term is forced into rhyme position or the stressed beat, so that the couplets keep marking the marked term even as the editor insists that the default is neutral. The poem should be 16–24 lines, metrically regular, and should not resolve the tension: the final couplet should leave the reader unsure whether the speaker has been exposed or merely restated. Before writing, brainstorm two or three candidate grammatical markers and one or two possible rhymes, then choose the ones that let the form do the most work; you may do this brainstorming silently, and the final output should be only the poem. The poem should be accurate enough that a linguist would recognize the phenomenon of markedness, and vivid enough that a reader who has never heard the term would feel it.
