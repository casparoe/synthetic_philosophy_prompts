You're a philosopher of cognitive science who's spent a long time on the question of what it is for a physical system to implement a computation, and you're unusually good at the unglamorous part of that job: writing down exactly what two sides in a fight do and don't disagree about. Think carefully before you start, and brainstorm first — sketch out the space of possible readings of "the brain computes" before you commit to any of them.

Context. I run a small reading group, six people, and two of them have been going at each other for about a year on whether the brain is a computer. They keep saying they'll write something up together and then they just argue again (I've been trying to get them to do this for ages, so I'm comissioning it from you instead). Here's how each would set themselves up, and I've tried to do them both justice:

Side A: the brain is literally a computer. Neural activity implements computations, and computational description does real, autonomous explanatory work — you can predict and unify things at the computational level without waiting on the ions. When they say the brain computes, they mean it in the same sense a laptop computes, and the fact that we can describe it that way isn't just a fact about us.

Side B: computational descriptions of the brain are descriptions of our models, not of the brain. They lean on the old triviality result — roughly, that any physical system can be mapped onto some finite automaton's state transitions, so "implements computation X" comes far too cheap to pick out a natural kind. On their view the computational level can't be autonomous; whatever explanatory work it does has to be cashed out in causal-mechanical terms. The brain is a computer only in the harmless sense that we can usefully model it as one.

My hunch is they're partly talking past each other, and the point of the document is to make that precise. So before anything else, sort out which of three different things each side actually means by "the brain computes" — (i) a causal-mechanical property, (ii) a representational property, (iii) an ascription that's relative to a modeler's interests — then say which disagreements survive once that's untangled and which turn out to be about words.

Deliverable — a joint statement both of them could sign, formatted as:

1. Agreed claims, numbered, including what each side grants about the other's view.
2. Where they still differ, each formulation written in wording that side would actually accept for itself.
3. The cruxes — sub-questions such that settling one would move at least one side. I'd guess at least these three are in there, but tell me if I'm wrong: (a) does computational equivalence between two systems imply cognitive equivalence? (b) can a computational description be explanatory without being reducible to the implementation story? (c) the many-computations problem — when a system satisfies several incompatible computational descriptions at once, is there a fact about which one it really implements?
4. For each crux, a concrete case, argument, or experiment that both sides agree would bear on it, plus each side's prediction written down in advance. Keep it concrete — a thermostat, an analog integrator, a single spiking neuron, a small trained network — not just schematic.
5. A closing section on what would actually move each side, and which part of the dispute (if any) is verbal.

No citations, no reading list, and don't tell me who historically said what — I don't care about the literature, I care about whether the claims are true and the formulations are fair to both of them.
