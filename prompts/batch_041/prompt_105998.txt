For context: I'm a backend engineer at a mid-size software company with no formal training in philosophy or economics, and I'm hoping you can help me adjudicate an argument I witnessed rather than one I took part in. Our employer runs a program in which each employee directs two thousand dollars a year of company money to a charity of their choosing, and last year it added an internal dashboard that scores participating organizations by an estimated cost per unit of good delivered. Anika, a data scientist on my team, thinks the dashboard should settle the matter and that anyone who overrides it is mostly buying themselves a pleasant feeling. Devin, who spent several years at a nonprofit before joining us, thinks the dashboard is not a neutral instrument but a filter that systematically favors interventions with short causal chains and individually attributable outcomes, and that it therefore starves work whose benefits are diffuse, delayed, or unattributable — his example being a small organization that helps city agencies publish and act on better operational data. Both of them are quick, neither was obviously confused, and they went six rounds over lunch. I've reproduced the exchange below as faithfully as I can.

1. Anika: If we're giving the money away regardless, the only live question is which grant does more good per dollar, and the dashboard is our best available answer to that. Funding the deworming charity is defensible; funding Devin's data outfit is a coin flip dressed up as a principle. 2. Devin: The dashboard doesn't measure good per dollar, it measures good per dollar of the kind we can count. A grant that changes how a city of two million buys and runs its software might do more than any deworming grant ever will, but there's no control group and no clean attribution, so it scores at or near zero. When your metric silently assigns zero to what it can't see, that isn't caution, it's error in a consistent direction. 3. Anika: I'll grant the metric is incomplete. But incomplete isn't the same as biased in a knowable direction, and 'we can't measure it' is not evidence that it's large. Every ineffective program in history has a story about diffuse long-run benefits. If his outfit is so promising, somebody should be able to put a rough range on its effect, even a very wide one, and the fact that nobody can is a reason for caution rather than a reason to give. 4. Devin: A wide range is exactly what I'd give it: it might do nothing, and it might change how a dozen cities operate for a decade. The dashboard can't hold that range at all, because it wants a point estimate per dollar per year, and structural work compounds — the returns land in year fifteen, not in the grant year. I'm not refusing to estimate; the shape of the estimate doesn't fit the format. And the bias compounds too: if the dashboard becomes how we give, next year's applicants will all be shaped to fit it. 5. Devin: And the same logic got our friend Ravi to take a job at an ad-tech firm he privately thinks is corrosive, on the grounds that the salary would fund more good than working at the nonprofit directly. He isn't confused about the arithmetic. I think the framework lets him pay off the thing he actually cares about with money and then stop worrying about it. 6. Anika: Or the framework is what let him notice the tension at all, twice — once when he took the job and again when he quit it. A 'follow what feels meaningful' ethic would have licensed the same job with a lot less self-scrutiny. And if the format is the problem rather than the scoring, we could let the dashboard accept ranges and long horizons; I'd still rather start from the thing we can defend than from the thing that moves us.

Please go through this move by move rather than giving me a general both-sides-have-a-point summary: for each of the six, say whether it's mistaken, defensible, or right, and briefly why. Then tell me which disagreement is the actual crux, whether that crux is an empirical question or a conceptual one, and what each of them is missing that the other isn't in a position to supply. Don't split the difference for the sake of balance — if one of them is clearly ahead on some point, say so plainly, and if the six moves don't hang together as a single disagreement, tell me where the two of them are really talking past each other.
