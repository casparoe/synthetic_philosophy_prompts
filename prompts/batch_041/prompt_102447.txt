I am a retired physicist — my field was relativistic cosmology — who has spent the last several years reading philosophy, and I have become particularly interested in Gödel's later work on time and relativity. I run a small seminar for advanced undergraduates and graduate students in philosophy and physics, about a dozen people, and I have ninety minutes for one session. I would like to design an in-class activity that makes them feel the force of Gödel's argument that in a rotating universe, time in the ordinary sense — the global, objective "now" that flows — is not a feature of reality.

I want them to appreciate the specific structure of Gödel's claim, which is stronger than the relativity of simultaneity from special relativity. In special relativity, different observers disagree about the simultaneity of distant events, but there is still a well-defined local time order and a global spacetime structure. In Gödel's rotating universe, the rotation is so thoroughgoing that there is no global time coordinate at all: any attempt to define one is arbitrary, and different choices are equally consistent with the local physics. The local causal order is well-defined, but there is no fact of the matter about which events are "now" together. Gödel took this to undermine the reality of time, and he connected it to a kind of idealism about time.

The activity should be a runnable game, role-play, or simulation. It should have clear rules, materials, roles, and a debrief. The point should emerge from what participants do, not from what I say. They should run into the problem themselves — the impossibility of establishing a global time — before it is named. I want them to try to do something reasonable, fail, try again differently, and only then see that the failure is not a failure of ingenuity but a feature of the situation. I want them to feel the vertigo of a universe without a global now, and then to feel the temptation to impose one anyway, and then to see that the imposition is arbitrary.

The debrief should map the moments of the activity onto Gödel's argument, tell me what will predictably go wrong when I run it and how to handle it, and be honest about which features of Gödel's view the activity cannot reproduce. I am particularly interested in the step from "no global time" to "time is unreal," and I want the debrief to be frank about how much of that step the activity can carry and how much I will have to supply.

I am not a philosopher by training, and I am still learning the vocabulary, so I would appreciate it if the activity and debrief could be framed in terms that a physicist would find natural, without sacrificing the philosophical point. I don't want a reading list or citations; the activity should be self-contained. Also, please write your response in continuous prose — no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables. Write it as you would a letter to a colleague.
