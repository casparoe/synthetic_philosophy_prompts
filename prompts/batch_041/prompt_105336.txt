I am presenting a paper at a small political theory workshop next month and would like a preparation sheet for the discussion period. My thesis is that the will of the people is a category mistake whenever it is invoked as an independent ground of democratic justification. The argument turns on a distinction between two things that democratic rhetoric habitually runs together. The first is the aggregate preference: the distribution of individual preferences across a population, which is a real and in principle measurable fact. The second is a popular will: a single intention properly attributed to a collective agent, in the way an intention is attributed to a person. My claim is that the characteristic populist move — I call it the ventriloquist move — is to infer the second from the first: because a majority prefers X, it is said that the people will X, and this will, being the will of the sovereign, is held to trump the objections of any individual or institution. I argue that no such inference is available, and I propose a test that any claim of the form "the people will X" must pass, which I call the dissent test: the claim must specify what it does with the citizens who do not will X, without either counting them among the people and so contradicting itself, or expelling them from the people and so forfeiting its majoritarian premise. I take the test to be failed by the familiar reply that the dissenters are merely mistaken about their own interests, though I expect to be pressed on whether an informed dissenter changes the verdict.

The audience is small and I know roughly who will be there. One senior scholar works on Rousseau and the general will, and will treat my use of "will" as a naive appropriation of a concept he takes to be doing something quite different. Two deliberative democrats in the Habermasian tradition will ask whether my account leaves any room for the rational formation of a common will through discourse, rather than the mere aggregation I accuse populism of. One quantitative researcher on populist voting is impatient with conceptual argument and will want to know what difference my distinction makes to any empirical finding. One historian of political thought will worry that I am reading a contemporary phenomenon back into older vocabulary. And one graduate student, well read in the recent normative literature on populism, will raise the meta-question of whether my objection is to populism or to democracy as such.

Please draft the preparation sheet. I want the eight or so objections I will actually get from this room, ordered by how likely they are and how much damage they would do, each phrased in the vocabulary of the person likely to raise it, with a two-minute reply and a candid note on where that reply is weakest. Tell me which objections I should concede before I am forced to, which I must resist at all costs, and identify the single exchange on which the paper's reception will turn. Finally, include the question I am hoping nobody asks.
