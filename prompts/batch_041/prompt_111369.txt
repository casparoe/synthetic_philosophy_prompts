I recently read an editor's introduction to a collection of essays on the ethics of belief, and its thumbnail sketch of the James–Clifford disagreement left me uneasy. Reconstructed as faithfully as I can, the summary runs something like this.

Clifford and James disagreed about whether we may believe something without sufficient evidence. Clifford's principle — that belief ought to be proportioned to the evidence — is meant to be exceptionless: he holds that it is wrong in every case, for every person, to believe anything on insufficient evidence, since beliefs are not private possessions but are acted upon and passed on to others, and so the credulous believer is a public hazard. To illustrate, the editor offers Clifford's shipowner, who sends a vessel to sea on the strength of a sincere but unfounded conviction that it is seaworthy, and remarks that Clifford's point is that the man is culpable because of the harm his belief causes. James replies that when an option is live, forced, and momentous, and when the evidence cannot settle it, we have the right to believe whatever our emotional nature prefers; such a choice is not really an intellectual one at all, so the intellect has no authority over it. James is thus defending wishful thinking — or at least the liberty to believe whatever makes life bearable — while Clifford stands for stern, unbending evidentialism. The two are flatly opposed: Clifford treats the ethics of belief as absolute, whereas James, denying that there are any epistemic duties, reduces belief to a matter of what it pays us to hold.

How fair is this? Please go through it clause by clause and separate what a careful reader of both essays would accept, what is a defensible simplification, and what is a genuine misrepresentation — naming the kind of error where you can, whether a conflation of two claims the position keeps apart, a modest thesis overstated, a familiar objection presented as a refutation, or a technical term used loosely. I would particularly like to know: first, whether the editor states James's condition accurately in saying the evidence "cannot settle" the question, or whether that blurs a distinction James is careful to draw; second, whether it is right that James abandons epistemic duty altogether; and third, whether the two positions are as flatly opposed as the editor claims, or whether there is common ground the sketch erases. Where the summary distorts a view, say what the view actually holds and whether the distortion runs in one side's favor. I am inclined to think the sketch is harder on James than the texts warrant, but I cannot locate where the unfairness begins, and I would not be surprised to be wrong.

The editor ends by drawing a moral: that the exchange shows the evidentialist and the pragmatist about belief simply talk past each other. Does that conclusion survive your corrections?
