There's a strike brewing on our campus and it's split my department down the middle, so I've spent the week hearing the same arguement reheated at every coffee break. I teach a seminar on the ethics of work and I'm thinking of using a version of this as a case study, but first I want to see how a model handles it — I genuinely couldn't work out who had the better of it, and I'm not sure it's a case where 'they're both partly right' is an honest answer.

The setup. Our maintenance and facilities staff — the people who keep the boilers, the fume cupboards and the labs' gas lines running — have voted for industrial action. The odd thing is that their central demand isn't about pay at all. It's that the university divest its endowment from fossil fuels and publish a plan with dates. Their tactic is a rolling one: on any given day one trade is out (electricians Monday, plumbers Tuesday, and so on), and on the days they're in they work strictly to contract — no overtime, no covering for each other, no favours. Management calls it a rolling nuisance; the union calls it the least disruptive way to keep pressure on without losing a week's wages.

Two colleagues went at it about this. Roughly how it went:

1. X, a labour economist, said the whole thing is illegitimate because it's a political strike dressed up as an industrial one. The university isn't the body that decides national energy policy, so the workers aren't negotiating with anyone who can give them what they want; they're using economic leverage to force an employer to adopt a political position. And leverage of that kind — 'do as we say or we'll make your buildings cold' — is coercion, whatever the cause behind it. We don't let firms do it to each other, and we shouldn't let unions do it to universities.

2. Y, a political philosopher, said 'terms of employment' and 'politics' can't be pulled apart like that. Pay, pensions, the state of the sector — all of it is set politically; the endowment is a decision the employer controls that workers have a stake in, so it belongs on the table. And she said 'coercion' was doing all the work in X's argument: withholding your labour is declining to sell something, and declining to sell isn't coercion. The university is free to hire other people. If it can't, that's a fact about the labour market, not a wrong done by the workers.

3. X said the 'declining to sell' line ignores what a strike is for: a coordinated refusal, aimed at inflicting enough disruption that the other side gives in. That's what coercion means. And the freedom to hire others is a fiction here — the strike works precisely because replacement is slow and expensive. He added a seperate point: even granting every individual right in the world, a union is a cartel. It's an agreement among suppliers to withhold a good in order to raise its price, and competition law forbids exactly that when firms do it. That unions are exempt from competition law is a political settlement, not a moral argument.

4. Y said the cartel analogy breaks on something that matters: a firm withholds a commodity it owns and can warehouse indefinitely; a worker withholds themselves and can't. Labour isn't separable from the person doing it, which is why the law has never been comfortable treating it as a commodity — you can't sell your labour without selling your time, and your time is your life. And if the university is the dominant buyer of pipefitters in a small city, the union isn't a cartel, it's the only thing on the other side of the scales. Whether a strike is justified depends on the market, not on the form of the action.

5. X said that if the right to strike depends on market structure, then it isn't a right, it's a policy that happens to be useful in bad markets — and he's happy to grant it there. But it's a right against the employer, not a licence to hurt third parties. This action is designed to hurt students — exams, lab sessions, cold rooms in the middle of a marking period — and those students never agreed to anything, can't exit, and get no vote. He said the rolling and partial form makes it worse rather than better: you keep the job, you keep the pay for the days you're in, and you choose the timing, so the cost lands on whoever can't plan around it. If you're going to withdraw your labour, withdraw it; don't stage a nuisance on a rota.

6. Y said that principle proves far too much. Every strike imposes costs on people who aren't the employer — the bus strike strands the nurse, the bin strike leaves the rats. If third-party inconvenience were disqualifying, nothing would ever be justified, so either X's principle is empty or it's an argument against all strikes. And the partial-strike point gets the contract backwards: work-to-rule withdraws discretionary effort, and the employer was never entitled to your goodwill, only to what you signed up for. There's no such thing as a fully complete withdrawal of labour short of resignation, so 'all or nothing' isn't a line, it's just a preference for the more visible kind of harm.

Please adjudicate this move by move. For each of the six, say whether it's sound, sound but overstated, or mistaken, and why, in a few sentences each. Then tell me: is either of them answering a different question from the other? Is the political/industrial distinction doing real work here or is it a red herring? Does any version of the cartel objection survive Y's reply in move 4? And who has the better of it overall — I want a verdict with reasons, not a both-sides summary. If there's an important consideration neither of them raised, put it at the end. No citations or reading lists, please, I'll do the reading myself. British English.
