I have been reading a general book on paradoxes, and its chapter on Thomson's lamp strikes me as possibly unfair both to the paradox and to the reply that is usually made to it. I should like you to audit the chapter's summary.

Its set-up is this: a lamp is on at the start; a mechanism switches it off after half a minute, on again a quarter of a minute later, off again after an eighth of a minute, and so on, each interval half the previous one, so that by the end of the minute the lamp has been switched infinitely many times. The chapter's reconstruction of the argument is that at the end of the minute the lamp must be either on or off, but neither is possible: it cannot be on, since being on would require a last switch-on, and every switch-on is followed by a switch-off before the minute is out; and it cannot be off, for the same reason with the roles reversed. Since on and off exhaust the possibilities, the description is contradictory, and the very idea of a supertask — an infinite sequence of operations completed in a finite time — is therefore incoherent.

The chapter then reports the standard reply, which it says is that the lamp's final state is the limit of the sequence of states it runs through, and that since on, off, on, off ... has no limit, the lamp is in no state at all at the end of the minute. The description, on this view, fails to determine the lamp's final state; and the chapter takes this to confirm the original verdict, concluding that the case is not a puzzle about lamps but a proof that infinite sequences of tasks cannot be completed in a finite time — which is also, it says, why Zeno's paradoxes of motion were never really solved. It adds, as an aside, that the lamp would in any case be physically impossible, since no real switch can be driven to operate at ever shorter intervals without limit, and, as a further moral, that the parity of the number of switches would settle the matter if infinity had a parity, which it does not.

I am inclined to think that there must be a fact of the matter about whether the bulb is lit at the end — a lamp is either on or off — and so I am half-persuaded by the chapter's conclusion. But I have also heard that the standard reply is usually read as refuting the argument rather than confirming it, and I cannot tell whether the chapter is entitled to say that the final state is 'undefined'.

Please go through the summary sentence by sentence and tell me what a philosopher working on supertasks would accept, what they would wince at as a simplification, and what is plainly wrong. In particular, I want to know whether 'the description does not determine the final state' and 'there is no fact of the matter about the final state' come to the same thing; whether the parity remark and the physical-impossibility aside do any work; and whether the chapter's conclusion — that the case shows supertasks to be impossible and disposes of Zeno — follows from anything it has established, or whether it survives only because the reply has been misdescribed.
