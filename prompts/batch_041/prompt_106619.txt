I am assembling materials for the practical-reason unit of an advanced undergraduate seminar, and a colleague in the economics department has sent me the following paragraph, which he proposes we distribute as background on the sunk cost fallacy. I would like you to audit it as I would audit a draft I intend to teach from: not as a grader assigning a mark, but as someone trying to decide what to keep, what to correct in the margin, and what to warn students against repeating.

The paragraph reads:

"Economists regard the sunk cost fallacy as the clearest case of irrationality there is. A sunk cost is money, time, or effort already spent and impossible to recover; since it is gone, it ought to play no role whatsoever in a decision. Any decision that takes a sunk cost into account is therefore, by definition, a mistake — and the frequency with which people make such decisions, staying to the end of a film they are not enjoying because of the ticket price, or sinking more money into a failing venture because of what they have already put in, only shows how deeply irrational we are. The lesson is simple and universal: disregard the past and weigh only the future. A theory of rationality that permits the honoring of sunk costs is not a theory of rationality at all."

I suspect the paragraph is right in spirit and wrong in several of its words, but my colleague knows the formal apparatus better than I do, and I do not want to correct him from ignorance. Go sentence by sentence, and for each tell me what a careful decision theorist would endorse as written, what they would want to qualify, and what is simply false. I am particularly interested in whether the "by definition" clause and the closing claim that no theory permitting this deserves the name of rationality survive the distinctions, and in whether there are recognizable settings — I am thinking of cases that turn on self-control and precommitment, and cases in which past expenditure carries information about the future — in which honoring a sunk cost is defensible rather than confused. Finish by telling me which sentences a defender could retain verbatim, which need rewriting, and what the corrected lesson for my students would say.
