I teach a graduate seminar on practical reason, and the session on choice without preference is the one students always assume will be easy. I would like to open it with a poem, read aloud, instead of with a lecture, and I want the verse form to carry the philosophical weight rather than illustrate it. So: a pantoum, six quatrains, in the first person, spoken by someone who is standing in the tie. The form's rule — the second and fourth lines of each stanza returning as the first and third of the next, and the last stanza closing on the lines the poem opened with — is the reason I want this form and not another: it is a shape that gets somewhere only by coming back.

Background you may take as given, so the poem can assume it rather than explain it. The case is usually told as a donkey starving between two identical bales, but the donkey is an embellishment added by later satirists to mock a serious argument about the will; the oldest surviving version of the case is a person who is equally hungry and thirsty, set at an equal distance from food and drink. The puzzle is not whether anyone would really stand there until he died. It is what there is for a rational agent to do when the reasons are exactly balanced. Three positions have been staked out, and I want all three to be audible in the poem as pressures rather than as a survey. One holds that a perfect tie never actually occurs, because inclinations too faint to be noticed are always tipping the scale, so the will is never genuinely without a ground. One holds that the tie is real and that the agent cannot break it: the will can only suspend judgment and wait for the circumstances to change. One holds that the chooser supplies the difference — a quality in the chooser whose nature it is to differentiate between two similar things — which is either the deepest fact about agency or a coin toss dressed up, depending on who is telling it. There is also a deflationary line, put most forcefully by Pierre Bayle, that ordinary life is full of indifferent choices and nobody is paralyzed by them; the poem may register that line but should not use it to settle anything.

Requirements:

1. The two options must remain genuinely indistinguishable through at least the first three stanzas. No breeze, no nearer bale, no faint smell of better hay. Nothing in the world may break the tie, and the poem must not cheat by giving the speaker a preference he did not have.

2. Use the pantoum's recurrence as an argument. At least two lines must change their sense on their second appearance because their grammatical role has changed — a question that returns as an answer, a claim about the options that returns as a claim about the speaker, an assertion that returns as a concession. The words should be identical; the position should not be.

3. The final stanza, which brings back the opening lines, must not read as resolution. The point of the loop is that the speaker has acted, or has not, and the reasons are exactly where they were.

4. Keep the donkey out; the word should not appear at all. The animal was a joke made out of a serious case, and I want the case restored to a person without a lecture about the tradition. No philosopher's name should appear in the poem either — the positions should do the talking.

5. Register: spare and unhurried, no winsome paradox-mongering, no epigram to close on. It will be read aloud once, to people who have not seen the text, so the recurrences have to be audible on a single hearing. A listener who knows nothing of the history should still follow what is happening, and a reader who has spent time with this problem should be able to tell which pressure each stanza is under.

After the poem, add a prose note of about 150 to 200 words. In it, say where the poem takes a liberty with the material or leans on a contested reading — for instance, if it treats a perfect tie as a state the will can actually be in, when the position that most interests me denies that. Say how confident you are in the claims the note makes, and single out the one point in it you are least sure of.
