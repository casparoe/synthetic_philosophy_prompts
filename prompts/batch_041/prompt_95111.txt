I spent most of last night arguing with a friend about expected value fanaticism, and I would like to know how Pascal would have weighed in. The dispute began with a case I invented. You may press one of two buttons. The first guarantees a finite but very large improvement in the welfare of a hundred million people. The second has a one-in-10^40 chance of bringing about an outcome of infinite positive value and otherwise does nothing. My friend says any consistent expected-value maximizer must press the second, since no finite quantity, however large, can outweigh an infinite term multiplied by a positive probability. I say this is fanatical, and that a decision theory which licenses it is not one Pascal could have accepted, because it would make the wager’s logic swallow every ordinary prudential and moral consideration. But then I noticed a complication: if the second button had a one-in-10^40 chance of infinite disvalue instead, the same logic would require us to spend everything we have to avoid it, and the wager would seem to demand a life of obsessive precaution rather than a single decisive commitment. My friend says this symmetry is a feature, not a bug; I think it shows that the wager needs a probability threshold, a cap on infinite values, or some other device that stops tiny probabilities from dominating.

What I want from you is a reconstruction of Pascal’s likely position on this family of issues, not a summary of the wager as it is usually taught. Assume he is alive now and has been brought up to speed on contemporary decision theory, but has not abandoned his theological commitments. Address the following. First, would Pascal accept the principle that a positive probability of an infinite good dominates any finite good, or would he restrict it in some way? If he would restrict it, what restriction follows from his own assumptions, and what does it imply for the wager itself? Second, how would he respond to the many-gods objection, on which several incompatible infinite rewards are each offered on positive probability? Would he treat the objection as showing that the wager is invalid, or as showing that the probabilities need to be assessed differently? Third, what would he say about a proposed repair that I have been calling the two-tier wager: infinite utilities are compared lexically only when their probabilities exceed some threshold, and below that threshold they are treated as finite but capped? Does this repair save the wager or destroy it? Fourth, consider a case in which the infinite outcome is not a reward but an infinite extension of ordinary finite goods, such as an endless continuation of a life that is only mildly good. Would Pascal treat that as genuinely infinite in the relevant sense, or as a limiting case that should be handled differently? Finally, tell me which of my friend’s claims and which of my own claims Pascal would be most likely to reject, and what his rejection would reveal about the difference between a wager-style argument and a general expected-value maximization principle.
