I have been in a reading group for years that keeps returning to the same argument about democratic backsliding, and by now it has the texture of a debate that cannot move. What I want is a diagnosis of why, not a verdict on the cases.

The erosion side holds that backsliding is a real and identifiable process: a government wins an election, then legally rewrites the rules so that it keeps winning — court capture, media capture, changes to franchise and districting, the slow replacement of neutral administration with loyalists. On this view the phenomenon is defined by a widening gap between the de jure forms (elections, constitutions, courts) and de facto power, and the absence of drama is the point: no coup, no tanks, only a sequence of individually defensible legal steps. Hungary after 2010, Poland after 2015, Turkey around the 2017 constitutional change, and the United States around 2020 are the standing exhibits.

The skeptic side holds that the term presupposes liberal democracy as the baseline and smuggles an evaluation into a description. The same trajectory — a popular majority using its mandate to override veto points held by unelected bodies — is called consolidation or reform when it moves toward policies the observer favors. The term is also asymmetric: there is no comparably sharp word for the mirror-image process, and a court blocking a legislature is not said to be backsliding. The skeptic's challenge is: name the case where you would apply the word against your own side, and I will believe it is a concept rather than a team jersey. Poland after 2023 sharpened this, since the attempt to reverse the earlier changes immediately raised the question of what undoing backsliding is even supposed to look like.

My own hypothesis, held loosely, is that the two sides are not disagreeing about facts: that democracy names a procedure for one and a bundle of goods for the other, and that the empirical quarrel is downstream of that. But I have also wondered whether they are simply answering different questions under one word — how much has changed, measurably? versus should we be alarmed, and what should we do? — in which case the impasse is a category error rather than a deep disagreement.

So: verbal dispute, substantive disagreement, or two questions under one heading? Is a hidden empirical or formal question doing the work — for instance, whether a backsliding measure predicts or explains anything its components do not already? Is the term a thick concept in the way cruelty is, descriptive and evaluative at once, and if so is that a defect or just how political vocabulary works? What would count as progress here — a distinction, an operationalization, a decisive case, a change of question — or should none be expected, because the dispute bottoms out in values neither side can argue for? And if you taboo the word backsliding and restate what each side actually claims without it, how much of the disagreement survives?
