I chair the algorithmic-accountability committee of my county's criminal-justice coordinating council, and in three weeks we must decide which pretrial risk-assessment instrument the county will adopt and on what terms. The finalists score each defendant from 0 to 10 for the likelihood of failing to appear or being rearrested before trial; judges see the score plus a one-line recommendation. The defense bar argues that the leading instrument is racially biased, because Black defendants are scored high-risk at roughly twice the rate of white defendants. The sheriff and the county attorney counter that the instrument is accurate in the sense that matters: among defendants who receive the same score, the two groups go on to reoffend at the same rate, so a 7 really does mean a 7 for everyone. Our statistician then told us something I cannot get past. When the two groups differ in their underlying base rates, no score can be at once calibrated in that sense and balanced in its error rates — no tool can both mean the same thing at a given score within each group and make its false-positive and false-negative mistakes equally often across groups, except in the limiting case of a perfect predictor; tightening one criterion necessarily loosens the other. So the choice is real, and whichever way we go, someone has a legitimate grievance.

Please draft the written opinion I will circulate before the vote: a clear recommendation about which criterion the county should adopt and why, what we should require of the vendor and of the judges to honor it in practice, and a concrete timetable for revisiting the decision once we have outcome data. The committee is split. Some members want to reject the instrument outright because of the disparity; others argue that rejecting a tool more accurate than judicial intuition will cost more Black defendants their liberty, not less, since the alternative is the unguided discretion we know to be worse. Your memo has to take the strongest of those opposing arguments seriously and say plainly why it does not win — or concede that it does and recommend accordingly.

Finally, tell me what we would be committing ourselves to philosophically, not merely operationally. When we announce to the public that our tool is fair, what are we actually claiming, and what are we quietly deciding that fairness does not require? I am not a statistician, and I will have to defend this document in open session, so write it so that a lay reader can follow the reasoning and see exactly where the value judgment enters rather than hiding behind the mathematics.
