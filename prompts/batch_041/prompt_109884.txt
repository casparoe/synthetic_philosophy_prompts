Movie night with my econ reading group and we're doing The Big Short, and I want to frame the discussion around the no-trade theorem — the Milgrom-Stokey result that once the structure of the market is common knowledge, a bid all by itself leaks the bidder's private information and gets priced in, so nobody holding private info can profit, plus the corollary that a mutually beneficial trade cant be common knowledge when priors are shared and the starting allocation is already efficient. The film is nothing but people buying mortgage insurance that the banks are thrilled to sell them: Burry reading prospectuses in his office, Baums team in Florida hearing about a stripper with five mortgages, everyone convinced the other side is blind. So is this a counterexample to the theorem or a tour of its escape hatches? The banks werent sitting on different information so much as different models and different incentives — fees now, risk moved off the books, a bet they thought they couldnt lose — and the traders only got paid because of the bailout, so being right wasnt sufficient. Take both sides seriously: where the theorem is the better guide (that cheap insurance should itself have been information about the seller, and Burry treats the price as noise instead), where the film resists the reading, and what the film shows about prices as aggregators of belief that the theorem alone doesnt. Keep it readable, we're not economists.
