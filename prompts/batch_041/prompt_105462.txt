Design a self-contained case that puts the Frege–Geach problem for expressivism under maximum strain — the problem, that is, of explaining how a normative sentence can mean the same thing when asserted and when it occurs unasserted: negated, embedded in a conditional, or in a disjunction. The case should be a brief stretch of ordinary argument (a short exchange between two people will do) in which one and the same normative sentence appears asserted, negated, and as the antecedent of a conditional, and in which the surrounding inference is valid only if the sentence carries the same meaning in all three positions. Two design constraints, both intended to take the case past the textbook examples: the normative predicate must be a scalar one (cowardly, rude, generous) rather than a flat one such as wrong, and the case must include a comparative use of that predicate and at least one inference whose other premise is plainly non-normative. After the scenario, set out what an expressivist who identifies the meaning of the sentence with the attitude it expresses must say about each occurrence, and show why the scalar and comparative occurrences are precisely the ones that resist the usual reply that the same attitude is present throughout, merely in a different mode. Then say whether an expressivist who identifies meaning with inferential role fares any better, or only relocates the difficulty. Close by naming the single feature of the case that does the work, what becomes of the case once that feature is dropped, and the strongest line of defence open to the expressivist.
