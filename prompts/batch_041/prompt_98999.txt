For most of my career I worked on the immune system, and since retiring I have been reading philosophy with more appetite than discipline. I have become interested in the family of views, often gathered under the label ubuntu, according to which a person is not first an isolated individual who then enters into relations, but rather becomes a person through those relations — that personhood is in some sense conferred by, or constituted by, one's standing within a community. I would like you to write a dialogue between two philosophers who hold opposing positions on this, and I want both of them to be genuinely competent, so that neither reads as a foil for the other.

One of them should defend the relational account as the correct account of what a person is. The other should defend an individualist account — that personhood is intrinsic to the individual, grounded, say, in psychological continuity or in the capacities of the organism — and should argue that making personhood depend on others' recognition is both false and morally dangerous, since it leaves the isolated, the ostracized, and the cognitively disabled hostage to the attitudes of those around them.

Please have them work through a specific case rather than trading generalities. The case I keep returning to is this: a woman is the last surviving member of a small community whose language and customs have died with the others, so that she is now the only person who holds the relations that the relational view says constitute her personhood. Does the relational account imply that her personhood is diminished or merely unrecognized, or does it have the resources to say that she remains fully a person? Let the individualist press this hard, and let the relationalist give a serious answer rather than a dodge.

I would also like one of them to raise something from my old field, because I suspect it is more than a curiosity. Fetal microchimerism: cells from a fetus can persist in the mother's body, including her brain, for decades after birth, and cells from the mother persist in her children, so that many of us are, in a plain biological sense, cellular mosaics of more than one individual. Have them disagree about whether this is relevant — whether it shows that the biological individual is already a composite rather than a bounded unit, or whether it is a red herring, on the grounds that cells are not the sort of thing that could constitute a person's moral standing.

Keep it a real exchange: each should answer the other's actual points, concede where a concession is warranted, and press where pressing is warranted. No strawmen, no lecture transcripts, and no citations or reading lists — I want the ideas argued rather than attributed.
