I retired three years ago after thirty-six years as an experimental physicist, most of them spent building instruments, and I have since been working my way through the philosophy of science that I never had time to read while I was doing it. One issue has resisted me. My entire professional formation assumed that evidence selects among theories, and that when two theories survive every test we can devise, that is a fact about the state of our instruments rather than about the world. I am told this assumption is naive, but when I press the point the discussion tends to turn into a dispute about what counts as a distinct theory, and I lose my footing.

I would like a single number from you, followed by the reasoning that produced it, on this proposition:

Some mature empirical science contains a pair of rival theories, T1 and T2, such that (a) no body of evidence that could be obtained in principle would give a rational agent grounds to prefer either one, and (b) T1 and T2 are not merely notational variants — they differ substantively in what they claim about the parts of the world we cannot observe.

Give me your credence that this proposition is true. Then decompose it. I want the sub-questions your number rests on, a rough probability for each, and a line of justification for each: whether empirical equivalence can be characterised without either trivialising the claim or begging the question; whether empirical equivalence should be relativised to finite agents, who must actually compute an answer, or defined against an idealised reasoner with unlimited resources, and how much turns on which we choose; whether any actual historical case survives scrutiny as permanent rather than merely temporary equivalence; whether the holism of testing, and the associated worry that meaning shifts with theory, collapses the proposition into a different and less interesting claim; and whatever further terms you think belong. Show me how the terms combine, and tell me which of them your final number is most sensitive to.

There is one sub-question I want handled explicitly, because it is what prompted this letter. Suppose two formulations of the same subject matter yield exactly the same predictions but differ sharply in how hard they are to use: one returns an answer to any well-posed question in a page of algebra, the other is perfectly well defined and computationally intractable for every case anyone cares about. No evidence bears on the choice between them, and they are not notational variants, since they differ in what they permit a working scientist to do. Should that count as underdetermination of theory by evidence, or does it fall outside the concept altogether — and whichever way you answer, does your overall number move? My own instinct is that a theory's being unusable is not a fact about the world, and yet I notice that I choose between such formulations every day and that my choices are not arbitrary. I would like to know whether that instinct is doing philosophical work or merely autobiographical work.

For what it is worth, my own credence in the proposition is about 0.2, and it has not moved in a year. If yours differs materially, tell me where you think the disagreement actually lives.
