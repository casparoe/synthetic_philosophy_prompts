I did 34 years as an experimental physicist, retired now, and for the past two years ive been reading philosophy on my own — mostly decision theory and moral philosophy, which is probably why my wife says I've gotten unbearable at dinner.

Here's what's bugging me. In my old line of work we taught, and I taught it to grad students, that when you face a gamble you multiply each outcome by its probability, add it all up, and if the number comes out positive you take the bet — expected value, full stop. Clean, obvious, used it for everything from detector design to which grants to chase.

But then look at how the world actually handles risk and nobody behaves that way. An insurance company charges you more than your expected loss, and it caps what it will pay out — it will not, at any price, sell you a policy with genuinely unlimited liability. A casino puts a maximum on the table. And the St. Petersburg lottery — the one where the prize doubles every time the coin comes up tails and you keep flipping, so the expected payout is infinite — nobody will sell you that ticket at any finite price, and everyone agrees refusing is the sane thing to do.

So there's a practice here — call it capping your exposure, or paying above expected loss for insurance — that every real institution follows, and it looks flatly inconsistent with the expected-value rule I was raised on. My question is what that practice actually presupposes about value. Does it require believing utility is bounded, that there's a ceiling on how good anything can get for a person? Or only that utility curves off — the old diminishing-marginal-utility idea, the 1000th dollar worth less than the first? Or could it be that we just don't weight tiny probabilities straight, treating a one-in-a-million chance as effectively zero? Or that we discount the future? I can see the practice surviving on any of those, which is part of why I'm stuck.

Reconstruct the view that the practice can't do without. Then separate what it strictly needs from what it merely sits comfortably with — I suspect those are different things and people blur them. And tell me where the practice is underdetermined: could several different theories of value all license the same capping behavior, so the practice doesn't actually decide between them?

One more thing I cant make up my mind on. The same actuaries who cap everything still talk about expected losses and expected returns all day long, as if EV were fine. Is that just bookkeeping convenience, or is the practice committed to EV being right in the bulk of the distribution and wrong out in the tail? If the latter, that's a strange hybrid view and I'd like to know whether it even hangs together.
