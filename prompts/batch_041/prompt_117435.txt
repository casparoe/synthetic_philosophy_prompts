I'm a features writer at a general-interest ideas magazine, midway through a long piece on karma as a theory of moral desert — on whether the doctrine can really do the work that desert theories of punishment are supposed to do, and why nearly everyone I interview seems to be answering a question adjacent to the one I asked. A philosopher of punishment I spoke with keeps treating karma as a retributive ledger; a scholar of Buddhist ethics tells me there is no ledger and no one to keep it; a friend who teaches yoga describes it as a kind of physics that has nothing to do with deserving. I have stopped believing these are disagreements about a single claim, and I want a taxonomy that shows me where the joints actually are. Please build it as a decision tree whose branches are questions rather than labels and whose leaves are positions: every internal node should be a question a reader can answer about a concrete case without already knowing which tradition the answer leads to, and every leaf should carry a name for the position, or the word unnamed where I would be inventing the occupant. At the end I want the unoccupied branches listed and diagnosed — is nobody there because the combination is incoherent, because it is unattractive, or merely because nobody has bothered — and any two leaves your tree cannot actually separate flagged as such rather than papered over. It is written for readers who know the analytic literature on desert and punishment but not South Asian philosophy, so the questions must be self-contained; assume no prior vocabulary.

Build the tree around what I have come to think are the load-bearing commitments. First: is the relation between deed and outcome one of fittingness — the outcome is deserved — or only of causation, with the moral vocabulary doing no work at all? Second: if fittingness is real, is it intrinsic to the deed-outcome pair, or conferred by something that appoints it, whether a judge, a law with a purpose, or a magistrate of the cosmos? Third: does the account require a persisting subject who both performs the deed and undergoes the outcome, and if it does not, what is left of the word desert? Fourth: is the fit exact, proportional, and exhaustively determined, or rough and defeasible? Two further questions cut across these: whether the mechanism tracks intention or mere outcome, and whether the fit can be escaped or reassigned — by insight, by devotion, by merit transferred to another — since a desert claim that can be voided or shifted is doing something different from one that cannot.

For the cases, use ones that force a choice rather than invite agreement: the donor who gives generously and entirely for the sake of being seen; the child born with a condition no one chose; the soldier who kills under orders he believes unjust; the practitioner said to have ended the cycle by insight alone; the family that suffers for something an ancestor did; and the cheerful scoundrel who dies old and unpunished. Each branch has to earn its place by separating at least two leaves on one of these cases, and no question may take the form of asking whether karma is real. I want at least one leaf that denies karma is a desert theory at all — a purely causal or psychological reading in which the word deserved never appears — one that keeps desert real while treating the deserver as a fiction of convention, taking the no-self problem seriously instead of dissolving it; one that makes the fit exact and mechanically determined; one on which the residue is a subtle material stuff adhering to the soul, so that the relation is nearly physical rather than juridical; and one on which the fit is real but transferable or voidable. If the causal reading and the psychologized one turn out to be the same leaf under two descriptions, say so; if the tree cannot distinguish the reading on which karma is an impersonal law from the reading on which a divine apportioner dispenses its fruits, tell me which case would be needed to split them and why I probably cannot use it. I need no texts, authors, or dates — where you are unsure which tradition owns a leaf, give the position a descriptive name rather than a school label and let the tree stand on the cases. Twelve leaves at most, plus a short closing note on what an instrument of this shape is structurally unable to sort.
