I've been chewing on Maimonides' take on the animal sacrifices and I want you to push it somewhere he never went. The basic idea, as I understand it: the sacrificial system wasn't God's ideal from the start, it was a concession. The Israelites were soaked in a world where you slaughtered animals to feed your gods, so rather than ask them to give the whole thing up cold turkey, the law took a practice they were already devoted to and bent it toward the one God, hoping to wean them off it gradually. So underneath it is a theory about how to legislate for people as they actually are, not as you'd wish them to be. Now jump him forward to our world — industrial farms, billions of animals, meat so cheap that basically nobody needs it to survive anymore, a real animal-rights movement making a serious case, and lab-grown meat starting to become viable. What does his own logic make him say? Does he read our meat-eating the way he read the ancient sacrifices, as something a wise legislator tolerates and quietly redirects rather than outlaws, in which case cultured meat starts to look like the perfect Maimonidean solution — the sacrifice without the suffering — or does that miss what the practice was actually for? Or does he say the anology collapses, because the sacrifices were bound up with the Temple and the Temple's gone, so meat-eating is a different beast entirely? And while you're at it: there are people today who want to rebuild the Temple and restart the sacrifices. By his own lights, is that a step forward, or a regression back into the very concession he thought we were being weaned off of? I want you to actually argue this out from his principles instead of just reporting what he said about the ancients, and to be honest about where his accommodationism proves too much or runs out of road. Keep it as continuous prose, no headings, bullet points, bold, or tables — just talk it through the way you would to a friend.
