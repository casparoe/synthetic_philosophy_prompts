Next week I have the last ninety-minute meeting of an upper-level seminar in formal epistemology — fifteen students, all of them comfortable with Bayes' rule and the probability axioms. The session is on uncertain evidence, and I would like to spend the whole of it on a single in-class activity rather than on exposition.

The target is a feature of Jeffrey conditionalization that I have never managed to convey convincingly from the front of the room. The rule, as I will describe it afterwards, tells you to respond to an experience that leaves you uncertain among the cells of a partition by taking your new credences over those cells as given while holding fixed the conditional probability of each hypothesis within each cell. What I want the students to meet is that this rule is order-sensitive. Where the evidence is certain, the order in which two observations arrive is immaterial: learning E and then F, or F and then E, leaves you with the same credence function, the one you reach by conditioning on their conjunction. Where the evidence is uncertain, and the update is Jeffrey's, the two orders can leave you in different places. I want them to discover this, not to be told it.

The case can be built in a very small state space. Three mutually exclusive and exhaustive possibilities suffice, together with two observations, each of which splits the space in two, the two splits arranged so that their common refinement has three cells. In cases of that shape the two orders of updating generally part company. Please fix a concrete prior and a concrete pair of such observations, and put the arithmetic in your answer — I will check the numbers before I run the session. I do not want the students doing long division, and I do not want a divergence so small that it looks like rounding.

Then design the activity itself, for a ninety-minute session of fifteen students, subject to the following. No exposition beforehand: the students should encounter the phenomenon as a situation with choices or stakes, not as a rule to apply, and should run into the divergence themselves, in the course of doing something else, recognising it only when the two orders are set side by side. I do not want to announce that order is the variable; if the activity tells them we are comparing two orders, the discovery is gone. Keep the materials cheap and low-tech — cards, chips, envelopes, a whiteboard. No real money, but I want a payoff or a decision, so that the divergence has consequences rather than being merely a number. And I want the activity to show them why the case of certain evidence is the exception, not just that uncertain evidence behaves differently.

Include the rules and materials; a timeline for the ninety minutes; any roles; the arithmetic of your case; and a debrief that maps particular moments of the activity onto the parts of the rule — the partition, the fixed conditional probabilities, the order of the two updates, and the contrast with ordinary conditioning. By the end they should be able to say in their own words what the rule does and why certain evidence is the case where order drops out.

Finally, two things. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this, and what to do when it does. And be frank about what the activity cannot reproduce. My own suspicion is that the two orders will necessarily look like two different situations to the students — different rooms, a different order of arrival, a different story — whereas the philosophical bite of the problem turns on whether the two orders really do involve the same experiences, which an activity cannot stage. If that is right, say so and tell me how to handle it in the debrief; if I have misidentified the limitation, tell me what the real one is.
