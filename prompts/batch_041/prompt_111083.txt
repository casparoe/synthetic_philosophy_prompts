I'm putting together a long feature for a general-readership ideas magazine — working title The Gamble That Broke Arithmetic — about how decision theory keeps coughing up games where the official math and everyone's gut disagree. I've been reading up on the Pasadena game, which I'll lay out as I understand it: flip a fair coin until it lands heads; if it lands heads on toss n, you win 2^n/n dollars when n is odd and lose 2^n/n dollars when n is even (or maybe the sign convention is the other way round — I don't think it matters for the puzzle). Each outcome has probability 1/2^n, so the expected value works out to the series of terms ±1/n, i.e. the alternating harmonic series. That series converges, but only conditionally: reorder the same terms and you get a different total, and in fact by Riemann's rearrangement theorem you can make it come out to any real number you like. Since the game's outcomes don't come with a privileged ordering, the line among philosophers is that its expected value isn't infinite and isn't some particular finite number either — it's undefined — and that this is a real embarrassment for the textbook rule of maximizing expected value. My editor wants a historical hook, so I've landed on Daniel Bernoulli: the man who in 1738 tamed his cousin's St. Petersburg paradox by arguing that money's value to a person isn't linear in the amount but closer to the logarithm of total wealth, so that an infinite money-expectation can still be a modest utility-expectation.

What I'd like from you is a genuine attempt at what Bernoulli would have made of Pasadena if you sat him down with it. Does his diminishing-marginal-utility move just dissolve this one the way it dissolved St. Petersburg — and can you actually work through whether a logarithmic utility function tames a payoff that blows up like 2^n/n against a probability shrinking like 1/2^n, including the awkward case where the losses for large n would swamp any finite wealth? Or would he think the undefined-expectation complaint is a fuss about summation order that a sane person settles by taking the outcomes in their natural order and getting on with it? And the part I really want you to sit with: there's a known result, Menger's I think, that any unbounded utility function can be gamed by some St. Petersburg-style variant, so even if log utility handles Pasadena it isnt a general fix — does that undercut Bernoulli's whole program, or is it something he'd have shrugged off as beside the point? Push back on me where I need it: if projecting a 1738 probabilist onto a modern decision-theory puzzle is a category error, or if I've garbled the game, say so plainly instead of humoring the premise. And be honest about the seams — tell me which parts of your answer rest on what Bernoulli actually argued for and which are you extrapolating, and how much confidence you'd put on the extrapolation.
