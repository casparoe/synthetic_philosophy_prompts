I'm in a third-year ethics course and we just finished the unit on negative utilitarianism, and something from the seminar has been bugging me. The lecturer put up "lexical threshold" negative utilitarianism as the grown-up middle path: there's some threshold of suffering such that, as long as a creature is above it, no amount of pleasure or mild good can justify pushing it below; but once you're past that line, ordinary weighing of pleasure against pain resumes. A classmate then defended the rival I actually find more honest — keep everything on one comensurable scale, where pleasure and pain are measured in the same currency, and just give pain a big weight (or a critical level you have to clear before anything counts). These two seem to be answering the same question — how much suffering can a benefit buy? — with totally different machinery, and I can't tell if the lexical version actually does something the weighted version can't, or whether it's just the weighted version with the exchange rate cranked to infinity in one region.

Can you compare them properly? Specifically: does the lexical threshold view need genuine incommensurability between suffering and pleasure, or can it be rebuilt as an ordinary single scale with an extreme exchange rate? What does each presuppose about the structure of welfare — a discontinuity in the value function, a lexical ordering like the ones in decision theory, or just a number? Are they real rivals or notational variants? And which handles the stock cases better — whether a huge pile of mild annoyances can outweigh one torture, whether it's ever right to inflict a little suffering for a lot of pleasure, and the awkward one where the threshold gets applied per creature in some versions but to total suffering in others, which gives different verdicts. Also tell me where each view is underdetermined or is just borrowing a metaphor it hasn't cashed out. I don't want a reading list or citations, just the comparison and a view on which one survives better.
