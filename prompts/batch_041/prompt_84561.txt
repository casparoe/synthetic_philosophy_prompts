For thirty-one years I was a soil chemist — most of that time on the behaviour of phosphate in agricultural soils, a subject that teaches you patience with slow variables — and I retired four years ago into a second life of reading philosophy. It has been humbling in the way I imagine a first-year graduate seminar would be for someone who had never run a titration, but I have been auditing a course in applied ethics and have got stuck on the question of what counts as corruption. Specifically, I keep meeting the suggestion that the word carries a Western, and more particularly a modern-bureaucratic, set of assumptions, so that to speak of corruption in a society organised around patron-client ties, gift exchange, and personal obligation is either a category mistake or the export of one culture's institutional furniture to another's. My instincts as a scientist tell me that corruption is probably not a natural kind and that the quarrel is at least partly verbal; I do not want to leave it there, so I have written the essay sketched below and would be grateful for your comments.

The argument is this. The familiar debate asks whether a single standard of corruption can be applied across societies, and the usual answers are that bribery is bribery wherever it occurs, or that the standard is parochial. Both answers assume the dispute is about values. I want to suggest it is about domains. Distinguish two kinds of exchange. In a role-governed exchange the value of what is exchanged is indifferent to who performs it: a permit to build is worth the same to me whether the clerk who issues it is my cousin or a stranger, and the clerk's obligation is to issue it on the merits. In a relation-governed exchange the value is constituted by the particular relationship: the help my cousin gives me is not the help a stranger could give, and to abstract from the relationship is to destroy the thing. Corruption-talk is apt, I claim, only in the first case, and the apt charge is always the same one — that a role-governed exchange has been conducted as though it were relation-governed. Call this the substitutability test: if a qualified stranger could have rendered the same service with the same meaning, the exchange was role-governed, and a payment extracted for it is a bribe; if not, the exchange was relational, and calling it corruption is a misdescription.

If that is right, the charge of cultural imperialism gets the diagnosis right and the location wrong. What varies between societies — and within a single society, from one domain to the next — is not the moral standard but the extent to which domains have been institutionalised as role-governed. A health system can be thoroughly role-governed while its permit offices are not, and that is a fact about institutions rather than about ethos. So the interesting question is not whether Western standards are universal but how a domain comes to be role-governed, and what becomes of the relational obligations that were doing the work before. That second question seems to me the one nobody asks. When a domain is converted, the cousin is not merely forbidden to help; he is forbidden to be a cousin in that setting, and the loss is real even if the gain is larger. A corollary concerns the well-known defence that payments to officials grease the wheels of an over-regulated economy. I think this is not a defence of corruption at all but a proposal to convert role-governed domains back into relational ones, and it should be argued for as such, as a claim about which domains ought to be de-institutionalised, rather than as a claim about efficiency. Whether it is even true as a claim about efficiency seems to me unsettled — the empirical literature apparently finds both grease and sand, and I am not competent to adjudicate it. I note also that the most widely used comparative measures are perception-based, which means they record what informed observers believe, and informed observers bring their own norms; if my distinction holds, they may be measuring the density of role-governance rather than the quantity of wrongdoing, which would be a different thing wearing the same name.

I would like your comments on three things, in this order of importance. First, whether the substitutability test is a genuine criterion or merely a redescription of the ordinary difference between a bribe and a favour, and if it is the latter, whether anything of mine survives. Second, whether any of the empirical or conceptual claims above are simply wrong — I would rather be told plainly than handled gently, and I have no stake in the conclusion. Third, whether the move from which domains are role-governed to corruption-talk being domain-relative does any work that the ordinary relativist answer does not already do. If you think the whole essay collapses, tell me which single objection does the most damage and stop there rather than listing five mild ones. Please write your reply as continuous prose — no headings, no numbered lists, no bold or tables — the way one would write a letter; I read this sort of thing the way I read correspondence, and I would like it to sound like a person thinking rather than a report.
