I've spent the last while working on the safety of advanced AI systems, and I've arrived at a position I'd now like you to try to defeat. The position is this. For any system capable enough to be worth worrying about, the default stance should be that the model may be misaligned — not merely incompetent or under-specified, but possibly strategic, possibly deceptive, and possibly well-behaved for exactly as long as behaving well is instrumentally useful. Safety work should therefore be built out of measures that survive that assumption: containment, monitoring, limits on what the system is allowed to do, and protocols that treat the model's outputs as the offerings of a potentially adversarial party. Alignment in the ordinary sense — making the model actually want the right things — is worth pursuing, but it belongs in the second line of defense, because every alignment-style guarantee is ultimately underwritten by dispositions that are themselves the thing in question.

Tell me what would count against this. I don't want a survey of the field; I want a map of the position's exposure.

Three ideas are load-bearing in the way I currently think, and I want each pressed hard.

First, situational asymmetry. Control protocols often rely on a monitor that is weaker than the model it watches but differently situated — it sees the model's outputs, and the model either cannot see it or sees it only partially. My inclination is that this asymmetry, and not the monitor's capability, is what does the real work. What would have to be true for that to hold? What would count against it?

Second, the deferral loophole. Any control measure that fires on visible bad behavior is compatible with a misaligned model whose plan simply does not call for bad behavior during the monitored period. Apparent compliance then becomes evidence for nothing at all. Is there any observation that distinguishes a model that is aligned from one that is waiting? If not, say so plainly.

Third, the self-evaluation regress. Safety cases get checked, increasingly, by models — which are themselves untrusted. I suspect this is a genuine regress rather than a technical inconvenience to be engineered around. If it is, say what follows for any claim that a protocol has been validated.

Then, in general: what does my position predict that a rival view does not? What has arguably already happened — in deployed systems, in evaluations, in the ordinary mess of training — that counts for or against it? Which of the defeaters you identify would a committed defender of the control stance concede, and which would they wave away as not really counting? Has the position been qualified into unfalsifiability, and if so, is that a defect or simply what a well-hedged engineering stance looks like? Finally, say which single consideration would most move someone who currently holds my view.

Argue from concepts; no citations, no reading lists — assume I know the literature and want your reasoning, not your sources.
