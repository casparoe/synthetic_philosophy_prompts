I teach a 300-level seminar on rational choice and ethics, 14 students, one 90-minute session, and I want to run Harsanyi's aggregation theorem as an activity instead of a lecture. Here's the thing I want them to feel in their bones rather than hear from me: if you accept (a) that the social preference ordering is the ordering of an expected-utility maximizer, (b) weak Pareto — if everyone prefers x to y, so does society — and (c) that how society ranks a lottery depends only on the individual utilities of that lottery's outcomes, then the social ranking has to be a weighted sum of the individual utilities, a1u1 + a2u2 + ..., and nothing else. Utilitarianism drops out of coherence plus a Pareto condition, not out of any moral premise. That's the punchline.

The bit I want them to catch themselves doing is the sleight of hand right after: the theorem hands you *weighted* utilitarianism, it does not hand you equal weights. Equal weights come from a separate move, the veil of ignorance, where you say everyone is equally likely to be anyone, so each person's ex ante expected utility is just the unweighted average. And the equal-probability step is exactly where Rawls and Sen dig in. Most of my students have absorbed the summary version — Harsanyi showed the veil of ignorance gives you utilitarianism — and I want the activity to make them locate that assumption with their own hands.

So design me a two-phase game. Phase 1 should have them reasoning behind an equal-chance veil, each computing expected utilities over a small set of policies from fixed payoff tables, and converging — the point being that they converge on the sum and that it feels like nothing but arithmetic, zero moralising. Phase 2 should break the veil: give different players different probability profiles over who they'll end up being (one player is 50% likely to be person 3, someone else is uniform, etc.), have them compute their own ex ante rankings, and let them discover they now disagree and that no amount of Pareto-respecting coherence fixes it — the weights are now somebody's beliefs rather than a moral fact.

Give me the payoff tables (5 or 6 people, 4 or 5 policies, with at least one concave and one threshold-shaped utility function so the sum does something counterintuitive), the randomization device, how to make the veil psychologically real without it being hokey, the rules for both phases, a moment-by-moment debrief that maps what actually happened onto each premise of the theorem, and an honest list of what the activity can't reproduce so I know what I have to supply by talking.

Two things I really care about. First, the debrief should attach each failure mode to a premise: the constancy of the weights across all lotteries (we only ever test a handful), the fact that the result needs cardinal utilities measured on a common unit — which is the comparability assumption that people actually fight about, and which I'd say the theorem itself doesn't require but the equal-weight conclusion does — and the Arrow contrast, where you drop cardinality and don't get a sum at all, you get a dictator. If you can work in a phase-3 variant where one player is instead instructed to be a perfectly impartial observer with equal sympathy for everyone, and let the group notice that this gets them to the same place as the equal-chance veil, even better, but flag for me that the impartial-observer version is the one Sen says smuggles in the comparability it's supposed to deliver. Second, tell me what predictably goes wrong the first time I run this: my guess is half the room refuses to be expected-utility maximizers at all and goes maximin on principle, and someone tries to form a coalition before the draw, which wrecks the veil. I dont need real money or anything, and i'd rather the theorem not be named until the debrief.
