I teach a 90-minute session in an interdisciplinary ethics course, 18 students, mixed backgrounds. Design a runnable activity that makes them feel the force of a hypothesis I hold about honor, dignity, and victimhood cultures: that the three differ less in what they condemn than in who is authorized to settle a grievance and who bears the burden of restoring the score. In honor regimes, resolution runs through the offended party's own credible threat; in dignity regimes, through an impartial adjudicator who ignores standing; in victimhood regimes, through a third-party audience whose sympathy must be earned and can be withdrawn. Set up three rounds of the same few grievances under rules that change only who may move an entry -- who may demand redress, who may confirm it, and what counts as settled -- so that the cultures emerge from the rules before anyone names them. I want them to discover that the scarce resource differs by regime (credible threat, neutral adjudication, sympathetic audience) and that each regime leaves something else unresolved. Give me the rules and materials, the timeline, the debrief that maps each round's moments onto the three cultures, what predictably breaks the first time I run it, and an honest note on which feature of the comparison the activity cannot reproduce and I will have to supply.
