I spent thirty-five years as a biochemist and retired a couple of years ago, and since then I've been reading philosophy pretty much non stop. At the moment I'm working through the Dennett–Caruso back and forth about free will and just deserts — the compatibilist who thinks we can keep desert, the skeptic who thinks we should scrap it and go abolitionist. It's the sentencing side that's been nagging at me, though, because the practice on the ground doesnt seem to line up with either of them.

Here's what I mean. Take how a real sentence gets built up. In a lot of places the number depends on things that have nothing to do with what the person did. If you plead guilty early you get a discount; I gather some systems actually write the scale down, so much off if you own up at the first opportunity and less and less the longer you hold out. Insist on a trial and lose, and you eat the whole thing — and judges will sometimes say out loud that the extra time is for showing no remorse, or for putting the victim through having to testify. Then the same system moves in the other direction: a rotten childhood, a violent home, a school that failed you, and the sentence comes down. Prior convictions push it up, so the same burglary is worth a few months the first time and years the third time.

So the one institution seems to be saying all at once: (a) how long you sit in a cell depends on a choice you made afterwards, in a courtroom, with a lawyer whispering in your ear; (b) it depends on your attitude, which you may or may not be able to help; (c) it depends on your history, which you definitely couldn't help; and (d) it depends on what you actually did, which is what the retributivist says is the only thing that should count.

What I want is the theory of desert this practice actually presupposes, as opposed to the one it advertises. Is desert fixed at the moment of the act, in which case the plea discount and the remorse premium are pure consequentialism bolted onto a retributive frame? Or is the real object of assessment the person's whole trajectory — desert tracking character — in which case the act is just one piece of evidence? Are the mitigation for a bad childhood and the aggravation for no remorse consistent with each other, or do they pull in opposite directions about whether a person's history and attitudes are really theirs? Which pieces of the practice genuinely need desert and which would run just as well on deterrence or incapacitation? And if I swapped the presupposed view out for Caruso's — nobody ever deserves anything, punishment is only justified by reducing future harm — what would actually have to change on the ground, and what would carry on unchanged because it was never about desert in the first place?

I spent my working life with error bars, so tell me how confident you are in each thing you claim, and single out the one point you're least sure of. I'd rather see the uncertainty flagged than smoothed over. Dont bother with citations, I'm not going to chase them down.
