Consider the dogmatism paradox in a form that does not rely on any particular theory of knowledge: if an agent knows some proposition P, and knows that P entails not-Q, then she knows not-Q, so any evidence that would otherwise support Q can be dismissed as misleading, since her knowledge already settles the matter. Suppose a research collective adopts what we can call an epistemic lockdown policy: once it takes itself to know a core hypothesis H, it treats every apparent counterexample, anomaly, or piece of evidence that would ordinarily lower its confidence in H as a misleading artifact to be explained away. Please describe the most important implications of the dogmatism paradox for this policy, both for the collective's long-run reliability and for the epistemic rationality of individual members who follow it. In doing so, address the difference between dogmatism about knowledge and dogmatism about high credence; the role of auxiliary hypotheses in absorbing anomalies; how the policy interacts with peer disagreement and with the possibility that the collective's initial acceptance of H was itself unreasonable; and what the paradox implies about whether an agent can rationally resolve to become less dogmatic later. Answer as a numbered list of exactly five items, each one or two sentences, with no introduction or conclusion.
