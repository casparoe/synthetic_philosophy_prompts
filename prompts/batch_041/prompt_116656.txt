I've been doing a self-study on teleosemantics (long story, I got into it via the whole "can you naturalize intentionality" thread on LessWrong and then went down a rabbit hole) and I keep hitting the same wall, so I want an actual number rather than a vibe. The sales pitch is that you get content for free from biological function: a state represents whatever it was selected to indicate. The problem is the indeterminacy / distality stuff. The frog's fly-detector was selected to fire at flies, but also at black dots, and also at "small dark moving things" generally, and nothing in the selection story seems to single one of those out as *the* content. The standard fix is consumer-side -- the producer's job is fixed by what the consumer needs to be true for the whole system to do its thing (Millikan's the obvious name here) -- but that just relocates the indeterminacy, as far as I can tell.

Here's the thing I actually want a credence on. I've been kicking around a half-baked idea that the indeterminacy is a feature rather than a bug, and that the right move is to stop hunting for the unique content-fixing fact and instead treat content as a graded, population-level property: a state represents F to degree d, where d reflects the proportion of the lineage's selection history in which F -- rather than some narrower or broader description of the same goings-on -- was the thing that made the consumer's use of the signal work. So "the frog represents flies" comes out more true than "the frog represents black dots," neither is the unique answer, and thats fine, because content-talk was always a coarse, interest-relative gloss on a messier underlying story. Give me your credence that some version of this graded / statistical teleosemantics can be made to work as a genuinely naturalistic theory of content -- specifically that it (a) doesn't smuggle in the intentionality it's supposed to explain, and (b) still yields determinate-enough attributions to do the explanatory work we actually want content for. Decompose it: P(some teleosemantic strategy is the right naturalistic approach at all), P(a graded version escapes the indeterminacy objection), P(it doesn't collapse into "content is whatever its useful for us to ascribe," which is the failure mode I'm most scared of), P(it survives the cases where function and content seem to come apart -- states selected for one thing that now reliably indicate another, the swampman-ish worry, etc.), plus any other term you think belongs, a number and one line of justification each, then the combination.

For context I'm arguing about this with a friend who thinks teleosemantics is a dead end and that you should go full instrumentalist / predictive-processing about content, so if you can also say where our disagreement most likely lives, bonus points. And tell me which of your terms you're least sure about and what evidence or argument would move it most.
