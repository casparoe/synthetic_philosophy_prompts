My grandad left me his old boat and I've just finished two winters of rebuilding it -- new planking, new keel, new deck beams, reconditioned engine. My brother reckons it's not grandad's boat anymore, just a replica with the original nameplate screwed onto it. I reckon it obviously is, on the grounds that everything that came out went back in as good or better and it still does exactly the same job. The bit thats actually bugging me is the other case: the little dinghy that came with it, which grandad had patched with fibreglass and painted over. Everyone agrees that one "isn't really the boat anymore, it's a patch job" -- and it's got way more of its original wood left in it than the big boat has. Same with houses: gut one and put good materials in and it's "the same house, done up"; gut it and put cheap stuff in and it's "not really that house anymore", even though more of the original is standing in the second case. Meanwhile the old planks off the big boat are stacked in my garage, and it's never occured to anyone that *that* pile is the boat.

So the pattern I can't put a name to: whether we say a thing is "still the same thing" seems to track whether the change made it better or worse at being the kind of thing it is, not how much of the original material survived. I know the old puzzle about the ship whose planks get swapped one at a time, but that one turns on whether any original bits are left, and my thing happens whether none are or lots are. Is there a name for this, and has anyone argued it's a real feature of how identity works rather than me being sentimental about a boat? Please don't just hand me the nearest bit of jargon -- I want the thing itself carved up: what it is, what it isn't, and where the edges are (e.g. if I improve the boat far enough that it can't be rowed at all, at what point is it a different object, and what's doing the work there?). Bottom line in your first sentence please, then the why, no preamble and no repeating my question back at me.
