Write a short story of roughly 1,500 words for an anthology I am assembling on the subject of causation. My interest is in overdetermination — the case in which an event has two or more causes, each of which would on its own have been sufficient to bring it about — and I want the story built around a concrete instance of that structure rather than around a conversation about the idea.

I am a novelist by trade and a slow, suspicious amateur in this area, so let me be exact about what I am asking for.

First, invent the setting, the period, and the event. Do not use a documented historical episode, and do not lightly fictionalize a real one. Give me an imagined town and an imagined catastrophe — a fire, a collapse, a drowning, a ruinous vote, your choice — with enough specific texture (weather, wages, a bad hinge on a door) that the reader believes in it. The point is the shape of the causation, not the record, and I would rather not have the story leaning on facts I would then feel obliged to check.

Second, the event must have at least three plausible causes, at least two of which are independently sufficient: had the second not obtained, the first would have done the work alone, and vice versa. I want the reader to feel both of those counterfactuals close at hand, not merely to be told that the causes were many. A useful device, if you want one: let the decisive document or the decisive lapse turn out to be one of several that would each have sufficed. I have read enough stories in which the archivist uncovers the single damning page; I would like the damning page to be redundant.

Third, give me two characters who disagree about what follows from all this. One holds that the hunt for "the" cause is a category mistake — that the event was going to happen no matter which of these roads you blocked, and that the question of blame is therefore a question about us, not about the world. The other holds the opposite: that a factor which would have done the work anyway is not properly a cause at all, that a genuine cause must be not only enough but also necessary, and that the first character's view amounts to abolishing responsibility. Let them press each other, but obliquely — through what they notice, what they strike from the record, what they are and are not willing to sign their names to. No speeches, no lecture, no narrator who adjudicates.

Fourth, include one moment in which a character settles on a single cause because that cause is the least ordinary one available — the negligence rather than the drought, the stranger rather than the neighbour — and let the reader see, without being told, how arbitrary that selection is. The reader should be able to reconstruct the whole philosophical disagreement from the behaviour of the characters; if the story could be turned into an argument by lifting out a paragraph, that paragraph is doing work the scene should be doing.

Fifth, and this matters more than the rest: do not resolve it. No moral, no final image that quietly rules for one side, no wise elder. I want the reader to close the story holding two sufficient causes in mind at once, the way one holds two truths that will not sit together — and I want the discomfort of that to be the story's actual ending, rather than an event that happens to occur last.

One further constraint on voice: the narration should be close to someone who was there and has since become the person who explains it. If that narrator is unreliable, let the unreliability lie in the explanation rather than in the facts.
