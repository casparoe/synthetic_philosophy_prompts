Last night I spent several hours arguing with a friend about money pumps and diachronic rationality, and I came away less sure of a view I had been treating as settled. I hold that money-pump arguments against intransitive preferences—and, more broadly, against cyclic or incomplete preference structures—derive their normative force from a diachronic picture of the agent. On this view, the agent is a single locus of choice persisting through time, and what the pump shows is that such an agent will be a systematic source of free money to others unless their preferences satisfy certain structural constraints. The money pump is therefore not primarily a synchronic consistency requirement; it is a requirement of diachronic self-governance. My friend pressed that this makes the argument hostage to a controversial theory of agency, and that a resolute agent with a standing policy to refuse further trades can have intransitive preferences without being pumpable. On his telling, the argument proves at most that one should be resolute, not that one's preferences must be transitive.

I want you to tell me what would count against my view—what would have to be true for it to be false—not just the textbook objections, but the specific evidence, arguments, intuitions, and formal results that should move me, sorted from fatal to merely awkward. In particular: Are there versions of the money pump that work even for an agent who chooses only once, or who has no preferences over time, and if so do they undermine the diachronic reading? Does the distinction between exploitability and irrationality cut against me, or can I accept it without loss? What should I say about resolute choice, sophisticated choice, and the possibility that the agent's policy itself is a preference that changes the choice set? Are there cases where intransitivity is not merely awkward but rationally required—say, in certain cases of incommensurable values or changing tastes—and if so, what does that do to the claim that the pump establishes a genuine requirement? What would a defender of the standard synchronic reading concede as counting against their view, and what would they dismiss as beside the point?

I would find it most persuasive if you could tell me whether the diachronic reading has any discriminating consequences that the synchronic reading lacks, and whether the view has been qualified into unfalsifiability. If it has, say so plainly, and say whether that is a defect for a view of this kind or simply the price of doing normative theory. I want the map of its exposure, not a defense.
