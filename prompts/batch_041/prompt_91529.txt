I have been arguing with a libertarian friend for years about whether taxation is theft, and we have reached the point where each of us can recite the other's moves and nothing lands. He says taxation is theft because it takes property without the owner's consent, and the fact that a majority voted for it does not make the taking consensual. I say it is not theft because property rights are defined by the legal system, and the tax code is part of that system, so the state is not taking something that is already yours in the relevant sense; he replies that this is question-begging, since it assumes the very authority over property that is in dispute. A third friend, a graduate student in philosophy, now tells me the whole debate is verbal: he says 'theft' has a legal sense, in which taxation is plainly not theft because the law authorizes it, and a moral sense, in which the answer depends on whether the state can legitimately define property rights at all; on his view the question is either trivial or question-begging, and the real issue is the legitimacy of the state, not the word 'theft'. I suspect he is wrong — that there is a substantive disagreement underneath — but I cannot articulate why, and I am not sure the debate has stalled for the reason he gives.

Please diagnose the impasse: is this a verbal dispute about the word 'theft', a substantive disagreement about the moral status of property and consent, or two different questions — one about what the law says, one about what morality requires — running under a single heading? Use what each side actually says, assess my friend's verbal-dispute hypothesis on its merits, and tell me what, if anything, would count as progress — a clarification, a distinction, a change of question — or why the impasse should be expected to persist. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give the reasoning; no preamble and no restating of my question.
