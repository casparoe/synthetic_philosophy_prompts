I'm a third-year undergraduate currently taking a seminar on language and ethics, and we've just spent two weeks on the difference between lying and merely misleading — saying something false versus saying something true in order to get your hearer to draw a false conclusion — and whether that difference carries any moral weight of its own. For the final paper I'd like to write a dialogue rather than an essay, partly because my intuitions keep flipping depending on how the example is set up, and I want to see two people actually argue it out instead of taking turns delivering speeches. So: please write a dialogue of roughly 1200 to 1600 words between two equally competent, equally well-read interlocutors. The first holds that the distinction is morally fundamental — that a lie wrongs the hearer in a way a misleading truth does not, because a lie presents itself as something to be believed, takes responsibility for the falsehood on the speaker's own authority, and closes off inquiry, whereas a misleading truth leaves the hearer's rational agency formally intact and leaves them in charge of their own inference. The second holds that this is at most a difference in mechanism, that the morally relevant question is whether the hearer was manipulated rather than whether the speaker uttered something false, and that a carefully engineered misleading truth can be worse than a clumsy lie: it is deniable, and worse still, it recruits the victim's own reasoning against them and makes them complicit in their own deception. They might begin from a case like a company press release that is accurate on every individual point but assembled so that no careful reader draws the true conclusion, though I'd rather they not stay there.

I'd like the argument to move past the standard examples into less well-worn territory, and I've set out below the points I want them to reach. Neither should be allowed to win by redefining terms, so if one of them tries to make lying simply mean any intentional deception, the other should say plainly that this is a change of subject rather than an argument. First, whether the distinction is context-dependent: it may be real between parties dealing at arm's length, but does it survive when the speaker occupies a role that carries a duty of candour — a physician, a fiduciary, a government spokesperson, an officer conducting an interrogation — such that the hearer's failure to ask the right follow-up question becomes the speaker's problem rather than the hearer's? Second, whether misleading is parasitic on the very norm that lying violates: since implicature only works because hearers assume speakers are cooperative and truthful, the misleading speaker seems to be free-riding on other people's honesty, and one of them should press this as a reason to think misleading can be the graver offence. Third, misleading that does not run through implicature at all but through presupposition — a question or a definite description that smuggles in a falsehood the speaker never asserts and can later disclaim — and what that does to the claim that the misleading speaker at least respects the hearer's agency. And fourth, the deniability itself: the misleading speaker can afterwards deny having said anything false and leave the hearer doubting their own reading of the exchange, which looks like a further wrong with no parallel in plain lying. Each of them should concede the other's strongest point at least once; the exchange should end without a tidy resolution, a slight shift of ground being fine and capitulation not; and there's no need to attribute any of this to named authors, since I care about the arguments rather than the bibliography.
