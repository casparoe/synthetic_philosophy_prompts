For roughly thirty years I was a field geologist, and that mostly meant long drives on roads where the next town lay two hours off and the next tow truck, for practical purposes, did not exist. I picked up hitchhikers when I could, and once, in my twenties, I was one — stranded with a dead battery and a jug of water going warm in the sun, until a stranger turned back for me. Since retiring I have been reading moral philosophy for the first time, and I keep returning to a case I have met more than once in that reading: a traveler stranded in the desert promises a driver a large sum of money if the driver will carry him to the nearest town, knowing perfectly well that once he is safe, and the money is his own again, he will have no reason at all to pay it. What holds me is not the promise but the tangle beneath it. Before he is rescued, the traveler's interest lies in being a man who pays out of gratitude rather than calculation — a driver who can read character will turn back for that man and not for the other. Yet he cannot become that man by resolving to, and if he cultivates gratitude because it pays, it is no longer gratitude, and the driver, who is watching, can tell the difference. The man who makes the promise and the man who reaches town now seem to me two different people, and only the first one is generous.

I would like a poem about this, spoken by the rescued traveler, with the form doing part of the arguing. Please write me a pantoum of five quatrains. I have in mind that the pantoum's interlocking repetitions — each stanza's second and fourth lines returning as the first and third of the next — might enact the loop I cannot get out of: the promise coming back and coming back, each time a little less able to move the speaker, while the calculation that quietly empties it arrives on the same breath. Let the two recurring kinds of line, the pledge and the reckoning, trade places as the poem proceeds, so that by the last stanza the pledge is the thing that sounds hollow and the reckoning the thing that sounds like a vow; then let the poem fold shut on its opening line, the way the traveler keeps arriving back at the wish to be someone he cannot choose to be. Keep the desert, the road, the water jug, and the driver's glance concrete, and end unresolved rather than moralized — I do not need to be told whether he pays, only to feel why that question cannot be settled from inside the moment of deciding. He should sound like a man implicated in the case, not a lecturer explaining it.
