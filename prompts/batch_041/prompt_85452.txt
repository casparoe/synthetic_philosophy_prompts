I cover science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and each year I teach a guest session on quantitative evidence in a master's programme for science journalists. Below is the written component of this year's session and the answer one student submitted. I have to return a grade and comments, and I want an assessment I can defend in detail rather than an impression, so please work through the answer claim by claim.

The question I set:

"A hospital system has built a model that scores patients on their risk of an unplanned admission in the coming year, and uses the score to decide whom to enrol in a preventive-care programme. Patients fall into two groups, X and Y, whose annual rates of unplanned admission differ substantially — roughly one in ten in X, roughly three in ten in Y. The model is calibrated within each group: among patients in group X who are given a 20% score, about 20% are admitted within the year, and the same is true within group Y. The model is far from perfect: few patients are assigned scores near 0 or near 1.

(a) What, if anything, follows about the false-positive and false-negative rates of the model in the two groups? Give the reasoning, and say whether the disparity you identify is a defect of this model or a constraint that would apply to any model of this kind.
(b) The vendor argues that within-group calibration is what fairness amounts to here, and that any remaining disparity in error rates is an artefact of biased historical data that better data would remove. Assess that argument.
(c) A colleague proposes eliminating the disparity by removing group membership, and every feature correlated with it, from the model's inputs. Assess that proposal.
(d) A second colleague proposes instead to equalise the false-positive and false-negative rates across the two groups. Say what this would require, whether it is achievable for this model, and what it would cost."

The student's answer:

"(a) This is an instance of the general impossibility result for risk scores. When two groups have different base rates for the outcome, no scoring rule can be calibrated within groups and at the same time produce equal error rates across groups: something has to give. The group with the lower base rate is the one that pays on false positives, because its non-cases are more numerous — a threshold that catches the same share of genuine cases in each group will sweep in a larger share of the people who will not be admitted. So group X will show the higher false-positive rate. None of this is a flaw in this particular model; it is a property of the joint distribution of scores and outcomes, and it holds however good the data are. COMPAS is the best-known illustration: ProPublica found that Black defendants who did not reoffend were more likely to be flagged than white defendants who did not reoffend, while the instrument's scores were calibrated by race, and the tool was about as accurate as the instruments it displaced. Both sides were describing real features of the same score, which is why the dispute could not be settled by appealing to accuracy.

(b) The vendor is half right. A gap in error rates is not by itself evidence of an unfair tool: on the calibration criterion the tool is not unfair, and the error-rate gap is the price of that calibration. The vendor is also right that the difference in admission rates is a social fact the model did not create — it is reproducing an unequal distribution of illness and of access to care. What the vendor cannot claim is that better data would dissolve the conflict. Short of a perfect predictor, which this model is not, the conflict is arithmetic: as long as the base rates differ, calibration and equal error rates cannot be had together, whatever the data look like. If the base rates converged the conflict would fade, and a fairer distribution of care upstream would do that, but that is a change in the world rather than in the data pipeline.

(c) The proposal fails. Removing group membership does not remove the information: prior admissions, neighbourhood, employment and the rest will carry it, and the model will reconstruct the group closely enough to reproduce the same disparity. This is the standard objection to fairness through blindness.

(d) Equalising both error rates is what the equalised-odds criterion asks for, and it would require group-specific thresholds at the decision stage. But the impossibility result bites here too: when base rates differ, that criterion cannot be satisfied — Hardt and colleagues, who introduced it, show as much — so in practice people settle for the weaker equal-opportunity requirement of matching true-positive rates, which is compatible with calibration. The second colleague's proposal is therefore not available to us, and if it could be enforced the cost would be a loss of overall accuracy. The real choice is between calibration and error-rate balance."
