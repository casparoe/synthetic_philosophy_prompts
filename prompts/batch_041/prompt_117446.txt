I'm putting together the take-home final for my 400-level metaphysics of time seminar and I need one essay question on the Rietdijk–Putnam argument. My recurring problem: every year half the class writes some version of "special relativity says simultaneity is relative, therefore the block universe, therefore no free will," and it's a paragraph of vibes. I want a question that can't be answered that way, but that's still fair to a student who did the reading.

Here's what I want them forced to do. The argument as it's usually given picks two space-like separated events, notes there's a frame where they're simultaneous, concludes that if you're at one you should call the other real, and then chains that across a series of observers until everything comes out real. The step I care about is that the conclusion only lands if you let the word "real" slide from something indexed to a particular here-and-now ("real relative to this event") to something unindexed ("real, period"), and you also assume the relation composes — if B is real relative to A and C is real relative to B, then C is real relative to A. I want a question where they have to isolate that step, say which of those two moves is actually doing the work, and then say what becomes of the argument if you refuse it. Then the hard part, which is really the whole point: is refusing it a real option or just a way of hiding from the physics? If reality is only ever reality-relative-to-something, has the presentist saved anything, or has she just renamed her view and hoped nobody noticed? I don't want an answer that says "well, the relativizing reply says..." and stops there.

I also want them to keep three claims apart, because they always mush them together: (a) there is no privileged global present; (b) the future is as real as the past; (c) the future is fixed or inevitable. Rietdijk's paper was sold as a proof of determinism, and I want them to say what the argument actually gives you about (c) once (a) and (b) are sorted out — and whether (b) is even the right way to state the conclusion. Make them engage the cheap-looking version too (walk toward Andromeda and its "now" jumps by days) and say why that version is doing less work than it appears to.

Level: seniors plus two MA students, mostly philosophy, two from physics. They know Minkowski diagrams. They've had a week on the relativizing reply and a week on presentism's escape routes (privileged foliation / neo-Lorentzian, tenseless tensed facts, etc.). Keep it to light cones — I don't want this turning into a physics problem set.

Deliverables: the question itself, one essay, answerable in about 90 minutes of writing, so 800 words max; a model answer of 700–800 words I can hand out as a specimen; and a marking scheme out of 20 built around five must-hit points, with the marks for each and a one-line note on how students typically blow each one. Then, in a line or two, tell me what separates a low A from a solid B — the thing only the A students do. And if there's a move that's wrong but sounds sophisticated enough that I might be tempted to give it full credit, flag it so I don't.
