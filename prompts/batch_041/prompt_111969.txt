I am in the late stages of a paper defending a hedonist account of welfare, and a colleague has offered an objection by analogy that I would like you to audit rather than answer in general terms.

The weakness he is exploiting is the familiar heterogeneity of pleasure: the pleasure of cold water when thirsty, of a chord resolving, of scratching an itch, of finishing a proof, of another person's misfortune. If welfare consists in pleasure, hedonism appears to need some common feature, yet the candidates — a shared phenomenal quality, a shared attitude of being pleased, a loose family resemblance — each look either circular or too thin to carry the theory.

His argument runs as follows. Hedonism now stands where vitalism stood: a folk category awaiting dissolution by science. The term pleasure is a lay label stretched over a miscellany, and the history of science is largely the history of such labels being split — jade turned out to name two unrelated minerals, memory turned out to cover systems with little in common. He concludes that the heterogeneity problem is not a puzzle to be solved but a symptom: the category has no theoretical unity, hedonism has no subject matter, and the honest response is pluralism or eliminativism about pleasure rather than a hunt for its essence. Note that he runs two distinct historical patterns together: the jade and memory cases are cases of a category dividing, whereas the vitalism case is a case of a posited entity evaporating.

A second colleague offers a rival analogy. Pleasure, she says, is more like sweetness: one taste quality, realised by sugars, by sugar alcohols and by certain proteins, unified by a common receptor and a characteristic response rather than by the chemistry of the stimulus. On that model, heterogeneity among the realisers is exactly what one should expect, and hedonism is salvageable at the level of the response. She adds a third comparison: colour terms answer to no common physical property of surfaces — metamers are indistinguishable yet physically unlike — and colour science is nonetheless a going concern. So a kind may be real and explanatory without a shared physical essence.

What I want from you: a table of correspondences for each analogy, saying precisely what maps onto what; where each breaks down; which disanalogies matter for my colleague's conclusion and which are idle; what each analogy quietly assumes — about who is entitled to adjudicate a taxonomy, about whether the value-making feature of pleasure must be a natural kind, and about whether classification should track essence or role; and finally whether his conclusion, that hedonism has no subject matter, survives once the disanalogies are on the table, together with a better analogy if one exists.

Please write in British English throughout, in both spelling and usage, and keep the audit self-contained: no citations or reading lists.
