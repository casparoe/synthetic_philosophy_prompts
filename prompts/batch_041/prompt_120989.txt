I work in decision theory and applied microeconomics — Savage acts, Anscombe–Aumann, subjective expected utility, ambiguity aversion in the Gilboa–Schmeidler style, robust control, and the standard pathologies of unbounded utility — and I have no theological training. Please restate Pascal's wager as faithfully as you can in that vocabulary, and then give me a residue report. My hunch, which I want you to test rather than accommodate, is that the wager is best read not as a decision under risk but as one under ambiguity, and that most of the standard objections are symptoms of that misclassification.

Begin by fixing the formal apparatus: the state space, the act space, the consequence space, and the preference relation, and say which representation theorem you are implicitly invoking. Be explicit about which strand of the passage usually labelled the wager you are translating — the dominance-flavoured strand, the expected-value strand, the strand that turns on the infinite, and the practical advice about inducing belief — because they do not all translate the same way, and I suspect at least one of them is not a decision-theoretic argument at all. Where Pascal treats the choice as forced, so that declining to wager is itself a wager, tell me what that corresponds to formally and whether it is a substantive claim about the act space or an artefact of how the act space was drawn. Where the argument assigns infinite utility, tell me what survives once the utility function is required to be real-valued, and whether the argument can be reconstructed with a sufficiently large finite payoff or genuinely needs the infinite. Where the objection about competing religions offering competing infinite payoffs arises, restate it as a claim about the agent's state space or about ambiguity over which partition is the correct one, and say which formal treatment it demands: a larger state space, a set of priors, a second-order prior, or something else.

Then the residue report, which is the part I actually want. Which elements map cleanly onto standard machinery? Which require distortion — in particular, does the recommendation to act as though one believed, in order to bring belief about, fit the act-and-consequence framework at all, given that the agent's own psychology is simultaneously the object of choice and a determinant of the payoff? And which elements have no counterpart in this vocabulary whatsoever? For each item in the last category, tell me whether the failure indicts decision theory or instead indicates that Pascal is doing something other than decision theory — for instance, whether the voluntarism about belief that the wager appears to presuppose is an assumption decision theory cannot state, or merely one it has no need to state.

Lead with your bottom line about how much of the wager survives translation, in the first sentence, and then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of my question. State how confident you are in each part of the translation and in the residue report, and single out the one point you are least sure of.
