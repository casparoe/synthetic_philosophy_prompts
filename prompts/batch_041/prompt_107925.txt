I serve on the working group replacing our county's tool for prioritizing families for a scarce home-visiting program. The board has set two requirements that the replacement must satisfy at once: a positive prediction must carry the same meaning in each racial group, so that the same share of those flagged in each group actually go on to experience the outcome; and the tool's false-positive and false-negative rates must be equal across the two groups. Our staff methodologist says that in any realistic setting these two requirements pull against each other — that once the groups' base rates differ, a predictor that meets the one will fail the other — and she calls this a fairness impossibility result. The rest of us suspect she is applying a theorem about one particular kind of model to a problem it does not really cover. Our tool emits a continuous risk score rather than a yes-or-no label; we train it without race as an input; we are tripling the training sample; and rather than fixing a single cutoff we intend to let caseworkers choose their own, with the AUC checked for equality across the two groups. Each of these looks to me like a separate route around her result. I would be grateful if you would tell me which of them, if any, actually works, and, if none does, whether the obstacle is a limitation of present methods that a larger model or more computing power might overcome, or a constraint on the problem itself that no predictor could evade.
