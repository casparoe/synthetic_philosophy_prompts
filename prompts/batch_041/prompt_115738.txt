I'm a software engineer with no real philosophy background, and I've somehow ended up running the last few sessions of a lunchtime quantum-computing reading group at work. The final session is graded, so I have to write one question, a model answer, and a marking scheme, and I'm out of my depth on the last two.

The obvious question -- does Bell's theorem prove the world is nonlocal? -- gets the same two-sentence answer out of everyone: it rules out local hidden variables, but there's no signalling, so relativity is fine. Full marks, no thinking. So I've handed them a distinction I made up and I want the question to force them to actually use it. Three senses of 'nonlocal': (1) signalling -- measuring here changes what you can predict over there in a way you could exploit to send a message; (2) causal -- there's a real influence going from one wing to the other faster than light, you just can't use it to signal; (3) non-separability -- the two particles don't have separate states of their own; the joint facts about the pair aren't fixed by the facts about each particle.

Here's the question I want to set: which of those three does Bell's theorem actually force you to accept, and can you have (3) without (2), or (2) without (1)? Then the part I really want them to nail: a mate of mine keeps saying 'if there's no signalling then there isn't any nonlocality, its all just bookkeeping in the maths' -- can you actually hold that, and what does it cost you? I want them to say what the standard escape routes (no facts of the matter before measurement / a real nonlocal mechanism / the settings were never free to begin with) each have to say about senses 1-3, and to come down on one of them instead of shrugging.

Constraints: 45 minutes, about 700 words, 20 marks, and the students are mostly engineers who've done the reading but have no physics or philosophy degrees. Make the marking scheme specific enough that I can tell an A from a B -- the exact moves that have to be in a top answer, and the exact plausible-sounding muddles that should cost marks, since I definitely can't spot those myself. And tell me how confident you are in the model answer and which single point you're least sure of, so I don't mark a student down for landing on the other side of a genuinely open question.
