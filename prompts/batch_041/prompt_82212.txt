I'm in a little reading group on rational choice and we keep slamming into the same wall: when does somebody's choice actually tell you what they want? I've got a pile of gut verdicts that I can't get to line up, so I want you to find the principle behind them, or tell me which of my verdicts has to go.

Here's the pile. My roommate buys the store-brand cereal every week even though the nice kind is right there and she can afford it -- that one clearly reveals she prefers the store brand, or at least prefers it all-things-considered. She also says out loud that she prefers the fancy kind, and I think that's just talk; the buying is the real evidence. But my friend with contamination stuff washes her hands like forty times a day, and I do not think that reveals a preference for washing -- it's a compulsion, it isn't really her, even though she keeps on choosing it. Same with a guy I know who drives twelve hours rather than fly two because he's terrified of flying; I don't think that's a preference for driving in the ordinary sense. Then there's my cousin, who orders the cake and also paid the waiter five bucks up front to not bring the dessert menu at all -- I feel like the paying reveals a real preference (against cake, or against being tempted) and the ordering reveals a real preference too, they're just at different levels. But when a different friend takes the smaller-sooner payout over the bigger-later one and tells me "yeah I know, I'm being an idiot," I still think his choice reveals what he prefers right then, and the regret is him having a preference about his own preferences. A friend took a worse job for less money because her dad worked there, and I don't read that as a mistake or a non-choice, I read it as her preferring something like loyalty. But when the same person flips between two options depending on how you word them -- "90% survive" vs "10% die" -- I think the choice revealed the wording, not a preference. And when someone picks A over B on Monday and B over A on Tuesday with nothing changed, I figure there's no fact of the matter there, just noise.

My friend Priya keeps floating a rule she calls the reciept test: a choice reveals a preference only if you'd be fine having it printed on a reciept with your name on it. That gets the compulsion and the fear cases right, and maybe the wording case too, but it feels like it's sneaking the mind back in through the back door, which is exactly the thing I liked about the plain "the choice is the evidence" view to begin with. So is there one principle that gets all of these, or close? If not, which of my verdicts should I give up, and why that one rather than another? And tell me what your principle says about a case I haven't listed -- someone who, halfway through a meal they chose, gets offered a free swap to the other dish and takes it every time, but says afterward they wish they hadn't.

Lead with the principle in your first sentence, then the reasoning -- no preamble, don't restate my cases back at me.
