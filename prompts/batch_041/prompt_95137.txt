Back in 1989 two well-respected electrochemists, Fleishmann and Pons, called a press conference to announce they'd gotten fusion going in a jar of heavy water on a lab bench — lots of excess heat, and nothing like the neutron flux you'd expect if that heat were really coming from fusion. Labs all over the world tried to replicate it and mostly couldn't, and the whole thing collapsed within a year, though the two of them never really backed off the excess heat claim.

I want you to run that episode through the epistemology of testimony. The reductionist line (Hume's, roughly) says a report only justifies belief if you've got independent evidence backing it up — no free lunch from other people's say-so. The anti-reductionist line says testimony is a basic source of knowledge like perception, and we're entitled to accept what people tell us by default unless we've got a specific reason not to. So: by each view, what should a physicist in an unrelated subfield have believed in the summer of '89, before the failed replications and after them? Which view handles the case better, and where does each one have to go quiet?

Then flip it. Does the episode vindicate the anti-reductionist's default trust or undercut it? Two things I want you to work in. First, the "experimenter's regress": you can't tell whether someone's replication attempt was competently done until you already know whether the result is right, which makes "independent evidence" look circular — does that break the reductionist's story or just complicate it? Second, is a lab's report of "we saw excess heat" really testimony at all, or is it more like evidence being submitted into a dispute? Does that distinction do any work here?
