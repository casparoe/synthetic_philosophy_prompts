I'm a postdoc in philosophy, and next month I'm giving a workshop paper in our department's ethics seminar defending what I have been calling a hard Augustinian line on lying: that lying is never permissible, that the wrongness of a lie is fixed by the speaker's intention to deceive rather than by any weighing of consequences, and that the familiar hard cases — the murderer at the door, the patient given a placebo, the lie that spares someone a devastating prognosis — do not create exceptions so much as tempt us to redescribe what we are doing. The room is mostly contemporary normative ethicists, two of whom work on medical ethics and the ethics of deception, one who knows the Augustinian corpus far better than I do, and a visiting epistemologist who is sceptical that intention can do any real work in a definition. I want a Q&A prep sheet, not a defense: the eight or so objections I will actually get from this audience, ordered by how likely and how damaging each is, each phrased the way the particular objector would put it, with a two-minute reply and an honest note on where that reply is soft.

The objections I am already bracing for, so that you can tell me which is the real threat: that dropping the deception condition is now the majority view in the philosophy of language, and that bald-faced lies are counterexamples to my definition; that my position is a rigorism no one can live with and that it will be quietly abandoned at the bedside; that the criterion of the divided heart either overgenerates, sweeping in jokes, fiction and self-deception, or undergenerates, since the misleading truth-teller and the equivocator who conceals without asserting anything false seem to escape it while doing the same damage; that the case from the correspondence that occasioned Augustine's later treatise against lying — where he would not permit a person even to feign an allegiance he did not hold in order to expose a dangerous sect and protect the innocent — shows the doctrine is not merely strict but cruel; and that if intention to deceive is what does the work, then puffery, strategic ambiguity and misleading implicature end up on the same moral footing as outright lies. Please also give me the one objection I am hoping nobody in the room will raise, and be blunt about whether the position survives it. I do not need citations or a reading list; I need to know what I will be hit with, from whom, in what words, and where I am weak.
