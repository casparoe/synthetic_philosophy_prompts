I am three years into a postdoc and have to decide what to spend a two-year fellowship on. My area is the metaphysics and philosophy of mind of collectives, and I have become convinced that the cleanest test case is not the human group but the social-insect colony — the sort of thing Hofstadter dramatized in the ant-colony dialogue of Gödel, Escher, Bach, in which the colony (Aunt Hillary) is credited with memories and a personality even though no single ant carries them, and in which the colony can "die" when its ants merely become disorganized. I want to work on whether such a colony can literally have a mind, but the question fragments into a dozen sub-questions and I cannot tell which of them are load-bearing.

Assume the setup I take to be fixed: the colony's behavior is, in principle, fully accounted for by the mechanisms of individual ants and their interactions with one another and the environment; nothing further is added. Granting that, here is my provisional list of candidate open questions. (1) Whether a colony-level mind can be more than a useful description — whether there is anything it is like to be the colony, as opposed to merely a level at which we can predict and explain it. (2) Whether the colony is one subject or a bundle — whether its apparent memories, persisting across the turnover of every ant or across the splitting and reconnecting of the colony, make it a single mind over time, and what would individuate one colony-mind from another. (3) Whether the colony can have propositional attitudes — beliefs and desires — or only a lower-grade analogue such as a structured set of dispositions and goals. (4) Whether attributing a mind to the colony is answerable to anything beyond the success of our predictions, that is, whether any conceivable evidence could settle it. (5) What the colony case implies for the other candidates — the human group, the corporation, the distributed artificial system — and whether those are the same question or a different one wearing the same words. (6) Whether the whole framing of levels of description, patterns, and substrates is a trap, so that the genuinely interesting question is about what we mean by "mind" in the first place.

I do not want a survey of the literature or a reading list. I want a defended agenda. Order these questions — adding any I have missed and deleting any I should drop — by how much other work depends on them, by how tractable they are for a philosopher working alone with no laboratory, and by how neglected they are relative to their importance. For each, explain why it is upstream or downstream of the others, what a two-year project on it would actually look like, and what argument or result over the next couple of years would move it up or down the ranking. If you think the right response is to refuse the frame rather than answer the questions inside it, say so and defend that instead.
