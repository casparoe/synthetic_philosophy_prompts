I'm in a seminar on the ethics of belief and I have to write something, but before I pick a side I want to get clear on what the target actually is, because every discussion I've been in just re-litigates Clifford vs James and never says what would count as getting it right. So skip the verdict for now and give me the desiderata. What does any adequate theory of the norms governing belief have to deliver?

And I want it aimed at the case I think is the real crux, the one people gesture at and then move on from: beliefs that help bring about their own truth. The climber who can only clear the gap if he trusts that he'll clear it; the student who tests better because she believes she'll test well; the negotiator who wins because he's sure he'll win; the partner who believes in the other person's loyalty and maybe, by believing it, makes the relationship more likely to last; and the ugly mirror image — the insomniac who can't sleep because he's certain he won't, the guy who bombs the interview because he's convinced he'll bomb it. Some of these read as Jamesian licence, some as straight self-deception, and I honestly can't tell from the cases alone which is which, which is exactly why I want the criteria first.

So give me the requirements list: the cases the theory has to get right, each with a line on why it's a requirement and not just a curiosity; the distinctions it can't be allowed to collapse (believing that vs. believing in or trusting someone; believing vs. merely accepting or acting as if or hoping; a reason to believe vs. a reason to want to believe vs. a reason to try to make yourself believe; assessing the belief vs. assessing the believer; self-directed belief vs. a belief that lands on someone else); and the results it has to stay consistent with — that belief isn't under direct voluntary control, that some beliefs are genuinely self-fulfilling, that some self-fulfilling beliefs are textbook self-deception, that we do in fact criticize each other's beliefs and seem to mean it. Then tell me which of these are non-negotiable and which are just the house style of one camp (I have a suspicion that the demand that only evidence can justify belief is doing alot of disguised work in these debates, and I want to know whether it's a genuine requirement or a Cliffordian prejudice). Flag any subset you think can't be jointly satisfied — a theory that says self-fulfilling belief is fine whenever it helps can't also explain why we call some of them self-deception, and a theory that says you can't help what you believe has trouble explaining why we blame people for it — and tell me which of those tensions is real rather than rhetorical. Finally, tell me which single requirement knocks out the most candidates.

I'm using this to adjudicate an actual case, so keep the purpose in mind: a doctor deciding whether to encourage a patient's hopeful belief about recovery, or a parent wondering whether to cultivate an inflated belief in their kid — I need a list strict enough to rule things out. A short scorecard at the end is welcome (straight evidentialism, Jamesian permissivism, the pragmatic-encroachment line, the view that beliefs are just bets), but the list is what I'm after. Also, say how confident you are in the requirements themselves, and point out the one you're least sure belongs on the list.
