I teach a graduate seminar on aesthetics, and I've spent most of this term argueing with a colleague in our conservation department about forgeries, and I've ended up defending a view I'm honestly not sure I can defend properly: that the aesthetic value of a painting supervenes entirely on its intrinsic, non-relational perceptual properties -- the stuff you could in principle see on the surface -- so that a molecule-for-molecule duplicate made last Tuesday has exactly the same aesthetic value as the original, provided no one could ever tell them apart. On this view whatever we pay for the original over the copy is real value, maybe, but it isn't *aesthetic* value; it's historical, sentimental, fetishistic, take your pick, and the market's premium is just a price on that non-aesthetic fact. What I want from you is the case against this, not a survey of the literature -- I'm after defeaters, sorted by how decisive they'd actually be, with a clear line between what should be fatal to the view and what's merely awkward. Cases, intuitions, arguments, commitments of mine that turn out to be unacceptable, things the view predicts that its rivals don't -- all fair game. And I want you to be straight about which of these my own side would concede rather than only the ones critics enjoy pressing.

Let me flag the pressure points that worry me, since I don't want a generic list. First, whether my view can make any sense of the thought that some aesthetic properties are constituted by causal history -- the achievement character of a work, say, the fact that this surface is the record of someone actually solving a problem, so that the duplicate has every surface property but the achievement belongs to the other one. Is the achievement aesthetically relevant or not, and does my view have a principled way to decide, or does it just help itself to whatever answer keeps the thesis intact? Second, the familiar point that learning a work's provenance can change what features you're in a position to notice: does that give a difference in perceptual or aesthetic content even on my own terms, or does it collapse into "different attention, same content," and if it collapses, does anything of the argument survive? Third, the awkward cases: a canvas that three centuries of restoration have left more repaint than original, so that it's a ship of Theseus made of brushstrokes -- does my view really imply it's aesthetically on par with a competent modern pastiche of it? And the twin-museum case, two institutions, one with the original, one with a perfect duplicate, visitors who can never tell -- do I have to say the collections are aesthetically equal, and does that sound like a consequence I should accept or one that shows the view is missing something? Also the case where all we've got are copies of something lost, which seems to put pressure on the other side rather than mine, but I want to know if that's a real asymmetry or just a nice story.

The thing that nags at me most is that the view might be unfalsifiable by construction. If I define aesthetic value as the value that supervenes on surface properties, then of course no historical fact can count against the claim that aesthetic value supervenes on surface properties -- I've just stipulated my way to victory, and the whole dispute with my colleague may be verbal. So I want an honest verdict on that: is this a view with genuine exposure that happens to be well armored in places, or a definition wearing the costume of a thesis? If it's the latter, say so, and tell me whether that's a defect of this particular position or a sign that the original/copy dispute was never a substantive one to begin with. For what it's worth, the thing that would actually move me is a case where two objects with identical surfaces must differ in aesthetic value -- if you can give me one that survives scrutiny, that's the one I care about, and I'd rather hear about that than about the print-and-edition cases, which I suspect are a seperate mess I'll deal with next term.
