I'm a graduate student in intellectual history, and for the past year a reading group I'm in has kept returning to the same fight about historical objectivity, to the point where I've started to think we are talking past one another rather than genuinely disagreeing. On one side stands the older, source-critical ideal we associate with Ranke: the historian's task is to work past the partisanship of chroniclers and reconstruct the past as it essentially was, letting the documents speak for themselves, so that objectivity means fidelity to the evidence and the suppression of the historian's own commitments. On the other side are the positions we borrow from Carr and from Hayden White. From Carr: that the historian cannot help selecting and arranging, that the facts of history are never raw but always already the historian's facts, chosen for their bearing on a question the historian brings. From White: that the deeper objectivity-claim is close to a category mistake, since the historian imposes a literary shape — romance, tragedy, comedy, satire — on a chronicle that has no such shape of its own, so that the meaning of a historical account is a product of emplotment rather than a discovery. Every time we meet, the Rankeans accuse the constructivists of being relativists who don't want to do the archival work, the constructivists accuse the Rankeans of being naive about how narrative works, and nobody has moved an inch in a year.

What I want from you — as someone who knows both the historiography and the philosophy of history — is a diagnosis rather than a verdict. Don't tell me who is right; tell me what kind of disagreement this actually is and why it has stalled. Is it a substantive disagreement about the metaphysics of the past — whether there is a fact of the matter about, say, what a given event meant or whether it was a turning point? A verbal disagreement trading on two senses of objectivity, objectivity-as-fidelity-to-evidence versus objectivity-as-absence-of-perspective? Or two parties answering different questions under one heading, one asking how we establish what happened and the other asking how historical writing means? My own hypothesis, which I'd like you to test rather than assume, is that the impasse partly dissolves once we separate two things the word construction is doing — selection, which may be unavoidable and innocent, and emplotment, which may be an additional and optional act — and I want you to say whether that distinction actually helps or merely relabels the problem. Consider seriously, too, the possibility that a hidden empirical or formal question is doing the work (is narrative form genuinely optional to historical understanding, or constitutive of it?) and that the real dispute is about what an explanation in history has to accomplish.

Please reason carefully and, before you commit to anything, briefly lay out the strongest version of each position, since a diagnosis that a partisan would not recognize as their own is worthless. Then tell me what, if anything, would count as progress given your diagnosis: a distinction, a change in the question, a criterion for when narrative imposition is distortion rather than necessary selection, or an argument that no progress should be expected here and why. I am much less interested in a survey of the literature than in an argument about the structure of the impasse, so please commit to a diagnosis and defend it.
