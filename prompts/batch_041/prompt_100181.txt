Last night I lost an argument to a friend who has spent years working on post-conflict justice: when I pressed her on whether it is worth prosecuting aging perpetrators decades after the fact, she closed the discussion with 'justice delayed is justice denied,' as though that line disposed of the question and settled things in favor of the truth-commission-and-amnesty path. I suspect the phrase is being made to carry more weight than it can, but I cannot say precisely why. Could you lay out, as a menu rather than a single favored reading, the distinct claims that sentence might be making — the procedural one (a defendant is entitled to a timely trial), the evidentiary one (delay corrodes proof), the retributive one (does desert decay with time?), the expressive or recognitional one (delay tells victims their suffering was never taken seriously), the deterrent one — and for each say whether it is plausible and whether it actually supports her conclusion? I am also struck that some legal systems appear to codify the slogan, in statutes of limitation, while others appear to reject it, in the imprescriptibility of crimes against humanity; tell me whether those are two faces of a single claim about time and justice or two quite different claims. And of the readings you give, which is she most likely to have meant, and does any of them survive the observation that in some transitions the delay is deliberate design rather than mere failure?
