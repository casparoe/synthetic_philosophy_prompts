I'm a longtime reader of the usual rationalist-adjacent blogs and forums — the kind of person who got pulled into the "how old are nations, really?" question through the modernist thesis that nations are modern constructs whose apparently ancient traditions were largely manufactured over the last couple of centuries (the Hobsbawm-and-Ranger line about invented tradition, Anderson's imagined communities), and who keeps noticing that this framing never quite matches what states actually do. I'm trying to turn a vague unease into a sharp question, and I'd like you to act as a political philosopher or social ontologist who specializes in exactly this reconstruct-the-implicit-theory-behind-a-practice kind of analysis. Think carefully and brainstorm a little before committing to a line, and flag your confidence as you go.

Here's the practice I want to interrogate, stated as concretely as I can. Modern states run a large, expensive apparatus whose explicit purpose is to make a nation feel old: ministries of culture fund heritage designations and intangible-cultural-heritage registers, national museums present a continuous story running from some deep origin to the present, school curricula teach a canonical national narrative, language academies standardize and purify a national tongue, and the state stages commemorative days, restoration projects, and ceremonial pageants that present themselves as revivals of something immemorial. The same states, in the same breath, insist on authenticity — that the restored building, the revived rite, the standardized dialect is the genuine article rather than a fabrication — and treat any suggestion that it was assembled recently as an insult or a scandal. What I can't decide is what theory of the nation this whole apparatus presupposes. Does it require officials and citizens to actually believe the primordialist story (nations are ancient, continuous, natural), making the practice a machine for producing a belief the state needs? Or does the apparatus only make sense under a constructivist self-understanding, where the people running it know perfectly well the nation is being built and are doing so deliberately? And is there a coherent middle position — some notion of authenticity that is about continuity of practice or sincere collective commitment rather than literal antiquity — that would let the state have both?

So: reconstruct the implicit theory of nationhood this apparatus presupposes. Separate what the practice strictly requires from what it merely fits comfortably with; say which rival theories of the nation it quietly rules out and which it leaves untouched; tell me whether its parts presuppose consistent things or whether the insistence on authenticity sits in genuine tension with the manufacture of continuity; and identify where the practice is philosophically underdetermined, such that several incompatible theories would license it. Finally, tell me what would actually have to change — in the museum, the curriculum, the ceremony — if we swapped the presupposed theory for a frankly constructivist one. A concrete worked example or two would help; I'm happy for you to choose them, though I'd rather you pick cases you can describe from general knowledge than lean on specific scholarship. No need for citations or a reading list — I'll add those myself. What I want is the reconstruction, the argument for it, and an honest statement of how confident you can be that the practice has a single implicit theory at all rather than a bundle of locally convenient ones.
