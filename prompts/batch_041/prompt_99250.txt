I host a small podcast on philosophy of biology, and next week's guest is a philosopher who defends the reality of free-floating rationales: reasons that natural selection can be said to have acted on, or to have tracked, even though no organism, gene, or selection mechanism ever represents them. She is very good at deflecting objections with a move I have heard her make twice—she says the rationale is in the selection process, not in a mind, and that asking for a reasoner is like asking for the author of a market price. I want the interview to go beyond letting her restate the view, so please act as an expert in philosophy of biology, teleosemantics, and the metaphysics of reasons, and draft eight to ten interview questions, each with a follow-up keyed to the answer she is most likely to give. The questions should be in the weeds of the position, not generic invitations to summarize, and they should be answerable by someone reasoning from the view itself rather than by someone reciting a literature review.

I need at least one question on each of the following, though you may interleave them: first, the ontological status of a free-floating rationale—whether it is a cause, a norm, a disposition, or an interpretive posit, and what explanatory work it does that ordinary function-talk does not; second, whether a rationale requires any actual selection history, or whether one can float free even of selection, as in a trait that arose by drift and was never selected, a trait whose fitness advantage existed only in an environment that no longer exists, or a counterfactual rationale for a trait that would have been favored had it appeared; third, whether free-floating rationales can ground genuine representational content and correctness, or only success and failure in a purely instrumental sense; and fourth, how the view handles cases where the apparent beneficiary is absent, obscure, or multiple—spandrels, exaptations, parasitic traits, and traits that benefit no one. Please also include a question about the indeterminacy problem: when two incompatible selective stories both predict the same trait, what makes one of them the rationale rather than a merely possible rationale, and a question about whether the same apparatus extends to non-biological systems such as market prices, cultural evolution, or reinforcement-learning agents without becoming merely metaphorical. For each of these, press the dialectic: anticipate the standard reply, include the follow-up that makes the reply harder to use, and end with one question about what evidence or argument could count against the reality of free-floating rationales.

Before you draft, brainstorm the strongest objections and the most likely deflections, then select the questions that force the guest to do philosophical work rather than recite a position. Write the questions in a polished, conversational but rigorous register suitable for a serious interview. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, citations, or suggested sources; the questions must stand on their own for someone who cannot look anything up. Do not put words in the guest's mouth beyond the stock deflection I mentioned, and do not invent specific publications or attributions.
