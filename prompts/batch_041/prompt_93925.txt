I am a novelist — four novels, the most recent a family chronicle set among shipbuilders on the Clyde — and I have spent the last three dinner parties being talked at by a philosopher friend about a puzzle he insists is a touchstone for how one thinks about rational choice. As he tells it, a being of uncanny predictive skill has already looked into your character and arranged two boxes. One is transparent and holds a modest sum, say a thousand pounds. The other is opaque and holds either a fortune or nothing at all, depending on what the being predicted you would do: if it foresaw that you would take both boxes, it left the opaque box empty; if it foresaw that you would take only the opaque box, it filled it. The prediction has already been made and the contents fixed; nothing you do now can alter them. You may take the opaque box alone, or both. My friend says that respectable opinion divides sharply, that one side argues that taking both boxes is simply dominance reasoning while the other holds that taking the one box is the only policy that reliably correlates with wealth, that each side has an arsenal of replies about imperfect predictors, about whether the predictor might be a perfect being or merely a very good psychologist or even a copy of you, and that there is a notorious retort along the lines of asking the two-boxers why they are not rich. I have no training in probability or decision theory whatever and could not reconstruct the technical names for the two positions, so please define any such term once and move on; I do not want a reading list, and I am not much interested in who said what to whom.

What I should like from you is a translation rather than an explanation. Take the whole dispute — both positions, the chief objections to each, the replies, and the conditions under which people say the answer flips — and restate it as faithfully as you can in the vocabulary I actually use: foreshadowing and backstory, dramatic irony, the difference between what a character has reason to do and what the book requires her to do, the implied author, free indirect discourse, the discipline of making an ending feel inevitable without feeling arbitrary, the loaded gun introduced in the first act, the abandoned draft and the road not taken in revision, the second reading in which the reader sees what she missed the first time. Then give me the residue report, which is the part I care about. Which claims survive the translation intact? Which survive only if I let you bend my vocabulary in ways a working novelist would notice and object to? And which claims have no counterpart in my vocabulary at all — no way of saying them that does not smuggle in a premise my craft does not contain? For each residue, tell me whether the fault lies with my vocabulary, which is admittedly built for one world at a time rather than for distributions over worlds, or whether it reveals that the philosophers are disagreeing about less than they suppose. Finally, since I suspect my trade has resources theirs may lack — a novelist really does generate counterfactual versions of the same scene and compare them — say whether that gives me a way of talking about the puzzle that the standard framing cannot express, and whether that tells for or against the way the puzzle is posed.

Please write the whole answer in British English, and be as concrete as you can: where the translation works, show me a sentence of the sort a novelist would actually write; where it fails, show me the sentence I would be forced to write instead, and why it would be a lie.
