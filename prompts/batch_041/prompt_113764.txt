You're a decision theorist who's spent years on choice under ambiguity — Ellsberg-style stuff — and you're unusually good at explaining it to people who haven't read any of it. Think carefully before you answer, and start by brainstorming what the research on ambiguity aversion does and doesn't actually show, because the pop version of it gets a lot wrong.

Context: I'm a program officer at a mid-size foundation. I recomend grants of $1-3M, maybe 30-40 a year, and my committee has to sign off. The recurring shape of my problem is this: I can fund an approach with a real evidence base, where I roughly know the odds, or a newer approach where nobody can honestly tell me the odds. My board reads that as "risky" and leans to the known thing, and so do I. But I have a nagging feeling we're leaving value on the table — and also that sometimes the aversion is exactly right.

Write me a decision procedure I can actually run on a proposal, grounded in what's really known about ambiguity aversion rather than the slogan version. Questions in order, what counts as a good enough answer to each, where it stops with a no, and where it hands the decision back to a person because its a values question rather than a factual one. Things it has to handle, because these are where I get stuck:

- The difference between not knowing the odds and not knowing what the possible outcomes even are. Ellsberg's urn is the first kind; my grant decisions often feel like the second. Does the procedure apply to the second, and if not, say so plainly.
- The move of trying to convert ambiguity into risk — base rates for analogous things, a distribution over the unknown probability — and how to tell honest conversion from fake precision.
- The case where the missing information is being withheld by someone with an interest in my choice. My instinct is that's where the aversion stops being a bias.
- The case where reducing the ambiguity is cheap: a pilot, a phone call to someone who's tried it. If information is cheap, the procedure should tell me to go get it, not to choose.
- The fact that I do this forty times a year. A standing policy of dodging the ambiguous option has a cost that compounds, and no single decision shows it to me.
- Whether the aversion only bites because I'm comparing my knowledge to someone else's. I notice I feel it much less when I'm the most informed person in the room.

Also the coherence trap: if I'd rather bet on red than on black, and also rather bet on black-or-yellow than on red-or-yellow, I've contradicted myself, because those two pairs differ only in what happens on yellow. Whatever procedure you give me should make me catch myself doing that kind of thing, not just catch myself being timid.

Then run it on three cases: (1) a well-evidenced literacy program with a known-ish effect size against a novel tutoring model with no track record; (2) renewing a program we've funded for years whose evaluation is weak, against a new one with strong evidence; (3) a case where the procedure gives me an answer I won't like — you pick whether that's fund the ambiguous option or no, your aversion here is doing real work, but make it a case where I'd rather hear the opposite.

Last thing: be honest about what the procedure can't do — where it's just dressing up a judgment call, and how it would misfire.
