I recently stopped buying factory-farmed meat and dairy, mainly for animal-welfare reasons, and I have not been able to settle whether the change is genuinely justified or merely expressive. What I want from you is a credence, an actual number, that the following proposition is true: an individual's reduction of his or her purchases of factory-farmed animal products by roughly ninety percent over the next five years makes a positive expected difference to the number of animals raised in those conditions, and that difference is large enough to make the reduction morally obligatory on act-consequentialist grounds.

Please decompose the question rather than assert a view. At a minimum I want separate probabilities for: (1) that a marginal reduction in demand translates into any reduction in production at all, given that supply may be lumpy, since producers re-contract and adjust herd sizes at discrete thresholds and an individual's foregone purchases may fall entirely within slack that is never removed; (2) that any resulting reduction is not reabsorbed through price effects, whereby the purchases I forgo lower prices and are simply taken up by other buyers; (3) that the correct comparison class is the counterfactual in which people situated like me act in concert, rather than the counterfactual in which only I change; (4) that some non-causal account, whether complicity in a harmful practice or a universalizability requirement, grounds the obligation even if (1) and (2) come out near zero; and (5) any further term you think belongs and I have omitted. Give each term a number and a single line of justification, show how the terms combine into your final figure, and identify which term that figure is most sensitive to. My own credence is about 0.7; my partner, who regards the whole exercise as self-indulgent, sits at about 0.15. Tell me which term we most likely disagree about, and which single piece of evidence or argument would move your number the most.
