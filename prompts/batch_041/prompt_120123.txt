I run a tiny two-person board game studio — abstract strategy stuff, two players, perfect information, no luck — and I've got alot of prototypes in a drawer and no good way to tell which ones are broken before anyone plays them. We start a new one every few weeks and I'm the one who signs off on what goes to a publisher, so it needs to be something I can defend to my business partner, who is not going to sit through a proof. The one tool I half-remember from a maths blog is the strategy-stealing thing, and I'd like to turn it into an actual checklist I can run on a prototype in an afternoon. Let me say the idea back to you so you can see where I'm at: suppose the second player had a guaranteed winning plan. The first player can make some throwaway first move that can't possibly hurt them — the argument assumes there's always one of those — and then spend the rest of the game pretending to be the second player, following that same winning plan. Now both players are winning, which is impossible, so the second player never had a winning plan, so if the game can't be a draw the first player must be able to force a win. That's it, that's all I've got, and I know the throwaway-move bit is where it's supposed to go wrong (chess, zugzwang, I remember that much).

What I need from you:

1. The procedure, in order. As questions I ask about a prototype, with what counts as a pass, what a fail tells me, where it stops with a verdict, and where it stops because the question is a judgment call I have to make myself. Written for someone who hasn't done a proof since undergrad.

2. The extra-move step is the one I care about most: how do I actually test whether an extra move can ever hurt me in a given game? I want something I can do at the table with cardboard, not a proof. And tell me when the honest answer is that I can't check it and the procedure is stuck there.

3. What each verdict licenses. If it stops at "the second player can't have a winning plan" and my game can be drawn, what have I learned? If it stops at "the first player wins" but hands me no strategy, is that a diagnosis I can act on or just trivia? Its not like I can build a bot from it or verify it by playing.

4. The fixes, and whether they work. Swap/pie rule, komi, giving the second player a head start, auctioning the first move. My guess is that the moment I add any of those the argument stops applying — but does that mean the game is now fair, or only that I've lost the only tool I had? Tell me how to check a fix rather than just asserting it works.

5. Then run it on three of mine: (a) a connection game on a rhombus where each player owns two opposite sides and wins by joining them; (b) a shrinking-board game where you must move every turn and lose when you can't move, so moves are sometimes poison; (c) a placement game we've spent eight months on, both players placing identical stones and scoring at the end, where playtests say it's dead even but I have a bad feeling. If the procedure kills (c), say so.

6. Where the procedure can't decide, mark it as such. I'd rather have a checklist that says "here a human decides" than one that pretends every step is mechanical.

Last thing: don't sell me a consensus that isn't there. Separate what's actually settled about this argument from what people who know the area still argue about — for instance whether a game that's provably a first-player win is unshippable, or whether a swap rule really does neutralise the advantage, or whether a proof that gives you no strategy should count as knowing anything at all. If you're not sure whether something is contested, say that rather than picking a side.
