A colleague on our town's civic committee has been pressing a proposal to replace our annual budget referendum with a citizens' assembly of twenty-four residents chosen by lot, and I have been asked to write a short piece for the local newsletter setting out the epistemic case for and against. Before I put my name to anything, I want to test the authority I have been handed.

The committee's chair, who is well read but not a specialist, circulated a summary of Condorcet's jury theorem drawn from a recent book on democracy. As best I can reconstruct it, it runs as follows:

'Condorcet's jury theorem is the mathematical proof that democracy works. It shows that if each voter is even slightly more likely than not to get a binary question right, then the larger the electorate, the more likely the majority is to get it right, and in the limit the probability of a correct majority approaches certainty. Adding voters can therefore never hurt: even the least informed citizen can only push the majority toward the truth, which is why universal suffrage is not merely just but epistemically optimal, and why any smaller body, however expert, must be less reliable than the electorate it was drawn from. Nor does the theorem require that everyone be equally competent, only that the voters be better than chance on average, so it survives quite ignorant electorates. Its one real assumption is independence: each voter must form their own view without consulting the others. That is why the theorem fits Condorcet's idealized jurors but not us, since we all read the same headlines and watch the same feeds, and correlated error destroys the result outright. Condorcet himself, who was no friend of the crowd, drew what he took to be the obvious conclusion: that the franchise should be restricted to those whose judgment cleared the threshold, with the rest of the population represented in the assembly but not voting. And the analysis cuts more than one way: it also shows that once a majority prefers A to B and B to C, it must prefer A to C, so majority rule is internally coherent as well as truth-tracking. What the theorem really establishes, in the end, is that on a binary question truth is simply what the majority decides; there is no further standard to which we could appeal.'

I suspect this is a mixture of things I should trust and things I should not, but I cannot tell which is which, and I would rather not build the newsletter piece on a foundation I have not tested. Would you go through it sentence by sentence and tell me, for each claim, what a careful student of the theorem would accept as stated, what they would treat as a defensible simplification, and what is simply wrong or misattributed? Where a claim is wrong, I want to know not only that it is wrong but what the correct version is, what a defender of the theorem's use in democratic theory would say in reply, and whether the error actually matters for the practical question I care about — whether a twenty-four-person assembly can be defended, or criticized, on these grounds. I am inclined to accept the point about independence and to be suspicious of the last sentence, but I do not trust my instincts here, and I do not need a reading list; I need to know which sentences to trust.

Finally, please say how confident you are in each part of your assessment, and single out the one claim you are least sure of — the place where a specialist would most plausibly correct you.
