I have been drafting a short paper on the lottery paradox and the preface paradox, and I would like a close critical reading of it. The draft is below in full. I am interested in whether the argument works, not in whether it is well connected to a literature, so please reason about it directly rather than surveying what others have said about these paradoxes.

EXPOSURE: THE LOTTERY AND THE PREFACE ARE NOT ONE PROBLEM

It is usual to treat the lottery paradox and the preface paradox as two instances of a single difficulty. I want to argue that they are two different difficulties, and that the difference is arithmetic. If that is right, the standard menu of responses — give up closure of belief under conjunction, give up the requirement that rational belief be consistent, or raise the standard for belief to certainty — is aimed at the wrong target in one of the two cases.

1. THE CASES

Lottery. A fair lottery has n tickets, exactly one of which wins. For each ticket i, the agent's credence that ticket i loses is (n − 1)/n. Suppose her threshold for belief is at or below that. She then believes, of each ticket, that it loses. She also believes that some ticket wins, since that is fixed by the setup and her credence in it is 1. Her beliefs are jointly inconsistent, yet each is backed by the same evidence and each is supported to degree (n − 1)/n.

Preface. An author has written a book containing m claims. Each was carefully checked, and she gives each a credence of p = 1 − ε. She also knows her own record: books of this kind always turn out to contain at least one error. So her credence that at least one of the m claims is false is 1 − p^m, and if that exceeds her threshold she believes it. Her beliefs are again jointly inconsistent.

2. THE APPARATUS

Call the allotment of a belief B the agent's own estimate of the chance that it is false, 1 − Cr(B). Call the exposure of a set S of beliefs the sum of the allotments of its members: A(S) = Σ (1 − Cr(B)) for B in S. By linearity of expectation, the exposure of S is exactly the expected number of false beliefs in S.

I propose, tentatively:

Ledger Norm. An agent may hold a set of beliefs simultaneously only if its exposure does not exceed 1.

The thought behind the norm is that an agent is entitled to one mistake at a time: the expected number of false beliefs she is carrying at any moment must not exceed one. I flag at once that this is the least secure part of what follows, and I return to it in section 5.

3. THE ARITHMETIC

Lottery. Exposure = n × (1/n) + 0 = 1. Exactly at budget, for every n. The risk here is normalized: the n allotments partition the possible outcomes, and they sum to one however many tickets there are.

Preface. Exposure = mε + p^m. Write f(m) = mε + p^m. Then f(1) = ε + p = 1, and f(m + 1) − f(m) = ε(1 − p^m) > 0 whenever p < 1. So f is strictly increasing, and for every m ≥ 2 the exposure exceeds 1, growing without bound as the book grows. The risk here is unnormalized: each claim carries its own independent chance of error, and the meta-belief adds its own allotment on top.

So the lottery agent is exactly at budget no matter how many tickets there are, and the modest author is over budget no matter how few claims she makes.

4. WHAT THIS IS SUPPOSED TO SHOW

If the Ledger Norm is right, the two paradoxes come apart.

(i) The lottery agent's inconsistent set violates nothing. Her inconsistency is free. The response menu is therefore misdirected: there is nothing for her to give up, because nothing has been overspent.

(ii) The modest author's trouble is not that her beliefs are inconsistent. It is that she has spent more than she has. On this diagnosis the right question is not whether to keep conjunction but which item she should surrender, and the budget answers: she cannot keep all m claims together with the meta-belief, though she may keep all m claims alone (exposure mε, within budget whenever mε ≤ 1) or the meta-belief alone (exposure p^m).

(iii) Exposure decomposes usefully. For a set consisting of first-order beliefs plus a meta-belief saying that at least one of them is false, exposure = (expected number of false first-order beliefs) + (chance that all the first-order beliefs are true). The second term is exactly the allotment of the meta-belief, since the meta-belief is false precisely when the first-order beliefs are all true. For the lottery agent the first term is 1 and the second is 0, so she spends the whole budget on the first term and gets the meta-belief free. For the modest author the first term is mε and the second is p^m, and it is the sum that overruns. Her budget is blown by her willingness to hold a belief whose truth is the conjunction of all her first-order beliefs being true.

5. OBJECTIONS I CANNOT ANSWER

(a) The budget is arbitrary. Three beliefs held at credence 0.6 each are mutually consistent, and yet have exposure 1.2, and so are forbidden. That is absurd, so the norm as stated is too strong. I can see three repairs and cannot choose between them: relativize the budget to beliefs that are deployed together in a single inference; let the budget scale with the number of beliefs held; or replace the absolute budget with a comparative requirement that the agent minimize exposure subject to her evidence. I do not know whether any of these keeps the lottery and the preface on opposite sides of the line, and if none does, the apparatus is an artifact.

(b) The norm is blind to dependence, which is what really separates the cases. What distinguishes the lottery from the preface is that the lottery's errors are mutually exclusive while the preface's are independent. Exposure is a sum and cannot see the difference. So the fact that the norm returns opposite verdicts may be a coincidence of the numbers rather than a diagnosis. I would like to know whether the coincidence can be turned into an argument.

(c) The meta-belief may be second-order. In the preface, the belief that at least one claim is false is a remark about the set of first-order beliefs. If such acknowledgments do not draw on the first-order budget, the modest author is not over budget and the paradox returns unchanged. I do not know how to settle whether the preface belief is a member of the set or a commentary on it, and I suspect the arithmetic cannot settle it.

(d) A rival principle. A different principle separates the cases just as sharply: an agent may hold an inconsistent set if she is certain of the member that would have to be surrendered to restore consistency. The lottery agent is certain that some ticket wins; the modest author is not certain that one of her claims is false. On that principle too the lottery is licensed and the preface is not. It is worth asking which principle is doing the work, since they diverge on a case: a preface whose author is certain, from a source she trusts absolutely, that at least one of her claims is false. The Ledger Norm forbids her; the rival permits her.

6. WHERE I HAVE GOT TO

Tentatively: consistency is not the constraint on a rational set of beliefs; a budget on expected error is; and the price of inconsistency is set by the agent's own credences rather than by logic. I am not confident that the budget of one can be motivated except by the intuition I began with, and I am not confident that the lottery and the preface would still come apart if it could.
