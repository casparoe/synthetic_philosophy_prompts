I teach a second-year undergraduate module on practical reason, and I want a session that makes the internalism–externalism dispute about reasons for action land as a live problem rather than as a taxonomy of positions students can recite. The group is sixteen students, we have seventy-five minutes, and the room can be rearranged. What I want from you is a structured role-play — a game with rules, roles, scenario cards, a scorekeeping rule that needs no money, and a facilitator's script — in which participants run into the phenomenon themselves: the gap between a consideration being true of someone's situation and that consideration actually moving them. Please design it as an activity rather than as a set of discussion questions; the words 'internalism' and 'externalism' should not be spoken until the debrief, and I don't need a reading list or citations — describe the positions in your own terms rather than leaning on names and dates. Take on the role of an experienced teacher of practical reason who also designs games, and think carefully about the incentive structure before you commit to anything, because the whole thing collapses if the rules make it cheap to fudge.

Here is the shape I have in mind, though you should improve on it where you see fit. Participants work in triads that rotate: an Agent, an Adviser, and a Registrar who keeps the record. Each Agent receives a private motivational profile card — a short list of things this person genuinely cares about, some of them stated plainly and some latent, with at least one item that could plausibly be engaged by the scenario if the Adviser is clever. The Adviser receives a situation card built around a consideration that is clearly true of the Agent's circumstances but that appears nowhere on the profile. The standing rule of the game is that the Agent may act only if the Adviser's argument reaches the action from something already on the profile, using true beliefs and correct deliberation; the Agent may not simply adopt a new concern because it is true that they ought to have it. I want four rounds: first a bare external consideration; then the same situation with a countervailing item already on the profile, so that deliberation has to do real work; then a round in which the Adviser receives an additional true fact that connects the consideration to something the Agent already cares about, so that the same reason statement now travels; and finally an open round in which no connecting fact is supplied but the Agent is permitted to add a new item to the profile if and only if they can write down the chain of deliberation leading from the existing items to it. Scoring should reward the Adviser for producing action and the Agent for honesty, with the Registrar auditing any move against the profile card and awarding nothing for a move that cannot be traced back — and please build in at least one round that gives the externalist something to point to, so that the internalist verdict is not simply stipulated by the rules.

Then give me the debrief, mapping each round onto the parts of the idea it exercises: the picture on which a reason must connect with what the agent already cares about, the disputed notion of a sound deliberative route from those existing concerns to a new one, and the difference between a reason statement being true and its being able to explain anyone's action. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it — I expect students to act out of politeness, to confuse compliance with being moved, to accuse me of rigging the deck, and to spot the connecting fact as a cheat — and how to handle each. Finally, be frank about what the activity cannot reproduce and that I shall have to supply myself: in particular, that it stipulates a closed motivational set, whereas the real dispute turns on whether deliberation can genuinely generate new concerns, and that by handing each Agent a card I have settled by fiat a question about the fixity of character that the philosophical argument has to earn.
