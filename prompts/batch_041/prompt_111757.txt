I'm a retired geochemist who finally got around to philosophy of science after thirty-odd years of treating the mantle as a real object and not losing sleep over it. I now run a small interview series for our local philosphy-of-science reading group, and next month's guest is a philosopher who thinks the pessimistic meta-induction, as usually run, is a sideshow. His line: you don't need the history of science at all, because the parts of a theory that do no predictive work never get any credit from the parts that do — and realism is exactly a claim about the non-predictive furniture, so predictive success is simply irrelevant to it. He calls this the no-free-credit principle and he is rather pleased with himself about it.

Could you draft me eight to ten questions for him? I want them to actually press: where credit comes from if not success, whether a posit that *is* doing predictive work and then gets abandoned (ether, caloric, whatever) doesn't blow up his distinction, why he isn't just an instrumentalist with extra steps, and what his principle does to the realist's best argument. Give me a follow-up for each, keyed to the answer he's most likely to give, and finish with the one question he'll hate: what would change his mind.
