I'm a novelist, midway through a book in which a character is very good at getting people to do things by telling them little hypotheticals — would you pull the lever, that kind of thing — and I've gotten intrested in the machinary of how that works. This is research for me, so I dont want a reading list, I want the argument itself.

Here's the thing I want you to look at. Take the trolley problem: the runaway car you can divert onto a side track so it kills one instead of five, and then the variant where the only way to stop it is to shove a large man off a footbridge. Everyone knows the puzzle. What interests me is what happened to it after. It got picked up by psychologists, who ran it on people and found most say yes to the lever and no to the shove, and that asymmetry hardened into something like an experimental result. Then it got picked up again by the driverless-car crowd: there was a big MIT website running from about 2016 to 2020 where millions of people in more than two hundred countries answered trolley-ish questions about a car with a failing brake, and the upshot was that people's preferences vary by culture, clumping into roughly three groups, with age and number and jaywalking and species all turning up as factors.

Now look at all that through the idea of an intuition pump — Dennett's term, and I like his other name for it, a persuasion machine: not an argument, a device for getting you to see something a particular way. My own gloss, take it or leave it: a pump has to paint a flat backdrop so you'll look at the figure, and then everyone argues about the figure while the backdrop gets quietly repainted. Where the word surely shows up in the write-up is usually where the backdrop is doing the work. And here's my suspicion, which I want you to either kill or confirm: the Moral Machine didn't test the pump at all. It promoted the backdrop to the figure — age, species, social status, who was jaywalking — which makes it a preference-elicitation machine, a different beast. If that's right, then the cultural clustering everyone found may say almost nothing about whether the original pump travels.

So give me the analysis running in both directions. What does the pump lens actually explain about how the trolley problem travelled — why the lever/shove asymmetry survived as a finding, why a device built to tease apart doing-and-allowing from intending-as-a-means ended up as a measurement instrument, and what it means that its output then got treated as a design spec for cars. Where does the lens let me down or overreach — I suspect it's very good at debunking and bad at telling me when a pump is doing honest work, and I can't tell whether the cultural variation means the pump is broken or just, as advertised, audience-relative. Then the other direction: what does this episode show about the pump idea itself — does the trolley problem's afterlife confirm it, or embarrass it, given that this pump plainly did generate a research programme, which makes persuader-versus-engine-of-discovery look like the wrong contrast.

Be straight with me about how confident you are in each claim, say where you're guessing, and single out the one point you're least sure of.
