Two engineers on my team are deadlocked over a piece of legacy code, and they've asked me to weigh in as a neutral third party. I don't have a philosophy background, so I can't tell who has the better of it.

The code is a nightly scheduled job that nobody now at the company can explain. It has never fired in any environment we can observe, and all it would do is write a handful of rows into a table we don't otherwise read. One engineer argues it should stay, on Chesterton's fence grounds: you don't tear down a fence until you know why it was built, and since no one can account for why this exists, removing it is reckless. The other makes three replies. (1) The fence principle is a warning against acting in ignorance, not a standing presumption that anything old is load-bearing; we have investigated, found no live dependency, and that discharges the ignorance, so the fence can come down. (2) Keeping the job isn't free — it's live code with its own failure modes and its own maintenance cost, so leaving it alone is itself a choice rather than a safe default. (3) The principle really only bites for things that were deliberately built for a purpose, whereas this job may be an accretion, a path worn by habit rather than a fence anyone chose, and there is no builder whose reasons we owe deference to. The first engineer responds that (3) actually strengthens his case: if we can't distinguish a fence from a worn path, we should take the cautious reading, and in any event accreted things persist because something keeps selecting for them, so they have reasons too, which we would need to understand before deleting them. The second engineer counters that this proves far too much — on that reasoning nothing is ever safe to change, because everything that exists has survived something — and that his colleague has quietly turned understand-before-you-act into don't-act-under-uncertainty, which is neither what the principle says nor a workable engineering rule. The first engineer's closing point is an asymmetry: a needless job costs us some clutter, while a load-bearing one we delete could corrupt data or break a customer, so the downside is lopsided enough to justify leaning toward keeping it.

Please adjudicate this move by move rather than just declaring a winner: which of the three replies land, whether the selection-gives-it-a-reason argument and the asymmetry argument actually rescue the keep-it position, and what both engineers are missing.
