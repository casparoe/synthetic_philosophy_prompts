In six weeks I am to give the closing address at a small interdisciplinary symposium on self and narrative, and I would like to walk into the question period prepared. I am a novelist, not an academic, and the claim I mean to defend is this: the first-person narrator's "I" is not a picture of a self but the working model of one — a strange loop of the kind Hofstadter describes, a symbol that the text turns back upon itself — with one modification that I have come to think is the whole point. The loop never closes inside the book. I call that gap the leak, and the moment a reader notices that the circuit runs through her the hand-off; a self, on my account, is what a leaky loop feels like from the inside. The novel I will read from ends its second chapter with the narrator transcribing a letter that turns out, line by line, to be the page in the reader's hands.

The room will be mostly empirical psychologists who study self-concept and autobiographical memory, together with two analytic philosophers of mind who work on self-representation and metacognition and a handful of literary scholars who think about narrative voice. They are friendly to narrative and impatient with metaphysics, and each will hear my claim in the vocabulary of their own field.

Prepare my question-period sheet: exactly five objections, ordered by how much damage they would do to the claim, beginning with the one I am hoping nobody asks. Phrase each as the person who would raise it would phrase it — the psychologists in theirs, the philosophers in theirs, the narratologists in theirs — and give me, for each, what I should concede, a reply I can deliver in two minutes, and the point at which that reply goes soft. Do not offer me citations, reading lists, or authorities to invoke; I could not produce them on the spot and they would not help me.

Answer as a numbered list of exactly five items, each no longer than two sentences, with no introduction and no conclusion. Write in American English.
