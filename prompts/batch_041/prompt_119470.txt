I'm a columnist who covers science and ideas, and for the better part of a year I've been reporting on a mid-sized grantmaking foundation — a tech-fortune endowment that has quietly become, in the small world of effective-altruism-adjacent philanthropy, a test case for what the cause-prioritization triad of importance, tractability, and neglectedness looks like once a real board has to vote on it. I have four years of its internal decision memos, and the pattern is considerably harder to read than the foundation's public page about how it chooses causes. It funded a cheap, well-evidenced public-health program in an area where three large funders were already active, reasoning that those funders had exhausted the obvious opportunities but left this particular one open. It turned down a speculative, potentially enormous project — the kind whose payoff, if it ever arrived, would dwarf everything else in the portfolio — because no one could say what a successful first year would even look like. It funded a small field-building grant in a subject with no other funders and no near-term deliverables, on the grounds that if it didn't, nobody would. And it turned down a proposal its own staff rated highly cost-effective, because the space was crowded and a marginal dollar would mostly displace other donors' money. The board evidently regards those four decisions as perfectly consistent with one another, which is the part I can't yet explain to readers.

Now the case I'll be filing about. The next agenda item is a decade-long, unusually expensive program in an area that until recently had almost no funders, but where a national research agency and a billionaire have each just announced major commitments; the underlying science is speculative, though its proponents point to one concrete intermediate milestone — a shared benchmark that would make progress at least partly measurable — and the proposal's own case is that the new money is complementary, aimed at infrastructure and a training pipeline rather than at the thing this grant would pay for. Treat the four precedents as data rather than decoration. Which rules about how importance, tractability, and neglectedness combine are consistent with all four? I can construct two or three that fit, but I want to know which ones you'd defend, which of them say yes to the new proposal and which say no, and which precedent is the closest analog. Then give me your best guess at the rule this board is actually running, with the reasons for preferring it over the rules that fit equally well, and name the single further case that would most cleanly separate the surviving rules from one another, along with what each would predict about it. Finally, for the sentence I have to write in the column: does this track record show that the foundation treats neglectedness as a gate, as a tiebreaker among otherwise acceptable candidates, or merely as a serviceable proxy for whether an extra dollar would have changed anything? I need the reasoning, not a reading list.
