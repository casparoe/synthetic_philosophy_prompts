I am a doctoral student in philosophy writing on relational accounts of personhood, and I have spent the last several months on the Southern African family of views usually gathered under the heading Ubuntu. My difficulty is not in understanding the broad claim — that one becomes a person through other persons, that personhood is achieved and sustained in relations of mutual recognition rather than possessed by a solitary subject — but in getting anyone to say what that claim actually commits one to. The view is stated at a level of generality that lets it absorb any case, and the disagreements that matter never surface because no one sets out the cases the account is answerable to. I would like to try the reverse order: here are my own verdicts on a range of cases, and I want you to tell me what principle, if any, they add up to.

A newborn, I think, is a person — fully, not a candidate for personhood or a person-in-waiting — even though it cannot yet reciprocate recognition or take part in any communal practice. A man in advanced dementia who no longer recognises his family and can no longer participate in the practices that are supposed to constitute personhood: still fully a person. A hermit who has lived alone for thirty years and severed every relation: I want to say still a person, but this is where I become uneasy, because on the view I am drawn to there seems to be nothing left for his personhood to consist in. A man permanently expelled from his community for a grave crime: I want to say he remains a person but has lost standing — something has been taken from him, though not everything. A long-dead ancestor whom his descendants still name in lineage ceremonies, and around whose life they still orient their own: I am inclined to say he is still a person in the relevant sense, or at any rate not merely a remembered one. A corporation, by contrast, is not a person, however densely it is embedded in relations of recognition and obligation. An artificial companion that a household names, consults and treats as a member is not a person either, no matter how much recognition is actually directed at it. And a group whose personhood is systematically denied by the wider society — a caste, a despised minority — consists of persons regardless of the denial.

My tentative proposal is that I am running together two things that should be separated. There is a thin layer of personhood — being a locus of moral concern, a subject whose life can go better or worse — which is not constituted by any relation and cannot be lost; and a thick layer — full standing as a member, with the claims and authority that go with it — which is constituted by relations of mutual recognition and can be gained, diminished and forfeited. I suspect the thin layer is what is doing the work in my verdicts about the infant, the man with dementia, the hermit and the shunned criminal, that the thick layer is what I am really tracking when I say the shunned man has lost something, and that both are needed to explain why the ancestor is not simply gone while the corporation and the artificial companion are not persons at all. But I cannot tell whether this is a genuine distinction or a way of having the view both ways, and I cannot tell which of my verdicts it obliges me to give up. What I want from you is a principle, or a small number of candidate principles, stated precisely enough that I can see what each clause is doing: for each clause, the case that forces it; what the principle says about cases I have not put to you — the person in solitary confinement for decades, the last surviving speaker of a dying language, someone in a persistent vegetative state, the not-yet-born, and whatever case you think does the most damage to my proposal; and an honest account of which of the verdicts above the principle cannot accommodate. If the thin/thick distinction turns out to have the right shape, say so and say what fixes the boundary between the layers; if it turns out to be a fudge, say that instead and tell me which verdict has to go. I would rather have a simpler principle that overturns one of my verdicts than an elaborate one that saves them all by making personhood do whatever I need it to do in each case.
