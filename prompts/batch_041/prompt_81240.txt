Ok so I'm in a lab that does verified software, and we write all our proofs in one of those proof assistants where excluded middle isn't just handed to you — you have to postulate it, and the lab norm is basically dont, on the grounds that we want to be able to pull a running program out of the proof at the end. The thing that keeps bugging me: the rest of my math education was wall to wall proof by contradiction. Bolzano–Weierstrass is a real theorem, I got a grade on it, nobody told me it was only true in some second rate sense. So what does our rule actually commit us to? That existence really means you can build one? That there are two senses of 'exists', and the classical one is legitimate but somehow not real existence? Or is the rule just an engineering constraint about extracting code, with no philosophical baggage at all — in which case is the lab using a constructivist tool while quietly ignoring what constructivists actually claim? And it's not like we're purists about everything, there are axioms floating around in the standard library that dont compute either, so why is excluded middle the one that gets flagged?

What I really want: (1) the most defensible reconstruction of what the practice presupposes, and what it merely fits well with rather than strictly requires, (2) what would actually break if we took the constructivist line seriously — which standard results would have to go or be reformulated, and whether the classical theorems we'd keep using anyway would still be true in any interesting sense, (3) if existence just is constructibility, what happens to truth — is a statement true only once we have a proof, so that the classical mathematician's true-but-unprovable is a mistake rather than a discovery? I've also heard you can sometimes still squeeze a program out of a classical proof if the statement has the right shape; if that's right, does it undercut the rule or not? And please seperate what's genuinely settled here from what people who know the area still fight about — I don't want one camp's position dressed up as the consensus.
