ok so I want a satirical employee handbook. Premise: a small startup — like 15 people, open plan, one kombucha tap — has decided to actually implement the philosophical theory of shared intention as company policy. The theory, roughly: when two people do something together (walking together, moving a couch, painting a house), what they've got isn't just two matching private intentions that happen to line up, its a genuinely shared intention that neither one owns by themselves, that makes each of them answerable to the other for it, and that one of them can't just unilaterally cancel by changing their mind and leaving. In the literature this gets called a joint commitment, or a we-intention, and the classic move is that it isn't reducible to each person privately intending the thing plus believing the other does too — there's something irreducibly joint, and it generates obligations and entitlements between the parties rather than just predictions about each other. Some versions go further and say the "we" is primitive: us intending to do something together can't be built out of one of us intending that we do it. There's also a we-mode versus I-mode distinction people draw, and the idea that free-riding on a shared plan is a breach of the commitment, not just bad manners.

So: write the section of the handbook that explains all this to new hires. Deadpan, numbered clauses, roughly 700-900 words. The jokes have to come from getting the theory right — why a change of heart doesn't dissolve a joint commitment, what mutual responsiveness actually demands of you day to day, what happens when someone's private intention conflicts with the shared one, what "we" even refers to once half the people have quit (does the group's action survive its members?). Include at least one worked example that starts with something totally ordinary — two coworkers walking to get coffee, say — and let the rules extrapolate from there until the reader recoils. Add a clause on dissolution, a clause on exit interviews, and a deadpan footnote from Legal.

Don't announce the joke anywhere, and don't let anyone in the document be self-aware about how dystopian it's turning out. But someone who's read Bratman or Gilbert or Searle should find it fair, and someone who's never heard of any of this should still be able to follow it. The objections — that we-intentions are just a fancy way of describing individual intentions plus mutual belief, that a group isn't the kind of thing that can really have obligations, that this is indentured servitude with extra steps — should be visible in the absurdity, never stated.
