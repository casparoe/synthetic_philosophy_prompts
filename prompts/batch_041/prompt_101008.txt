Hey, I'm a third-year PhD student in decision theory (philosophy dept, but I hang out with the econ people alot), and I've basically talked myself into a position I want you to try and break.

The position: diachronic rationality requires that your preferences be immune to a money pump. If your preferences over outcomes are cyclic — say you prefer A to B, B to C, and C to A — then someone can offer you a series of trades, each of which you strictly prefer to your current holding at the moment its offered, that leaves you with the same good you started with and less money. That isn't just an odd vulnerability; its a genuine failure of practical rationality, and it's the main reason intransitive (or otherwise cyclic) preferences are irrational rather than merely unusual.

I want the defeaters, and I want them in the weeds. In particular:

- Which premises of the pump are actually load-bearing? That you'll accept each trade because you strictly prefer its outcome; that you don't see the whole sequence coming; that money is a good you always weakly prefer more of; that ending with the same good back plus less money is worse. For each, what would the agent or the world have to be like for it to fail, and are any of those escapes live rather than just logically possible?

- The sophisticated/resolute chooser stuff. If the agent foresees the pump and adopts a policy of refusing, or forms a preference not to be the kind of agent who trades, does the pump fail, or have you just moved the cycle up a level — now it's a preference over policies that's cyclic, or a preference that contradicts the outcome preferences? I want to know whether resolve is a real escape or a redescription.

- Does the argument actually show a diachronic failure at all? The intransitivity is synchronic; the pump is just what makes it exploitable over time. If so, is the interesting claim really about coherence of preferences at a time, with the diachronic framing doing no work? Or the reverse — the synchronic incoherence is harmless and only the diachronic exploit matters?

- Is there a real difference between being makeable-worse-off and being irrational? Some people seem to treat exploitability as a symptom rather than the disease. What would it take to show that an agent with cyclic preferences is fine and the pump is just a clever adversary?

- Has the view been insulated? It feels like any refusal to trade can be redescribed as a change in preferences, which would make the thesis hard to falsify. Is that a real problem or am I overthinking it?

Rank them fatal to merely awkward, and tell me which ones a committed defender would themselves concede versus wave away. I'm most worried about the resolute-chooser line, but I don't realy trust my reasons for that. And if the whole thing rests on a distinction between preferences over outcomes and preferences over choice situations, say so, because I've never been sure which one the argument is about.
