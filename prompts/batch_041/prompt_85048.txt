I am preparing a position piece on how two parties should divide a surplus when they do not agree about their own outside options, and I want the argument made rather than surveyed. Please argue for the position I set out below, not merely explain the territory around it.

The setting I am working with. Two players bargain over a closed, convex feasible set S of utility pairs, together with a disagreement point d in S; the problem is assumed non-trivial, so some feasible agreement is better for both players than disagreement. Let the utopia point u* be the pair in which each player i receives the largest payoff u*_i achievable by anyone in S. The Nash solution is the point of S that maximizes the product of gains (u_1 − d_1)(u_2 − d_2). The Kalai–Smorodinsky solution is the maximal point of S on the segment running from d to u*; equivalently, it is the Pareto-efficient point at which the two players' proportional gains are equal, so that (u_1 − d_1)/(u*_1 − d_1) = (u_2 − d_2)/(u*_2 − d_2). The Nash solution is characterized by Pareto optimality, symmetry, invariance under positive affine transformations of the utility scales, and independence of irrelevant alternatives. The Kalai–Smorodinsky solution keeps the first three and replaces independence of irrelevant alternatives with a monotonicity condition: if the feasible set expands in such a way that, for every utility level one player might demand, the maximum the other player can simultaneously reach is weakly larger, then the payoff assigned to that other player must not fall.

The wrinkle that makes this a live problem rather than a textbook exercise. In the negotiations I care about — two firms dividing the gains from a joint venture, two states dividing the benefits of a shared waterway, an employer and a union dividing a wage bill — the disagreement point is not a publicly observable fact. It is a claim that each side makes. Each party announces a self-serving threat payoff, the announcements are cheap to make, and neither is easy to verify. The proposal I want you to defend is this: take the arithmetic mean of the two announced threat points, then apply the Kalai–Smorodinsky solution to the problem whose disagreement point is that mean. Call the resulting procedure averaged-threat Kalai–Smorodinsky, or AT-KS. Argue that AT-KS is the right default procedure for dividing the surplus in cases of this kind — as against Nash applied to the announced threat points, Nash applied to their mean, and the midpoint of the two allocations one would get by applying Kalai–Smorodinsky separately to each party's own announced threat point.

The case needs to do at least the following work. Say what makes averaging the two announced threat points more than an ad hoc split of the difference: tie it to a defensible conception of what the disagreement point is supposed to represent, or to something in the axioms, or both. Explain why the Kalai–Smorodinsky allocation rule, rather than the Nash rule, is the one that belongs with an averaged threat point, and why that pairing is not merely convenient. And work through a small numerical example of your own construction, with two or three feasible options, so that a reader can see what AT-KS selects and how it differs from each of the three rivals named above.

Then confront the objections rather than gesturing at them. The case for Nash on the strength of independence of irrelevant alternatives is the obvious one, and it needs to be met on its merits, not dismissed. The manipulability charge is the sharpest: averaging the announced threat points halves each party's incentive to inflate its announcement rather than eliminating it, since inflating one's own claim still moves the mean in one's favour, and the other party's inflation still cuts against one. Say whether that is a defect that sinks the proposal, a tolerable cost, or something a modest modification of the procedure can fix — and if you propose a fix, say what it costs. Also address whether the argument survives generalization beyond two parties, and whether it survives when the feasible set is not common knowledge but is itself contested. One more thing worth settling: in a symmetric problem with truthful announcements, does AT-KS coincide with Nash? If it does, say plainly whether that is a point in its favour or evidence that the proposal is doing less work than advertised.

Do not hedge into a survey. I want a position defended, with the places where it is weakest marked honestly.

Format the response in the following sections, in this order, with roughly the indicated lengths:

1. Verdict (~120 words): your position, and the single strongest reason for it, stated up front.
2. The problem and the proposal (~300 words): the setup made precise, AT-KS defined, and your numerical example.
3. Why averaging the threat points is principled (~400 words).
4. Why Kalai–Smorodinsky rather than Nash is the right partner for an averaged threat point (~450 words).
5. Objections (~500 words): at least the independence-of-irrelevant-alternatives objection, the manipulability objection, and the many-party question, each stated in its strongest form before being answered.
6. Where the case is weakest (~250 words): the objection you cannot fully answer, and what argument or evidence would change your mind.
7. What follows if the argument holds (~150 words): what this would change for negotiators, arbitrators, or anyone designing the procedure.

Roughly 2,000–2,500 words in total. No citations or reading lists are needed; reason from the definitions and axioms given above.
