ok so i'm in this 300-level philosophy of science class and also in the undergrad poetry workshop, and the workshop's final assignment is to bring in a poem in a received form -- pantoum, villanelle, sestina, whatever -- where the form isn't just decoration, it has to be doing something the poem couldn't do in free verse. i want to write mine about grue, because it's the one thing from phil sci that definately keeps me up at night. here's how i understand it, so you know what i'm working with: 'grue' is supposed to mean green if you look at it before some future date t and blue otherwise, so every emerald anybody has ever examined is both green and grue, and the hypothesis that all emeralds are grue is supported by exactly as much evidence as the hypothesis that all emeralds are green -- they don't even disagree about anything until t comes around. and if you make up a matching word the other way ('bleen' = blue before t, green after), then it's the green hypothesis that starts looking like the contrived one, which is the part that bugs me, because it means the reason we trust green can't just be that green tracks nature. the prof keeps saying the answer is entrenchment -- green is projectible because we've been projecting it, the predicate has a track record as a predicate, which sounds like it's about us and not about the stones -- and a couple people in class say no, it's about natural kinds instead. i don't need you to settle that, i need a poem.

what i want is a pantoum, four quatrains, strict recycling (the 2nd and 4th lines of each stanza come back as the 1st and 3rd of the next, and the poem closes by handing back the lines it opened with). the trick i'm after: i want the line that opens the poem and the line that closes it to be the same line, and i want it true the first time and not true (or not obviously true) the second time, because the poem has crossed t in between. and i want the first couple of stanzas to be readable as one prediction instead of two -- since green and grue agree about everything so far, i'd rather the poem not announce the split early, let the same words do both jobs, and let the recycling itself be the thing that pulls them apart. someone who knows the problem should be able to point to the exact line where the grue reading and the green reading stop being the same sentence, and to see that the poem decides which one to keep by repeating it, not by finding evidence. you don't have to use the word 'grue' at all -- honestly it might be better if you don't, or only once -- but the emerald, the date, and the fact that nothing about the stone itself changes when the date passes all have to be in there somewhere. set it wherever you want; i keep picturing a jeweler's counter, or a lab, or somebody filling out a form at 11:58pm.

last thing: it still has to be a poem for the people in the workshop who've never heard of any of this, so it needs to be good on the surface -- somebody should be able to read it as a poem about waiting up for a date to pass and get something real out of it without having the philosophy explained to them. keep it to the 16 lines, the zine has a hard page limit.
