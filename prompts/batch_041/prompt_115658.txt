I am a third-year undergraduate taking a seminar on the ethics and politics of humanitarian intervention, and concurrently a course on causal inference, so I am reasonably fluent in the potential-outcomes framework: treatments and counterfactuals, selection into treatment, effect heterogeneity, interference and the SUTVA assumption, external validity, and the do-operator sense of an intervention. I am a student rather than a practitioner, so pitch the translation at that level rather than assuming research-level fluency.

I would like you to restate the debate over humanitarian intervention and regime change, as it runs through the standard cases — Kosovo, Iraq, Libya — in the vocabulary of causal inference rather than in the vocabulary of just-war theory or international law. Be as faithful as you can, and keep the participants' actual claims intact rather than substituting the version of the argument that the framework handles best.

Then give me the residue report. For each of the main claims in the debate — that an intervention made things worse overall, that the relevant counterfactual is in principle unknowable, that an intervention can be justified regardless of how it turned out, that intervening in one case changes what is possible in others — say what translates cleanly, what translates only with distortion, and what has no expression in the causal vocabulary at all. I am especially interested in three points. First, whether the spillovers across cases, which the framework treats as violations of SUTVA to be modelled around, are in fact the substance of the argument rather than a nuisance. Second, whether the authority to intervene, as distinct from the effects of intervening, can be represented at all, given that the technical notion of an intervention is value-free and coarse in a way the political notion is not — it would count sanctions, arming a rebel faction, and a bombing campaign as so many treatments. Third, whether the idea of an action that was permissible but leaves a moral remainder has any counterpart in a framework that assesses decisions by their expected effects.

Finally, tell me whether the residue looks like a defect of the causal-inference vocabulary — something it simply cannot say — or like evidence that the original debate was doing normative work in empirical clothing. I would rather have the honest answer than a reconciliation.
