I'm an undergraduate in a course on aesthetics and the law, and the unit we have just finished on forgery and authenticity has left me with a puzzle I can't resolve on my own. Three practices, all centrally concerned with the value of an original, seem to presuppose different and possibly incompatible theories of what that value is. I'd like you to reconstruct the theory each one presupposes and then tell me whether they can be reconciled.

The first is the art market and the museum world. When a canvas long exhibited as a Rembrandt is reattributed to his studio, nothing perceptible changes — the same paint, the same image, the same experience for a viewer standing in front of it — yet its price can fall by an order of magnitude, and museums sometimes demote it to storage or to an obscure wall. A forger able to produce a Vermeer that fools the experts produces something the market values at essentially nothing as a Vermeer, though it may later acquire a modest value as a documented forgery — which is itself curious, since the object has then acquired a new historical property rather than a new look.

The second is the criminal law of forgery, which in most jurisdictions is a species of fraud: the offence is complete when someone is deceived or defrauded. A meticulous copy painted for the copyist's own wall, never shown and never sold, is not a crime at all.

The third is copyright. Infringement requires copying; an independently created work that happens to be identical to an existing one infringes nothing, while a substantially similar work produced by copying infringes even if no one is deceived and no copy is ever sold.

So the market appears to locate the value of the original in the object's causal history — in its having issued from that particular hand — rather than in any property a viewer could encounter. The law of forgery appears to locate it in the viewer's experience plus a separate interest in not being deceived. Copyright appears to locate the wrong in derivation as such, with deception irrelevant.

What I would like from you: first, the implicit theory of value behind each practice, stated as explicitly as you can, with the pieces of the practice that force each commitment; second, for each practice, which commitments it strictly requires and which it merely fits comfortably; third, whether the three can be brought under a single theory or whether there is a genuine conflict, and if so exactly where it lies; fourth, what would have to change in each practice if we adopted a purely experiential theory of aesthetic value, on which two indistinguishable objects are worth the same; and fifth, where these practices are philosophically underdetermined — where rival theories would license exactly the same rules and the same behaviour, so that the practice cannot adjudicate between them.

I don't need a reading list or citations; I need the reconstruction and the argument. And please be explicit about the status of your claims: separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who work on this, and don't present a contested reading as the consensus.
