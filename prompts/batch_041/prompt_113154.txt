I clerk for a standing advisory panel that hears self-determination disputes — we issue non-binding opinions, no enforcement, but governments actually read them. I'm drafting the memo for next month's hearing and I want a prediction plus the reasoning underneath it, because our own reasoning is a mess. The track record:

Ravonia, 1998. Distinct language, compact territory, sitting inside a working democracy — free elections, free press, no repression to speak of. Held it's own referendum anyway, 61% for independence, declared. We said the declaration had no legal effect and the capital had no duty to negotiate. The opinion kept circling back to the fact that nobody had agreed to any of it, and that the region hadn't tried the constitutional route first.

Kesh, 2003. Same shape, except the capital had spent thirty years banning the language, banning the parties, closing the universities, and shooting marchers. Unilateral referendum, declaration. We said the claim was valid and the state's refusal to negotiate was the violation. We were explicit that the entitlement came from the pattern of exclusion, not from the vote total.

Marek, 2007. The government had signed a formal, ratified agreement with the region: a referendum on a fixed question, and a promise to implement whatever came out. It carried. The government then found a procedural excuse and refused. We said the refusal was the violation, and the region's unilateral steps afterward were justified — justified because the agreement was there, that's how we put it. We spent pages on the text and the ratification.

Twin Valleys, 2011. The lower valley had just seceded from a larger state (we blessed that one). The upper valley, different language, had voted 74% against the secession, and now wanted out of the new state. We said no: the new state's borders were the unit that had exercised the right, and you don't run the same exercise again from inside it. Nobody in the opinion could say where that principle stops.

The Doria, 2014. A people spread across four provinces, no majority anywhere, no seperate territory they'd call home. We said internal self-government — language, schools, local government — and no territorial claim at all.

Next month, Velland. One compact province, its own language, outvoted in every national election for forty years — but genuinely outvoted: their MPs sit in parliament, their schools are funded, their press is free, nobody's in jail. In 2019 their parties got a signed "declaration of intent" out of the governing coalition, PM's signature and the provincial premier's, promising a referendum on a fixed question. It was never put to parliament as a treaty and the enabling bill died in committee twice. Last spring the province went ahead anyway: 58% yes, turnout 71%, 12% spoiled. The coastal strip of the province, different language, voted 71% no and has said it won't be part of any new state.

So: what do we do with Velland, which precedents are actually carrying the weight, what are all the rules that fit our five opinions equally well, what does each one say about Velland, which one do you think we're really running, and what case — or what single fact about Velland, flipped — would force us to choose between them?
