Two colleagues of mine, both of whom teach in a history-of-science programme, spent most of a long train ride arguing about a television documentary that presented Atlantis as a real maritime civilization of roughly twelve thousand years ago, destroyed by a comet, whose survivors went on to seed Egypt and Mesoamerica, and that treated the conventional chronology of the ancient world as a residue of error and suppression. Neither of them believes any of it; the quarrel was entirely about what to call the documentary's claims and about whether the label pseudoscience is doing honest work. I was in the room, said almost nothing, and could not decide afterwards who had the better of it. What follows is the exchange as accurately as I can reconstruct it.

1. A said the claims are pseudoscientific in the first place because they are unfalsifiable: the absence of Atlantean harbours, hulls, inscriptions, DNA or crops is not counted against the story but folded into it, as the evidence being under the sea, or destroyed, or unexamined because the academy will not look. No possible discovery could dislodge it, and that, she said, is the defining mark.

2. B said this cannot be right, for two reasons. First, the claims are perfectly falsifiable and have in fact been falsified: if a large maritime civilization had existed at that date there would be archaeological and genetic traces, and there are none. Second, falsifiability is neither necessary nor sufficient. Flat-earth and moon-landing-hoax claims are falsifiable and are still pseudoscience; the many-worlds reading of quantum mechanics is not obviously falsifiable, and no one calls it pseudoscience. Whatever is wrong with the documentary, it is not unfalsifiability.

3. A accepted this and proposed a different criterion, which she called asymmetric accounting and defined as follows: a doctrine is pseudoscientific when the same class of observation is treated as evidence when it favours the doctrine and dismissed as loss, corruption or suppression when it does not. A resemblance between pyramid-building on two continents is entered in the ledger as proof of contact; the total absence of the supposed homeland's material remains is either not entered at all or written off as a debt the sea has cancelled. On her account the defect lies not in the content of the claim but in a systematically one-sided way of keeping books.

4. B objected that this proves either too much or too little. Too much, because fragmentary records force honest researchers to say we have not found it yet, and because the same one-sidedness appears in data-poor stretches of respectable science; it grades practice along a continuum, and a continuum cannot do the work demarcation was asked to do, namely sorting what belongs in a science classroom from what does not. Too little, because asymmetric accounting names a species of bad reasoning, and bad reasoning is everywhere, including inside the sciences; to find it here leaves you with these people reason badly, which is not the same as this is not science. He added that the genre of new-chronology writing, which holds that the conventional ancient chronology is largely a duplication of the medieval record, shows the criterion is not even tracking the class A wants: whatever is wrong with that genre, the charge is not normally that its proponents explain inconvenient finds away.

5. A replied that the line she is after runs neither through the content of claims nor through the logic of particular inferences but through the social character of the enterprise. Pseudohistory, she said, is a genre constituted by its relation to expertise: it is made for a lay audience, its proponents confer their own authority, they never submit their central claims to the scrutiny of the specialists who work on the relevant evidence, and they read expert disagreement as proof of a cover-up. Demarcation is then a claim about communities and their practices, not about propositions.

6. B said that reduces the matter to credentials and gatekeeping, which cannot be right, since outsiders without standing have overturned expert consensus and turned out to be correct, the reception of continental drift and of the impact explanation of the end-Cretaceous extinction being the standard examples, and since the criterion cannot distinguish an honest heterodox amateur from a purveyor of nonsense. What separates them, he said, is how they handle objections, not who they are.

7. He then observed that the empirical spine of the documentary, the claim that a comet airburst triggered the abrupt cooling at the onset of the Younger Dryas around 12,900 years ago, was introduced in a leading multidisciplinary journal and is still defended in a running controversy among specialists; so one cannot tell pseudohistory from contested science by inspecting content. A answered that this shows only that a fringe hypothesis can reach print, and that the leap from there was an abrupt cooling whose cause is disputed to a particular lost civilization existed, seeded two continents and left no trace because it sank is not a scientific inference at all but a story that recruits whatever evidence is handy. B said he agreed with that sentence, and that it is a point about evidence and inference rather than about a category.

Please adjudicate the exchange point by point. For each of the six numbered moves, give a rating running from clearly mistaken through mixed to clearly right, with a brief justification of the rating; where a move contains several distinct claims, separate them and rate them individually. Then say, separately, who is right about the falsifiability question, who is right about the asymmetric-accounting criterion, and who is right about whether the disagreement is one about propositions or about communities. Finally, say who has the better of the closing exchange about the comet hypothesis, and why. I do not want a bare declaration of a winner: I want to know which specific claims each of them got right, which they got wrong, and what both of them are missing, including any respect in which they are answering different questions rather than disagreeing — and, in particular, whether pseudoscience is best understood as a natural kind, as a family resemblance, or as a policy category, and what follows for the practical uses to which each of them wants to put the word.
