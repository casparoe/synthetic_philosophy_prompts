I am the evidence lead at a small development fund. Roughly twice a month our investment committee asks me to rule on whether a proposal's causal story about a country's poverty holds up, and the story is nearly always one of the familiar three: extractive institutions, geography and disease burden, or culture. What I cannot tell them is which of the three the committee is actually entitled to act on. The three are each other's confounders, and by the time an explanation reaches us it has usually been assembled backwards from the outcome. I have an afternoon, no budget for new data, and a committee containing two economists and a historian; the committee signs off.

Please write a decision procedure I can run under those constraints, grounded in what the philosophy of causal inference from observational history actually settles — identification and the counterfactual, mechanism versus effect, explaining a level versus explaining a change, and the treatment of culture as whatever remains once the other two have been controlled. Give me the questions in the order I should ask them, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops with a no, and where it hands the decision back to the committee because the remaining question is one of values or risk tolerance. Then run it on two proposals: one attributing a slow-growth record to a colonial-era land tenure arrangement, and one attributing a region's thin industrialization to a disease environment. Include the case in which the procedure tells me to reject a story I find persuasive, and be explicit about what the procedure cannot do. No citations needed; I will add them.
