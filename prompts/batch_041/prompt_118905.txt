Ok, I need help settling something that's been driving me up the wall. My flatmate and I have a recurring argument — it started about queueing and has now swallowed tipping, jaywalking, and whether you have to say yes to a wedding invite you don't want to go to — about whether these everyday conventions are real moral obligations or just local habits people enforce because they're used to them.

His position: they're customs, nothing more. Cutting a queue or leaving no tip when a tip is expected is at worst a bit rude or mildly annoying, but it's not *wrong* in the way that lying or stealing is wrong. Unless you actually harm a specific person, there's nothing moral on the table. He thinks I'm just moralizing about etiquette, dressing up "this is not done here" as "this is forbidden."

My position: when a norm is up and running and everyone else is keeping it, flouting it *is* a wrong. Cutting the queue is free-riding on a line everybody else is honoring, and skipping an expected tip is treating the people who expect it with contempt, even if nobody is measurably worse off. He says that's circular — you can't get an obligation out of "everyone happens to do it," and anyway most of these norms are arbitrary, so there's nothing there to be bound by.

Here's the frustrating part. We agree on all the facts. We agree no one is harmed in any concrete sense, we agree the conventions are arbitrary and vary place to place, we agree that people get annoyed when you break them. And yet we've had this exact conversation maybe twenty times and neither of us has moved an inch. He's not secretly a relativist and I'm not secretly a rule-worshipper; it just won't resolve.

It gets worse with the visitor case, which is where we really grind to a halt. I think a visitor *has* to follow local conventions — if you're somewhere people don't tip, don't tip; if you're somewhere jaywalking is genuinely not done, don't do it. He thinks that's incoherent: how can a stranger be bound by a rule they never agreed to and that isn't justified on its own terms? And honestly, when he presses me on *why* the visitor is bound — is it to the locals, to the practice, to something about respect? — I can't give him an answer that isn't just "because it's the local custom," which is the thing he's denying.

My working theory, and I want you to tell me if it's right: we're not actually disagreeing about anything, we're answering two different questions and using the word "obligation" to paper over the gap. He's asking whether the convention is *justified*, and I'm asking whether you can be *blamed* for flouting it, and those two can come apart — a rule can be arbitrary and still be one you're a jerk for breaking. Or maybe it's the reverse, and we do disagree substantively: maybe the whole fight is about whether a practice that's arbitrary can generate a reason *just by being the local practice*, which is a disagreement about where reasons come from, and neither of us can argue for our starting point.

So: diagnose it. Is this verbal, a hidden empirical disagreement (something about coordination or about what "harm" really covers), a disagreement about what has to be true for something to count as a moral obligation at all, or two questions under one word? Ground the diagnosis in what we each actually say above — don't just tell me the general shape of relativism. And then tell me what would actually move it, if anything: is there a distinction, a case, or an argument that would do the work, or is this the kind of standoff that bottoms out in intuitions neither of us can defend? And tell me which diagnosis the visitor case supports, since that's where we stall hardest.
