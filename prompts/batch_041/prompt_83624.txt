FREE LOOKS (my essay)

There's a thing I read about somewhere called Good's theorem, I think that's the name — it was on a blog and the bloke who worked it out was apparently called Good. The idea is this. Say you're about to make a decision and someone offers you some information for nothing, before you have to decide. Then taking the information can never make your decision worse, on average. Not that it always helps — usually it changes nothing — but it can't do you harm. Free means free, obviously; nobody's charging me for it. The reason, as I understand it, is that you can always just ignore what you've been told and do what you would of done anyway, so all the information does is give you options, and having more options can't hurt.

I've made this into a rule of life: never turn down a free look. I read the hotel reviews after I've already booked. I look up my symptoms at two in the morning. I ask my mate what people said about me after I'd left the party.

Two things are bothering me though.

The first is my friend Katy. She finally went for a test she'd been putting off for years, got a bad result, and she's been in bits ever since. Nothing changed about her treatment — same pills, same appointments, nothing they can do any differently either way. She says she wishes she'd never gone. I said the theorem proves she's better off, because knowing can only help. She said that was the stupidest thing anyone's ever said to her. I still think I'm right in the maths sense, but I can't explain why she doesn't feel it, and it's eating at me. I've started calling the bit where knowing makes you feel worse the dread bill, and my view is that the dread bill always gets paid off in the end, otherwise the theorem wouldn't be true.

The second is that another mate told me the theorem only holds if you're the sort of person who always picks the option with the best average, and whose happiness doesn't depend on what you believe. He reckons that if your peace of mind depends on not knowing a thing, then the theorem simply doesn't apply and information can genuinely be bad for you. That feels like a fudge to get out of it. It can't only be true for people who already want to know everything, surely?

So: have I got the theorem right? Where does it actually stop applying? What should I have said to Katy? And is any of this interesting, or is it definately all stuff people have already thought through?
