I am a graduate student in philosophy working through the First Day of Galileo's Dialogues Concerning Two New Sciences in the Crew and de Salvio translation, and I have hit a passage that I can follow sentence by sentence but cannot see the shape of as a single argument. Salviati has just granted that one infinite line may be longer than another, then reverses course and argues that we cannot say of infinite quantities that one is greater than, less than, or equal to another. The stretch that puzzles me runs as follows:

"Not only so, but the proportionate number of squares diminishes as we pass to larger numbers, Thus up to 100 we have 10 squares, that is, the squares constitute 1/10 part of all the numbers; up to 10000, we find only 1/100 part to be squares; and up to a million only 1/1000 part; on the other hand in an infinite number, if one could conceive of such a thing, he would be forced to admit that there are as many squares as there are numbers taken all together."

Sagredo: "What then must one conclude under these circumstances?"

Salviati: "So far as I see we can only infer that the totality of all numbers is infinite, that the number of squares is infinite, and that the number of their roots is infinite; neither is the number of squares less than the totality of all the numbers, nor the latter greater than the former; and finally the attributes 'equal', 'greater', and 'less', are not applicable to infinite, but only to finite, quantities. When therefore Simplicio introduces several lines of different lengths and asks me how it is possible that the longer ones do not contain more points than the shorter, I answer him that one line does not contain more or less or just as many points as another, but that each line contains an infinite number."

My difficulty is not with the arithmetic. I can see that the squares can be paired one-to-one with their roots, and the roots with all the numbers, so that by that method the squares and the numbers come out equally numerous. What I cannot decide is what work the intervening observation about the diminishing proportion of squares is doing. Salviati notes that up to 100 the squares are one tenth of the numbers, up to 10,000 one hundredth, and up to a million one thousandth, and then says that in an infinite number "he would be forced to admit" that there are as many squares as numbers. Is the diminishing proportion an independent argument, a concession to Simplicio's way of thinking that is then undercut, or a rhetorical flourish? More generally, I would like help seeing how the pieces of the passage hang together as an argument, and what exactly Salviati's conclusion amounts to.

I would also like you to assess a reading that a colleague has proposed and that I am unsure how to evaluate. On this reading, Salviati is not making a mathematical claim that later turned out to be false, but a conceptual one: that the terms "greater," "less," and "equal" have their sense only within practices of counting, measuring, or comparing by inclusion, and that since infinite collections admit of no such procedure, those terms simply have no application to them. The colleague takes this to be a proto-Wittgensteinian point about the grammar of "same number" rather than a failed anticipation of Cantor. Please assess this reading against the text, distinguishing (i) what the passage itself commits Salviati to, (ii) what subsequent mathematics has settled, and (iii) what remains genuinely contested among historians of mathematics and philosophers. I would rather have a careful separation of these than a smooth synthesis; please flag explicitly where you are confident and where you are reconstructing or speculating, and do not present any contested interpretation as if it were the consensus.
