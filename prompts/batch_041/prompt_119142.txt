I am assembling teaching materials on the Mencius–Xunzi dispute about human nature, and I need a case that is not one of the illustrations the two texts themselves supply.

Construct a thought experiment that forces the two positions apart in their practical consequences, subject to the following constraints.

- It concerns a single adult whose character is already formed. Nothing about her childhood, upbringing, or education may appear in the case, and the case may not be about how she came to be as she is; the disagreement about nature must do its work in adult life rather than in development.
- It is set in an ordinary environment — a household, a shop, a school, a clinic, a workplace — and runs to a single paragraph.
- It avoids the stock illustrations: no endangered child, no mountain stripped of its trees, no crooked wood and press-frame, no farmer tending shoots, no sage-king arriving to set things right.
- It contains two things and no more: one brief episode in which the protagonist's untutored response to a situation shows itself, and one intervention that an ordinary person in her life could realistically undertake afterwards. The episode and the intervention together must be readable in two ways — as a seedling that needs protecting and nourishing, and as raw material on which a form has to be imposed — and the two readings must yield different, checkable predictions about what the intervention would produce.
- The verdicts must be generated rather than stipulated. Do not declare that the protagonist is by nature compassionate, or by nature selfish, and then read the theories off that declaration; the episode must be neutral enough that a reader could take it either way until the theories are applied to it.
- The case may not trade on the familiar polarity of a hopeful Mencius and a dour Xunzi. What separates the two readings must be a claim about what the moral raw material is and how it is to be worked, not a claim about whether the protagonist is nice or nasty.

Then do the following.

1. Give the case.
2. State what Mencius's position commits him to saying about the episode, and what Xunzi's position commits him to saying about it, and say whether these are two different claims about the same psychological fact or two descriptions of one claim.
3. Identify the single feature of the case that does the discriminating work, and say what happens to the case if that feature is removed.
4. Give the strongest Mencian and the strongest Xunzian response to the case, including the reply that the two thinkers disagree only about the word 'nature' and not about anything psychological, and say whether that reply succeeds here.
5. Say which of the two positions the case leaves worse off, and whether it is strong enough to establish that the disagreement is substantive.

No citations or textual references are needed; argue from the positions themselves.
