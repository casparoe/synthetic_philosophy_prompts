I teach a 4th-year epistemolgy seminar — mixed undergrads and MA students — and the final is a single 90-minute essay: one question, 25 marks. The unit I'm examining is Nozick's tracking theory and the closure stuff. The problem is that every year they answer it with the same paragraph: sensitivity fails for "I'm not a brain in a vat," so Nozick denies closure, so he has to swallow the abominable conjunction, so either you bite the bullet or you go safety instead. It's the most recitable paragraph in the whole subject and it tells me nothing about whether they can actually run the machinery.

So: could you take the role of someone who's taught this material for years, think it through carefully, and maybe sketch two or three candidate questions before you commit to one? What I need back is (a) one essay question, written so that the case below is printed on the paper, (b) a model answer of about 700 words, and (c) a marking scheme. No citations or reading lists anywhere — it's a closed-book exam and the model answer has to stand on its own.

Here's the case, in my own words, so dont worry about matching anyone's phrasing, it's mine:

Dana is looking at a second-hand motorbike. The odometer reads 24,000 km. She looks at the dial and forms the belief that the bike has done 24,000 km — call that P. She also believes that nobody has wound the odometer back — call that Q. She's never given Q a moment's thought, but she'd agree if you asked her, and she can see that P couldn't be true unless Q were.

I want the question to force them through four moves. First, state exactly what the tracking theory says about P and about Q — and I mean both halves, sensitivity and adherence, because they always forget adherence exists. Second, say whether this is a closure failure, and whether it's the same species of failure as the brain-in-a-vat case and the painted-mule case or a different one, and what that difference shows about what's really driving the failure. Third — the part I care about most — get them to see that the verdict on Q isn't just sitting there in the case waiting to be read off, because it depends on how you fix the method she's using to arrive at the belief and on which counterfactual world you're calling nearest. So they have to say whether the closure failure is a discovery about knowledge or an artifact of the theory's silence about method, and commit to one. If some of them want to argue she doesn't even believe Q in the first place, I'd like that to be a rewarded move rather than a dodge, as long as they do something with it. Fourth, make them price the two obvious repairs: bolting a closure condition onto tracking (last term someone wrote "just add closure" as if it were free — it isn't, and I want the scheme to make them say what it costs Nozick), and swapping sensitivity out for a safety condition. If a strong student wants to say this is really a warrant-transmission failure rather than a closure failure, the scheme should leave room for that too.

Marking scheme: five must-hit points, 25 marks total, and for each point one line on how students typically miss it, plus a note on which moves separate an A from a B. And please make sure the question itself doesn't give the analysis away — the case on the paper should look neutral, like it just fell out of a classified ad.
