I've been arguing with a friend about when a thing really has a disposition and when it just looks like it does, and my verdicts keep not fitting under one rule. I'll just lay out the cases and what I think, and you tell me what principle (if any) is behind them.

A wine glass sitting in a cupboard, never dropped: fragile. Obviously.

The same glass packed in a foam case so it could never break however you dropped it: still fragile. The glass didn't change; the foam's just in the way.

The same glass, but with a chip in it that turns the glass to rubber the instant it leaves your hand, so it never breaks when dropped: not fragile. The whole point of calling it fragile is that dropping it breaks it, and this one is rigged so that dropping it is exactly the moment it stops being breakable.

Sugar cube wrapped tight in plastic so water can't get to it: soluble. It's still sugar.

Sugar cube coated in something that, the moment water touches it, seals it in an impermeable shell: not soluble, for the same reason as the rubber-chip glass.

A poison everyone in town has been vaccinated against, so it can't hurt anyone here: still poisonous. What it does to a body hasn't changed, there's just an antidote in the way.

A compound that, once you swallow it, makes your stomach pump out a neutralizer before it can do any damage: not poisonous.

A match soaked in water: not flammable.

A match sealed in a glass tube with no air: still flammable.

That much I feel solid about. Here's where it comes apart. Take the rubber-chip glass again, and suppose the chip isn't keyed to dropping but just runs on a timer, so the glass is unbreakable between 11pm and 1am and perfectly ordinary the rest of the time. Drop it at noon and it shatters. I want to say that one *is* fragile. But its insides are doing the same kind of thing as the drop-triggered glass -- some internal mechanism is standing between the fall and the break. If I call the timed one fragile and the drop-triggered one not fragile, my rule can't be "it depends what the object's own machinery is up to," because the machinery is the same in both.

Second one that wrecks me. A glass that's shatterproof except when someone is deliberately trying to find out whether it's fragile, in which case it becomes as breakable as any glass. If I drop it just to see, it breaks. So it breaks under the exact conditions a fragility test would use, but only because I was testing. Fragile or not? I lean not, but I can't say why without also tossing out the timed glass.

And a last pair from outside objects. An irascible guy who's been on tranquilizers for years and never so much as raises his voice: I want to say he's still irascible, the temper's just sedated. But a placid guy with a neurological quirk that makes him fly into a rage the instant anyone insults him: I want to say he's *not* irascible, the rage is the quirk, not him. Those two look like the same structure to me -- a stable trait that only fails to show up because of a standing intervention -- and yet I split them.

My tentative rule was just "would it show the behavior under the normal triggering conditions, unless something intervenes?" -- and I already know that's too weak, since it makes the drop-triggered glass fragile, which I don't want. Can you find a principle, or a short list of them, that gets as many of these as possible? I'd rather know which of my verdicts is the one that has to go than get a rule that saves all of them by piling on clauses. And please say what your principle says about two cases I haven't given: a sugar cube that dissolves in acid but not in water (soluble or not?), and a person who is genuinely generous but only ever acts generously when someone's watching.
