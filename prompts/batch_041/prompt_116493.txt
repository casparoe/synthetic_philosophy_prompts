ok so i'm taing the paradoxes unit in a second-year phil of probability-ish course and we do the two envelopes problem in week 9. last year i just put the two expected value formulas on the board and half the room nodded and the other half didnt believe me, so this year i want to open with a poem instead of a slide.

act as a poet who actually knows probability theory, not someone who knows the vibe of the puzzle. think carefully before you write: brainstorm a bunch of candidate lines first and sort them into (a) the amount in my envelope, (b) the amount in the other one, (c) the conditional-expectation step, (d) the missing distribution underneath it, (e) the symmetry that kills the whole thing, and only then start drafting. if a line only works because you fudged the math, throw the line out.

what i want is a pantoum. 5 quatrains or so, usual interlocking rule, and close the loop at the end -- the last line should be the poem's first line. the form is the whole point, so use it: in a pantoum a line comes back in a new slot and means something slightly different the second time round, which is exactly what happens to "the other envelope" every time you swap, and the loop at the end is the thing i want them to feel before we name it.

content: run the argument straight, then put the flaw in the right place, which isnt the arithmetic. E(other | x) = (1/2)(x/2) + (1/2)(2x) = 5x/4 is fine as arithmetic. whats rotten under it is that a conditional expectation needs a distribution over the possible amounts, and the thing that would make "you're as likely to be the smaller as the larger, whatever you see" true for every value isnt a real distribution -- it's the scale-free improper one (or a merely finitely additive one), and with that the expectation either diverges or depends on how you cut it off. so the honest picture is: for each amount you could see, switching looks better, and averaged over anything that is actually a probability distribution, always switching gains exactly nothing, by symmetry. thats what i want the poem to carry, and i dont want it stated as a moral at the end -- the returning line should do that work. bonus if you can get the version where you actually look inside and see a number, since thats where people really get stuck.

avoid: name-dropping philosophers, citations, footnotes, epigraphs, anything i'd have to verify. no prose lecturing inside the poem. dont hedge the math to fit the meter. a philosopher of probability should be able to point at the line where the flaw lives, and a student who's never seen the puzzle should still get a real poem. keep it under about 28 lines total, i want it on half a page. preamble of two sentences max, i mostly just want the poem.
