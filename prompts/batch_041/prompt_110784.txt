I am a PhD student working at the intersection of machine ethics and control theory, and I have been asked to comment on a design pattern that a robotics group I collaborate with has adopted for an autonomous vehicle. The architecture has three layers. The bottom layer is a hard constraint: the vehicle must never intentionally steer into a pedestrian, even if doing so would avoid a larger accident. The middle layer is a consequentialist planner that minimizes expected harm, but it may only choose among actions that satisfy the hard constraint. The top layer is a moral-uncertainty module: when the consequentialist planner's expected-harm estimates are close enough that two ethical theories would rank the options differently, the system defaults to a conservative braking maneuver rather than choosing. The team describes the design as theory-neutral because it does not commit to any particular ethical theory. I am skeptical of that description. What philosophical commitments does this layered architecture actually presuppose? Does the hard constraint presuppose a deontological view of rights, or can it be justified on consequentialist grounds? Does the moral-uncertainty module presuppose a particular metaethical view, for instance that moral truths are determinate but unknown, or that moral theories are merely useful heuristics? And does the claim of theory-neutrality survive the fact that the architecture assigns lexical priority to the constraint layer over the consequentialist layer? Please reconstruct the implicit theory, distinguish what the design strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with, and say where the design is philosophically underdetermined. Finally, if we replaced the layered architecture with a single expected-utility planner that treats all harms as commensurable, what would have to change in the design, and what philosophical commitments would that alternative presuppose?
