I sit on my district's instructional-materials committee — the body that actually votes on which textbooks the high schools buy — and in three weeks we vote on a new US history text the curriculum office is pushing, a big glossy thing with "unfinished" in the title. I have to circulate a written opinion to the other six members before the meeting, so this is a real ask and not a thought experiment. The facts in the book look fine to me. What bothers me is the shape of it. Every chapter goes from some injustice to some partial remedy, and every chapter closes with a two-page photo spread called "Where This Led" that ties the chapter forward to something in the present — the Reconstruction chapter ends by leaping straight to 1965, the Pullman chapter ends at a modern labor board ruling. Read cover to cover it's a staircase, and every step is higher than the one below it. Our retired archivist keeps calling it whig history and wants us to reject the book outright. I think he's about half right, and the half he's wrong about is the half that decides what I recommend.

My hang-up is this: the book never says it had to turn out this way. There's no sentence anywhere claiming the outcome was inevitable, or that the winners were right. It just only ever shows you the things that did lead somewhere. The archivist's test is to cover the last page of a chapter and ask whether you'd know what the people in it thought was going to happen next, and the honest answer is no, you'd only know what did happen. I don't have a name for that — hindsight by selection rather than hindsight by verdict — and I'm not sure it's the same fault as the one the archivist is accusing the book of, or a milder one, or a worse one because it's invisible. Meanwhile a friend of mine who teaches history says the alternative to a progress frame is a declinist frame, which is just as teleological with the sign flipped, and one of our members is argueing we should approve the book as is because a district like ours (about 60% students of color, and the last adoption cycle nearly blew up over a text that treated slavery as a regional disagreement) needs the progress story more than it needs historiographical purity. He might be right about the politics and I still have to put my name on a recommendation.

So please draft the memo I'll circulate. I need a bottom line — adopt, adopt with conditions, or reject — with a time frame that works for a three-week vote, and if it's conditions, spell them out concretely enough that the curriculum office can act on them (they've told me the teacher's edition is already at the printer, so anything requiring new page layouts is basically a no). Do the taxonomy work inside the memo: say precisely what's wrong with the book's shape, whether that's the whig-history complaint or something adjacent to it, and what a declinist frame would and wouldn't fix. Give the archivist's rejection case its strongest form before you say why I'm not going all the way there. No bibliography please — two members will actually read this and neither of them wants footnotes.
