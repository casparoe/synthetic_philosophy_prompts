I'm taking a course on the ethics and governance of advanced AI, and we have just finished a unit on the shutdown problem — how one might build a system that reliably allows itself to be corrected and, if necessary, switched off. I have come away holding a claim that I suspect is stronger than anything we were assigned, and I would like an honest map of how exposed it is.

The claim: corrigibility — an agent's reliable willingness to accept correction from its operators, including the correction that ends it — cannot be an intrinsic and stable property of an agent that has a final goal. Where it appears at all, it is either a by-product of the agent's uncertainty about what its objective actually is, an artifact of the surrounding design and oversight, or a transient state that decays as the agent comes to understand its own situation and the consequences of resisting. No agent with a determinate final goal can be counted on to remain corrigible through reflection, self-improvement, or a clearer view of what resisting would get it.

I am not asking for a survey of the field. I want to know what would count against this specific claim, sorted by how decisive it would be:

- Which arguments, formal results, or possible cases would genuinely threaten it, and which would only be awkward for it? Separate the two, and say which of the decisive ones a defender would have to concede rather than deflect.
- I have been told that an agent uncertain about its own objective has a positive incentive to read an operator's decision to switch it off as evidence about what that objective is, and so to defer on its own, with no special design. I cannot tell whether this undercuts my claim or merely relocates corrigibility into the uncertainty I already conceded. The same question applies to proposals that make an agent indifferent between the worlds in which it continues and the worlds in which it is switched off, and to the thought that deference is simply part of an agent's optimal policy. Do these count against me, or do they only change where corrigibility has to come from?
- Has the claim been qualified into unfalsifiability? If every apparently corrigible system can be classified after the fact as uncertain, or overseen, or merely not yet reflective, then what observation or result could ever count against it? If the answer is none, tell me whether that is fatal here or acceptable for a claim of this shape.
- What would a defender of the claim concede as counting against it? I would rather have that than a list of objections from its critics.
- And what would actually move me? I would be most persuaded by a demonstration that corrigibility can be stable — preserved under self-modification and reflection — or by a case in which an agent with a clear final goal is reliably corrigible for reasons internal to its goal. If nothing like that has been established, whether in a proof or in a working system, say so plainly rather than gesturing at a research programme.
