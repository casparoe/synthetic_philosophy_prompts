My friend and I spent most of last night arguing about risk-weighted expected utility. She thinks it captures something real about rational risk aversion: when you face a lottery, you don't just weight each outcome by its chance, you weight it by a transformed cumulative chance, so the chance of the worst outcomes gets extra weight. I think that is either expected utility with extra steps or a model that gives up on the idea that rational choice maximizes expected value. We didn't settle it, and I want my students to feel the pull of both sides in their own choices rather than hear me summarize the debate.

I teach an upper-level seminar in decision theory for philosophy students. There are 14 students, and I have one 90-minute session. Design an in-class activity—a betting market, an insurance game, or a structured series of forced choices—that makes them run into the difference between expected utility and risk-weighted expected utility before either view is named. The rules, roles, materials, and payoff scheme should not require real money. Ideally they first make choices that look risk-averse, then discover that those choices cannot be represented by any expected-utility function but can be represented by a risk-weighted one with a particular risk function; I would like them to infer or construct their own risk function from their choices before I supply the formal definition. Include a minute-by-minute timeline, a role for a risk auditor who computes each participant's implied risk function from their rankings, and lotteries with at least three outcomes so that rank-dependence actually matters. Then a debrief that maps what happened onto the formal machinery: the risk function, the cumulative weighting, the Allais-style pattern, and the distinction between risk aversion and outcome weighting. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle it, and be frank about which feature of risk-weighted expected utility the activity cannot reproduce and that I will have to supply in the debrief.
