I chair the clinical AI review board of a mid-sized public hospital network, and our advance-care planning office is asking us to sign off on a tool that would infer patients' care preferences from choices they have already made inside our system — which appointments they attended, which treatments they accepted or declined, how promptly they followed up, whether they opted for home dialysis over in-center — and then, when a patient has lost decision-making capacity and never completed an advance directive, to use that inferred preference model to select the default care plan, which the clinical team would then have to justify departing from. The method is described as recovering a reward function from demonstrated behavior under a noisy-planner assumption: the patient is modeled as mostly rational, mostly forward-looking, and stochastically optimal with respect to an unknown utility over health states, symptom burden, and time spent in institutional settings. The proposal is candid about two things I want the memo to take seriously rather than score points off. First, it concedes that many different preference models explain the same record of attendance and refusal equally well, and says it breaks such ties with a prior that favors the preferences the care team regards as clinically reasonable. Second, it reports that inferred preferences matched a later-elicited statement of preferences in roughly three-quarters of pilot cases — though the pilot cohort came from a different clinic than the one where deployment is planned, the elicitation was conducted by the team that built the model, and the interface presents clinicians with a single ranked plan plus a confidence number that our own informatics staff tell me clinicians already treat as decisive once it passes about 0.7.

Please draft the written opinion I will circulate before Thursday's meeting. It has to end in something the office can act on: approve, reject, or approve subject to named conditions with a timeline and a named owner for each. Two arguments need to be engaged on their merits, not waved at. The first is the defenders' claim that the relevant comparison is not between an inferred preference and the patient's true preference but between an inferred preference and a clinician's unaided guess, and that since the clinician's guess is at least as opaque, at least as biased, and at least as insensitive to anything the patient actually did, the objection proves too much and should not block deployment. The second is the autonomy justification itself: the tool is being defended as a way of honoring what patients want, yet the behavior it learns from was generated under conditions this board has flagged before — automated reminders, financial penalties for missed sessions, transportation the hospital arranges and cancels, appointment slots we control — so I need the memo to state plainly what a behaviorally inferred preference can and cannot represent when the behavior was shaped by the very institution that now proposes to interpret it, whether the tie-breaking prior is where our own clinical values quietly re-enter as the patient's, and whether any of this is remediable by something short of prospective elicitation while the patient can still speak for herself. Keep the whole thing to what a busy board can read in one sitting, and make any conditions concrete enough that a later auditor could check whether they were met.
