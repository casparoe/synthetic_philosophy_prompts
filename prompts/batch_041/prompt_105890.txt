I'm a software engineer with no formal training in philosophy, and I've been reading my way into the Dutch book literature ahead of a reading group. I'd like an audit of whether the following commitments can be held together; I would rather be told that something has to go than be reassured that it all works out.

1. The Dutch book test is the right criterion of coherence for credences: if a bookie can offer me a combination of bets that leaves me worse off no matter how things turn out, my credences are incoherent, and if no such combination exists, they're coherent.
2. My credences are estimates of how likely things are, not dispositions to bet. On most empirical questions I would decline a bet at my own stated odds, and I don't think that refusal says anything about my beliefs.
3. Updating on evidence is just conditionalizing: my credence in a hypothesis after learning something should equal my prior credence in it given that thing.
4. I don't accept the principle of indifference except where there is a genuine physical symmetry. For an unknown proportion, the fact that I have no reason to prefer one value over another tells me nothing about which distribution I should have.
5. The version of the Dutch book argument that targets people who revise their credences in some way other than conditionalizing looks like a trick to me: it only bites if I have already agreed to accept bets at whatever odds I will hold later, and I would never agree to that, so I don't see it as a point about rationality.
6. I think the real reason to satisfy the probability axioms is that a set of credences that violates them is less accurate, i.e. further from the truth, than some available alternative; losing money to a bookie is a symptom rather than the disease.

Which of these actually pull against each other, and which only look as though they do? Rank the genuine tensions by severity, give the cheapest repair for each along with what it costs me, and say whether one commitment is doing most of the damage. Please be explicit about how confident you are at each step, and identify the single point you are least sure of.
