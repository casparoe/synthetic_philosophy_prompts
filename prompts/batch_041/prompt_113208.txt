As a novelist, I keep meeting a comparison that seems to me to carry more weight than it can bear. Defenders of conspiracy theorizing often say the conspiracy theorist is simply a good detective: you begin with the loose ends the official account cannot tie up, you decline the convenient explanation, and you follow the evidence wherever it leads, even to a hidden hand; on this picture the mainstream is the lazy constabulary that closes the case in the first chapter, and the theorist is the one still pulling the thread. I would like you to audit the comparison rather than the politics. Set out the correspondence explicitly — what answers to the detective, the clues, the red herrings, the culprit, the closed case — then say where the mapping breaks, and in particular which features of genuine detective work are quietly imported into conspiracy reasoning and which features of conspiracy reasoning have no counterpart in a plotted story. My own suspicion, which I would like you to test, is that a detective's world is intelligible only because a single authorial intention lies behind every detail, and that the analogy smuggles that guarantee into a world that has no author. Does the conclusion the comparison is used to license — that the conspiracy theorist is the more rational party — survive once the disanalogies are on the table, or was the analogy carrying it all along? And if the picture is misleading, what would be a better one for what conspiracy reasoning actually does?
