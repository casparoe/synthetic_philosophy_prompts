I am a novelist; the book I have just finished is, among other things, an attempt to think through Nāgārjuna's tetralemma, which is why a small festival has asked me to run a ninety-minute session on it for a dozen other novelists. They are intelligent and well-read in fiction, innocent of Buddhist philosophy beyond a paperback of Zen aphorisms, and professionally suspicious of anything that smells of theory. I do not want the idea explained to me; I want a plan for explaining it.

Begin with the order of the ideas and the reasons for it: what has to be in place before the four corners can be set out without their sounding like a conjuring trick, and what depends on what. My own guess is that dependent origination must come before emptiness, and the two truths before the tetralemma, on the grounds that otherwise the fourth corner is heard as a retreat into the ineffable. Correct me if the dependency runs otherwise, and say at what point Nāgārjuna's insistence, when an opponent presses him, that he holds no thesis of his own has to enter the sequence — since that is precisely the moment at which a room of novelists will conclude that he is being evasive.

Then the carrying example. My instinct is to build the whole session on the fact that a character in a novel has no properties beyond those the text and its readers give her; I suspect this teaches fictionalism, which is not emptiness, and that I should spend the rest of the session undoing it. Adjudicate the example, tell me the one that ought to carry the session instead, and say why the obvious alternatives — the chariot and its parts, the rope mistaken for a snake — are worse than it.

Then the misconceptions, in the order in which they will actually surface: that emptiness is nihilism; that the two truths are two worlds, one of them a lie; that the fourth corner is a gesture at the unsayable; that the tetralemma is a four-valued logic (one of them will have read this somewhere); and, the one I dread most in that particular room, that this is deconstruction by another name, so that the four corners become a licence for indeterminacy of meaning. Mark the point in the sequence where each should be headed off.

Close with the single question I should put to them at the end to show whether they have understood the question rather than merely acquired the vocabulary, and tell me what to leave out at this level and to say out loud that I am leaving out.
