I am a software engineer with no formal training in philosophy, and I have been reading about famine — the Bengal famine of 1943, the Irish famine of the 1840s, and the catastrophe that followed the Great Leap Forward — and I keep meeting the same argument about whether these were natural disasters or political crimes. Before I read any more of the competing accounts, I want the specification first: write the desiderata that any adequate theory of famine must satisfy. Give me the full list of requirements, each with the case or consideration that motivates it and with a note on whether you regard it as non-negotiable or merely the preference of one camp.

I want the list to cover at least the following, though you should add whatever I have missed: the cases the theory must get right (a famine with no serious aggregate shortfall of food, a famine where the harvest genuinely failed, and a famine that was overwhelmingly the product of policy); the distinction between what triggered a famine and what caused it; the claim that famines do not occur in a functioning democracy with a free press; the distinction between deaths from hunger and deaths from epidemic disease, which I gather often outnumber the former; the fact that food is frequently exported or hoarded while people starve; and — the requirement I care most about — the theory's ability to support a verdict on whether a given famine was a natural disaster or a political crime, that is, to assign responsibility and not merely to explain.

Two things I especially want from you. First, flag any pair or subset of these requirements that cannot be satisfied together: whether a single theory can both deliver a causal explanation and ground a judgement of culpability, and whether the demands imposed by the Irish case and the Bengal case pull in opposite directions. I suspect the list is more interesting as a set of tensions than as a checklist, and I would rather be told that a theory cannot do everything than be handed one that quietly drops a case. Second, tell me which single requirement does the most work in ruling out the popular accounts, and which of those accounts — the food-availability or Malthusian view, the entitlement approach, and the political-economy view that treats famine as a policy failure — survives the list best. Keep the emphasis on the criteria and the cases behind them; a brief verdict on the contenders at the end is enough, and do not trouble yourself with citations, as I shall look those up myself.
