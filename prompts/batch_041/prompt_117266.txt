Ok so here's a thing I've been chewing on. In the equality of what? debate everyone fights over whether the right currency is welfare, resources, or capabilities. I want you to argue for a fourth option I think gets ignored: equalize people's discretionary time. Roughly, the hours in a life that aren't already spoken for by paid work, caretaking, commuting, errands, and the basic upkeep of being alive. Not leisure in the fun sense, and not just hours off the clock: the amount of time you actually get to allocate by your own lights.

Make the case for that as the currency of egalitarian justice. And do it properly, which defintely means taking on the objections instead of hand-waving them. Some of the ones I keep running into:

- Time isn't transferable the way money is. You can tax a rich person and hand the cash to someone poor; you can't siphon an hour out of someone's Tuesday and pour it into someone else's. Does that make temporal egalitarianism a non-starter, or just a different kind of ideal (equality of opportunity for time rather than equality of outcome)?
- The expensive tastes problem comes back in a weird shape. Some people genuinely love their jobs, so a 60 hour week costs them nothing; other people are wrecked by 30. If you equalize clock time you're unjust to one group or the other, depending which way you go. Is your currency hours or felt burden, and can you pick one without quietly collapsing back into welfarism?
- If you equalize time, who does the bad work? Somebody has to be on call at 3am. Say how your view handles that. Maybe some jobs get compensated in time itself, maybe not — argue it out.
- Adaptive preferences. People who've never had discretionary time may not report wanting any. Does that matter for your metric, or is it a bullet you bite?

I also want to know how your view comes apart from a resource view in practice. If two people have the same money and the same time, hasn't the resourcist already said everything there is to say? Give me concrete cases where the two diverge, not just the abstract claim that time is a distinct metric.

And finish with the single strongest objection you think your own position can't answer.
