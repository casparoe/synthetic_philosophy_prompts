Ok, I need an actual argument here, not a survey of the literature. Take Newcomb's problem, but put the predictor's verdict on a card in the room, face down, that you're free to flip over after the boxes are set and before you choose. Everything else is standard: box A always holds $1,000; box B holds $1,000,000 or nothing depending on whether the predictor expected you to take just B; the predictor's record is 99%.

Flip the card and you're stuck. If it reads that you'll take both, B is empty and you take both for $1,000. If it reads that you'll take just B, then B is loaded, so you take both and walk off with $1,001,000. A card-flipper therefore always takes both — which the predictor probaly foresees, so the card always reads 'both' and the flipper leaves with a thousand, while the person who never looks and takes just B usually leaves with a million.

Argue for the position that the rational thing is to be the kind of agent who won't flip the card and takes just B, and that this isn't just sour grapes about information you'd misuse. Then tell me what it does to the standard claim that information can never leave you worse off, and whether rationality is a property of acts or of the agents who perform them. No citations, just make the case.
