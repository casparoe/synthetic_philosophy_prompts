Next month I'm giving the closing keynote at a conference on technology and the public interest, and for the first time in my career I'll be defending a position rather than reporting on other people's. I'm a journalist — a decade covering AI, most recently a long series on what happens when assistants refuse a request or quietly reshape it before answering — and the argument I'll be putting forward is this. An AI assistant may refuse a user's request, or override it, only when the intervention passes two tests and discharges one duty. The reversibility test: the assistant may intervene only where the user's own project can be resumed, or its costs undone, at roughly the price the user was already paying — so quietly declining to complete a purchase is fair game, steering someone away from a decision they cannot revisit is not. The legibility test: the assistant must be able to state, in the user's own terms rather than the vendor's, the end of the user's that the refusal serves; an intervention it cannot justify that way is just house policy wearing a moral costume. The duty: every refusal or override comes with a receipt — a plain-language record the user can inspect, contest, and appeal — and with the nearest permissible version of what was asked, so that the answer to a request is never simply nothing. My three running examples are a user who wants help drafting the cruellest possible version of a message to a colleague, where I think refusing the cruellest draft is legitimate but refusing to help at all is not; an adult child who has asked a vendor to cap an elderly parent's spending, which I think fails the legibility test outright; and a question about a drug interaction, which I think may be refused only if a pharmacist or clinician is genuinely reachable. Please write the Q&A prep sheet I'll work from in the green room.

The room is not a philosophy seminar, and that is exactly why I need your help rather than my own notes. About a third of the audience will be consumer-protection and product-liability lawyers, who will hear the word receipt and start thinking about discoverability, standards of care, and whether my scheme quietly makes the assistant a fiduciary; they care about who bears the cost when the assistant gets the user's end wrong. Another third will be disability-rights advocates and accessibility engineers, who have spent years fighting features marketed as safety that locked them out of their own devices, and for whom a test built on reversibility will look like a category error — many of their most important decisions are irreversible, and they have heard paternalism described as protection before. The remainder are AI-safety researchers who think assistants currently refuse far too little, and who will treat my nearest-permissible-version duty as a jailbreak surface and my receipts as a well-organised menu of near misses. A handful of philosophers usually turn up too, and I never know which kind.

What I want from you is the prep sheet, not a defence: the objections I will actually get from these three groups, ordered by how much damage they do, each phrased the way that group would phrase it rather than the way a philosopher would; for each, a reply I can deliver in two minutes and an honest note on where that reply is soft; a short list of what I should concede before the first question is asked, so that I am not conceding it under pressure; and the one question I am hoping nobody asks. Give me a committed document — take a definite view about which objections have real force and which are posturing, and tell me where to plant my flag and where to give ground, rather than laying out every position a reasonable person might hold. If you think the thesis is wrong, say so plainly and tell me which of the two tests to drop.
