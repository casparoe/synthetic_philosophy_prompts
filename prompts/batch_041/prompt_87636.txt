I run the forecasting desk at a mid-sized specialty insurer — we're four people, and we crank out probability estimates all day for underwriters, actuaries, and the occasional lawyer who wants a number in a deposition. My problem is that we keep having the same fight and I want it to stop. We'll assign, say, "0.15" to something and then spend an hour arguing about what we even did: did we read it off a historical frequency, pull it from a model, just gut it? Half the team thinks a probability for a one-off thing (will this named competitor file a claim by Q3? will this one patient respond?) is a category error and we should refuse to give a number; the other half says refusing is just cowardice and of course you can have a degree of belief about a one-off. And whenever I say "well, it depends what you mean by probability," everyone groans. I want a decision procedure we can actually run on a job, ideally as a form we fill in, that takes a quantity we're asked to price and walks us to (a) what kind of probability claim we're entitled to make, (b) how to actually generate a number under each kind, and (c) how to say it out loud to the client without overclaiming.

We already tag every request with a "source type," if that helps as scaffolding: F = we have a big historical reference class (e.g., claim frequency for a policy class); C = it's a chance setup with a known-ish mechanism (a die, a component with a rated failure distribution, a radioactive source); J = basically a judgment call with no obvious reference class (a competitor's move, a regulator's decision); M = the number came out of a model and nobody's quite sure what it means. I'd like the procedure to route each tag to the right interpretation — frequentist, subjective/Bayesian, propensity, whatever the right one is — and to be honest when the interpretation you'd want isn't available, e.g. when the client wants a "real" chance for something that only has a degree of belief. Then run it on three of our recurring items: (1) the chance that a specific named supplier defaults within 18 months (we have a small pool of similar suppliers, and the team keeps fighting over whether they're even exchangeable with this one); (2) the chance that a particular industrial press fails in the next 30 days given its age and usage (we have lots of failure-time data, but this is one machine); (3) the chance that a specific pending bill becomes law (basically a political call). One of these the procedure is going to tell us we can't price the way the client wants, and I'd like to know which and why. Also flag the steps that are just judgment wearing a lab coat, and tell me what the procedure can't do — where two people running it honestly will still disagree about the number itself.
