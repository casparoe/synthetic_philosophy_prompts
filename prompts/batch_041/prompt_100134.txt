I am assembling a set of worked examples for the later sessions of a graduate seminar on the problem of verisimilitude, after the familiar objections to Popper's original proposal have been dealt with. I want an example that carries genuine conceptual weight and that is not a restatement of the usual counterexamples. It should turn on a distinction I would like the students to feel rather than merely define. Say that a false theory is extensionally close to the truth to the extent that the propositions it asserts are largely true and its false assertions are peripheral; say that it is structurally close when it represents the relevant causal or mathematical relations correctly while misstating magnitudes, parameters, or boundary conditions.

Give an example of a conceptual issue that this distinction generates. I want a single concrete pair of rival theories — scientific, historical, or everyday, but worked out in enough detail that the two orderings are visible — in which the theory that is extensionally closer is structurally further, together with an account of what, if anything, could settle which of the two rankings is the correct one. Then explain what a defender of a single, unified notion of truthlikeness would have to maintain about the example in order to keep the two orderings aligned, and whether that commitment is available.

The example should be assessable without appeal to any particular formalism for measuring truthlikeness, and it should be one you construct yourself rather than one drawn from the existing literature.
