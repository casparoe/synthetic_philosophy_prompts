So my friend Nadia brought a puzzle to dinner last week and it turned into a two-hour fight between her and our other friend Emmett that I mostly watched while refilling glasses, since I honestly couldn't tell who was making sense. The puzzle was the Platonia one from Hofstadter's Metamagical Themas: some trillionaire picks twenty people, tells each of them privately that if exactly one of the twenty sends him a telegram by noon tomorrow that person gets a huge pile of money, and if zero or more than one does, nobody gets anything, and the twenty aren't allowed to talk to each other. (1) Nadia said the answer is obvious once you see it: you send your telegram with probability 1 in 20. Her reasoning was that all twenty of you are in exactly the same position with exactly the same information, so whatever you work out the other nineteen work out too — the only live options are all twenty send or nobody sends, and neither of those pays anyone. So you have to randomize, and then you pick the probability that maximizes your chance of being the lone sender, which comes out to 1/20. (2) Emmett said that's a trick. If the others really do whatever you do, then randomizing doesn't help either, because all twenty run the same rule and you're back to all-or-nothing; and if they don't really do whatever you do, then you're just in an ordinary game with mixed strategies, and the equilibrium there is everybody sending for sure, in which case the money is never paid out at all. So on the first reading the argument is vacuous and on the second it's just wrong. (3) Nadia said he was conflating two things: the other nineteen share her decision rule but have their own private randomness, so the rule is the same while the coin flips are independent, and that's exactly why 1/20 both breaks the symmetry and gives sensible odds. She also said that plain game theory saying "everyone sends, nobody wins" is the whole reason the puzzle is interesting — it's a case where being Nash-rational gets the group nothing. (4) Emmett said fine, but then 1/20 is a sucker's move, because if the other nineteen really are sending at 1/20 he should send for sure and win with probability about 38%, which is twenty times better than what the 1/20 rule gives him, and no theory should tell him to leave that on the table. Nadia said you can't price that counterfactual, because the only worlds where they're playing 1/20 are worlds where they reasoned exactly as you did, and if you'd reasoned your way to "send for sure," so would they. Emmett said that's the part he can't swallow, because it makes your deliberation a cause of their behavior, which is just magic with extra steps. Nadia said it doesn't have to be a cause to be evidence, and that "not causal" doesn't mean "not a reason."

Then Emmett dragged in the other half of the thing, the Luring Lottery, which Hofstadter actually ran in his Scientific American column back in the eighties: readers were told to send a postcard with a number on it, that number being how many entries they wanted, and the prize was a million dollars divided by the total number of entries received, awarded to one randomly chosen entry. (5) He said this proves his point, because the sensible move there is to enter some enormous number — the real readers did exactly that, some of them writing googolplex — while the superrational advice (roll a die with as many sides as there are expected entrants, and send only if you roll a 1) works only if every one of tens of thousands of strangers independently does the same thing, which they definately won't. So superrationality isn't a theory of what to do, it's a theory of what to do if everyone is already doing it. (6) Nadia said that's backwards: a decision theory isn't refuted by people ignoring it, and the lottery is precisely the case where you need something other than best-response reasoning, because the best response to whatever other people do is to match their total number of entries, which explodes and leaves everyone with nothing. So the only way anyone gets paid is if everyone restrains themselves for a reason that has nothing to do with their own payoff given the others' behavior.

I want a verdict that's more useful than "they're both partly right." Go through the moves as I numbered them and rate each one, saying which are actually right, which are confused, and where somebody has the right conclusion but the wrong reason. Then tell me what both of them are missing — I have a suspicion that neither is being careful about what "identical" is supposed to mean in the Platonia setup or about what the Nash analysis of that game really says, but I don't trust myself here. And at the end, say who has the better of the overall dispute, if either does.
