I run a monthly ninety-minute theory lunch for the engineers on my team — about a dozen people, mostly backend engineers plus two machine-learning researchers, no mathematicians and nobody with formal logic training. I have no formal background in this material myself, so the session has to teach itself: I want the group to feel, rather than hear, the difference between knowing that a winning strategy exists and being able to write one down.

My vehicle is Chomp. It is played on a rectangular bar of squares; a move is to take a square together with everything below it and to the right of it; the player forced to take the top-left poisoned square loses. For any bar larger than a single square, the first player can win, and the standard proof of this — the strategy-stealing argument — never produces the winning move. Assume the second player has a winning strategy; have the first player open with a single square in the far corner; then let the first player follow the second player's supposed strategy, on the grounds that an extra eaten square can only help. Both players would then be guaranteed to win, which is absurd. The argument is entirely silent about what to actually play. Square bars and two-row bars have simple explicit strategies, but for a general rectangle nobody can write down the winning first move; you can only brute-force boards of modest size.

Design the session so that the group collides with this before anyone names it. Give me the rules and materials, a minute-by-minute plan, the twists or roles that do the real work, and a debrief that maps what happened onto the steps of the argument — including the assumption it silently leans on (that an extra move is never a disadvantage, which fails in games like chess, where zugzwang means you would rather pass) and the fact that it rules out a second-player win only if you also know the game cannot be drawn. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle it, and be honest about what the activity cannot reproduce and I will have to supply myself. I would like one moment where the room is promised a win and then watches someone lose anyway. No lecture up front, and no jargon until the debrief.
