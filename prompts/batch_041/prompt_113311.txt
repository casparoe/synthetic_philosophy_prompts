For the past decade I have written a column on science and ideas, and for three years I have moderated the closing session of our city's literary festival. This year the session is about a memoir that everyone has read and no one can classify. Its author has acknowledged that two of its most memorable scenes are composites, that a conversation she reports verbatim could not have taken place on the date she assigns it, and that she has changed names, chronology, and in one case an outcome. She calls the book a memoir; the critic who has checked it line by line calls it a novel; the publisher's lawyers have settled on "a work of memory." The panel — a memoirist, that critic, and a philosopher of language — will disagree for ninety minutes in front of an audience of ordinary readers, many of whom will expect the question to be settled by the end.

I have six minutes of opening remarks to deliver. I want them to make plain what is at stake without pretending the dispute has an easy answer, because I do not believe it does. The two most careful positions I know give different verdicts. On the first, every predicate, however vague, has a sharp boundary; we simply can never know where it falls, so our uncertainty about whether this book is a memoir is ignorance of a fact. On the second, there is no boundary to be ignorant of: the predicate is indeterminate here, and a sentence such as "this is a memoir" is true only if it is true on every admissible way of drawing the line, and false only if it is false on every such way — so in the borderline region it is neither true nor false, and ought not to be asserted. These two positions are usually called epistemicism and supervaluationism, though I will not use either word aloud.

I have been persuaded by each of them at different times, and I do not want to settle the question on the panel's behalf. So write the remarks so that nothing in them is false on either view: every claim I make about the book should hold wherever the line is drawn, and where the book falls in the borderline region the remarks should describe rather than classify. My audience will not know either position by name, so the remarks must not become a lecture; the philosopher on the panel, however, will catch the first sentence that overreaches. I do not intend to quote or cite anyone from the stage, so the remarks have to stand on their own. Give me the remarks as I would deliver them.
