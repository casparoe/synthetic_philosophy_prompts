A friend has constructed an argument that seems to prove there are no propositions, and I can find no flaw in it — only price tags. (1) A proposition has constituents, say a and b, and is more than a mere collection of them, so something must make it one thing. (2) Whatever unifies those constituents must itself be a further constituent, on pain of doing no work: it is one more item alongside a and b. (3) Call it u. Then u must in turn be unified with a and b, which by (2) requires a further item u′, and so on. (4) Every proposition therefore needs an infinite sequence of unifiers, and a whole that needs infinitely many items in order to be one thing is never actually one thing. (5) So there are no propositions — and no other complex entities either: no tables, no arguments. (6) Yet the argument is itself a complex entity, so by (5) it is not one thing and asserts nothing: if it is sound it is not an argument, and if it is an argument it is not sound. When I protest that the regress is harmless — that each level is already unified, and the regress shows only that no finite list of constituents articulates that unity — she answers that this merely renames the difficulty: unity becomes a primitive posited at every level, and the question of what makes a and b one proposition is never answered, only postponed. Which step should I give up, and why did it look acceptable in the first place? For each exit I can see — denying (2), denying the move from (4) to (5), or accepting the regress as benign — state what it commits me to and what it costs. I would like to keep the argument standing and (5) false; tell me whether I can have both.
