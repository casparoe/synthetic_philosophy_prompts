I'm a third-year philosophy student, and I also sit on our university's academic misconduct panel as the student rep — we hear maybe four or five cases a month, mostly cheating on problem sets, plus one big essay case last term. The thing that keeps coming up and that I keep getting wrong is the "I couldn't have done otherwise" defense. Last month a guy plagiarized but argued he was going to fail the course regardless because he'd bombed the first two midterms, so cheating "made no difference"; another student said her roommate had the only laptop and basically pressured her into sharing answers; a third claimed that even if he hadn't copied, the TA's curve meant he'd have been caught anyway. My instinct as the no-nonsense student rep is "you did it, you're responsible," but our faculty chair keeps invoking the principle of alternative possibilities — no alternative, no blame — and I don't think that's right either. I've read Frankfurt's cases in my free will seminar and I buy that you can be responsible even when you couldn't have done otherwise, but I couldnt tell you how to turn that into something we can actually run in a 30 minuite hearing. One more wrinkle: our chair sometimes says things like "she had no real option" when he means she had no *good* option, and I have a feeling those are two different claims we keep smooshing together.

Can you write me a decision procedure — a set of tests to run in order — that the panel could actually follow for these "I had no choice / it wouldn't have mattered" defenses? I want the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops with "responsible anyway," where it stops with "not responsible," and where it just has to hand the decision back to the panel because it's a values call rather than a fact. Keep it usable by people who haven't read any of this — half the panel are grad students from other departments, and I'm the only one who's taken a free will course. Then run it on the three cases above and be honest about where it misfires; I especially want the case where it tells me the student I most want to blame isn't responsible, and I want you to flag the point where a flicker-of-freedom objection (my seminar friend's line — that there's still something the agent could have done differently, even if only "chosen otherwise" internally) would make the whole thing wobble. And it has to be honest about the fact that we can never actually know the counterfactual — we're deciding on evidence, not omniscience.
