Ok so I'm building materials for a unit on virtual worlds — the bit where students have to decide whether a sword in a game is a real sword and whether having it deleted is a real loss. I want a thought experiment built to order, and I want it in the weeds, because they've seen alot of these before and every one is either virtual real estate or "what if the NPCs are conscious", and they can recite those in their sleep.

You're a philosopher who works on the metaphysics of virtual objects and on what makes a harm a harm. Take your time on this one, brainstorm a few candidate cases before you commit to any of them (dont show me the rejects), then write up the winner.

Here's what I want. Two tests, which I'm inventing here, so don't go hunting for them in the literature:

The delete-everything test: suppose every trace of the thing were removed at once — from every mind, every record, every server. If nothing else in the world would be different, the thing wasn't real. If something would still be different, it was.

The receipt test: would an institution — a court, an insurer, a tax office, a platform's own dispute system — treat the thing as something that can be owned, lost, stolen, or compensated? If so, it's real enough to be part of the world.

These two are supposed to come apart, and I want a case where they come apart in opposite directions at the same time. So: one scenario containing a virtual object and a virtual harm, such that (a) the object passes the receipt test but fails the delete-everything test, while (b) the harm passes the delete-everything test but fails the receipt test. Both halves in the same scenario, same game, same two or three people if you can manage it.

Constraints:

- Present-day consumer tech only. A headset, a phone, an MMO, a marketplace. No uploading, no brain-computer interfaces, no conscious NPCs, no simulated time running faster than ours.
- Nobody in the case is confused about whether it's a game. They all know. Nobody thinks the dragon is alive.
- The people are ordinary — a student, a shift worker, whatever — not billionaires or pro esports athletes.
- Classroom-safe: nothing sexual, nothing that needs a content warning. If the harm can be a loss or an insult rather than an injury, do that.
- The case itself should fit in about a paragraph and be tellable out loud in ninety seconds.
- The verdicts have to fall out of the two tests, not be written into the scenario. And if some part of the case only works because I've smuggled in two differences where I claimed there was one, say so — I'd rather have a leaner case that works than a baroque one that cheats.

The point of the exercise, and this is what fixes whether the case is any good: I want students to see that "it's just pixels" and "but it really hurt" are both non-answers, because the object and the harm can come apart. If your case doesn't show that, it isn't doing the job.

Then the analysis, and please do the honest bit rather than declaring victory:

- Run both tests on both halves, giving the verdict each test returns and why.
- Tell me which single feature of the case is load-bearing — which knob, if I turned it, flips a verdict.
- Then actually turn it: change that one thing and say what collapses, and whether the two tests snap back into agreement.
- Say what a defender of each test would say to reject my case, especially someone who thinks the two tests aren't really two tests, and that one is secretly the other. Is that objection right? I want your view, not a survey of positions.
- Finally, tell me whether the case shows what I want it to show for the teaching purpose, or whether it only shows that a virtual item can have a price. Be blunt if it's the latter.

Format: no preamble, no "here's a thought experiment for you", just start with the case itself. When you get to the commentary, lead with the bottom line in the first sentence and then give the reasoning. Don't restate my question back at me.
