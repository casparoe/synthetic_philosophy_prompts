(a) State what Harsanyi's aggregation theorem says about the form of the social welfare function. (b) Identify exactly which assumptions are doing the work. And here's the student's answer:

"Harsanyi's aggregation theorem shows that if both individual preferences and the social preference ordering obey the von Neumann–Morgenstern axioms, and if social preference respects the Pareto principle, then the social welfare function has to be a weighted sum of individual utilities, W = Σ a_i u_i with a_i ≥ 0. So it rules out any non-additive way of combining utilities, like multiplying them or taking the minimum.

Which assumptions do the work? I'd say it's really the Pareto principle. As soon as you insist that if everyone is better off then society is better off, you've basically forced a positive weighted sum; the expected-utility axioms are just there to make the utilities cardinal enough to be added. That's why the result is so robust.

Does it prove a rational planner has to be a utilitarian? Yes, and specifically classical utilitarianism, because the weights come out equal. Harsanyi gets this from the original position: behind a veil of ignorance where you don't know which person you'll be, you assign each position equal probability, and maximizing expected utility then hands you equal weights on everyone. It's basically Rawls's veil of ignorance with maximin swapped out for expected-utility maximization. One thing Harsanyi is often credited with — and I think it's the real payoff — is that he shows we don't actually have to assume interpersonal comparisons of utility; the theorem derives them for us, which is a big improvement over the old British utilitarians.

On leximin: yes, the theorem rejects it. Leximin violates the Pareto principle, because there are cases where everyone's utility goes up but the ranking of the worst-off person changes, so it can't be a Pareto-respecting social welfare function."
