So: what does a theory of health and disease actually have to get right to be worth anything? Give me the full requirements list — the cases it has to classify correctly, the distinctions it can't collapse, the facts it has to stay consistent with — and for each one say why it's a genuine requirement and not just something one camp happens to want. A few things I definately want in there, but don't treat this as the whole list:

- It has to do something honest with the pre-disease / risk-factor cases — pre-diabetes, osteopenia, high-normal blood pressure, that whole grey zone. Are those diseases, and by what principle?
- It can't just wave at "dysfunction" without saying what makes a function-ascription true or false. If the answer is "statistical normality," tell me what it does with a trait that's rare but harmless; if it's "selected effect," tell me what it does with a trait no one ever selected for.
- It has to survive the fact that the same bodily state can be a disease in one place and a harmless variant in another. Sickle-cell heterozygote is the obvious example, so give me a cleaner one if there is one.

And here's one I keep circling back to that I'd like you to actually run through the list: imagine a heritable variant that, on a high-salt diet, reliably produces hypertension and stroke by fifty, but on a low-salt diet does nothing at all, ever. Is the variant a disease, a risk factor, or neither? Does your theory give a principled answer there or does it just stipulate one? Same question for a trait that only disables someone because the buildings are built wrong — the theory needs to say whether disease is intrinsic to the body or relational to an environment, and it can't have it both ways.

Also worth deciding: does the list treat aging as a disease case the theory has to call, and does it distinguish "is a disease" from "warrants treatment," or does it let those slide into each other? I think a theory that collapses that second distinction is in trouble but convince me either way.
