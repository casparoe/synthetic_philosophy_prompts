I'm setting the final for my upper-level pragmatics seminar (mixed 3rd/4th years and a couple of MA students) and I want the long essay to be on Grice, but not one of those questions where everyone dumps out the Cooperative Principle, the four maxims and the three properties of conversational implicature and walks off with a B+. I want them to have to argue from data, not recite teh maxims. So I've built the question around a little data set from an invented language -- nobody can look it up, which is the point.

Here's the data. Call the language Tuvik, verb-final, with an optional verbal suffix -tle:

(1) Mara pano karessa. -- "Mara baked the bread." Plain assertion.
(2) Mara pano karessa-tle. -- Same truth conditions as (1), but the speaker signals she isn't vouching for it from her own knowledge; she's passing it on. My consultant calls this "the hedge".
(3) Mara pano karessa-tle-nu. -- "Mara didn't bake the bread." The hedge survives negation: she isn't vouching for the denial either.
(4) "If Mara pano karessa-tle..." -- the hedge survives in the antecedent of a conditional too.
(5) Cancellation: Mara pano karessa-tle, and I watched her do it myself. Speakers find it odd but they don't call it contradictory.
(6) Detachability: swap karessa for the near-synonym vursa and the hedge goes away with -tle; the same proposition asserted without -tle carries no hedge. The hedge travels with the morpheme, not with the content.
(7) Truth conditions: the consultant won't accept a situation where (1) is true and (2) false, or the reverse. So the hedge isn't an entailment.
(8) The wrinkle I actually care about: a speaker who did watch Mara bake, and knows it perfectly well, can still utter (2). It reads as a put-on, a joke, pretending to be reporting.

I want the question to make them (a) go through the data and say, item by item, whether the hedge is part of what is said or something the speaker implicates, and why; (b) confront the fact that the usual tests pull apart here -- projection and detachability point one way, cancellability points the other -- and say whether that's a problem for Grice's conventional/conversational distinction or just for how people apply the tests, and specifically whether the hedge can be derived the Gricean way, by flouting Quantity (the bare form is sitting right there and unmarked, so choosing the marked one says something); and (c) deal with (8), which I think is the crux, and say what it shows about whether the hedge is speaker meaning or sentence meaning.

Please write the question as it would appear on the paper, then a model answer of about 600 words, then a marking scheme out of 20 built on five must-hit points at 4 marks each, with a line on what seperates an A from a B and a one-line note on the mistake that usually costs marks on each point.
