Last term I audited a graduate seminar on the contractualism of T. M. Scanlon, and afterward two of the students, Ravi and Lena, carried the discussion out into the corridor while I waited for a bus. The subject was the so-called aggregation problem, and because I am writing up my notes on the session I would like a referee's account of who was right at each stage, since I honestly could not tell. I am reporting what they said as a bystander and have no stake in which of them comes out ahead.

Ravi opened by defending contractualism against the charge that it cannot handle numbers. His line was that the theory's central commitment is the separateness of persons: whether an act is wrong is settled by asking which principles no individual could reasonably reject, and each individual's complaint is assessed against those principles on its own, never pooled with anyone else's. So, he said, a principle that imposes a modest burden on each of a very large number of people in order to spare one person a grave burden does not become justified merely because the burdens are numerous; the one person, whose complaint is gravest, may still reasonably reject it, and the many cannot convert their many small complaints into a single large one. Lena replied that this proves too much. She conceded that summing complaints across persons looks like exactly the kind of trading-off contractualism means to forbid, but insisted the numbers must enter the verdict somewhere, because in a plain rescue case, where one person is stranded on one side and several on the other and there is time to reach only one group, everyone agrees we should save the larger group, and a theory that cannot say why is not a theory of right and wrong but a refusal to answer.

Ravi answered on two fronts. First, he said, saving the larger group does not require aggregating anything: the lone person who is not rescued has no complaint that the others were rescued, because their being rescued does not leave her worse off than she would otherwise have been, so there is no principle she can reasonably reject. Second, he said, contractualism is not forbidden from aggregating within a single person's complaint, since a person may consider how a principle would affect her own life taken as a whole, so the model is more flexible than the caricature suggests. Lena pressed that both answers were suspect. The first, she said, works only because in a rescue the loser loses out no matter what; change the case so that the many are made positively worse off relative to a baseline in order to spare the one, and the no-complaint-about-benefits-to-others reply evaporates, leaving the one's grave complaint to defeat a mountain of small ones, which she doubted was the right verdict. The second, she said, quietly concedes that aggregation is legitimate and merely relocates the boundary; she wanted to know what stops a defender from drawing that boundary wherever it suits the intuitive answer. She then added a wrinkle I had not considered: many of the people a policy endangers are unidentified, so that no one can point to them, and she suspected that a merely statistical complaint is either weaker or stronger than an identified one, and that Ravi's framework had no principled place to put the distinction. Ravi replied that the distinction was a red herring, since reasonable rejection is about the reasons a person has and not about whether we can name her. They went back and forth a few more times without converging.

Please go through the exchange move by move, taking Ravi's opening, Lena's first objection, each of Ravi's two replies, Lena's two rebuttals, and the statistical-person wrinkle in turn, and say who is right about which specific claim, where each of them is overreaching, and what, if anything, both are missing about how a non-aggregative theory can or cannot accommodate numbers.
