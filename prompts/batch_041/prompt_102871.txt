I am a software engineer with no formal training in philosophy, and I am trying to work out how much credence to give to something I read in an engineering blog post about voting features and preference aggregation. The post presented the following summary of a result it attributed to Amartya Sen, and I would like an assessment of it:

'The economist Amartya Sen proved that a society cannot be both liberal and efficient at the same time. His famous illustration involves two people, a prude and a lewd, and a single copy of Lady Chatterley's Lover: the lewd would like to read it, the prude would rather it were destroyed, and each of them also has views about what the other should do. Because the two of them have contradictory preferences, there is no way of ranking the outcomes that respects both of them, and the social preference ends up going round in circles. What this really shows is that the whole business is Arrow's impossibility theorem in disguise: whenever you respect individual rights, you have simply handed somebody dictatorial power over the outcome. The catch, according to the post, is that the paradox only arises because the prude is being nosey about what the lewd reads. If a society counts only the preferences people have about their own lives, the problem evaporates, and this is the resolution Sen himself favoured. The practical moral, the post concluded, is that there is no point designing a system that tries to honour both individual choice and the majority's preference, so for something like the voting feature we are building it is better to nominate a single owner and let them decide.'

I have also seen the result referred to as the impossibility of a Paretian liberal, though I do not know whether the blog is describing the same thing.

Please go through the summary claim by claim, quoting its wording as you do so: tell me which parts a specialist in social choice would accept as stated, which are loose popular simplifications of something defensible, and which are straightforwardly mistaken, and whether the summary fairly represents Sen's own position. I am inclined to believe the closing recommendation, since it matches my own suspicion that trying to please everybody is a waste of time, but I would like to know whether it actually follows from anything true in the summary, or whether it is drawing a lesson the result does not support.
