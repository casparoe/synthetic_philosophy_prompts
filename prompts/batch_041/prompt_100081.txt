I'm in a reading group on perception that has now spent two sessions circling the disjunctivist reply to the argument from illusion, and the objection that keeps winning the room is the simplest one: if you can write down everything the subject has to go on in a case of seeing, and then write down exactly the same thing for a case of its merely being as if one were seeing, then whatever those words describe is something the two cases share, and the denial of a common experiential element looks like a refusal to look at the transcript. I would like a poem that takes that objection seriously enough to be interesting rather than one that simply asserts the disjunctivist line. So please write me a poem of roughly twenty-four to thirty lines, in the first person and present tense, arranged as two halves of equal length with a single line after them, where the halves are two tellings of one scene: the same room, the same window, the same person pausing in the doorway, the same strip of light on the floor. The second half must be a re-telling of the first, drawing its words from the first half and adding only a handful of new ones, and each new word must report the speaker's inability to check rather than add anything to the scene -- no new furniture, no new sounds, no new weather. Neither half may use the words hallucination, illusion, dream, unreal, or imaginary, and the poem must not announce anywhere which half is which, since from inside either one the speaker cannot tell. The one line after the two halves is the only place the poem may settle the matter, and it has to settle it by naming something that was never available from the inside of either half, a fact about the world rather than an ingredient of an experience. If the poem works, the sameness of the two tellings should read as a fact about what the speaker can say, not as evidence that the two states have a part in common; give it a title that fits either half equally well and that names the position of the subject rather than anything in the scene.

The ending is the part I most want to see done well. A reader who accepts the argument from illusion should finish the poem knowing exactly which line she has to argue with, and it ought to be the closing line, where the difference between the two halves turns out to be located outside the speaker's experience altogether -- if seeing and its being as if one were seeing made no difference to what could have been discovered from the inside, that shows the inside is not where the difference lives, and the poem should let that thought land without stating it in those words. Build the first half as a plain, patient description with nothing philosophical in it, so that the second half's borrowed vocabulary has to do all the work, and keep the closing line as the single moment where the poem stops describing and says where the difference is. It should be accurate enough that nobody in the group can complain that the disjunctivist got a poem while the common-factor view got a caricature, and plain enough that someone who has never read a word of the debate still finds it a good poem on a second reading.
