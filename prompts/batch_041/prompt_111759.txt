I have been trying to pin down what the cross-cultural challenge to analytic philosophy's method actually amounts to, and I have arrived at a deflationary position that I would like you to press as hard as it will bear.

The position is this: most of what gets reported as cross-cultural variation in philosophical intuition is variation at the wrong level. I find it useful to distinguish three loci at which a subject's response to a case can differ. There is the intuition itself, what actually seems to the subject to be the case about the scenario. There is the uptake, the evidential weight the subject assigns to that seeming, whether it registers as a datum any adequate theory must accommodate or as a defeasible hunch. And there is the theoretical verdict, what the subject concludes about the philosophical question, given the intuition and whatever background commitments they bring to it.

My claim is that the documented variation, whether about knowledge, reference, personal identity, or the trolley family of cases, is overwhelmingly variation at the second and third loci, and that variation there is both expected and philosophically benign. It shows at most that different populations have different habits of deference to their own seemings, or different background theories, not that they intuit differently. The challenge would be serious only if we found variation at the first locus: a case on which competent subjects from different cultures genuinely seem to see the situation differently, in a way that survives translation of the case and is not explained by mere unfamiliarity with the genre of the thought-experiment. I suspect very little variation of that kind survives.

So what would count against me? I want the defeaters sorted by how much they should move someone who starts where I do. What would it actually look like for variation to sit at the first locus rather than the second or third, and is that even detectable in principle, or does my framework risk becoming unfalsifiable by re-describing every finding as a difference in uptake or background theory? Which existing lines of work would a committed deflationist have to concede count against them, and which could they wave away without embarrassment? What does my position predict that its rival, the view that takes the variation at face value, does not? And if it should turn out that my position is well insulated from evidence, say so plainly, and tell me whether that is a virtue or a defect in a view of this kind.

I would find it most persuasive if you could construct a case, real or hypothetical, in which the variation is unambiguously located at the first locus, or else show that no such case could be constructed. Argue from the structure of the problem; you need not cite any literature.
