You are advising a small foundation that has decided to devote its next grant cycle to the theory of self-replication — not wet-lab origin-of-life work, but conceptual and mathematical work by people who think with pencil, paper, and a laptop. The program's organizing idea is the cluster of questions that Hofstadter's typogenetics was designed to dramatize: a formal toy genetics in which strings over a small alphabet are translated, through a fixed code, into enzymes that operate on strings, and in which some strings turn out to be self-replicating; together with the alleged isomorphism between that setup and Gödel's self-referential constructions on one side and the way DNA encodes machinery that copies DNA on the other. The foundation can support roughly six researchers for two years, and it wants a defended agenda rather than a survey or a reading list.

Which open questions in this area most deserve that money, and in what order? Here are candidates I have been turning over, but do not treat the list as exhaustive: whether the property "this strand is a self-replicating one" is decidable, and what the answer would say about the general claim that nontrivial self-replication requires a description; whether the DNA–Gödel isomorphism is deep or a pun, given that Gödel coding is systematic and compositional while the genetic code is arbitrary and non-compositional; whether self-replication genuinely requires von Neumann's double use of a description — read in one guise to build, copied unread in another — or whether template and autocatalytic replicators are real counterexamples; whether a single fixed-point result of the kind Kleene's recursion theorem or Lawvere's categorical version supplies covers Gödel sentences, quines, and self-replicators alike, and what that unification would buy; and whether the self-reference picture explains open-ended evolution, which needs variation and selection, or is simply orthogonal to it.

For each question, tell me what makes it worth two years of someone's life: how many other questions depend on its answer, how tractable it is for a theorist with no laboratory, how neglected it currently is, and whether a negative result would still be worth having. Distinguish the questions that are upstream of the others from those that merely look foundational. Say which of my candidates you would drop as overrated or as not really following from the isomorphism. Finally, the program officer is skeptical that a toy system can yield anything beyond pedagogy; tell me whether the toy is load-bearing here or merely illustrative, and what evidence over the next two years would move a question up or down the list.
