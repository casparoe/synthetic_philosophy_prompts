I chair the standards committee for our forecasting tournament here at the policy school — semester-long, about forty grad students plus a few staff, a few hundred in-house questions per season (does the bill clear committee, does that preprint get retracted, does the department make its hire, does the port strike start before the holiday), cash prize for the winner plus a line on the "judgment" certificate the dean likes to hand out. My own work is in formal epistemology, so I'm the one who keeps saying we should be running a proper scoring rule, but I've stayed out of the rule-writing until now and the dean wants this settled at next week's meeting, so I actually have to produce something. Here's the mess. What we've been using is the plain linear rule: 100 points times the probability you assigned to the outcome that actually happened, averaged over the questions you answered, small penalty for abstaining. It rewards shouting, and we've had two seasons of people parking 1.0 on their hunches and beating careful forecasters, which I'm fairly sure is because the rule isn't proper — the way to maximize it is to put everything on your best guess — but I get tangled up in the algebra whenever I try to make that argument out loud in the room. My colleague Ravi has proposed what he calls "accuracy with humility": same 100 points times the probability you put on what happened, minus 20 points for every question where you put more than 0.9 on any outcome, and his pitch is that this is just the linear rule with a small correction bolted on so the overconfident can't run away with it. Half the commitee thinks that's obviously right and can't see what I'm fussing about. The other half thinks it's a Frankenstein rule that will have everyone reporting 0.89 and nothing else, forever. I think both camps are confused, and I need it sorted out in writing rather than in another meeting where we talk past each other.

Two cases from last season also need rulings, and they're tangled up with the rule question, though I'd like them seperate from it if that's possible. First, Team Crank: two students with a homemade astrology-adjacent model of news cycles that posted the top score of the season — luck, or some weird genuine signal, I honestly can't tell, and their write-ups are not something I'd sign my name to; one of them said in the debrief that the model "isn't really falsifiable, it just fits." The runner-up used a boring base-rate-plus-updating approach and lost by a hair. Half the committee wants the prize to go to the best score, full stop; the other half wants a quality-of-reasoning clause written into the rules. Second, one student submitted ranges instead of point probabilities — "0.3 to 0.7" on several questions — arguing that forcing her to a single number would be epistemically dishonest, and asking to be scored at the midpoint of her range. I have some sympathy for that, and also a strong suspicion that a midpoint rule is the easiest thing in the world to game, so I want a decision I can defend either way.

Please write the memo I'll circulate before the meeting. Recommendation up front, concrete enough to implement: the exact rule we should adopt (formula included, so the TAs can code it), what it does about abstentions and ties, when it takes effect, and explicit rulings on Team Crank and on the ranges, each with reasoning a dissenting member could actually argue with. Engage Ravi's proposal on its merits instead of just telling us to use something off the shelf — I want to know what his rule does to someone who genuinely has a 0.95 credence, and how bad the damage is, and whether there's a repair that keeps whatever he's after. Also be honest about the strongest thing to be said for his rule, and for the members who want to reward reasoning rather than scores, since I may be on the losing side of that one. No citations or reading lists, please; I know the literature and the rest of the commitee won't read it anyway.

Approach this as someone who works on scoring rules and epistemic utility theory and knows which properties of a rule are doing the philosophical work here and which are just vibes. Start with a few bullets of brainstorming — the candidate rules, and the properties that matter (what happens when someone reports 0 or 1, how the rule treats hedging versus spreading probability around, whether honest reporting is optimal and whether it's uniquely optimal, how easily it's gamed, whether any penalty in it is continuous, how sharply it rewards sharpness) — then write the memo in American English, around 800 to 900 words. After the memo, give me a short structured summary as a JSON object with these fields: "bottom_line" (one sentence), "adopted_rule" (name plus formula), "decisive_properties" (array of short strings), "ruling_team_crank" (object with "verdict" and "reason"), "ruling_interval_forecasts" (object with "verdict", "how_scored", "reason"), "strongest_case_against" (string), and "what_would_change_our_mind" (string). Keep the JSON valid and put nothing after it.
