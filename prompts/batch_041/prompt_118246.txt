I have just spent an evening losing an argument to a friend who is writing a dissertation in the philosophy of science, and I would like to know whether I actually lost it. The fight started over a throwaway remark of mine — that Malthus was not so much wrong as early — and he pounced on it. He said that "right but early" is either vacuous or dishonest, that it is the same move as a fortune-teller insisting the stars were merely misaligned, and that philosophy of science should have buried the whole idea of a long-run prediction being vindicated after the fact along with phlogiston. Then he wrote the argument out on the back of a receipt, and I reproduce it here as faithfully as I can:

(1) A theory is refuted by any observation-statement it entails that turns out false, unless the falsity can be charged to an auxiliary hypothesis that is itself independently testable and that we have independent reason to reject.
(2) Malthusian theory entails that, other things being equal, population tends to grow faster than the means of subsistence, so that living standards are pressed down toward the level of subsistence.
(3) Over the two centuries since, both population and living standards rose, and the predicted downward pressure on living standards did not occur.
(4) The auxiliary assumptions doing the work here — roughly constant returns in agriculture, no major technological change, no significant decline in fertility — were not independent add-ons but part of the theory's core, so the escape clause in (1) does not apply.
(5) Therefore Malthusian theory is refuted.
(6) The only remaining defence is that the theory is "right but early" — true, but not yet given its chance.
(7) But a theory that can always be rescued by saying "not yet," or "the conditions weren't right," is unfalsifiable; and an unfalsifiable theory is not an empirical theory at all.
(8) Therefore Malthusian theory is either refuted or not an empirical theory.
(9) The reasoning is entirely general: any theory that makes long-run quantitative predictions about complex systems — resource exhaustion, the course of an epidemic, the distant effects of a policy — can be rescued in exactly this way, so either every such theory is refuted the moment its first dated prediction fails, or none of them is an empirical theory.
(10) That conclusion is absurd, since some long-run predictive theories are among our best science; so one of the steps above must be wrong.

My friend does not regard (9) as an embarrassment. He says it is the discovery rather than the reductio: it shows that long-run prediction is a rhetorical rather than a scientific activity, and that "right but early" is the tell. He then added a twist I cannot get out of my head. He said the Malthusian case is worse than an ordinary failed prediction because the failure is endogenous — the prediction was published, read, and acted upon, so the theory's being believed is itself among the causes of its appearing to be false. If that is right, he said, then no prediction about human affairs can ever be vindicated, because the act of predicting is always a further event in the world being predicted.

What I want from you is a diagnosis, not a verdict. Which step do I give up, and what does giving it up cost? I would very much like to keep (7) — I am not willing to abandon the demand that empirical theories be falsifiable — and I would also like to keep the thought that Malthus was onto something, so if those two cannot be held together I need to know precisely what I am buying and what I am selling. If the flaw is in the leap to (9), tell me exactly why the generalisation fails, and do not simply tell me that it feels too strong.
