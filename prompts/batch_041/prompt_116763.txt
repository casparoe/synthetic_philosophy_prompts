I am a philosophy professor, and next month I have to teach Sen's liberal paradox in a ninety-minute session for first-year graduate students in a political philosophy seminar. They have had one introductory course in social choice, so they know what a preference profile and a Pareto improvement are, but they are not comfortable with formal notation and they have a tendency to treat impossibility theorems as clever parlor tricks rather than as substantive claims about value. I do not want you to explain the paradox to me; I want you to plan the explanation. What has to be in place before the paradox can even be stated so that it feels like a discovery rather than a trick? Which single example should carry the whole session, and what must that example have and lack—should it involve a prude and a person who enjoys erotic literature, or a smoker and a non-smoker, or something less likely to make them moralize about the preferences? Where exactly in the sequence should I introduce the distinction between rights as constraints on outcomes and rights as powers over decision procedures, and how do I keep them from collapsing that distinction into the usual liberal talking points? Should I mention the game-form reformulation or the conditional-preference escape at all, and if so, before or after they have felt the force of the original impossibility?

What misconceptions will they predictably form—that the paradox shows liberalism is inconsistent, that it only arises because of interpersonal comparisons, that a better voting rule would solve it, that the Pareto principle is the real culprit, that it is a mere formal artifact—and at what point in the session should I head each one off? I also want one question I can ask at the end that would tell me whether they have understood the structure of the impossibility rather than just memorized the labels. Finally, be explicit about the parts of your proposed sequence you are least confident about, and say what would change your mind about the order or the carrying example—what feature of the paradox, or what evidence from the classroom, would make you revise the plan. I am not looking for a reading list or citations; I want the pedagogical architecture.
