Two members of a small economic history reading group I sit in on spent three sessions arguing over a question the group had been treating as its central puzzle — why the breakthrough to sustained growth happened in Britain rather than in China — and they never came to terms. I stayed out of it and took notes. I would like you to adjudicate.

The historian opened by saying the question is badly posed. It takes one actual outcome and one actual non-outcome and asks for the difference between them, and the difference is simply everything: political structure, religion, family system, legal doctrine, the price of coal, the shape of the coastline. With a single case on each side there is no way to tell which differences did the work, so no way to distinguish an explanation from a coincidence. Worse, he said, the question smuggles in a premise — that the default condition of a commercially developed agrarian society was to break through, and that China somehow failed to. Answers framed that way tend to reduce to "China lacked what Britain happened to have," which restates the outcome instead of explaining it.

The philosopher said this proves too much. Every why-question carries a contrast class; asking why something happened is always in part asking why something else did not. The choice is not between a contrastive question and a neutral one, but between a contrast chosen deliberately and one left hidden — usually the contrast with nothing happening at all, or with the same thing happening sooner. He denied the contrast here was careless. Both societies were large, commercially sophisticated, densely populated, technically inventive agrarian empires with huge internal markets and long traditions of mechanical ingenuity; if you want to know why one of them crossed the threshold first, the other is precisely the case to compare it with.

The historian replied that contrastive questions may be legitimate but the contrast class has to be a genuine alternative, and "China" is not one. It is a subcontinent-sized civilization being set against a small island kingdom — one unit is a state and the other is a world — and the units have been drawn to fit the outcome. The defensible comparisons are the Yangzi delta against England, or the lower Yangzi against the Low Countries, or Europe against Asia, and the answer shifts depending on which you choose. The philosopher answered that units are always drawn to fit the question. If your causal factor is coal seams and their distance from navigable water, you want regions; if it is a state that after 1688 could tax and borrow out of all proportion to its population, you want states; if it is the flow of silver and the global division of labour, you want the whole trading system. Comparing Britain with China holds a great deal fixed — both were commercially developed, both had large markets and literate elites — and asks what differed. That is not gerrymandering; it is the ordinary business of controlling for background conditions.

Round three is where they stopped hearing each other. The historian said they had been arguing about different questions from the start. His question is not whether China could have industrialized — he assumes it plainly could — but why the breakthrough happened anywhere at all, and why when it did. That is a question about a general mechanism, and a two-case comparison cannot answer it, because the mechanism has to be located in a larger population of societies and in a moment rather than in a winner. The philosopher said that if the mechanism is general, then "why Britain and not China" is simply that general question applied to two instances, and it should be answered by giving the general account and then showing how each case stands against it. But he added that he suspected the historian wanted to explain the outcome without ever saying what it was about Britain that produced it, which would leave the original question exactly where it was.

Please adjudicate this point by point rather than telling me who was more agreeable. Take the six moves in order — the charge that the question selects on the outcome; the reply that every why-question carries a contrast class; the objection that the units of comparison have been drawn to fit the outcome; the reply that units are always drawn to fit the question and that controlling for background conditions is legitimate; the claim that the real question is why the breakthrough happened anywhere and when; and the reply that a general mechanism still has to be shown to fit these two cases. For each, say whether it is sound, partly sound, or mistaken, and why. Then say what both of them are missing: whether there is a formulation of the question that preserves what each of them was protecting, and what would actually count as an answer to it. Judge the arguments as they stand; I do not need a survey of who has said what.
