I retired from a chemistry lab a few years ago and have been teaching myself game theory since, and I've got an argument I can't find the hole in. Wondering if you can help me locate it.

(1) A rational player never plays a strategy that does worse than another one no matter what the opponent does.

(2) "No matter what" is sneakier than it sounds, because sometimes no single pure strategy is worse everywhere, but a mix of two of them is. Take my own payoffs in this game (my rows, the oponent's columns):

        L    M    R
Top     3    0    3
Mid     0    3    3
Bot     1    1    1

Bot never beats Top at M, never beats Mid at L, and neither Top nor Mid beats Bot everywhere — until you mix Top and Mid half and half, which gives 1.5, 1.5, 3 and crushes Bot in every column. So a rational player won't play Bot, and you'd miss it entirely if you only ever compared pure strategies to pure strategies. (I gather the mixed version of this crosses off exactly the strategies that are nobody's best reply to any belief about the opponent, which I've checked on a few small examples and seems right.)

(3) Make it common knowledge that everyone's rational — everyone knows it, knows everyone knows it, and so on — and you can repeat the crossing-off: eliminate, look at the reduced game, eliminate again. I'm told that for this "no matter what" kind of dominance the order doesn't matter and you can never cross off everything, which is reassuring.

(4) Now the move I can't shake. Switch to the weaker notion: a strategy that is never strictly better than another and is strictly worse in at least one case. Surely a rational player won't play that either — you can't gain by it and you can lose by it. So iterate that, too.

(5) But look:

        L         R
Top     1,0       2,3
Bot     3,0       2,3

Cross off L first (the opponent never does better with it and does strictly worse at R), and Top and Bot become identical — nothing left to cross off, and Top survives. Cross off Top first instead (Bot is never worse and is better at L), and then L goes, and you're left with Bot and R. Two orders, two different answers.

(6) A rule about what's rational that gives different answers depending on the order you happen to apply it isn't a rule.

So (4) has to go. But (4) looks like it follows from (1)–(3) by the same reasoning, which is what's bugging me. Which step is actually doing the work, and what do I pay by giving it up? I'd rather lose (4) than (1) or (3), but I want to see the bill before I sign.
