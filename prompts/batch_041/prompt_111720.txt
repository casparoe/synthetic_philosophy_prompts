My friend and me are in a philosophy club at school and our advisor gave us dispositions as a topic for the what even is a property unit — you know, fragile, soluble, magnetic. My friend wrote up this argument on the bus home and now neither of us can get out of it. Here it is the way he wrote it:

1. To call something fragile (or soluble, or magnetic) is just to say what it would do if the right conditions came along. Fragile = would break if you hit it hard. That's the whole meaning of the word, there's nothing else in it.
2. But a disposition is a fact about the thing that has it. The glass being fragile is true of the glass, not of the room and not of us. Two glasses made of the same stuff, arranged the same way, are fragile to exactly the same degree.
3. Now imagine a fragile glass, except a wizard is watching it, and the instant anything hits the glass the wizard makes it unbreakable for one second, then puts it back the way it was. In that world, hit the glass and it doesn't break.
4. By 1, that glass isn't fragile, because there's no true would about it. Hit it and nothing happens.
5. But as far as the glass goes it's the same glass: same stuff, same structure. The wizard is out in the world, not inside the glass.
6. By 2, that glass is fragile anyway.
7. Contradiction, so 1 or 2 has to go.
8. We can't drop 2. If the glass's fragility isn't a fact about the glass, then it's a fact about the surroundings, or about us and our expectations, and a fragility that lives outside the thing isn't a fragility, it's a guess.
9. So 1 goes. No would-statement captures what it is to be fragile.
10. But if 1 is false, then when I say the glass is fragile I'm not saying anything about what it would do, and 2 says it's a fact about the glass — so what fact? We just said it isn't the would fact. So the word fragile isn't reporting any fact at all. Dispositions are stuff we read into the world.
11. He says it cuts both ways too: rig it so that a glass that isn't fragile would break anyway, because something outside makes it brittle on the way in, and you get the same contradiction in the other direction.
12. So no glass is ever really fragile. Nothing is fragile. Nothing is soluble.

Line 12 is obviously nuts — my phone screen is fragile, I'm not making that up. But I can't find the bad step. I'd really rather not give up 2, and honestly I don't want to give up 1 either, because 1 was the only thing that told me what the word meant. Our advisor said the wizard thing is a real puzzle that people argue about, so I don't think we get to just say the wizard is silly. He also said it has a name, something like a fink case, and that there's a cousin of it where nothing magic happens at all — the glass is just wrapped in foam, and that looks like the same problem. He called that one a mask.

So which line do I cut, and what do I have to pay? If the answer is that 1 was never a real definition, tell me what I'm supposed to say instead of it and what that costs me. And if the fix is to keep 1 but bolt on an unless clause, I want to know why that isn't just circular, because my friend tried that and it definately felt like cheating.
