I am a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I have been asked to lead a ninety-minute professional-development workshop for general-assignment reporters who cover courts, public health, and local government. Their work repeatedly puts them in front of cases where the contested question is whether a failure to act caused a harm: a utility that did not replace a corroded valve, a regulator that did not inspect, a physician who did not order a test. I want them to leave with a working grasp of causation by omission, not a tour of the literature. Plan the session for me rather than explaining the topic. What has to come first so that the question of whether an omission can be a cause feels like a genuine metaphysical question rather than a verbal dispute? What depends on what as the sequence proceeds? I am inclined to build the workshop around a case in which a city's failure to repair a levee is singled out as the cause of a flood, while a contractor's failure to deliver sandbags, a resident's failure to evacuate, and a regulator's failure to inspect are also in the story. Tell me whether that example can carry the whole session, what it must contain and leave out, and what I would need to add or replace if it cannot. I want the plan to make room for three harder ideas: the difference between an omission as a counterfactual difference-maker and an omission as a breach of a norm or expectation; the puzzle of redundant omissions, where two officials each fail to act and either failure would have been sufficient to prevent the harm; and the structure of double prevention, where one failure prevents another potential preventer from acting. Where in the sequence should those ideas appear, which of them should I leave out at this level, and what should I do about the fact that they pull in different directions? Which misconceptions should I expect—for instance, that omissions are causes only when they are morally blameworthy, that counterfactual dependence by itself settles the issue, that doing nothing is a single natural kind, or that causal language in law and in metaphysics must line up—and at exactly what point in the session should I head each one off? What should I deliberately refuse to explain at this level, and how should I mark that refusal? Finally, give me one question to ask at the end that would show whether the reporters can distinguish a metaphysical claim about omission causation from a normative or pragmatic one, and tell me what a good answer and a bad answer would look like. I do not want readings or citations; I want a teaching plan.
