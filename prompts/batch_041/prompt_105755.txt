I am assembling the specification for a seminar on distributive justice, and what I want from you is the specification itself rather than a survey of the field. State the desiderata that any adequate theory of interpersonal aggregation must meet — where by aggregation I mean any rule that says when, and how, the interests of some individuals may be subordinated to the greater aggregate good of others.

For each desideratum, give the consideration or case that motivates it, and say whether it is a genuine constraint on such a theory or merely the preference of one camp. I expect the list to cover at least the following, and you should add whatever I have missed.

First, the utility monster: what must a theory say about a being whose capacity for benefit is so great that the sacrifice of others is always outweighed? A theory that simply denies such a being could exist has not answered the case; I want to know what the theory does once the case is granted.

Second, and I care about this variant more than the standard one: contrast a monster whose utility is merely very large but finite — so that a sufficiently numerous set of donors could outweigh it — with one whose utility is unbounded, so that no finite sacrifice ever suffices. Must a theory treat these differently, and can it?

Third, a further variant: a monster that is not a single being but a coalition — many individuals, each of whom gains only a little, whose aggregate gain is enormous. Is this the same problem as the single monster, or is it ordinary aggregation in disguise? The distinction bears on whether the monster is a case about the scale of individual utility or about the number of beneficiaries.

Fourth, the anti-aggregation intuition: that it may be better to save one identifiable person's life than to cure a great many people's mild headaches. Why is this not simply a refutation of every aggregative view, and what must a theory do to accommodate it without abandoning aggregation?

Fifth, the population cases — the repugnant conclusion and the mere addition paradox. What do they demand of any theory that ranks outcomes containing different numbers of people?

Sixth, the separateness of persons. Is this a constraint that aggregation must respect, or a rival to aggregation? Say which, and what follows.

Seventh, the leveling-down objection: what does it forbid a theory from counting as an improvement?

Eighth, interpersonal comparability. What must the theory assume about the measurability and comparability of utility for the monster case even to be well-formed — and does the monster expose a defect in aggregative theories as such, or only in a particular assumption about comparison that those theories need not make?

Having listed the desiderata, do two things. First, mark which are non-negotiable and which are the parochial demands of one camp (utilitarian, prioritarian, Rawlsian, and so on). Second, and most important, tell me where the list cannot be jointly satisfied. I suspect the monster and the anti-aggregation intuition pull in opposite directions, and if some subset of the requirements is inconsistent, I want that stated as a result rather than smoothed over.

I am not asking for a scorecard of the major theories against the list; I will do that part myself. What I want from you is the standard they are all being measured against.
