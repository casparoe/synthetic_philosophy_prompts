ok so i've been keeping a running list of times someone told me something that counted against something i was sure of, and whether i think it was ok to just shrug it off, and i want to know if there's one principle behind my gut here.

- i add up my receipts and get $412; some online tip-calculator widget says the total is $421. i don't re-add. feels fine to ignore it.
- i put my keys in my coat pocket two minutes ago, i'm sure of it; my wife says she just watched me leave them on the kitchen counter. i go check the counter. feels wrong to just say "nope, they're in my pocket."
- i prove a little lemma on a problem set; a stranger online says it's false and gives a "counterexample" that's obviously circular. ignore, don't even bother. fine.
- a famous psych result that's been replicated like twenty times; one lab runs it and gets nothing. i now think the original is genuinely in trouble. i don't feel like the failed replication is "misleading" me -- i feel like the result is probably wrong.
- i'm sure i have two hands, i can see them right now. a guy says i could be a brain in a vat. ignore. fine.
- i'm sure of my atm pin, typed it a thousand times. but i also know i got a concussion last week and my memory's been swiss cheese since. i try the pin, it fails. now i really don't trust that i know it at all -- and honestly i'd say i never did know it, i just believed it.

so why is it fine to blow off the calculator and the crank and the vat guy, but not the wife or the failed replication? and why does the concussion case feel like it retroactively kills the knowledge, while the failed replication feels like the original result is just wrong now rather than me never having known it?

my tentative principle: you can ignore counter-evidence exactly when, if that evidence were correct, it would mean your belief-forming process was broken -- and you have independent reason to think it wasn't. does that survive the cases? it seems to get the calculator right (if the widget were right, my arithmetic is broken, but i have independent reason to trust my arithmetic). but then it seems to say i should ignore the wife too (if she's right, my memory's broken, and... my memory is the very thing in question, so no independent reason). ugh, so maybe it does get that right and i just don't like it.

tell me what principle actually fits, what it says about a case i haven't listed -- say, i'm sure of a fact because a friend i trust told me, and now a different friend i trust just as much says the opposite -- and which of my verdicts i have to give up. also, is the concussion case even the same kind of thing as the failed replication, or am i conflating two different phenomena?
