Ok so I'm putting together a session for our formal epistemology / decision theory reading group and I want to do something hands-on instead of another paper discussion. Around 18 people, mostly grad students plus two faculty, 75 minutes in a seminar room. Here's the thing I actually want them to feel in their bones rather than nod along to: the gap between treating yourself as a random draw from the observers who actually exist in your world, versus treating yourself as a random draw from all the observers there could have been. And how the second one turns you into the presumptuous philosopher — the guy who thinks the bare fact that he's around is evidence that the universe contains as many people as it possibly could.

The setup I'm picturing, and I want you to build it out into something I can actually run: I flip a coin out of sight. Heads, I pick exactly one person at random from the group and hand them, and only them, a sealed card that says they're in the chamber. Tails, everyone gets the card. Nobody can see anyone else's card and nobody talks. So from inside the head of someone holding a card: heads means they were the one-in-eighteen pick, tails means everyone's holding one and it tells them nothing about themselves specifically. First thing I ask them, cards in hand, is to write down a credence that the coin came up tails. My strong guess is most of them write something like eighteen-nineteenths, on the reasoning that if it had been heads they probably wouldn't have been picked.

Then I want to break that. Because if you do the self-sampling thing properly, being handed a card is guaranteed either way — every observer in the heads world is holding one, every observer in the tails world is holding one — so it's no evidence at all and the answer just stays at one half. The eighteen-nineteenths people are treating their own existence as a lottery ticket they might not have won, and I'm fairly sure that's the same move, in miniature, that makes the presumptuous philosopher presumptuous. I want the activity to make both readings live and tempting, not have me just announce which one is right.

A few things I specifically want in there. A second round where the tails world has a hundred observers instead of eighteen, same question — I want them to watch the naive answer march toward certainty as the crowd grows, because in the limit you become certain the world is the big one purely by existing, and that's the whole shtick. A debrief with a little market probe: I offer to sell them a chip that pays out if the coin was tails, they name the most they'd pay, then we run the whole thing twenty times for real and see who's solvent at the end. I have a hunch the eighteen-nineteenths crowd gets cleaned out, but please don't let the debrief pre-bake the moral — I want it to be honest about whether that shows their credence was wrong or only that credences and betting odds come apart in setups like this.

Rules, materials (index cards, envelopes, poker chips for stakes, a bag for the random pick, a coin), a rough minute-by-minute timeline, and the debrief. Also: tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time — I already expect at least one person to flat-out refuse to give a number, someone to insist I rigged the coin (should a participant flip it instead?), and the non-card-holders to check out entirely and start scrolling. And be straight with me about what the activity can't reproduce: I don't think it can touch infinite or unboundedly large worlds, it won't settle the reference class problem, and it can't show that the self-sampling answer is the correct one — it only shows the two come apart, so I'll have to supply the argument myself in the debrief. One more thing I don't want papered over: whether you count as an observer the setup created or one that already existed and got sorted into a room is definately up for grabs and I think they should fight about it, so build in room for that. No lecture up front, and don't let the names for any of this slip until the debrief.
