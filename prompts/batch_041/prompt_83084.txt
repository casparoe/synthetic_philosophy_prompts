I run the ideas section at a mid-sized general-interest magazine, which means I read maybe forty submissions a month and publish two, and I've started to notice how many of them lean on Gödel. Gödel proves science has limits, Gödel proves the mind isn't a machine, Gödel proves no legal system can ever be complete, Gödel proves an AI can never fully model itself; last week someone sent me one arguing that since no formal system can prove its own consistency, you can't ever really know your own motives. I took one logic course as an undergrad, I remember roughly what the incompleteness theorems say, and I can usually smell when somethings off, but I can't always articulate why, and I have to answer these people. I also don't want to become the editor who reflexively rejects anything with Gödel in it just because most of it is junk — I suspect there are legitimate uses and I'd like to be able to tell the difference.

So what I'd like from you is a triage procedure I can actually run, on paper, in about fifteen minutes per submission, without being a logician. The questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops and I can say no (and what I should say in the rejection so it's fair and doesn't just read as "I don't believe in math"), where it stops and I can say yes, and where I have to hand it off to someone with real training — I have a friend in the philosophy department I can send two or three things a year to, no more. The thing I most need it to do is separate cases where the theorems genuinely bear on the claim from cases where they're decoration, where the author would have said the same thing if they'd never heard of Gödel and it just sounds more respectable this way. Assume the writers are smart and mostly arguing in good faith, and that I'll be the one applying it, so the steps have to survive someone who's rusty rather than an expert.

Then run it on three pieces currently sitting in my inbox. (a) An essay arguing that because there are mathematical truths no formal system can prove, there are facts about the world science will never be able to establish, so we should be more epistemically humble than we are. (b) An essay arguing that a person can never fully understand their own mind, because a system can't prove its own consistency — the author is a therapist, the piece is genuinely moving, and I want to publish it. (c) An essay arguing that because we can see the Gödel sentence is true even though the system can't prove it, there must be something about human understanding that no computer could ever replicate. I'd like a clear verdict on each, and I'd like at least one of those verdicts to be one I won't enjoy hearing. And please be straight with me at the end about what the procedure can't do: which of these calls is really a judgment about how loose a metaphor is allowed to be, where a competent logician might disagree with my read, and where the honest move is to tell the writer I'm making an editorial call rather than a mathematical one.
