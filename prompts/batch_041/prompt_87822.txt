I am revising a short paper in which I propose an amendment to Lakatos's methodology of scientific research programmes, and I would like your detailed comments on the draft below: on its accuracy as an exposition of Lakatos, on the coherence of the amendment I propose, and on whether the historical case I use actually supports the amendment or tells against it.

Draft:

Lakatos models a research programme as a hard core of commitments that its practitioners agree not to abandon, surrounded by a protective belt of auxiliary hypotheses that bear the brunt of empirical testing. A negative heuristic forbids directing criticism at the core; a positive heuristic tells the practitioner how to build and rebuild the belt. The programme is progressive when this activity issues in novel predictions that are subsequently corroborated, and degenerating when the belt is rebuilt only to accommodate anomalies that are already known.

I want to add a quantity to this picture. Lakatos speaks as though the belt were a single ledger whose entries are either justified or not, but in practice belts differ in how cheaply they can be extended. Some positive heuristics manufacture fresh auxiliary hypotheses at almost no cost, because the surrounding theory is loose enough to tolerate almost any addition; others can absorb an anomaly only by revising something on which other parts of the theory depend. I propose to call the number of independently plausible auxiliary hypotheses a programme can spend before its belt ceases to be credible its anomaly budget, and I propose to relativise judgements of progressiveness to that budget. On this view, a programme with a small budget that secures one novel corroboration has done more than a programme with a large budget that secures ten, because the successes of the second are cheap while those of the first are not. Degenerating programmes, I suggest, are not programmes that have run out of ideas; they are programmes that have run out of cheap ideas.

I test the proposal against the contrast between the Ptolemaic and Copernican programmes. The Ptolemaic programme began as a progressive one: the models of planetary motion it generated yielded predictions of a kind that had not been made before, and those predictions were borne out to a striking degree of accuracy. What defeated it was not a single refutation but the exhaustion of its anomaly budget: by the time of the equant and the proliferating epicycles, every new agreement with observation was purchased by an auxiliary assumption that no independent argument supported. The Copernican programme, by contrast, operated with a far smaller budget: its core commitments about the centrality of the sun and the rotation of the earth could not be protected by adding convenient assumptions, so every anomaly it met forced a revision that propagated through the whole system. Its early predictions were, if anything, less accurate than Ptolemy's; on the standard account this makes it the inferior programme in its early period, but on the account I am proposing the comparison inverts, because the Copernican programme was spending scarce resources to buy its successes while the Ptolemaic programme was spending abundant ones.

What I want from you:

1. Is the anomaly budget a genuine extension of Lakatos's framework, or does it restate his own distinction between progressive and degenerating problem shifts in different words? If the latter, say exactly which of his commitments already does the work.

2. Is the quantity well defined? Can a budget be counted, or at least ordered, without appeal to hindsight, or does it collapse into a metaphor that only looks like a measure?

3. Does relativising progressiveness to the budget make the methodology unfalsifiable as a historiographical tool? If a programme with a large budget can be called progressive whenever it succeeds at all, and a programme with a small budget can be called progressive whenever it fails, the criterion risks immunising every verdict.

4. Does the Ptolemaic-Copernican contrast actually support the amendment, or does it undercut it? In particular, is the claim about the early predictive inferiority of Copernican models doing any independent work, or is it a decoration?

5. Identify any place where I have misrepresented Lakatos, and suggest the single change that would most improve the paper.
