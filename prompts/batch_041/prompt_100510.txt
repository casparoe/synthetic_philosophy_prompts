My friend and I got into a two-hour arguement last night about whether there's actually a principled difference between "revisionist" history and "denialist" history, or whether "denialist" is just the word you slap on the revision you don't happen to like. He came out swinging with the usual stuff — every generation revises the record, historians who buck consensus get ostracized, "denial" is a thought-terminating cliché you use to avoid engaging — and honestly I couldn't give him a clean answer even though I'm sure he's wrong. Why I care: I sit on the three-person accuracy panel for a decent-sized history podcast/channel, and maybe twice a month we have to make a call. A viewer, a guest, or a whole segment makes a claim that guts some accepted atrocity — something about the Holocaust, or the Armenian genocide, or Nanjing — and we have to decide whether we're looking at a legitimate revisionist arguement we should actually engage, or denial we should pull. We get about a week, we have one consulting historian who's stretched thin and basically just signs off, and none of us is a specialist in the period in question. Right now we eyeball it and it feels arbitrary and I hate it.

Can you build us a real decision procedure — a checklist we can run in maybe half an hour, in order, each step saying what counts as passing, where it stops and says "this is denial, pull the segment," and where it has to hand the thing to a person because it's turned into a values question instead of a factual one? Ground it in whatever the people who actually study this distinction argue, but do not hand me a reading list or citations — I can't look anything up and I don't want the procedure resting on sources nobody on my panel can check; give me the substance itself. Please bake in the tests I half-remember from the fight and whatever better ones you've got: the idea that denial is asymmetrically skeptical (maximum scrutiny on the mainstream account, none on its own), the "what would it take to change your mind" test, and the split between revising what happened and revising what it means. Then run it on three cases: one that's clearly legitimate revision (say, someone arguing a famine death toll was lower, backed by new archival material), one that's clearly denial (the classic package where the gas chambers were really delousing stations and the numbers are wildly inflated), and one where I'm emotionally on the wrong side — a claim I'd love to be true — so I can see whether the thing still bites when it costs me. And be honest about where this procedure will misfire, because if it just launders my priors I'd rather find that out now than in a public retraction.
