For the last two years, since retiring from a career in neurophysiology, I have been reading moral philosophy in a somewhat unsystematic way, and I have reached the point where I want to test an idea rather than simply absorb it. The idea is the one called the hedonistic imperative — the thesis, associated with David Pearce, that the abolition of suffering throughout sentient life is not merely a worthy aim but a moral requirement, and that biotechnology (genetic engineering, and in Pearce's own long-run vision, molecular nanotechnology) will eventually make it achievable. What draws me to it is the part a scientist can respect: the claim that the aversive quality of pain is a contingent feature of our motivational architecture rather than an inescapable cost of being alive, and that it might be replaced by something else without losing the information pain carries. What worries me is everything downstream.

I would like you to write a dialogue between two people who disagree about this, and I would like both of them to be formidable.

One speaker is an advocate of the imperative. Let her press the strongest version of the case: that suffering is intrinsically bad, and bad irrespective of anyone's preferences; that most of our suffering, considered as information, is surplus — noise left over from selection in an ancestral environment rather than a signal we still need; that clinical experience already shows the sensory and the affective aspects of pain can come apart, so there is no principled barrier to keeping the first and discarding the second; and that a technology which rewrote the valence system so that noxious input arrived as an alerting exhilaration rather than a torment would lose nothing worth having. Do not let her be a caricature of the cheerful technophile; let her acknowledge costs and argue that they are payable.

The other speaker is a critic, and I want him to be the more subtle of the two rather than the more conservative. His case should not rest on the sanctity of the given or on a vague appeal to the human condition. Let him argue, at least: that the advocate is conflating the badness of suffering with the goodness of its removal, and that the two can come apart in precisely the cases the advocate's technology creates; that a creature whose preferences have been rewritten cannot supply the endorsement the advocate wants, since its satisfaction is manufactured along with its preferences; that if valence is merely a design feature, then the advocate owes an account of why its negative pole retains the special normative status he assigns to it; and that the meaning, compassion, and achievement he says will survive the rewrite are an empirical bet for which he, and not the critic, bears the burden.

Let the two actually engage — each responding to the last thing the other said, conceding where a point lands, sharpening rather than repeating. At least once, one of them should be forced to modify a position in the face of the other's argument, and I would like the modification to be a real one rather than a rhetorical feint. The dialogue should end in a genuine standoff rather than a tidy resolution, but the standoff should be a precise one: the reader should be able to say exactly what the two are still disagreeing about and why neither has yet moved the other.

Two requests about the response itself. First, do not have either speaker treat a contested claim as if it were common ground; where a premise is disputed among people who actually work on this, let the dispute show. Second, after the dialogue, add a short note, in your own voice, separating the claims in the exchange that are more or less settled among specialists from those that are genuinely contested, so that I am not left mistaking a live disagreement for a consensus. I would rather you flag your own uncertainty than project confidence you do not have.
