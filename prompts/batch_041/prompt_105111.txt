I'm in the back half of a cognitive science degree and I keep bumping into the "frame problem" — it turns up in my AI course as this ancient unsolved thing, and my philosophy of mind lecturer treats it like the deepest reason machines will never really understand anything. But the more I poke at it, the more it looks like a fuss about something pretty mundane. As I understand it, the problem is just that when you write down in logic what an action does, you also have to write down everything the action *doesn't* change — the walls don't move when the robot rolls across the room, the sword doesn't vanish from the chest when a door opens in my friend's RPG, and so on — and if you forget, your reasoner will cheerfully conclude that the robot can now fly or that the sword teleported. That's a bookkeeping annoyance, and it seems like something you could just automate: generate the "nothing else changes" axioms for you, or use default logic and circumscription, and if that's still not enough, let a learned model figure out what stays put. We have probabilistic world models now and systems that predict the next frame of a video game pretty well, so hasn't the frame problem basically been solved in practice — and isn't the reason philosophers still wave it around just that they're attached to the old symbolic story?

My study partner complicates things further: she says the frame problem is really just the qualification problem wearing a hat, and seperately that it only ever applied to GOFAI, so neural nets made it evaporate. I can't tell if either of those is right, or if she's just as confused as I am. Can you set me straight — where exactly have I gone wrong, and what is the actual problem (if there even is one), in a way that survives contact with the modern machine-learning stuff rather than just restating the old textbook version?
