I teach a combined upper-level undergraduate and master's seminar on rational choice, social choice, and ethics, and I am putting together the take-home final. One essay has to be on Harsanyi's aggregation theorem, but I am determined that it not turn into the standard Harsanyi-versus-Rawls performance in which students recite that expected-utility choice behind a veil of ignorance yields utilitarianism while maximin says otherwise. What I want to test is whether a student can prise apart the formal result from the argument built around it and locate exactly where the normative work is being done. A colleague framed the worry for me in a way I would like the question to force the students to confront: the aggregation theorem looks either too weak to be interesting, because the individual weights are left free and every Paretian, welfarist social ordering that also satisfies the expected-utility axioms comes out additive in the individual utilities, or else question-begging, because the additivity is bought only once the individual utilities are already treated as comparable on a common cardinal scale. I would also like the students to notice that the theorem, read at face value, seems to leave no room for inequality aversion in the aggregation — that a concave transformation of the individual utilities, of the kind a prioritarian would want, sits badly with the expected-utility requirement imposed on the social ordering — and to say which of the theorem's assumptions a prioritarian would have to surrender.

Please write the essay question, a model answer of roughly eight hundred words, and a marking scheme worth twenty marks. The question should be answerable in ninety minutes by a strong student who has done the reading but has not memorized the secondary literature; it must not depend on naming particular papers, years, or authors beyond Harsanyi and Rawls themselves, and it should not ask for citations of any kind. Build the marking scheme around five or six must-hit points, give the marks for each, and add for each point a one-line note on how a weaker answer typically misses it — for instance by fusing the aggregation theorem and the original-position argument into a single premise, by assuming the theorem delivers equal weights for free, or by saying only that Rawls denies expected utility without identifying which structural assumption of Harsanyi's is thereby rejected. Finally, flag in the marking scheme the two or three moves that should separate a clear A from a solid B.
