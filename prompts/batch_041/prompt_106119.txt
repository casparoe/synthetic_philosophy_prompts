I run a small philosophy-of-science reading group and I'm trying to put together a handout that finally sorts out something we keep arguing about: when a thought experiment actually teaches us something about the world — the two-unequal-weights-dropped-together one, Einstein's elevator, Maxwell's demon, Schrödinger's cat, Newton's bucket — where exactly does the teaching happen? Every taxonomy I've found just splits people into rationalist vs empiricist and then lists surnames, which is definately useless for a discussion, so I want the thing built the other way round: first the questions that do the splitting, then the positions that actually land in the resulting space, and then a note on which combinations of answers nobody occupies and whether that's because the view is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexamined. The axes I care about are these. (i) Where the epistemic work is done: by the argument you can reconstruct from the scenario, by the intuition the scenario triggers, by the activity of manipulating the scenario in imagination, or by something else again, e.g. by the fact that the scenario is a genuine possibility the world happens to contain. (ii) What the deliverable is meant to be: knowledge, in the sense of a new warrant you didn't have before, or only understanding/redescription of something you were already entitled to. (iii) Whether the warrant is supposed to be a priori or to rest on prior empirical acquaintance with the relevant kind of system. (iv) Whether the reliability of the procedure hangs on the intuitions of competent judges, on the truth of the background theory, or on the thought experiment being embedded in a going research practice rather than performed in isolation. Give me six or seven branch nodes and six to nine leaves; label each leaf with a descriptive name for the position (not a surname — I don't want the handout to stand or fall on me remembering who said what, and I'd rather see each position described by what it's committed to) plus one sentence on the most awkward commitment that position has to swallow. And please no reading list — I'm not asking who defends what, I'm asking what the positions have to say in order to be distinct.

The nodes have to be answerable by someone who doesn't already know which leaf they lead to, so phrase them as questions about cases or claims, not as "do you think thought experiments are arguments in disguise?" — the whole point is that you find out where you stand by answering, not by picking a label. Four cases have to appear somewhere on the tree, either as a node's test case or in a leaf's awkward-commitment sentence: the old chain-over-a-prism proof of the inclined-plane law, where the reconstructed argument is airtight but the intuition seems to do no work at all; Maxwell's demon, where the scenario is physically impossible and yet pushing on it eventually produced a real constraint about what erasing information costs; Einstein's elevator, which was later vindicated by observation, so I want to know whether the positions can say the thought experiment had any evidential force before the observation came in; and at least one case you invent yourself — ideally something with no literature attached, so we can watch the taxonomy do work rather than recall a verdict. If you can, also give me a case where competent people's intuitions came apart and stayed apart, since that seems to be the one that hurts the intuition-based views and I want to see whether they can absorb it. Flag any two leaves your tree can't actually seperate, and say what's doing the conflating. And tell me which single branch node you're least confident about, and what argument or evidence would make you redraw it — I'd rather know where the tree is load-bearing than get a tidy-looking chart.
