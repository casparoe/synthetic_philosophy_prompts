Odd request, sorry in advance. I just got appointed to our state's forensic science commission (I'm the public member, no science background, which is kind of the point) and my first case has me definately out of my depth.

Why this is suddenly personal: my wife spent last spring on a dopamine agonist for her restless legs, and for a few weeks she kept doing things she says she never decided to do. Nothing dramatic, she'd just surface halfway through something and not recognize the decision as hers. The neurologist shrugged, changed the dose, said it happens with that drug. I've been chewing on what makes an action really someones ever since.

The case: a man was convicted of a serious assault. At trial the defense called a neuroscientist who testified about the Libet-style experiments. You know the setup, subjects watch a clock and move a finger whenever they feel like it, electrodes on the scalp show a slow signal building a fraction of a second before they report being aware of deciding to move. The expert said the act is initiated before the person is aware of choosing it, so it wasn't voluntary in the sense the law cares about. The judge let it in, the jury convicted anyway, and the appeal is what put this whole class of testimony on our docket. We're not retrying him. We have to say whether the testimony rests on valid science and what courts in this state should do with it going forward. We vote at the end of the month.

Our standard for any method is two steps: one, is the phenomenon the expert claims to measure real, and two, does the method actually measure it in the individual case in front of the court. One of our members wants the finding to say that an act is not voluntary if a predictive brain signal can be shown to precede the agent's awareness of deciding.

Two things from the record I don't know what to do with. First, some researchers apparently now argue the readiness signal isn't a decision forming at all, it's just background noise accumulating until it happens to cross a threshold, so it tells you about the timing of movement onset and not about when anybody decided anything. Second, on cross the expert conceded that the subjects in those studies had no reason to do one thing rather than another, that the decoding in the newer versions is a group-level result well short of certainty, and that he couldn't say anything about what was going on in this defendant's head on that night.

What I need: the short written opinion I'll circulate before the vote. Recommend a concrete disposition, say something the courts can actually apply, and give a timeline. Work through both steps of our test rather than just announcing that the science is inconclusive. Give the strongest honest version of the side I'm inclined to vote against, because the defense has a real point that if the signal genuinely comes first then we're protecting a flattering story we like to tell about ourselves, and I don't want to wave that away. And tell me which parts of this are empirical questions and which are conceptual ones, because half the commission thinks the second kind aren't our business. No citations please, I can't get behind paywalls and I need something I can read out loud at the meeting.
