My mother spent the last seven weeks of her life in a hospice, and the questions that place left me with have not gone away. Everything there was organized around the relief of suffering and around nothing else. Pain, breathlessness, nausea, agitation, constipation, and the depth of her sedation were all scored on the same zero-to-ten scale and traded off against one another; the staff treated her suffering as having physical, emotional, social, and existential dimensions that had to be worked on together; and when her distress became refractory they were willing to sedate her into unconsciousness, even though the same team was emphatic that they would never do anything to hasten her death. What struck me was the asymmetry. There was an elaborate apparatus for reducing pain and no comparable apparatus for producing pleasure; comfort was defined entirely as the absence of distress.

I have since come across a position that seems to describe what they were doing. On this view, below some threshold of suffering no amount of pleasure could compensate for a little more pain, so that relief has a kind of lexical priority, while above the threshold pleasure and pain are weighed against each other on one common scale, so that a small pleasure can be worth a small pain. Does the hospice practice actually presuppose a view like that? I would like you to reconstruct the theory of value the practice commits its practitioners to, if it commits them to any; to separate what the practice strictly requires from what it merely fits comfortably with; to say which rival views it quietly rules out and where instead it is underdetermined, so that several theories would license it equally well. Would a plain aggregative utilitarianism, or a deontologist who refuses to intend death, license exactly the same practice? And if the practice does rest on the assumption that pains and pleasures lie on a single scale, tell me what would have to change if that assumption turned out to be false. I have no philosophical training, so I would rather have the reconstruction in plain terms than as a survey of positions.
