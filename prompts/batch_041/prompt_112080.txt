I have no academic training, but I have spent a while reading around the question of why private sexual conduct has been treated as a crime, and I want to work one case through a line of argument I have only ever seen summarized. In an essay Bentham wrote in the 1780s and left unpublished, he is said to have opposed the punishment of sex between men on the plain ground that nothing was really arrayed against it except a distaste for a pleasure other people were taking, and that since the parties consent and no third party is injured, the law produced misery without preventing any.

Take the prosecution of Oscar Wilde in 1895 — the libel action against the Marquess of Queensberry that collapsed, the charge of gross indecency, the conviction, the two years' hard labour — and work it through that argument rather than around it. On the principle that the only warrant for punishment is the mischief an act produces, who is harmed by what Wilde was convicted of, and does the answer shift once the less obvious mischiefs are counted: the scandal, the alarm to the public, the example set by letting him be, and the fact that the men involved were younger, poorer and often paid? I would like that second question answered honestly, because I suspect the utilitarian case for the law was less empty than the argument makes it sound. Then reverse the direction and treat the case as a test of the argument: does it come out of the episode confirmed, or does the episode show something the argument never allowed for — that a society can be genuinely worse off for what it merely believes about a man and his desires? End by saying where the argument would have had nothing to say, and whether that silence is a defect or simply a limit.
