I've been arguing with a friend for weeks about the Gödelian case against mechanism — the Lucas–Penrose line that our ability to recognize the truth of a formal system's Gödel sentence, which the system itself cannot prove, shows that the mind outruns any machine — and we keep talking past each other because we never agreed on what such an argument is supposed to establish. He insists that any appeal to incompleteness either proves too little (we can always ascend to a stronger system that proves the sentence, so what has been shown?) or too much (that we have some non-computable insight into arithmetic that no machine could share), and that until someone states the standard the argument must meet, the whole debate is just noise. I've come to agree that we need the standard, though not with his conclusion. So before we resume: write me the desiderata. What would a Gödelian anti-mechanist argument actually have to deliver in order to succeed?

Lay the requirements out as a specification: the results from mathematical logic it must not contradict; the distinctions it cannot collapse (between a system being incomplete and our knowledge of it being incomplete; between a mind's outputs being computable and its being a Turing machine; between a machine being unable to prove something and its being unable to know it; between a single fixed formal system and an open-ended succession of them that a machine might traverse); and the cases it must get right (that for any consistent system we can name, a stronger one proves its Gödel sentence; that a machine might be permitted to revise or extend itself; that we cannot, as a matter of actual performance, prove anything a sufficiently strong machine could not). For each requirement, give the consideration that motivates it and say whether it is a genuine constraint or merely an assumption of one camp. Then tell me, plainly, whether some subset of these requirements is jointly unsatisfiable — that is the part I most want to see, because if the anti-mechanist must simultaneously isolate a distinctively human capacity and concede that any such capacity is matched by some system, the argument may be dead on arrival, and I want to know whether that is really so. Keep the weight on the requirements and their motivations rather than on a survey of who said what; and please don't hand me a reading list — I can't look anything up right now, and I don't want titles I have no way to check.
