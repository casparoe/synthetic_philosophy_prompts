I have no academic background in this area; I read and think about it on my own, and I have been stuck for weeks on an argument in a popular book I recently finished. The argument, in my own words, runs roughly as follows.

The deepest division between East Asia and the West concerns what a person takes a person to be. Westerners understand themselves as independent individuals, defined by their own traits, preferences, and choices; East Asians understand themselves as parts of a larger whole, defined by their roles and their relations to others. The author insists that this is not merely a matter of how people describe themselves. Asked to give twenty answers to the question of who they are, Americans list personal characteristics while Japanese and Chinese list social positions and family roles, and the same divide is said to reach down into perception itself, with East Asian participants describing a scene by its background and Western participants by the object at its center. Nor is the difference confined to description: Western individualism holds that the individual's rights and interests come first and that society is at bottom a contract among separate persons, whereas East Asian collectivism holds that the group comes first and that a person's own good is realized in serving it — the Confucian thought that one becomes fully human only through one's roles and relationships. The author then draws a further conclusion: since human beings evolved in small bands in which survival depended on the group, the collectivist picture is simply the more accurate account of human nature, and the self-sufficient individual of Western theory is a recent and parochial invention. He adds that this is why East Asian societies are so orderly and hierarchical, and why their citizens accepted strict pandemic rules while Westerners bridled.

I would like to know how much of this I should believe. Please work through it point by point: what a researcher who studies these differences would accept as fair, what would make them wince, what is straightforwardly wrong, and what a defender of the collectivist picture would object to in the way it is stated. I suspect the passage is quietly running two different things together — how people describe themselves, on the one hand, and whose interests they are prepared to sacrifice, on the other — and I want to know whether statements made about societies and averages can license the statements it makes about individuals. I would also like to know whether the parts that survive correction actually support the conclusion the author draws from them, or whether the evolutionary and historical claims are doing work the evidence cannot do.

For what it is worth, I lean toward the collectivist picture myself: we are plainly social animals, and the idea that we are separate selves who then agree to associate has always struck me as backwards. But I would rather not be argued into a position by an argument that does not support it. So tell me, finally, whether the question I actually care about — which of the two pictures gets the relation between the person and the community right — is genuinely being answered here, or whether it is only being smuggled in under cover of empirical findings about how people answer questionnaires. I do not need names, dates, or reading recommendations; I have no way to look them up, and what I want is the reasoning itself.
