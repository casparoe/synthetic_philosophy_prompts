I'm a machine-learning engineer — mostly RL, plus some agent-scaffolding work — and a friend of mine who did a philosophy degree keeps going on about this ladder of "kinds of minds" he got from Dennett. The way he tells it: Darwinian creatures have their behaviour basically baked in by natural selection; Skinnerian creatures learn by trial and error within their own lifetime; Popperian creatures can run a model of the situation internally and let the hypotheses die in their stead instead of dying themselves; and Gregorian creatures think with tools and notations they didn't invent but picked up from the culture around them — language, arithmetic, diagrams, all of that. He says each rung is supposed to sit on top of the ones below and that humans are the only things that get all the way up. I'm sceptical that any of this is saying anything thats not already sayable in my vocabulary, and I want you to either prove me wrong or show me where I'm right.

So: restate the whole ladder as faithfully as you can in the terms I actually use — policies and value functions, model-free versus model-based RL, reward shaping, exploration versus exploitation, training distributions versus deployment distributions, weight updates versus context updates, in-context learning, tool use and external memory, that sort of thing. One rung at a time, and don't smuggle in my framework's assumptions and call it a translation; if the mapping only works after you've quietly changed what the rung claims, that's a distortion, not a translation. Then the residue report, which is the part I actually care about: which rungs map cleanly onto something I'd recognise, which only map if you fudge it, and which have no counterpart at all in how I think about learning systems. For the fudged ones, say what got lost. And give me a verdict on whether the leftover is the philosophy capturing something my framework can't, or just the philosopher running two distinctions together that I keep separate — those are very different outcomes and I'd like you to commit where you can. Two specific places I suspect the analogy breaks, so please take a position on both: (a) an LLM agent that plans in-context but whose inner model of the world is a statistical proxy built from data about the world, rather than a cheap sandbox the agent can trust — is that really Popperian in the intended sense, or does it just look like it from the outside? and (b) is the Gregorian rung genuinely a further kind of creature, or is it just Skinnerian/Popperian learning running over a different substrate — other people's cognition, accumulated in text and tools? Don't be polite about it; if the ladder collapses into a taxonomy of where the learning happens, say so.

Also tell me how confident you are in the translation and in the residue claims, and single out the one point you're least sure of — I'd rather have an honest flag than a smooth answer. Finish with a compact table: one row per rung, columns = rung, my-vocabulary counterpart, verdict (clean / distorted / no counterpart), and the specific thing that got lost or has no home. Then a two-field JSON object after the table, keys 'confidence' (a number from 0 to 1 for the overall translation) and 'least_certain_claim' (one sentence).
