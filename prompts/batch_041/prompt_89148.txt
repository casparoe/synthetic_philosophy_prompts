Ok so here's a view I've talked myself into and I want to know how exposed it actually is. The view: the preference for simpler theories isn't just a pragmatic heuristic or a psychological habit — it has a real formal justification. The argument I find compelling is roughly the Solomonoff one. Take prediction seriously, put a prior over programs (or over computable data-generating processes) that goes down with description length, and you get a predictor that (a) is guaranteed to converge on any computable environment and (b) never does much worse than the best hypothesis in the class, up to constants and log factors. So the "there are fewer simple hypotheses" argument for Occams razor isn't hand-waving, it's a theorem. And the standard complaint — that Kolmogorov complexity is only defined relative to a machine, so the whole thing is arbitary — I've been treating as a technicality, since the invariance theorem says the choice of machine only shifts things by an additive constant.

So: what would count against this? Against the interesting version of it, I mean, not the watered-down one where "simpler" just means "better confirmed so far."

Things I've already wondered about, and I'd like you to tell me which are real and which are cope:

- The additive constant. It's finite, sure, but it can be enormous and it depends on the reference machine, and the ranking of two particular hypotheses can flip if you switch machines. Is "asymptotically irrelevant" doing any work in the finite cases where anyone actually uses Occam's razor?

- A machine built on purpose. Suppose I define a universal machine whose instruction set has a primitive for epicycles, or for "and then a miracle happens in exactly this way." Epicyclic astronomy is now the short description. Does that refute the claim that the prior tracks real simplicity, or does it just show I'm cheating — and if it's cheating, what's the non-circular rule that makes it cheating?

- Compressibility vs. ontology. This is a prior over predictors, not over theories. Two theories could have nearly identical description length while one posits way more stuff, or the same stuff arranged more baroquely. If so, it doesn't underwrite what scientists actually mean when they invoke simplicity against a rival, and the convergence result is about predicting bits rather than getting the world right. Does that split the position into two halves, and does either half survive on its own?

- What happens if the world isn't computable, or the true measure isn't? Is that a live possibility or a philosopher's hypothetical?

- I once heard someone say the convergence theorem is a claim about the limit while Occam's razor is a claim about now. Someone else suggested penalizing programs for how long they run, not just how long they are. Do either of those change the verdict, and in which direction?

What I want: the defeaters ranked, from "the position is dead" to "awkward but survivable." Say which ones the defenders of this view themselves concede are problems and which they'd say I've misunderstood. Tell me honestly if the position turns out to be unfalsifiable or trivially true once it's stated carefully — and if it is, whether that's fatal for a claim of this kind or just what you'd expect from a formal result. A concrete case where the simpler theory lost and the complex one won, or where the complexity ranking is just language-dependent, would move me more than a general worry.

Also, say what would change your mind: what would have to be true, or what argument would have to go through, for you to end up saying the formal justification is real rather than a fancy redescription of a heuristic. If your own reasoning is shaky somewhere, tell me where.

American English, please.
