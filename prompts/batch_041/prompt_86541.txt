The Standing Committee on Animal and Novel-Entity Welfare has asked me to turn its sentience policy into something a case officer can actually run. Our mandate covers everything from laboratory invertebrates to farmed decapods to the embodied AI systems two engineering groups now build, and it will soon cover cultured neural tissue. We sort entities into review tiers that determine what a facility must do before, during, and after using them, and our classifications are increasingly challenged by animal advocates and by the groups whose work we slow down. The committee's working premise — one I am not sure survives contact with our own paperwork — is that sentience comes in degrees, so that an insect, a shrimp, a thermostat, and a trained control agent all sit somewhere on one scale, just at different points. Our actual practice, though, is a set of on/off triggers: either a protocol needs review or it does not.

Write a decision procedure an officer can run on a single classification request. It should be usable in about ninety minutes on the documents a facility submits, by someone with no neuroscience training, with the result published and likely to be cited in litigation. Say what to ask, in what order; what counts as a sufficient answer at each step; where the procedure stops with a no; and where it must hand the file to the full committee because the remaining question is one of values rather than evidence.

The committee has drafted four indicators and wants you to work with these rather than substitute your own: (i) aversive responsiveness — the entity does more than withdraw from a noxious stimulus, it acts to terminate or avoid it; (ii) motivational trade-off — it will forgo a competing reward or pay a cost to avoid the stimulus; (iii) state-dependence — the avoidance is modulated by analgesics, by prior exposure, or by competing drives in a way that suggests a persisting aversive state rather than a reflex arc; (iv) integrative unity — the signals driving the first three converge on a single state that guides behavior across contexts, rather than several independent circuits. There is also a fifth, deliberately vague clause the committee calls the substrate question. For each indicator, say what it is evidence for and what it is not evidence for; say which of them a thermostat, a plant, or a bacterium could pass; and say what to do with an entity that passes the first three but fails the fourth, or that might pass the fourth vacuously.

Run the procedure on at least three cases, chosen to include one where it yields an answer the committee will not like and one where it withdraws protection from something the committee would rather protect: a Drosophila genetics lab that uses a noxious-heat assay on mutant lines; a shrimp farm seeking a low-tier classification; and a building-control agent that has learned to avoid a punishment signal. Address explicitly how the procedure handles entities that exist in enormous numbers, since a tier covering millions of individuals can swamp every other consideration in the office's caseload.

Finally, tell me where the procedure cannot decide, which of its steps are value judgments dressed up as facts, and what it will get systematically wrong. If graded sentience is the premise, say whether the tier system can be made consistent with it at all, or whether the office is quietly assuming a threshold and should say so in writing.
