I have been reading rationalist blogs and forums for about fifteen years, and I keep returning to the philosophy of the infinite — partly because it is one of the few areas where an amateur tradition and a technical literature are still genuinely in conversation with each other, and partly because I suspect that much of the conversation is wheel-spinning. I would like an agenda rather than a survey, so let me be specific about the puzzle that set this off.

Hilbert's hotel is almost always taught as a demonstration that infinite collections behave unlike finite ones: that a whole can be no larger than a proper part, and that the lesson is to stop importing finite intuitions into the infinite. But the argument goes through only on the assumption that one-to-one correspondence is the criterion of equal size, and the people who find the hotel absurd are generally rejecting exactly that criterion. So the standard presentation looks to me either question-begging or like a disguised stipulation, and I cannot tell which. The technical literature makes it worse rather than better. In standard set theory without the axiom of choice, infinite and Dedekind-infinite come apart: there can be infinite sets into which the natural numbers cannot be injected at all, so that the rooms cannot even be numbered, let alone filled and shifted one along; and there are infinite sets, the amorphous ones, that are not the union of two disjoint infinite pieces by any means whatever. If the word infinite in the hotel story tacitly means Dedekind-infinite, then a good deal of popular moralising about infinity is trading on an assumption nobody states, and I would like to know how much of the standard pedagogy survives that observation.

Here is what I want. My budget is two years of part-time attention, roughly ten hours a week, plus a reading group of six people who meet fortnightly and will follow where I lead. My mathematics stops at a good undergraduate level: I can follow a proof and I am comfortable with the basics of cardinals and ordinals, but I will not be contributing to set theory, and I am writing for a philosophical readership rather than a mathematical one. So the question is not what is important in the abstract; it is what deserves this particular budget.

Give me a ranked agenda of the live questions in this area — actual infinity, cardinality and the criteria of size, and the various finitisms, including the versions that deny that sufficiently large numbers exist or that exponentiation is total, and which look to me as though they might be hard even to state without presupposing the numbers they deny. For each item, tell me whether it is upstream or downstream of the others; whether it is tractable at all for someone with my background and my hours; whether it is neglected relative to its importance or has simply been worked over for a century; and what would actually change if it were settled — which arguments, in philosophy or in the applied uses of infinity that circulate in my corner of the internet (the ones that turn on placing a uniform probability over a countable collection, which cannot be done if the probabilities are to be countably additive), would have to be revised, and which would not move at all.

I also want you to tell me which of these disputes are verbal. My prior is that at least one of the famous disagreements — the part-whole principle against one-to-one correspondence, or whether there are as many even numbers as there are numbers — is a disagreement about what size is to mean rather than a disagreement about anything, and that a great deal of effort has been spent on it for that reason. But I do not want to assume that. If the criterion of size is a stipulation, tell me what would count as a reason to stipulate differently, and whether the part-whole criterion does any theoretical work better than the correspondence criterion does.

Finally, tell me what would move an item up or down the list within a year or two, and be willing to tell me that only two of these questions are worth my two years. I would rather have three things I can make progress on than a complete map of a field I cannot contribute to.
