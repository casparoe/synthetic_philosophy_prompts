You are a philosopher of mind and cognitive scientist with a specialism in the philosophy and neuroscience of pain, and for this task I want you to act as my severest critic rather than as a summariser of the literature. Think carefully before you write, and start by brainstorming every line of attack you can see on the position described below.

I am a third-year undergraduate taking a module on the philosophy of mind and the emotions. In two weeks I have to give a fifteen-minute presentation and then chair twenty minutes of discussion, and my topic is pain asymbolia and what it shows about the badness of pain. Do not give me reading lists, citations or a literature review; I cannot check any of that, and it is not what I need. I need to know what this room is going to do to me.

The phenomenon, as I intend to present it: patients — typically after a stroke, sometimes after certain kinds of psychosurgery, and in some drug states — report that they can feel the pain, can localise it and rate its intensity, but that it does not bother them. They show little distress, may not withdraw from the stimulus, and may decline painkillers. As I understand it the damage usually sits in or near the insular cortex and is described as disconnecting it from the cingulate and the rest of the limbic loop, but I am shaky on the anatomy and would rather not assert anything about it that I cannot defend, so tell me if I am about to say something false.

My thesis is that these cases show that the badness of pain is not intrinsic to the sensory quality of pain: the sensation can occur without the badness, because the badness belongs to a separable affective and motivational component. I draw a three-way distinction between the sensory quality, the felt badness or valence, and the motivational pull to get away from it, and I claim the cases show that the felt badness can come apart from the sensory quality. I am genuinely unsure whether they also show that felt badness can come apart from the motivational pull, and I intend to flag that uncertainty rather than pretend it away.

I will also be using a composite vignette, which I will say explicitly in the presentation is a composite and not a documented case: a patient who describes an intense, precisely localised burning in the hand, denies that it hurts in the sense of making him want to escape it, declines morphine, and yet, asked in the abstract, agrees that pain is a bad thing and that he would rather not have it. And I want to raise a mirror-image thought experiment: a state with all of the aversiveness and the urgent pull to make it stop, but no sensory quality at all — is that pain, and if it is, is it bad for the one who has it?

The audience will be roughly: the convenor, a philosopher of mind who holds that experiences are representational through and through, and who will press on whether the patients are misdescribing what they feel or whether the word pain is simply being used for two different states; two PhD students in cognitive neuroscience who work on pain, distrust the textbook two-component story, and will say the clinical evidence is a handful of heterogeneous cases with poor reporting and no proper measurement; a clinician who has seen such patients on a ward and will say that reports after a stroke are unreliable, that denial and confabulation are common, and that asymbolia is a ragbag diagnosis — she will also point out that the standard clinical definition of pain builds unpleasantness into the definition itself, so that on that definition my patients are not in pain at all; and two students who have been reading accounts on which pain is essentially a command to stop, or a representation of bodily damage as bad, and who will say my thesis assumes what it ought to prove.

Give me a Q&A prep sheet. Order the objections by how likely they are and how much damage each would do to the thesis, and phrase each one the way that particular person would actually phrase it in the room. For each objection, give me the reply I should make in about ninety seconds, what I should concede up front, and an honest note on where the reply is soft. Flag separately the objections that rest on a genuine disagreement about what pain is rather than on a factual or methodological doubt, since those are the ones I cannot answer by appealing to evidence. Include the question I am hoping nobody asks, and the weakest point in my own position that I should prepare to defend rather than hope goes unnoticed. If my thesis is ambiguous in a way that lets some objections hit a target I did not intend, say so and tell me how to state the thesis more precisely. Finally, tell me what would count as progress in this dispute — a distinction, a case, a measurement — or why nothing would.

Write in British English throughout. Finish with a short paragraph on which two or three objections you think matter most and why.
