The question:

Q4. (a) State Jeffrey's rule for updating on a partition {E1, ..., En} with new credences q1, ..., qn, and say precisely which of your old conditional credences it leaves alone — in lecture we called those the rigid ones. (b) An urn is drawn at random from three urns, each equally likely. U1 contains 70 black and 30 white balls, U2 contains 50 of each, U3 contains 30 black and 70 white. You get a glimpse of a ball drawn from the urn in poor light, and you come away with credence 0.8 that the ball is black and 0.2 that it is white. Using Jeffrey's rule, compute your new credences in U1, U2 and U3, showing your work. (c) True or false, with a short justification: if you update twice in a row, once on the partition {E, not-E} and once on a different partition {F, not-F}, the order of the two updates cannot affect the result. (d) In two or three sentences: does the correct answer to (c) give you any reason to think that Jeffrey's rule is not a rational way to revise your credences?

Her answer, typed up as she wrote it:

(a) Jeffrey's rule says that when your evidence leaves you uncertain between the cells of a partition, and moves your credences in those cells to q1, ..., qn, your new credence in any proposition H is the weighted average of your old conditional credences in H given each cell, with the new credences as weights: P'(H) = q1·P(H|E1) + ... + qn·P(H|En). You only change your mind about how likely the cells are; your opinion about H given each cell is held fixed. The rigid credences are the conditional ones: P'(H|Ei) = P(H|Ei), for every H and every cell Ei. The motivation is cases like the urn below, where the evidence doesn't make anything certain, so strict conditionalization has nothing to condition on. Strict conditionalization comes out as the limiting case: set q1 = 1 and you get P'(H) = P(H|E1), i.e. plain conditioning on E1. Rigidity is a bit stronger than it looks, though — it isn't only the conditionals on the cells that stay fixed. Any conditional credence P(H|X) is left alone as long as X isn't itself one of the cells Ei. So in the urn case P(U1|black) is unchanged (still 7/15), and so is P(U1|U1 or U3), which stays at 1/2 — the glimpse tells you about black versus white but nothing about how the two non-even urns split between themselves.

(b) P(black) = (1/3)(0.7) + (1/3)(0.5) + (1/3)(0.3) = 0.5. So P(U1|black) = 0.2333/0.5 = 0.4667, P(U2|black) = 0.1667/0.5 = 0.3333, P(U3|black) = 0.1/0.5 = 0.2, and by symmetry P(U1|white) = 0.2, P(U2|white) = 0.3333, P(U3|white) = 0.4667. Then P'(U1) = 0.8(0.4667) + 0.2(0.2) = 0.4133, P'(U2) = 0.8(0.3333) + 0.2(0.3333) = 0.3333, P'(U3) = 0.8(0.2) + 0.2(0.4667) = 0.2533. The glimpse shifts credence toward U1 and away from U3, which is what you'd expect from evidence that favours black.

(c) True. The rule is completely determined by the two things it fixes, namely the new credences in the cells and the rigid conditionals, and neither of those depends on when you apply the rule. So the two updates impose the same pair of constraints on your final credence function whichever order they come in, and only one function satisfies both. Where the partitions cross-cut, the joint partition just gets its credences fixed by the two rounds of weights, and you'd get the same thing by conditioning on the joint partition directly.

(d) No. What it shows is that the order in which evidence arrives can matter, which it obviously can — if I get the first glimpse and then the second, my total evidence isn't the same as if I'd got them the other way round. A rule that was insensitive to that would be the suspicious one. So I don't think the non-commutativity is a problem for the rule itself.
