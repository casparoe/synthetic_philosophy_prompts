I have been running a small reading group on the skeptical tradition for the better part of a year, and we keep circling back to the same knot: Moore's proof of an external world. The proof is almost embarrassingly plain — Moore holds up one hand, then the other, and concludes that at least two external things exist — and he insists this is a proof in the fullest sense, on the grounds that the premises are different from the conclusion and he knows the premises to be true. The skeptic's counter is equally plain: if you cannot rule out that you are now dreaming, you cannot know that the hand in front of you is really there, so the proof either assumes what it is meant to establish or establishes nothing. Here is my frustration. Nobody in the group is short of arguments. Everyone can rehearse the moves, the discussion is civil, and in twelve months no one has shifted an inch. I am no longer asking which side is right. I want a diagnosis of the disagreement itself.

My own hypothesis, which I would like you to test rather than accept, is that the two arguments are not rivals at all but the same conditional run in opposite directions. Both sides seem to grant something like: if I know this is a hand, then I know I am not dreaming. Moore runs it forward from the antecedent — I know this is a hand, so I am not dreaming, so there is an external world. The skeptic runs it backward from the denial of the consequent — I do not know I am not dreaming, so I do not know this is a hand. The entire dispute then reduces to which end of that conditional is the fixed point, and that is not a question more evidence could settle, because it is a question about epistemic priority. A friend of mine insists this shows the whole thing is merely verbal, a difference in the standards attached to the word know, so that both sides can be right at once. I suspect that is too quick, and I also suspect there is a second, independent strand here: a disagreement about what a proof is owed — whether it must proceed from premises the other party will grant, the way one proves something to a tribunal, or whether it is enough that the premises be known and the conclusion follow. Moore seems to be offering the second sort of proof and being judged by the first.

What I want from you is a diagnosis, not a verdict. Is this impasse verbal, a matter of two standards for the same word; is it substantive, a genuine clash over starting points that neither side can argue for; are the two sides answering different questions under one heading — whether we in fact know versus whether we can answer the skeptic; or is it evidence that the demand for a proof here is itself ill-formed? Engage what each side actually says, including the odd status of the claim about the hands: is it a premise, a conclusion, or something that cannot be either because it functions as a hinge on which the rest turns? And then tell me what would count as progress given your diagnosis — a distinction, a formal result about whether justification carries across known entailment, a deliberate change of question — or argue that none should be expected and say why. I am not looking for a survey of the literature, and please do not hand me a reading list; I want the structure of the standoff laid bare, with reasons.
