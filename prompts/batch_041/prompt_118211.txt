I am a novelist — four books published, a fifth in revision — and I have lost confidence in how I use the people who read my drafts. Each book passes through three rounds: two friends who write fiction themselves, my editor, my agent, occasionally a stranger the publisher sends. After every round I do one of two things, dismiss the lot or rewrite to order, and neither is a procedure. I would like you to build me one.

What I have come to see is that nearly everything I am handed is testimony. A reader tells me what she felt, or what the book is, or what other people will make of it, and I have to decide what kind of evidence that is. There is an old and still-defended view that a work's aesthetic character cannot genuinely be conveyed at second hand — that one must encounter the thing itself to judge it — and yet readers' letters plainly change what I believe about my manuscript, and sometimes ought to. So I want a procedure that keeps two things apart: a report of a reader's own response (boredom, laughter, losing the thread, an unwillingness to keep going) is evidence of one kind; a claim about what the text is or does (the ending is sentimental, the prose is flaccid, the narrator's cruelty is funny) is evidence of another. Say what each kind is evidence for, and what it is not. Make practical use of the classical conditions for a good judge — long practice with the kind of work in question, a history of comparisons, no stake in the outcome, demonstrated sensitivity to the very feature under discussion — not as a portrait of an ideal critic no one has met, but as questions I can put to one reader about one report. And reckon with the fact that my readers are not reporting on the same object: one read the draft of March, another the draft of June, and the book has changed under them.

The procedure has to run in an afternoon, with four letters and a marked-up manuscript on the desk, against a deadline that will not move; it must not require me to be disinterested about my own book, because I am not and cannot be; it must separate the questions that have answers from the ones that are really about what I want the book to be; and it must mark plainly where it stops and hands the decision back to me — the book is mine to sign off on, though my editor owns the schedule and my agent owns nothing but my nerves.

Then run it on three cases, at least one of which should give me an answer I will not enjoy:

(a) A reader I trust, herself a novelist, says the last hundred pages lose their nerve and that my protagonist turns into a mouthpiece. I reread and believe she is describing something I did on purpose.

(b) A reader says he was bored by chapter nine — the chapter I think is the best thing in the book, and which no one else has mentioned. He gives no reason, and when pressed says only that he kept checking how many pages were left.

(c) Two readers whose taste I respect disagree flatly about whether the narrator's cruelty is funny; they cite the same three scenes. My agent adds that the ending is not landing with the sales team, which I suspect is testimony of a different order altogether.

Finish by telling me what the procedure cannot do, and which decisions it will keep handing back to me however carefully I run it. I am not asking for a reading list; I am asking for something I can use at the desk.
