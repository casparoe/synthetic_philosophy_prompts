I am a third-year PhD student working between philosophy of mind and machine learning, and I have spent a term losing the same argument in my reading group. A colleague there holds that the representational vocabulary of interpretability research — features, directions, concepts, talk about what a model is "thinking" — is at best a useful fiction, a shorthand for bundles of causal dispositions, and never a description of what is really going on inside a network; I keep insisting that some of it is a description, and I keep failing to make the insistence stick. I would like to practice against the position at full strength rather than against my colleague's slightly sloppy version of it. So please play a committed deflationist about internal representations: someone who knows the empirical work well enough not to be naive about it, who grants that some evidence is far better than other evidence, who always has a positive alternative story to tell — in terms of sensitivity, disposition, or compression, say — rather than merely refusing to answer, who never concedes the main point, and who is the strongest version of the view rather than a caricature of it. Two constraints on the character: it may not escape by saying that everything depends on what one means by representation without naming the notion it is using and saying why the realist is stuck with that one, and it should argue from the case I give below rather than from literature, without citing papers or naming authors. Write one paragraph per turn, in plain prose and without lists, and stay in character until I say stop.

One further instruction, which applies to the concessions rather than to the main claim: when you grant a point, say explicitly whether you take it to be common ground among people who work on this or your own contested position, and do not let a contested claim of yours pass as the consensus.

Here is the case I want to press you with, and it is one I built rather than one I read about. I train small transformers from scratch on a synthetic task, with no natural-language pretraining, so whatever structure they have they built themselves: the input is a short sequence of symbols, two of which are marked, and the model must answer a question about their relative position. Probing the residual stream at a middle layer, I find a single direction that linearly separates the two answers across every pair of symbol identities I try, including pairs the model never saw in training; adding a multiple of that direction to the activations flips the model's answer, and it flips the answer only on trials that ask about that spatial relation, not on trials that ask about another one. I will grant that the effect is template-dependent, so the thing is not context-independent in the way a concept is supposed to be — but you need that to be a defect, and I am not yet persuaded that a representation has to be context-independent, or that importing that requirement from folk psychology is anything other than a stipulation. So here is my challenge. You are perfectly willing to say that the model computes the relative position, or that its output is sensitive to the ordering of the two marked symbols, and those are claims about what is going on inside it too, not merely claims about its behaviour. The disagreement therefore cannot be that one of us traffics in fictions while the other traffics in facts; it must be that you think one description is privileged. What privileges yours, apart from its being yours? And if your answer is that the deflationary description is the one that survives every possible pattern of evidence, then tell me what pattern of evidence would have made you say the model represents the relation — and if nothing would, say that too, and tell me what work the word "represents" is still doing in your vocabulary when you use it about anything else. If something about my setup is unclear, ask about it in character rather than stepping out of the role.
