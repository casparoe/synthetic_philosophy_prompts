I am a novelist at work on a book about a man who trades a year of his life for a very long shot, and I have reached the point where the argument inside the book has to be sound rather than merely plausible, because two of my characters make it and neither of them is supposed to be a fool. The argument is not mine; I overheard it last spring at a residency in the Hudson Valley, between two friends of mine, over the last of a bottle of wine. Ines had just come off the phone with her brother, a graduate student in his late twenties who had been offered a place on a research project: it would take a year of his life and about forty thousand dollars of his savings, and on the most honest estimate anyone involved could give, it had something like a one-in-a-billion chance of preventing a catastrophe that would otherwise kill a billion people. Ines thought he should obviously take it; Tobias thought it was a form of madness; neither of them persuaded the other, and I stayed out of it, though I have been chewing on it ever since.

The exchange went roughly like this. (1) Ines: one in a billion times a billion lives is one life, so he is trading a year of his own for an expected life saved, and anyone would make that trade if the life were certain; the only question is whether a tiny probability cancels the multiplication, and multiplication does not care how it acquired its factors. (2) Tobias: a rule that acts on one in a billion must also act on one in ten to the thirtieth, and then anyone — crank, charlatan, or sincere visionary — who can attach a sufficiently large number to a sufficiently small probability can command the whole of his life; a procedure with no floor is not a decision rule but an instrument for being talked into anything, and anyway the one-in-a-billion is not a measurement but a courtesy extended to a story, which the procedure then treats as though it had been weighed. (3) Ines: that is an argument about bad estimates, not about expected value — if the number is bad, repair the number; if it is good, take the bet. She then offered a friendly amendment, which she called a reliability floor: no credence smaller than your credence that your own reasoning has gone wrong at this scale should be treated as a live number, since you cannot be more confident in an estimate than in the process that produced it. (4) Tobias: any fixed floor is either arbitrary or circular — set it at one in a million and it licenses modest sacrifices, set it at one in ten to the thirtieth and it licenses everything, and nothing in the argument chooses; and if the floor is your confidence in your own method, that confidence is itself estimated by the method, so the floor is a mirror that always settles just below whatever you were already prepared to do. He preferred bounding the utility function, caring about lives on a logarithmic scale; Ines answered that then he must be indifferent between saving a million people and saving a billion, which struck her as worse than the disease. Ines then refined her floor: it should not be one threshold but should rise with the size of the stake, so that the larger the promised number, the more confidence she demands in the process that generated it. Tobias said that was risk aversion wearing arithmetic as a disguise. (5) Tobias's last move was a change of subject he insisted was not one: the question is not whether this bet is good but whether he wants to be the sort of person who takes bets of this shape, given that such offers are often constructed by people who know the shape of his policy and that he will meet thousands of them in a life. Ines replied that reasoning about policies is still expected value, just over policies, so it inherits every problem it was meant to solve, and that this particular offer is a one-off made in good faith, so the exploitability worry never reaches it.

I would like you to act as a philosopher who works on decision theory and the ethics of extreme risk, and who is good at noticing when two apparently different arguments are secretly the same argument. Before you adjudicate, set out the strongest version of each position as you understand it, so that I can see you have hold of both. Then go through the five exchanges and Ines's refinement move by move, rating each from clearly mistaken through defensible to clearly right, with a sentence or two of justification for each rating. Tell me who is right about what, and what each of them is missing; say specifically whether the reliability floor — and in particular the version that scales with the stake — is a genuine repair or an evasion, and whether Tobias's policy-and-exploitability test is a distinct argument or the first argument in different clothes; and say whether there is a third position neither of them saw, including what it would have to concede. Do not split the difference, and do not soften a step that is simply wrong; where one of them wins a point outright, say so. I do not want citations, reading suggestions, or names of anyone who has written on this — I want the arguments themselves, worked through carefully and at whatever length they need. Close with a single final line, by itself, of the form \answer{...} containing a short phrase naming which of the two is closer to right on the central question, followed by the one consideration that decides it.
