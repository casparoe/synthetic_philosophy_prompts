I run the country-risk desk at a small forecasting shop — eight analysts, mostly IR and econ backgrounds, nobody with any real stats training — and I'm trying to get us off vibes-based "is this regime about to fall" calls. We do about twenty country assessments a quarter for clients who want a number and a horizon (five years out), and I'm the one who signs the forecast and eats it when were wrong. Two days per country is what we actually have, so whatever you give me has to be something an analyst can run in an afternoon without a stats package.

What I want is a procedure, not a lecture. We all already believe in the outside view and reference classes and base rates in the abstract; the problem is turning that into steps. So: what do we ask, in what order, to pick a reference class for "does this regime survive the next five years"? What counts as a good enough answer at each step? Where does the procedure stop with a no, or with "we can't tell you"? And where does it hand the decision back to a human because the question has stopped being empirical and turned into a judgment call we're dressing up as arithmetic?

Things I specifically want it to survive:

- The class choice is where I'm sure we fool ourselves. "Empire", "regime", "autocracy" aren't natural kinds, and I can get the base rate to come out at 5% or 60% depending on where I draw the box. I don't want a procedure that hides that behind one number.
- The units aren't independent. One collapse in a region gets borrowed by its neighbours, so our forty cases might really be worth about three — I think this is what anthropologists call Galton's problem, and I only half understand it, so correct me if I'm mangling it and tell me what it should actually do to our arithmetic.
- Don't let the inside-view detail eat the base rate. The whole reason we're doing this is that our case-by-case storytelling is the thing that keeps being wrong, and every analyst will want to tell you why this country is the exception.
- The client wants a probability. I want to know when to give them a range, when to give them a refusal, and how to say the refusal without looking like we're dodging.

Then run it on three made-up cases: one where it works cleanly, one where two defensible class choices swing the answer so far that the procedure has to say something ugly about our confidence, and one where it hands me back a number I don't want to hear (or a refusal I don't want to give). Finish with what the procedure can't do and where it will misfire.

One more thing: keep the settled stuff seperate from the contested stuff. There's real disagreement among people who study forecasting about how much the outside view should dominate, whether a reference class is even well-defined, and how much of this is judgment in a lab coat — don't hand me any of that as consensus, and flag where you're giving me your own call rather than the field's.

No citations or reading list, please — I'm not going to check them and neither will my analysts.
