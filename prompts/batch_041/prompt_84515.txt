I'm a novelist and I've got to give the eulogy at my oldest friend's memorial next month. She was a poetry translator — Russian into English, mostly — and for the last twenty years of her life she banged on about that Hofstadter and Sander book, the one where the whole of cognition turns out to be analogy-making: you never just apply a concept to a thing, you recognise the thing as being like other things you've already met, and what we call a 'concept' is just the dried sediment of all those likenesses. She'd corner you with it at dinner. Anyway, I read the book, I can't unsee it now, and it's landed me in a proper corner with this eulogy.

Here's the corner. If everything I think is a likeness, then every true thing I can say about her is really a thing about someone or something else — she was like her mother, like the woman in the poem she was translating the week she died, like a teaspoon. (There she is again. I did that out of habit.) She used to say that when someone dies the language runs out of likenesses, that grief is the one place analogy has no purchase, and she made me promise to test it at her funeral: either I find one sentence about her that isn't a comparison to anything, or I stand up and admit, out loud, in front of everyone, that there isn't one. So I'd rather the speech actually try — and if it fails, fail honestly and say why, instead of cheating by smuggling in a simile and calling it a fact.

The room is half her family (her mum's a retired nurse from Wakefield, lovely, allergic to philosophy) and half translators and linguists from the poetry centre, who know the Hofstadter thesis better than I do and will spot it if I get it wrong or cheapen it. Five minutes, spoken, and no reading list please — a bibliography is no use to me standing at a lectern. Don't do the bit about her living on in our memories either, she'd have called that a comparison too, and a cheap one.

Keep the teaspoon in. It's the only scrap of her I've got that isn't a comparison to something else.
