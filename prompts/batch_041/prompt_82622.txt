I'm starting a three-year postdoc in the fall and I basically get to pick what I work on, full time, and I want it to be imprecise probabilities. Not because I'm already convinced they're right — I'm genuinely not — but because it feels like the kind of area where a philosopher with decent formal chops and no interest in running experiments can actually move something. So: what are the open questions here that matter most, and in what order would you actually take them on?

Here's the thing I keep bumping into and can't get past. Imprecise credences seem to get asked to do three pretty different jobs, and I rarely see anyone be explicit about which one they're doing: (a) they're a literal model of a doxastic state that's genuinely indeterminate or gappy, so a real feature of your mind; (b) they're just a convenient way of representing a determinate but only partially articulated state, in which case they're bookkeeping and the interesting work is somewhere else; (c) they have no doxastic significance at all — they're a decision-theoretic instrument for getting sensible behaviour under ambiguity. My suspicion is that a lot of the standard disagreements — dilation, whether spread-out credences can be genuinely responsive to evidence, whether imprecision solves the old problem of the non-unique prior or just relocates it, what maximality and E-admissibility are even supposed to be picking out — are really disagreements about which of (a)-(c) is on the table. So I want a prioritization that's explicit about that: which open questions actually depend on settling (a)/(b)/(c) first, and which ones are independent of it and can be worked on either way?

I also care about three more things. First, which of these a philosopher can make progress on without either reinventing decision theory from scratch or having to do empirical work on Ellsberg-style ambiguity aversion, since I can do the first badly and the second not at all. Second, where the area is crowded versus neglected — my impression is a lot of energy goes into inventing new decision rules and not much into the more boring foundational stuff, but tell me if that's wrong. Third, and this is the one I actually lose sleep over: is imprecise probability a live research program or a twenty-year detour? If it's the latter I'd rather find out now than in year three.

Rank the questions, and defend the ranking on the criteria you're using — importance, tractability for someone like me, neglectedness, and how many other questions depend on this one. Tell me what would move something up or down the list over the next couple of years: what result or argument would make you say okay, that one just got settled, cross it off. And say how confident you are in the whole thing, and single out the one claim in your answer you're least sure of.
