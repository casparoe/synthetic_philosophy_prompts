Exam question (15 points):

"(1) State Hart's and Fuller's positions on whether an unjust enactment can be law. (2) How would each of them treat a statute that satisfies every requirement of legality — it is general, publicly promulgated, clear, prospective, stable, and faithfully applied by officials — but commands citizens to inform on family members? (3) Is the disagreement between Hart and Fuller merely verbal?"

Student's answer:

"Hart and Fuller agree that law and morality are connected in practice; they disagree about whether the connection is necessary. Hart's separation thesis says that the validity of a rule is one question and its moral merit another, so a rule can be valid law and still be so iniquitous that we should refuse to obey it. Validity is fixed by the rule of recognition, the ultimate standard officials use to identify law, and that rule is a basic norm the officials presuppose rather than enact. Fuller denies that law can be separated from morality. His inner morality of law is the set of requirements a system must meet if it is to guide conduct at all, and it includes rules protecting persons and property, since no legal system could survive the hostility that would follow from their absence. On this basis Fuller holds that a sufficiently unjust enactment is not really law at all.

On part (2), Hart's answer is straightforward: the statute is valid law, and the question whether to obey it is a separate moral question. Fuller's answer is harder, but the same machinery applies: because the statute violates the inner morality, he would say it is not law, and a judge should say so.

On part (3), I think the disagreement is largely verbal. Once Hart concedes the minimum content of natural law, he has admitted that morality enters the very concept of law, and Fuller's inner morality is the same concession stated from the other side. What remains is a dispute about how to use the word 'law,' which is not a disagreement about anything."
