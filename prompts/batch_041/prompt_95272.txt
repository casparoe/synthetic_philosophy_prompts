Every year I teach the last chunk of Hofstadter's GEB and every year the seminar splits down the middle in exactly the same place, and I've gotten nowhere in fifteen years of arguing about it with the logician down the hall and the philosophy of mind person two doors over, so what I want from you is a diagnosis, not a verdict.

Here's the shape of it. The book builds a line out of Gödel: a formal system can be rigged to talk about itself, and Hofstadter says the self, or self-awareness, is that kind of thing, a loop that crosses levels and refers back to itself. The anti-mechanist crowd (Lucas back in the day, Penrose more recently) reads the same theorem the other way: no formal system can capture the mathematician, because the mathematician can see the truth of the sentence the system can't prove. And Hofstadter, as far as I can tell, draws pretty much the opposite moral from the very same theorem, that it shows how a self could come out of a machine rather than why it couldn't. So we have one theorem, two morals, and everyone dug in.

My hypothesis, and I'd like you to push on it rather than agree with it: the two sides aren't answering the same question. One side is asking a technical question, can this formal system prove this sentence, and the other is asking a metaphysical one, whether a mind could be a formal system at all, and the bridge between them is the analogy from self-reference to self-awareness, which I've never seen anybody actually argue for, they just assert it and move on. There's also a move my grad students keep making that I cant decide is deep or empty: the mechanist says the machine doesn't have to prove its own Gödel sentence, it just has to be the kind of thing that does what we do when we see the sentence is true; the anti-mechanist says that's a verbal trick, that printing a sentence isn't seeing anything. And then there's the consistency premise, since the anti-mechanist argument seems to need us to know we're consistent, and I've never understood how much weight that can bear or whether it's doing all the work.

So what kind of disagreement is this, really? A formal question in disguise, a verbal one about what counts as seeing or knowing, two different questions under one heading, or bedrock intuitions about whether a mind could be a mechanism at all? And given your diagnosis, is there anything that would actually count as progress here, a distinction, a formal result, giving up on the analogy altogether, or should I stop expecting the seminar to converge? Keep the two registers seperate for me: say plainly what is actually settled in the technical literature and what is genuinely contested among people who know this material, and please don't sell me a tidy consensus that isn't there.
