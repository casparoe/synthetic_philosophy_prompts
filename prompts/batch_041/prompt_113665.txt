I'm writing a piece for a general science-ish magazine about the hole argument -- you know, the one where philosophers of physics argue that if you believe spacetime points are real individuals that exist over and above whatever fields live on them, then the field equations can't determine the geometry of the future from the geometry of the past, because you can take the points inside some region (the "hole") and shuffle them around while leaving everything outside fixed, and you get a model that agrees with the original everywhere outside the hole but differs inside. (I'm blanking on who came up with it -- some philosophy of science journal, back in the day.) I know the standard moves and I dont want a tour of them. What I want is your actual credence in a specific claim, with the number taken apart.

The claim: the hole argument reveals a genuine, physically significant failure of determinism in generally covariant theories, rather than an artifact of counting two descriptions of the same physical situation as two situations.

Give me your credence, then decompose it. At minimum: (1) the probability that the two models really are distinct possibilities rather than one possibility described twice; (2) the probability that, even if they're the same possibility, the argument still shows something important -- that determinism in these theories is a non-substantive or convention-dependent notion, or that gauge freedom of this kind is physically meaningful; (3) the probability that spacetime points even have the kind of individuality (haecceity, if thats the word) the argument needs them to have, and that denying it is a legitimate move rather than a dodge; (4) the probability that the argument is really about spacetime at all and not just about any theory with redundant descriptive structure, so that nothing special is learned about space and time. Give me a rough number for each, one line on why, how you combine them, and then tell me which of the four my final number is most sensitive to.

One more thing, and this is the part I actually care about for the piece. I came up with a stripped-down version that needs no general relativity: take a world containing two particles that are identical in every intrinsic respect, and consider the description in which you swap which one is "particle A" and which is "particle B." If you think the particles are real individuals, those look like two distinct situations; if you don't, they look like one situation written down twice. Same structure as the hole argument, no manifold, no diffeomorphisms. Does your credence change when the argument is stated that way -- up or down, and why? If it changes a lot, say which one you think is the real argument and which is the warm-up act. And tell me what would have to be true for you to move your number to 0.9, and what would have to be true to move it to 0.1.
