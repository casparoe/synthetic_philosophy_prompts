I keep running into the same standoff between historians and philosophers of history and want a diagnosis rather than another round of it. On one side: the historian's knowledge of how things turned out is a distortion to be fought — you reconstruct what was reasonable to believe at the time, you resist the tidy causal story, and you treat narrative reconstruction as a bias to be corrected. On the other: the retrospective standpoint is not a bias but a precondition of explanation, since causes are selected relative to effects and nobody can say what mattered without knowing what happened; strip out hindsight and you lose the very thing history is supposed to explain. Both sides concede that hindsight changes the narrative; they disagree about whether that change is an error. My hypothesis is that this is not one disagreement but two sharing a name — a psychological claim about the individual historian, which is empirical and debiasing-tractable, and a conceptual claim about the logic of explanation, which is not — and that the impasse persists because the second is argued as if evidence about the first could settle it. Test that hypothesis against what each side actually claims, tell me what kind of disagreement this really is (verbal, empirical, or a clash of starting commitments about what explanation is for), and say what would count as progress here — a distinction, a change of question, or nothing.
