I'm a backend engineer with no formal training in philosophy, and I've somehow ended up running a small reading group at work where we've been working through David Lewis's account of convention — as I understand it, a regularity in behaviour that everyone in a group conforms to, expects everyone else to conform to, and prefers to conform to given that the others do, where some other regularity would have served just as well and all of this is common knowledge. What I keep tripping over is that the stock example everyone reaches for, namely which side of the road to drive on, turns out to have a real history, and that history doesn't look much like the slow, bottom-up, self-enforcing equilibrium the theory describes. I'd like you to analyse one such episode through the theory and then run the analysis back the other way: what the theory says the actors faced, which features of the episode it captures, which it misses or misdescribes, and what the episode in turn shows about the theory. The episode is Sweden's changeover from left-hand to right-hand road traffic, which took place on Sunday 3 September 1967. Since you can't look any of this up, here are the facts as I have them, and you should treat them as given: left-hand driving had been the Swedish regularity for decades; every country Sweden shares a land border with, including Norway and Finland, drove on the right, with something like five million vehicle crossings a year; more than ninety per cent of the cars on Swedish roads were left-hand-drive, so drivers were sitting on the outside during overtaking; in a 1955 referendum roughly eighty-three per cent voted to keep driving on the left; the Riksdag nonetheless approved the change in 1963 and set up a commission that spent four years on a public education campaign, complete with a campaign emblem, a song contest, reminders printed on milk cartons, and about 350,000 signs replaced or altered; on the day itself non-essential traffic was banned from the roads between 01:00 and 06:00, vehicles still on the road had to stop at 04:50, move to the other side, and wait until 05:00 to proceed; the predicted carnage did not materialise — reports describe 157 minor accidents that day, only thirty-two involving injuries, and a sharp temporary drop in claims that had largely reverted to normal within about six weeks, with accident rates back at their old levels by 1969; trams in central Stockholm and Helsingborg were withdrawn and replaced by buses, over a thousand new buses were bought with doors on the right, and some older buses were retrofitted with doors on both sides; and, finally, the railways and the Stockholm metro did not switch, and went on running on the left.

What I want from the analysis, in roughly this order. First, whether left-hand driving in Sweden satisfied the Lewis conditions before 1967, and what work the word arbitrary is doing here, given that Sweden's neighbours and its own car fleet gave one of the two options a clear practical edge — is this an arbitrary convention, a bad equilibrium, or something the theory has no clean slot for? Second, the mechanism by which the convention actually changed: Lewis's own remarks about how conventions give way to new ones through salient precedent and public signalling, Schelling-style focal points, or plain legislative fiat backed by a state that could manufacture common knowledge and clear the roads for five hours — and which parts of what happened the theory simply cannot represent, such as the cost of the transition, the danger of a population only half-switched, and the fact that simultaneity was the whole point. Third, what the referendum result and the apparent reversal of majority preference within a decade do to Lewis's treatment of preferences as given inputs rather than as things the convention itself shapes. Fourth, what the rail and metro exception tells us about how we are supposed to individuate the group for which a given regularity is a convention, and whether the theory has anything to say about nested or overlapping conventions in one country. Fifth, and most important, your verdict: is this episode a confirmation of the account, an anomaly it can absorb with minor patching, or a case it never anticipated, and where exactly is the theory silent. Please don't hand me a reading list or citations — I'm not going to check them and you can't produce them reliably — and please mark clearly which steps in your argument are Lewis's own, which are standard glosses, and which are your own extrapolation, because I'd rather see the seams than a smooth summary.
