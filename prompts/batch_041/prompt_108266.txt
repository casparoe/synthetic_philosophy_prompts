I teach a comparative epistemology seminar for advanced undergraduates and first-year graduate students, most of whom have had two or three courses in Western epistemology and almost no exposure to Indian philosophy. Next week I have ninety minutes to introduce the Nyāya school's inclusion of śabda (testimony) as a fourth pramāṇa alongside perception, inference, and analogy. The students have just finished Hume on miracles and are, almost without exception, reductionists about testimony: they think any knowledge we get from a speaker's words must ultimately be justified by perception and inference, and they are suspicious of anything that looks like an appeal to authority. They can handle technical distinctions—they have read Gettier and Goldman and can follow a regress argument—but they have no Sanskrit and no familiarity with the commentarial style, and they will default to treating the Nyāya view as a culturally specific curiosity unless the structure of the position is made to feel like a live option. I want them to encounter it as a serious, structurally different answer to a question they already recognize, but I do not want to spend the session on the metaphysics of the self or on the Nyāya–Mīmāṃsā dispute over the authorlessness of the Vedas, which would take us too far afield. Don't explain the Nyāya view to me; plan how I should explain it.

What has to be in place before the claim that testimony is a basic pramāṇa even makes sense to them—what do they need to understand about the Nyāya notion of a pramāṇa, about the distinction between a pramāṇa and the knowledge it produces, and about the role of the āpta (trustworthy speaker) in the account? What single example should carry the whole session, and why that one rather than the usual stock cases (learning a word's meaning, learning that there is water in a distant place, learning from an expert that a medicine works)? What misconceptions will these particular students form—I am guessing they will hear "testimony is a pramāṇa" as "whatever your guru says is true," and that they will assume the Nyāya view makes testimony infallible or immune to defeat—and at exactly what point in the sequence should I head each one off? What question should I ask at the end to see whether they have understood the Nyāya position rather than merely been able to recite it—something that would reveal whether they can state what a reductionist about testimony would have to deny in order to reject the Nyāya claim—and what should I openly refuse to cover in ninety minutes so that the session does not collapse into a survey?
