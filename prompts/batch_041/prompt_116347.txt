Ok so I need a dialogue for a thing I'm putting together, and I want it to get into the weeds in a way that a model can't just phone in from the usual textbook version. The setup: two characters, both sharp and both acting in good faith, arguing about how to represent an uncertain event. Call them Sharp and Interval. They're looking at a bet — someone puts a coin in front of them, a coin pulled from a jar of coins of unknown provenance, and offers even money on heads; they can take it, leave it, or name their own odds. Sharp's line is that every rational agent has a single credence for every event, one number, and the only real question is what that number is. Interval's line is that a rational agent can hold a whole set of credences for the same event, an interval, and that this isn't a cop-out or a confession of ignorance, it's the honest representation of what the evidence actually supports.

Now let them actually fight, and have them answer each others points rather than just take turns. Stuff I want them to hit, in whatever order comes naturally: the betting argument that a set of credences is really just a set of acceptable bets, so if you're not careful you can be exploited no matter which way the coin falls (the Dutch book thing); the comeback that a rational agent can simply decline to bet, or can be imprecise without being incoherent, so the exploit only bites if you insist on having a price for everything; whether the imprecision lives in the agent's head or in the world — i.e. is every interval just ignorance that a better-informed agent would collapse down to a point, or are there genuinely indeterminate events where even an ideal agent is stuck holding a set?; the strange fact that learning something can make your interval wider rather than narrower, which Sharp will find scandalous and Interval will treat as a feature; the 'you've just pushed the problem up a level' objection, since now you need some attitude toward the whole set; and then the decision itself — Sharp gets expected utility off the shelf, but Interval has to say what you actually do, and Sharp should press on whether 'take the option that's best in the worst case' or 'keep every option that's optimal for some member of the set' is a real decision rule or just a way of smuggling the hard choice back in.

Dont let either one be a strawman and don't force them to agree at the end unless it's earned.
