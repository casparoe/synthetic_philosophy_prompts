I am teaching a seminar that reads A Clockwork Orange alongside the situationist critique of virtue ethics, and I want a critical reading of the film, with the novel in view, that takes both the work and the philosophical literature seriously. I know the standard free-will reading — the chaplain's protest that the state has stripped Alex of the capacity to choose, and the suggestion that coerced decency is not decency — and I would rather you got past it than restated it.

The apparatus I want you to work with has two parts. The first is the situationist critique associated with Harman and Doris: the argument, drawn from Milgram's obedience experiments and from the studies in which seminary students hurrying to a lecture step over a man who has collapsed, that behaviour is governed by situational features rather than by broad character traits, and that the globalist conception of character on which virtue ethics depends is empirically unsupported. Doris's fallback is the notion of local traits, dispositions that are reliable only within narrow, situation-specific bounds. The second is the Aristotelian framework: the distinctions among virtue, continence, incontinence, and vice; the requirement that the virtuous action be chosen, for the right reasons, and from a settled state rather than under compulsion; and the claim that virtue is acquired by habituation, that we become just by doing just acts.

I would like you to place Alex on that map before and after the Ludovico technique — asking whether he is vicious beforehand, and whether afterwards he is continent or merely compelled, and whether the film has the resources to mark the difference at all. Ask whether the technique installs a local trait in Doris's sense, and whether that vindicates the situationist (the only reliable mechanism we ever see for producing decent behaviour is an external manipulation of the agent) or the virtue ethicist (what the technique produces is precisely not goodness, so virtue is not the sort of thing that can be installed from outside). Attend to the scenes that carry the argument: the demonstration before the officials, the chaplain's protest, the episode in which Alex is beaten by his former droogs now serving as policemen, and the final hospital scene, where he seems to be restored and the Minister offers him a future. The novel ends otherwise — Alex's appetite for violence peters out as he grows older — and I want you to say whether that ending is the habituation story Aristotle describes, or something short of virtue, or neither. Finally, treat the Ludovico technique as a fictional case of the present-day moral-enhancement proposal, that we might use biomedical means to make people morally better, and say what each camp would conclude about it and whether the film's own sympathies lie with either.

Throughout, separate what is settled in this literature from what is genuinely contested, and do not present a contested position as the consensus. Where the film resists the reading — where it declines to be an illustration of either side — say so plainly. End by saying what the film adds to the debate that argument alone does not.
