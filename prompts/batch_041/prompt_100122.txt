Consider yourself the examiner for a second-year undergraduate module on meaning and mind. I need a graded assessment of one student's answer to a short-answer question. The question carried 25 marks of the coursework total, and I mark on the following scale: 20–25 first-class (accurate, well-organised, critically engaged); 15–19 upper second (largely accurate, with some imprecision); 10–14 lower second (basic grasp, identifiable errors); 5–9 third (substantial misunderstanding); 0–4 fail. I have deliberately not told you my own view of the argument or of this answer, and I do not want you to assume the answer is sound because it reads fluently; I want your independent judgment.

The question set was:

"Consider the following proposal, devised for this examination. A researcher, whom we may call Dr. Neve, coins a term, 'grint'. On the first occasion on which she has a particular sensation she fixes the meaning of 'grint' by attending to that sensation; thereafter she applies 'grint' to a later sensation if and only if the later sensation seems to her to be the same as the first. She maintains that 'grint' is a genuine word with a determinate meaning, though a meaning that only she can grasp.

(a) Explain the objection that Wittgenstein's remarks on private languages raise against this proposal. (b) Assess whether that objection is best understood as a claim about meaning and rule-following or as a claim about the possibility of knowing one's own experiences. In answering, employ the distinction drawn in lectures between semantic privacy (a term that no one else could understand) and epistemic privacy (a term whose referent no one else could know), and say which kind of privacy Wittgenstein's argument is directed against."

The student's answer was:

"The target of Wittgenstein's private language argument is not the existence of sensations but the possibility of a certain kind of language. He does not deny that we have inner experiences; the beetle-in-the-box example makes this clear, since the beetle — the private object — turns out to be irrelevant to the meaning of the word. What the argument challenges is the idea that a word's meaning could be fixed by a private act of ostension.

Dr. Neve's proposal is exactly such an act. She fixes 'grint' by attending to a sensation and then reapplies it from memory. Wittgenstein's objection, developed through the diary example, is that this procedure supplies no criterion of correctness. If nothing can make her application wrong — if its seeming to her that she has got it right is all there is — then 'grint' has no rule governing its use. So 'grint' is not a genuine word: it is at best a signal, without normative status. This is why the private object is irrelevant to meaning — the meaning of a sensation word is not the private object but the role the word plays in public practice.

On the distinction drawn in lectures: Dr. Neve's term is a case of epistemic privacy, not semantic privacy. The difficulty is that no one else can know what 'grint' refers to. Wittgenstein's argument is accordingly aimed at epistemic privacy: it claims that a term whose referent is knowable only to the speaker cannot have a determinate meaning.

The argument is therefore best read as a claim about meaning rather than about self-knowledge. It does not say that Dr. Neve cannot know her own sensations; it says that she cannot give 'grint' a meaning. The reason is that meaning requires public criteria: a claim that cannot in principle be checked by anyone else is not a genuine claim. This is the verificationist element in Wittgenstein's thinking, and it is what does the real work in the argument.

A defender of Dr. Neve might reply that she need not check her application against anything public, because the sensation itself is the criterion. But this is precisely what Wittgenstein denies. The argument is a reductio: assume that a private language is possible, derive that correctness and incorrectness collapse into one another, and conclude that the assumption must be rejected."
