I am a novelist at work on a book about a woman who is a behavioral economist by training and a compulsive dieter by temperament. In one chapter she keeps a ledger in which she records, each evening, the discount rate she would have to apply to tomorrow's satisfaction in order to make tonight's indulgence come out as the rational choice; by morning the number has changed, and she calls it her future-blindness factor. My hope was that this would let me dramatize weakness of will without the old moralizing vocabulary: she is not weak, only time-inconsistent. But a philosopher friend tells me that hyperbolic discounting is the leading naturalistic explanation of akrasia, and now I worry that the friend has dissolved my subject. If akrasia just is the preference reversal that hyperbolic discounting describes—if the woman who stays up late and the woman who resolves at dawn to go to bed early simply have different preferences at different times—then is it not a mistake to say that she acts against her better judgment? She is only acting on the judgment she has at the moment. And if that is so, how can I write a genuine scene of self-betrayal, one in which she does what she knows, all things considered, she should not do, without making her either irrational in a way that no discount curve can capture or, worse, a different person from the one who made the resolution? I would be grateful for a diagnosis of where the confusion lies.
