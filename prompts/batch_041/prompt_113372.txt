At a departmental colloquium, two colleagues fell into an argument about whether a person could have originated from a different sperm and egg. I was a bystander and could not decide who had the better of it. Here is the exchange as best I can reconstruct it.

A: It seems obvious that you could have come from a different sperm and egg. If your parents had conceived a month later, a different sperm would have fertilized the same egg, and the resulting child would have been you—just with a different genotype. The timing of conception is not essential to who you are.

B: No, that child would have been a different person. You are essentially the product of the particular sperm and egg that actually united. Had a different sperm fertilized the egg, that would have been a different individual, not you. Your origin is essential to you.

A: But consider identical twins. They come from the same sperm and egg, yet they are distinct persons. So the particular sperm and egg cannot be what makes you you. If two people can share that origin, then origin does not individuate you.

B: That is a special case involving splitting. The zygote that became you is essentially you; the fact that it can split into two is a further fact about it. But in the ordinary case, the zygote and you are the same entity at different times. If a different sperm had fertilized the egg, there would have been a different zygote, and hence a different person.

A: But what if we imagine a possible world where your parents used IVF and a different sperm fertilized the egg, but the resulting child had all your memories, personality, and life history? Wouldn't that be you? If so, then your origin is not essential.

B: No, that would be a different person who is qualitatively similar to you. You are not just a bundle of memories and traits; you are a particular biological individual with a particular origin. The fact that someone else could have your memories does not make them you.

A: Then what about the intuition that you could have been conceived a day later? If a different sperm had fertilized the egg, you would still have been born to the same parents, in the same family, with the same upbringing. It seems arbitrary to say that the identity of the person depends on which of millions of sperm happened to win the race.

B: It is not arbitrary; it is constitutive. The sperm and egg are not incidental to you; they are the material from which you were formed. That a different sperm would have produced a different person is not a trivial fact about identity; it is the very thing that makes you the individual you are.

Please adjudicate move by move: rate each of the six moves from clearly mistaken to clearly right and briefly justify each rating. Also say what, if anything, both parties are missing.
