Ok so I need help writing a midterm question and I've made this weirdly personal. I'm a TA for an upper-level seminar on medieval philosophy of religion — we do the Islamic and Latin sides, Ghazālī and Aquinas and occasionalism and all that — and the prof just voluntold me to write the midterm essay question on Ghazālī. Normally I'd be fine with that, but I got into a dumb argument at a family dinner last month where my uncle said Ghazālī is the reason the Islamic world "lost science," because he denied that fire burns things, and I realized I couldn't actually say what Ghazālī is and isn't committed to. So now I care alot about getting this right.

What I want is one essay question, 60 minutes, that can't be answered by reciting "Ghazālī says the connection between cause and effect isn't necessary, so God causes everything, so he's an occasionalist, and that's why he thinks miracles are possible." That's the answer everyone gives and it slides right past the interesting parts.

Here's the stuff I want the question to force students to do:

- See that "the connection isn't necessary" and "created things have no causal power whatsoever" are two different claims, and that the argument in the Tahāfut may only establish the first — and maybe only dialectically, as a way of blocking the philosophers' claim that miracles are *impossible*, rather than as Ghazālī's own positive metaphysics.
- Explain how he can deny that fire burns cotton and still treat the natural order as reliable enough to do medicine and induction — I gather he grounds the regularity in God's habit or custom rather than in natures, which is why the "he killed science" thing doesn't obviously follow.
- Deal with the tension with human action: if God is doing everything, where does moral responsibility come from? I know the Ashʿarīs have this acquisition (kasb) business where the power is created in you but you "acquire" the act. I want the question to make students say what work that's supposed to be doing, and whether it actually does any work or is just relabeling.
- Put in play something a person in my reading group told me, which I can't evaluate: that in his later works Ghazālī might not be a full occasionalist at all, and may let created things be real secondary causes in some sense. If that's even half right, students shouldn't be able to just hand in the standard picture.
- Handle the Hume comparison, which everybody reaches for. Make them say where it's illuminating and where it's lazy — I have a half-formed sense that Hume's "custom" lives in us while Ghazālī's lives in God's will, but don't hand them that; make them work it out.

So please write: (1) the question itself, (2) a model answer of about 700 words, and (3) a marking scheme out of 20 marks. For the marking scheme, list the specific moves a top answer has to make and give each a weight, and for each one add a one-line note on how students typically blow it (like "treats the denial of necessity as if it were the denial of efficacy," or "thinks kasb hands the creature real causal power"). The A/B gap should be nameable: B answers get the standard picture right, A answers know which of these claims Ghazālī is and isn't entitled to and why the whole thing is contested.

One last thing, and I mean it: in the model answer, flag the spot where you're most likely to be wrong — which claim a real specialist would push back on — and tell me what would change your mind about it, like what you'd have to find in the texts for the opposite reading to win. I'd rather hand out an answer key that knows where it's exposed than one that pretends the reading is settled.
