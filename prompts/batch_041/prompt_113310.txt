So I'm a third-year and somehow I've ended up as the undergrad rep on our college's Student Organization Judicial Board — it's the student-run body that hears complaints between registered clubs, and 90% of it is room-booking fights and someone's exec team subtweeting someone else's exec team. But we've got a hearing next Wednesday and I have to circulate the written opinion beforehand, and this one has turned into an actual mess. Short version: Club A and Club B agreed to co-host a showcase. Per their own written arrangement, A was going to book the hall and put down the deposit, but only after B confirmed final numbers, because the booking fee scaled with headcount. B sent its numbers nine days after the date they'd agreed on. A never booked anything at all. No hall, no showcase, and B is out about $1,400 in deposits it had already paid to a caterer and a sound tech. B has filed a complaint asking us to order A to reimburse them. A's position is basically: we didn't *do* anything, and your own rules say the board only hears complaints about "actions" of member organizations — a non-booking isn't an action, so you don't even have jurisdiction; and even if you did, we didn't cause B's loss, because nothing happened in that hall.

That last bit is where I'm stuck. I'm taking a seminar on causation and responsibility this term and we spent a whole week on whether omissions can be causes at all, and now I can't tell if our chair is right. She's a philosophy grad student and she keeps saying an omission is just the absence of a cause, not a cause, and that if we rule against A we're punishing them for something that didn't happen. The other member thinks that's obviously wrong, because if A had booked, B would still have its money, so A's not-booking made the difference. My suspicion is that they're both half-right and that the actually hard part is which of the two non-actions you select as "the" cause when they interlock like this — B's late numbers and A's non-booking each seem to depend on the other — and separately whether our jurisdiction clause can reach omissions at all, or whether the complaint has to fail on that ground no matter what we think about causation.

Can you draft the opinion I'll circulate? It needs a real bottom line — do we have jurisdiction, is A responsible, and if so what's the remedy and by when — with the reasoning spelled out, and it should take A's strongest argument seriously instead of just asserting that inaction can be a cause. If the honest answer is that our rules don't reach this, say that and tell me what I should recommend instead (the board can refer things to the Dean of Students, and I'd rather do that than stretch the rules). And if you can, tell me how much of this is a genuine philosophical dispute versus us just needing to fix the wording of the rules, because I have to explain this to people who definately don't want a metaphysics lecture and I don't want to recieve one either.
