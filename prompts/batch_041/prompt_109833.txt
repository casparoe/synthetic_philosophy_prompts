I'm a math grad student who's been sitting in on the set theory seminar, and something about the everyday practice is bugging me. The standard move is: prove CH is independent of ZFC, then go on using CH as an assumption in some theorems and ¬CH in others, and nobody in the room treats one of those as being about the "real" universe and the other as being about a fake one. Then some of the same people will say that a forcing axiom like Martin's Maximum -- the kind that settles the continuum at ℵ2 -- is "true," and they'll talk as if the independence result just means we need better axioms. Other people say there's no fact of the matter and we should study all the models. The seminar leader says that's philosophy, not math, and we should get back to teh proof.

I want you to act as a philosopher of mathematics who actually knows set theory. Take a breath and think it through carefully. Start by brainstorming the different things "CH is independent" could mean and the different views of set-theoretic truth that are on the table. Then give me the philosophical anatomy of the practice: what does the routine of proving theorems from CH and from ¬CH, and of treating forcing axioms as candidate truths, presuppose about mathematical existence, truth, and what set theory is about? Does it presuppose a unique intended universe, a multiverse, some kind of structuralism, or is it underdetermined so that several views fit equally well? Which rival views does the practice quietly rule out? And if you swapped the presupposed view for a flat-footed formalism, or for a full-on multiverse view where every model is equally legitimate, what would have to change in the practice -- what would set theorists have to stop saying or start doing? No bibliography, no "so-and-so argued" -- just the commitments and the residue. Also be honest about what the practice strictly requires versus what it just happens to be paired with.
