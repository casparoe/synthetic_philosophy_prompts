I've been going down a Molinism rabbit hole lately, mostly because a friend in my reading group keeps insisting that middle knowledge is the only way to hold onto both serious divine providence and libertarian free will at the same time — the picture being that before God decrees anything at all, there's already a fact of the matter about what each possible free creature would freely do in each possible set of circumstances, and God consults those counterfactuals like a lookup table when deciding which world to actualize. I'm sympathetic, but I want to hold the view honestly instead of in the usual defensive crouch, so please play it straight with me: what would actually count against it? I mean the real defeaters, not the ones Molinists trot out to knock down in a paragraph — which arguments, formal results, intuitions, or possible cases would genuinely count against middle knowledge, sorted from fatal to merely awkward, and which of those would a committed Molinist themselves concede as counting against them rather than dismissing as a misunderstanding?

Here's the wrinkle, and it's why I'm asking rather than just googling the standard debate: the version I actually find most plausible isn't the full-strength one. I suspect God might only have middle knowledge for circumstances that are genuinely realizable given the rest of the creative plan — a kind of partial or feasible-set middle knowledge, where there are true counterfactuals of freedom about me in the situations I could actually end up in, but maybe not about every possible creature in every possible situation, and where the truthmakers for those counterfactuals aren't the bare creaturely essences but something about the whole package, essence plus the world God is building. So I'd like the analysis aimed at that modified view where it differs from stock Molinism, and I want to know whether the modifications buy anything or just relocate the problem. The thing that would move me most personally is a showing that counterfactuals of freedom aren't merely unexplained but actually incoherent — that what a free creature would do in unrealized circumstances has no truth value in the relevant sense at all, so the whole apparatus is empty rather than just unsupported.

I've read enough rationalist blogs to know that no possible observation counting against a view is supposed to be a red flag, but I also think there's a difference between a metaphysical claim being unfalsifiable and being unfalsifiable in a way that actually matters, so give me an honest read on whether middle knowledge has been insulated by its defenders into something that can't lose, and if so whether that's a real problem for it or just the ordinary condition of doing metaphysics. And if some of the escape routes are only logically possible rather than live, say so — I don't want a list of ways the view could technically survive, I want to know how exposed it really is.
