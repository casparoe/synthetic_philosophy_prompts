I have somehow become the volunteer bookkeeper for a credit club that about forty households in my neighborhood have been running for three years. Members earn credits by doing things for one another — an hour of childcare, a lift to the airport, help moving a sofa — and spend them later; the whole ledger is a spreadsheet I maintain, and a handful of local businesses have lately begun accepting credits as well, at a fixed one-to-one rate against the dollar. We hold $600 in a real bank account and have about 4,100 credits outstanding. Two things have now landed on me at once. A member who is moving across the country wants me to pay out her 240-credit balance in dollars, at par, and our rules say nothing about it; and I have discovered that some members have quietly been selling their credits to newcomers for cash at roughly eighty cents on the dollar, which nobody had thought to mention. When I raised the payout question at last week's meeting the room split in a way I did not expect. One member, an accountant, said the credits are not money at all — they are favors with bookkeeping attached, a device for keeping reciprocity honest, and handing over dollars for them would be a gift rather than a redemption; you cannot pay your rent or your taxes with them, and nobody outside four streets accepts them. Another member, who spent a career at a bank, said the reverse: the credits are money in every sense that matters, the dollar is no better backed than our spreadsheet is, and refusing to honor a balance at par would be plain expropriation. I cannot tell whether the two of them are disagreeing about a fact or about what the word "money" is for.

A friend who teaches philosophy, when I described all this, said that when people insist that something is "not real" they are usually running together at least three different complaints, and that the argument comes out differently depending on which one is meant. One complaint is that the thing exists, or has the boundaries it has, only because we keep it up, the way a border does — real, but on our account. Another is that the classification gets under the skin of the classified: people become the way they are partly because they get sorted that way. A third is that our way of carving things up serves somebody's interest rather than tracking a division that was already there. She added that in cases like ours there is usually a circular-looking structure underneath — the credits count as money because we treat them as money, and we treat them as money because they count as money — which makes it sound as though a community could simply decide such a thing by vote, and equally as though no vote could ever make it so.

Here is what I want from you, and I would rather have a verdict than a tour of the options. Tell me whether our scrip is money, and which of those three complaints is actually doing the work in the fight between my accountant and my ex-banker. Tell me whether the question they are fighting about has an answer that we could get wrong — something about the credits that holds regardless of what we decide — or whether it is the sort of question a community settles by deciding, in which case tell me what the decision ought to rest on. Then tell me what to do on Thursday: whether to pay out the 240 credits at par, and whether the eighty-cent trading is evidence about what our credits really are or merely a symptom of something else. Take a position and defend it; if you think one of my two colleagues is simply confused, say which one, and why.
