I'm writing the framing chapter of a dissertation on democratic inclusion, and I've decided to stop reading further until I have a specification of my own in hand. So before I take up any more theories of who belongs to the demos, write me the desiderata: what any adequate theory of democratic membership has to deliver. I want the cases it must get right, the distinctions it must not collapse, and the constraints it must be consistent with, each with the consideration behind it stated in a sentence or two, and each marked as either a genuine requirement on any theory or a preference of one camp. I don't need citations or a reading list; I need the criteria. The purpose I'm writing for is a theory that can do two jobs at once — justify a boundary in principle, and be applied by an institution that cannot simply take the demos as given. I have in mind a municipal charter commission deciding whether to extend local voting rights to non-citizen residents, and a river-basin authority whose decisions fall on people living in more than one state.

The cases I keep running into, and which I would want the list to handle explicitly, are these: long-term residents who are subject to every local law but hold foreign citizenship; citizens who have lived abroad for decades, are still taxed and liable for service at home, and vote there; children, and adults under guardianship; people in a neighbouring country who will be harmed by a dam or by emissions, where no common state exists; people not yet born who will have to live with a decision about long-lived infrastructure; a small community that declares independence and proposes to decide its own membership, so that the demos which would settle the boundary is itself the demos whose boundary is in question; and people affected by a decision only through a market, such as workers whose wages are set by a firm whose owners and customers are elsewhere. I also want the distinction between membership in the demos and mere subjection to its decisions kept intact, and I would like the theory to say something about degrees of inclusion, since I can think of people who have a voice without bearing the law and people who bear the law without a voice. Tell me whether the requirements can be met jointly: my own suspicion is that a demand for a boundary principle owing nothing to any actual decision, together with a demand that the demos be self-determining, cannot both be satisfied, and if that or something near it is right, it is the part of the answer I most want.

Finish with a brief scorecard — how the all-affected-interests family, the subjection-to-coercion family, the historical-association family, and territorial or stakeholder views fare against the list — and tell me which single requirement does the most to exclude candidates.
