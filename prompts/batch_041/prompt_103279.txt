Next month I'll be presenting a short paper at our regional high-school philosophy conference, and I've been told to circulate my thesis to the other delegates a week ahead of time. My club advisor has also arranged for me to rehearse it in front of our school's Ethics and Economics Society, which is a much rougher room, so what I actually need from you is not help writing the paper but a question-and-answer prep sheet for that rehearsal. My thesis, in brief: Parfit's hitchhiker case — you are stranded in the desert, the only passing driver will give you a lift only if you promise to pay her once you reach town, and once you are back in town there is nothing whatsoever to stop you from simply walking away — is nearly always presented as a puzzle about whether it can be rational to make yourself into the kind of person who would keep that promise even when keeping it buys you nothing. I want to argue that this framing is a red herring. Nothing irrational is happening; the case only looks paradoxical if you insist on evaluating a single act in isolation, and the genuinely interesting question is not whether you can bind yourself but whether a disposition to keep such promises can do any work for someone who has already been offered the ride. To sharpen the point I have invented a variant I call the relay case, and I would like the prep sheet to test it hard: a driver gives you a lift on the condition that you, in turn, help the next stranded traveller you are ever in a position to help, with no record kept, nobody watching, and no way for her to learn whether you did — so what you owe is owed to an anonymous future stranger rather than to the person who helped you, and breaking the arrangement wrongs no one in particular. My claim is that the relay case shows the hitchhiker's supposed solution to be a threshold problem rather than a personal one, since a private disposition is useless unless enough other people share it, and that once the beneficiary is anonymous the usual story about the wrongness of breaking a promise loses its grip.

The room I'm rehearsing for is about a dozen students from the economics and debate side, who think in terms of game trees, enforceable contracts, and reputation and will keep insisting that the whole thing is dissolved the moment you can sign something or interact repeatedly; two students from the psychology side, who will treat it as a question about willpower and self-control and will want to know why my normative claim is anything more than a description of how people happen to behave; and my club advisor, who teaches the ethics elective, is a Kantian, and will object that I have turned the promise into a mere instrument and that someone who keeps promises only when it suits her has not made a promise at all. Please give me the objections in roughly the order they will actually come and ranked by how much damage they do, each phrased the way that particular person would phrase it, each with a reply I could deliver in about ninety seconds and an honest note on where that reply is soft; tell me what I should concede up front and what I should hold firm on; and finish with the one question I am quietly hoping nobody asks. Bear in mind that neither I nor anyone in that room has any real training in academic philosophy, so keep the vocabulary to what a bright high schooler would already know, and don't cite any books or articles — I won't have anything to look them up with while I'm standing there.
