Question (10 points)

Hofstadter's careenium: a frictionless surface with a huge number of tiny marbles careening around on it. The marbles stick to one another in transient clumps, and the clumps that recur reliably are what he calls simmballs. The walls can be wired up so they're sensitive to the outside world, and in the extended version the simmballs can also drive effects outside the careenium. Hofstadter asks who pushes whom around inside the careenium. His answer, as we discussed in lecture, has two parts: (1) the simmball level is not a mere shorthand for the sim level -- when we explain what the system perceives, wants, and does, it's simmballs that are doing the pushing; and (2) saying this does not commit him to a second kind of causation running alongside the sim-level dynamics. Explain the setup, explain what work part (1) is doing, and explain how (2) is supposed to be consistent with (1).

Student's answer

The careenium is a frictionless billiard table with an enormous number of tiny magnetic marbles on it. The marbles -- Hofstadter calls them sims -- bounce off each other and off the walls, and because they're magnetic they clump together into groups of various sizes and stabilities. The groups that recur reliably are the simmballs. Since the walls can be made sensitive to what's going on outside the careenium, information from the environment gets into the marble dynamics, and the simmball patterns end up tracking features of the world. In the extended version, simmballs can also drive things outside the careenium, so the whole thing looks like it perceives and acts.

On part (1): Hofstadter's point is that you can't explain the system's behaviour purely in terms of individual marbles. If you want to know why the careenium turned left, the answer is going to be something like 'because it wanted the food', and that's a simmball-level fact, not a sim-level fact. Sim-level descriptions are true, but they're the wrong grain for explaining behaviour. So simmball talk isn't a convenience; it's the level at which the system's behaviour is actually intelligible.

On part (2): Hofstadter makes this consistent by saying the simmball level is a genuine additional causal level. If simmballs really push sims around, then there has to be causal influence from the higher level down to the lower one, which means the sim-level dynamics can't be causally closed all by themselves. So he's committed to downward causation: the simmball patterns exert a top-down force on the sims that make them up. That's why he thinks the analogy matters for the free will debate -- it shows the mental isn't just along for the ride.

This is also where Hofstadter parts ways with Dennett. Dennett's intentional stance says we can treat a system as if it had beliefs and desires, but that's an instrumental convenience; Hofstadter takes the further step of saying the simmball level is literally causally efficacious. He inherits the careenium itself from Dennett and extends it with the strange loop.
