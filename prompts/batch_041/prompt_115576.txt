You're a philosopher-historian with real expertise in the theory of moral change — the literature on how whole societies come to abandon practices they'd taken for granted for centuries. Think carefully and slowly here, and start by brainstorming before you commit, because what I'm actualy after is a judgement call and I'd rather watch you reason your way to it than get a list.

Context: I'm a philosophy professor. I've got a small pot of money for a three-year project — me plus two postdocs, one of whom is really a historian and the other works on ethics — and the topic is how moral revolutions happen. The framing everyone reaches for is: did slavery and dueling and footbinding end because the arguments finally won, or because interests shifted, or because of honor — i.e. someone managed to make the practice shameful to the very people who practiced it? You know the Williams grumble, that the case against slavery had been sitting there for a century and a half and didn't do the work; I can never remember which book he puts it in, so dont lean on that, but you get the target. Appiah's honor story is the other pole.

I'm not interested in a survey of that debate, and please don't hand me a reading list or a bibliography — I know the literature roughly and I can't use one. What I want is an agenda: which open questions in this area most deserve three years and two postdocs, ranked, with the ranking defended.

Some hunches of mine to use, attack, or ignore:

- We've got a selection problem the size of a house. Everyone studies the revolutions that happened. Nobody studies the campaigns that failed, or the practices that are still ticking along. If the question is what makes the difference, the failures are the whole ballgame — and I suspect they're also where the methodology falls apart (how do you count a failed campaign? when is a norm "still going" as opposed to "on the way out"?).
- I keep thinking there's a replacement condition nobody states cleanly. You can't end a practice until there's something to put in its place — you need wage labour before you can end slave labour, some legal remedy before the duel stops looking like the only recourse for an insult, some other way of being a respectable woman before footbinding goes. Is that a real mechanism or a truism people smuggle into their narratives?
- Related: the practices that fall are often ones that constituted the identity of the people who practiced them — the duel was part of what it was to be a gentleman. So the mechanism can't just be "make it shameful," you have to hand the practitioner a new way of being what they already are, or the shame has nowhere to land. Is that distinct from the honor story or just a version of it?
- Everyone says "argument" as if that's one thing. Rhetoric aimed at the uncommitted, arguments aimed at the perpetrators, arguments aimed at third parties who then impose costs on the perpetrators — those are different mechanisms, and I suspect the literature treats them as one.

So: which of these questions (mine, yours, or something I'm not seeing) are upstream of the others, such that answering them would make the rest easier or moot? Which are tractable for a small team in three years and which would want a historian's decade? Which look important but wouldn't change anything downstream — the kind of thing that gets a good chapter and then nobody uses? How much of each is philosophy and how much is empirical history I'd be subcontracting? And what should I drop to make room for whatever you'd add?

Give me a defended ordering, not a menu. Say what the criteria are, where they trade off against each other, and what evidence over the next couple of years would move something up or down the list. Finish with the one you'd bet the three years on, and say why that one and not the runner-up.
