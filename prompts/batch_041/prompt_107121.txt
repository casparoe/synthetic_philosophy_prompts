I run trust and safety policy at a mid-size social platform, which basically means I'm the person who decides what stays up and comes down in about forty countries. My whole mental toolkit is: one global rulebook versus per-community rules, carve-outs, appeal queues, context escalations, and the endless fight between applying one standard everywhere and letting communities set their own norms inside hard limits. I know that world cold. I have zero formal philosophy training and I get lost the second someone starts name-dropping -isms.

I keep being told that the big fight between French laïcité and Anglo-American multiculturalism is really my fight, and I want you to actually do the translation instead of giving me a vibe. Take the two positions — the French republican line that the public sphere should be a single neutral space where people show up stripped of their particular identities, and the multiculturalist line that neutral rules quietly encode the majority's culture and that minorities need recognition and accommodation — and restate each one in my vocabulary as faithfully as you can. Where a policy concept does the same work, say so plainly. Where you have to bend my vocabulary to the point of breaking to make something fit, say that instead of fudging it. I've also heard there's an internal split on the French side between a softer, live-and-let-live version and a more combative version that treats the neutral space as something to be actively defended — if that maps onto anything I'd recognise (say, a permissive moderation philosophy versus a zero-tolerance one), tell me which parts map and which don't.

Then the part I actually care about: the residue report. What in this debate has no counterpart in how a platform writes and enforces policy? And what do I have that the philosophers don't — the fact that I can measure outcomes, that my rules get executed by code rather than by courts, that my users can leave, that I answer to regulators in one country and civil-liberties groups in another? For each gap, tell me whether my framework is the one missing something or whether the philosophers' dispute was doing less work than it sounds. And answer the concrete thing that's been bugging me: our policy team just spent two weeks arguing about whether to ban religious symbols in profile pictures, and my instinct was that a single neutral global rulebook is obviously the fair option. Is that instinct just laïcité wearing a hoodie? If so, what is laïcité committed to that I'm not, and does the gap matter?
