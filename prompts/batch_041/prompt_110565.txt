I am a high school junior and the student representative on my school's Honor Council, which hears academic-integrity and conduct cases and decides what sanctions to impose. Over the past two years the Council has decided a number of cases, and I have begun to suspect that we do not actually know what we are doing. Here is our record as I remember it.

- A student copied from a neighbor during a final exam and was caught by the proctor in front of the whole room. One member argued for suspension, but we settled on a zero on the exam and a week of detention.
- A student was accused of stealing a watch from the lost-and-found, but we concluded that another student had slipped it into his backpack as a prank and that he never intended to keep it. No sanction.
- A student ran a group chat in which she and several others mocked a classmate for months. We removed her from her student-council position, required a written apology, and referred her to counseling.
- A student's essay contained several paragraphs lifted from a website, but we determined that her tutor had inserted them without telling her. We required her to rewrite the essay and entered nothing on her record.
- A student accidentally started a small fire in the chemistry lab by ignoring a safety instruction he had been given twice; nobody was hurt. He lost his lab privileges for the semester and had to attend a safety session.

I have noticed something about these five cases. In every one of them, either the student plainly deserved to be punished and punishing him or her also seemed to accomplish something — the offense was known, or a victim needed protection, or the student was likely to do it again — or else the student did not deserve it and punishing would have accomplished nothing. So I honestly cannot tell whether we punish people because they deserve it, because it works, or because of both.

Next week we hear a case I keep going back and forth on. A senior submitted a research paper with several paragraphs copied from an online source. Her teacher had not noticed anything. She came forward on her own, is visibly ashamed, has already rewritten the paper without being asked, has no prior record, and has asked that the matter be kept private. If we impose a sanction, it will not stay private — our decisions are summarized in the student newspaper, and she would have to explain an unexplained penalty to her family and to colleges — and because no one else knows what she did, punishing her would not deter anyone. I do not believe she would ever do it again.

What should we do if we want to be consistent with ourselves? I would like you to lay out the different principles our five earlier decisions could be following, say what each one predicts about this case, tell me which principle you think we have actually been following and what we should therefore decide, and identify which of our past cases is the closest analog. I also want to know what we would be committing ourselves to either way, and what kind of future case would force us to choose between the principles rather than letting us hide behind cases where the principles happen to agree.
