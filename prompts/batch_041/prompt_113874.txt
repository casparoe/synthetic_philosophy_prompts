I've fallen down the norms-of-assertion rabbit hole — the arguement about what rule actually governs just flat-out saying something: is it only ok to assert p if you know p, or only if p is true, or only if you believe it, or maybe there's no single rule at all — and I want to write a parody of it. Setting: a government regulator, the Bureau of Assertoric Standards, which has been enforcing the knowledge rule for decades and is now issuing a compliance bulletin after a botched reform. Deadpan regulator-ese, in-universe, no winking, and the jokes have to come from getting the epistemology right — someone who's been in this literature should laugh rather than wince. The standard objections to the knowledge rule should be visible in the mechanics of the policy and never stated as objections.

Stuff to weave in so it isn't just the textbook version: the Bureau can never actually inspect anyone's knowledge, so it needs a safe harbour keyed to what the evidence supported at the time; the law keeps having to decide whether an utterance that fails the standard is a violation or simply isn't an assertion at all, which forces a category of "assertion-like emissions" that falls outside its jurisdiction; and because what counts as knowing shifts with the standards in play in whatever room the utterance gets assessed in, the same sentence is compliant in one hearing room and a violation in the next, which nobody at the Bureau will admit on the record. Also work in the schedule of low-probability claims, and the list of prohibited patterns like "p, but I don't know that p." The reform itself split the rule into a correctness standard and a permissibility standard, so a statement can be fully in the clear on one and still get you fined on the other — use that as the frame rather than spelling it out.

Give me exactly five numbered clauses, each a sentence or two, with no preamble, no sign-off, and no explanatory notes.
