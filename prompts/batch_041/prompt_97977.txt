I'm a third-year philosophy student currently taking a course on the philosophy of science, and we have just finished the unit on the demarcation problem. What has been bothering me is that every criterion we have read seems to be knocked down with a single counterexample — a case where the criterion throws out something we want to keep as science, or admits something we want to expel — and I have come to suspect that this style of argument is concealing the fact that nobody has actually written down the job description. Before I read any more Popper, Lakatos, Kuhn, or Laudan, I want the specification rather than another contender. So please write the desiderata: what would any adequate criterion of demarcation have to deliver?

For each requirement, give me the case or consideration that motivates it — the phenomena it must accommodate and the distinctions it must respect. A few things I would like to see covered, though you should not treat my list as exhaustive: it has to sort the clear historical cases correctly, and I have in mind both textbook successes and the messier episodes — Einstein's general relativity, Blondlot's N-rays and the laboratory apparatus that seemed to detect them, the cold-fusion announcement, Weber's gravitational-wave claims, creation science in the courtroom — as well as cases where the verdict looks different in hindsight than it did to contemporaries. It has to say what the criterion is a property of: a single claim, a theory, a research programme, a method, or a community. It has to say whether the criterion must be applicable in real time by an outsider or only retrospectively by a historian. It must not collapse the distinction between a claim's being unscientific and its being false, irrational, or useless — much good science was unfalsified or even unfalsifiable at the moment it was proposed, and plenty of straightforwardly falsifiable claims are nonsense. And it must be usable for the purposes people actually want it for, such as deciding what belongs in a science classroom, what deserves funding, and what a court may admit, without smuggling a political preference in under the guise of an epistemic verdict.

Then tell me which of the requirements are genuinely non-negotiable and which are merely the prejudices of one camp — I would especially like to know whether the demand that a criterion be applicable by an outsider in real time is even coherent, or whether it quietly requires more than any epistemic criterion could supply. If some subset of the requirements turns out to be jointly unsatisfiable, say so explicitly, name the conflicting pair, and explain why they conflict; my own suspicion is that the real-time-applicability requirement and the verdict-on-the-hard-cases requirement cannot both be met, and I think that negative result would be the most valuable thing I could take away from this exercise. Finally, score the main contenders briefly against the finished list — naive falsificationism and Popper's more sophisticated version, the verificationist and inductivist criteria, Lakatos's methodology of research programmes, Kuhn's paradigm-based account, Thagard's multi-criteria proposal, and Laudan's argument that the whole problem is a pseudo-problem. Keep the scoring short; the criteria are what I need, and I would like you to flag which single requirement does the most work in eliminating candidates.
