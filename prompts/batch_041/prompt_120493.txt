I taught a graduate seminar on probability last term in which we reconstructed an argument to the effect that the reference class problem is not a nuisance to be managed but a proof that single-case probability judgments are never warranted. I set it out below as the seminar finally settled it, and my own diagnosis has never satisfied me. (1) Any warranted judgment of the form "this case has probability p of outcome O" must rest on a frequency, namely the proportion of O among the members of some reference class to which the case belongs. (2) Every case belongs to indefinitely many reference classes, and for any number between zero and one we can construct a class — tosses of coins that have come up heads ten times running, patients of this surgeon on Fridays, buses belonging to the company on this route — whose observed frequency is that number. (3) A frequency-based judgment is warranted only if there is a principled basis for preferring the chosen class to its rivals. (4) The only principled basis available is to take the narrowest class for which we have reliable frequency data, since its members are the ones that most closely resemble the case at hand. (5) But which class is narrowest depends on which data we happen to have collected; had we collected finer data the answer would have been different, so the preference tracks our ignorance rather than the world. (6) Therefore no single-case probability judgment is warranted. (7) This is not an idle result: it would mean that a physician cannot tell a patient her risk of recurrence, an insurer cannot price a policy, and a court cannot treat as evidence the fact that the defendant's bus company owns most of the buses running on the route in question. Since (7) is absurd, something in (1) through (6) has to go.

I would sooner keep (1) and (7) than keep (4), but I do not want that preference to do the work; if it forces me to give up more than I realize, I would rather know now. So tell me which step you reject and what rejecting it costs. If the flaw is that a step is ambiguous or slides from one claim to another, say exactly where the slide happens and why the step looked innocent; if the argument is valid and the real work is deciding which plausible premise is least costly to abandon, say which one and what has to be rebuilt without it. Note what the argument would license if we simply accepted a restricted version of (6) instead of rejecting any step outright, and be explicit about what survives of the physician, the insurer, and the court in each case. You may invoke whatever positions in the philosophy of probability you find relevant, but I want the diagnosis argued rather than labeled, and I want a committed answer: choose, defend the choice, and tell me what you are giving up by choosing it, rather than laying out the menu of options and leaving the selection to me. Please write in continuous prose, with no headings, numbered lists, bullet points, bold text, or tables — the way you would write a letter to a colleague.
