I'm giving the commencement address at a small college's 150th-anniversary graduation — and, as the trustees have just announced, its last, since the college will close. I want to build the address around Samuel Scheffler's thought experiment in which you learn that humanity will cease to exist shortly after your own death, and much of what you now value seems to drain of point. But I don't want to simply retail the conjecture. My own twist, which I'd like the address to argue for rather than merely mention, is that what does the work is not the survival of the species but the survival of a practice of valuing: a tradition can play the role Scheffler assigns to humanity, which is why a closing college can grieve its own future and not merely its students'. The audience is mixed — families and trustees who have never heard of the conjecture, and a philosophy department that will catch any fudge. Write the ten-minute address. It should land for the non-philosophers without saying anything the philosophers would call false, and the distinction between species and practice should shape what the graduates can be asked to care about rather than sit in the speech as decoration. Please write in American English.
