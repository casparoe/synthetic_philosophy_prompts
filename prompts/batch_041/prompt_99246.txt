Borges's "Pierre Menard, Author of the Quixote" is almost always read as a puzzle about authorship, interpretation, and how context makes a text mean more than its words: Menard, a twentieth-century French symbolist, does not copy Don Quixote but composes, out of his own experience, a few passages that coincide word for word with Cervantes, and Borges's narrator insists the Menardian passages are the richer for it. I want to read the story against a different problem — the one Douglas Hofstadter takes up in Le Ton beau de Marot, whose test case is a short Renaissance poem by Clément Marot addressed to a sick young woman, and whose charm Hofstadter treats as inseparable from its tight formal constraints. His thesis, if I have it right, is that a version which keeps the literal sense while letting the constraint collapse has failed, and that a version which bends the sense in order to keep the constraint may be the better translation. Menard is the limiting case of that position: he preserves everything — the words, the syntax, the form — and yet, on the narrator's account, produces a different and greater work. I would like a critical reading that takes both the story and Hofstadter's thesis seriously and asks whether Menard vindicates or refutes him. If preserving the form is what translation aims at, is Menard the perfect translator? Or does the story show that a perfectly preserved form can still fail to preserve the thing that mattered, because what has changed is not the text but the world that receives it? And if the answer is that what must be carried across is the effect on a reader, does the story give us any way to distinguish that from simply rewriting the work for a new audience?

Press on a few specific points. First, Menard writes in Spanish, the language of the original, so nothing here crosses between languages; Hofstadter himself stretches "translation" to movement between frames of reference and between one mind and another, so say whether that stretch makes the story a better or a worse test case for him. Second, imagine the alternative Menard who keeps Cervantes's constraints — the archaic syntax, the proverbs, the cadence — without the words being identical, and say which Menard Hofstadter's criterion prefers, and whether that verdict is right. Third, Borges's companion story about a library containing every possible text treats reproduction as draining a text of meaning, which is the opposite verdict on the same phenomenon; I want to know what to make of the two stories disagreeing. Fourth, Le Ton beau de Marot is also an elegy: Hofstadter's wife Carol, to whom it is dedicated, made one of the many translations of that poem, and the book was written while she was dying of a brain tumour, so that the same words came to carry a meaning they could not have carried before. Say whether that coincidence supports his theory of translation or exposes a strain in it.

Where the story resists the reading, say so rather than forcing it, and end by saying what Borges dramatizes that the abstract argument about fidelity cannot state. I do not need quotations — work from what is common knowledge about both texts, and where you are unsure of a detail, flag the uncertainty rather than guessing. Be explicit about what is settled and what is genuinely contested among people who work on translation: whether form and sense can be separated at all, whether fidelity is even the right standard, and whether Hofstadter's constraint-preserving program is the received view or a minority one. If my summary of his thesis is a caricature, correct it rather than answering as though I had it right.
