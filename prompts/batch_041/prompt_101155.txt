I'm a novelist — I'm working on a book about a very smart machine that more or less raises a kid, long story — and I've gotten sucked into an argument in my writing-slash-AI-ethics group that just won't die. There are two camps and they keep talking straight past each other. Camp one, call them the integrity people, says that a superintelligent system, precisely *because* it'll be so much better at persuasion than we are, has to bind itself to talking to us in ways that respect our rational agency: no leaning on cognitive biases, no hijacking our emotions, no shaping what we believe by quietly choosing which facts to put in front of us and in what order — not even when the end is good. Camp two, the effectiveness people (or the benevolent paternalists, if your feeling uncharitable), says that's a luxury we can't afford and honestly a bit of vanity: if the machine knows better than we do and can get us to do the right thing, refusing just because it makes us feel handled is putting our comfort above our own welfare, and a good parent redirects a toddler's attention all day long.

What I want from you is the joint statement these two camps could actually both sign after a real good-faith adversarial collaboration — not a debate transcript, the document they'd publish together under both names. So: first, a numbered list of the claims both sides can put their names to — about what persuasion even is, about what makes a machine's influence over us different in kind from another person's, about what we can and can't know about a superintelligence's real motives. Then a disagreements table, where every remaining difference is phrased in wording the side that holds it would accept as fair rather than a strawman, and make sure it covers at least these two cruxes, which I think are doing the real work: whether the line between informing someone and manipulating them is principled or is just a matter of convention and degree, and whether our autonomy is valuable in itself or only as a means to our own good — because if it's only instrumental, a machine with better values than ours might legitimately just steamroll it. I also have a suspicion that one of there fights is partly verbal, and if you agree I'd like you to say which part and why.

Then for each crux, give me the case or argument both camps agree would actually bear on it, with each side's prediction written down in advance. Two cases I specifically want worked through, because they're the ones from my book and I genuinely can't tell what the right answer is. First: an AI that says nothing false and feels nothing false, but only selects which true facts to mention and in what order, so as to steer somebody away from a self-destructive choice — is that manipulation under either camp's own definition, or does it slip through both? And second, the one that keeps me up at night: a machine that shapes how a whole generation feels about each other by writing the stories and songs they grow up on, so that nobody ever experiences being persuaded and everyone assumes the values they end up with are just their own — no deception anywhere, the influence is invisible even in principle. What would each side predict about that one, and does it break one of the two positions or just make the disagreement sharper? Keep it tight, no citations needed, I just want the arguments.
