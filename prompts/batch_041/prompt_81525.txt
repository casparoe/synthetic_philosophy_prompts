I am defending a paper at a departmental colloquium next month and need a Q&A prep sheet. The paper argues that the question whether a person survives the death of their body is not one the empirical facts could settle — not because the evidence is hard to obtain, but because our criteria of personal identity are not discovered in the facts and are not uniquely determined by them. Fix every fact about what experiences occur after a body stops, and you have still not fixed whether the subject of those experiences is the person who died; that further step is taken by a criterion, and the criterion is something we settle rather than find. I take this to be the defensible core of Ayer's position on survival, and I want to argue that his verificationist treatment of afterlife talk, read charitably, amounts to this point rather than to the cruder claim that such talk is gibberish. As a corollary I claim that the near-death episode Ayer reported in 1988 — the painful red light governing the universe, the two creatures charged with keeping space in order, his walking up and down waving his watch to attract the attention of the ministers of time — is evidentially inert with respect to the question of survival, though the wavering he reported afterwards, which he characterised as a softening not of the belief itself but of his inflexibility about it, is philosophically interesting for a different reason.

The audience: the department is mostly analytic philosophers of mind, several of whom work on personal identity in a Parfitian vein and are reductionists about the self; two historians of analytic philosophy who know Ayer's texts closely and are suspicious of charitable reconstructions; one philosopher of religion; and a neurologist who studies near-death experiences and thinks the empirical literature has been unfairly waved away by philosophers.

Prepare the objections I will actually get from this room, six to eight of them, ordered by how much damage they would do, phrased in the vocabulary each objector would use — the Parfitians in terms of psychological continuity and what matters, the historians in terms of what Ayer actually wrote and when, the philosopher of religion in terms of hope and the grammar of religious claims, the neurologist in terms of the timing of retrospective reports and the evidence from cardiac arrest. I want at least the following: the objection that I have conflated the question of identity with the question of what matters, and that reductionism properly understood makes my criterion-choice point either trivial or false; the objection that I am reading a later conventionalism back into a text whose point was semantic, and that Ayer's own discussion of survival is more empirically minded than I allow; the objection that if criteria are chosen then survival becomes stipulable, so that immortality is secured by fiat and the hope of surviving becomes a decision rather than a discovery, which is either absurd or a change of subject; and the objection that I have insulated the question from evidence, since the near-death literature is at least some evidence about whether experience can persist when brain function is severely impaired.

For each objection, give me a two-minute reply, then say plainly where the reply is soft — which premise I am leaning on hardest, and what I would have to give up if that premise fails. Add a short note on which objections I should concede in part and which I should refuse to concede at all. Finish with the one question I am hoping nobody in the room asks, why it is the dangerous one, and my least bad answer to it.

Three constraints on the answer. At least one objection must exploit the difference between the claim that there is no fact of the matter about survival and the weaker claim that we could never find out. At least one must press on the difference between the continued existence of a stream of experience and the continued existence of a person. At least one must press on whether the choice of criterion is constrained by anything at all, so that my view does not collapse into the claim that we may say whatever we like about who survives — and, relatedly, whether I am committed to the question of survival having different answers in different registers, legal, moral, biological, narrative, and if so which register answers the question as asked. Do not cite literature at me; I know the literature, and I need the arguments in the room's voice, ready to answer from the podium.
