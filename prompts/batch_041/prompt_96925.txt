A colleague of mine leans on Chesterton's fence as a general principle: you should not tear down a fence — or, by extension, an institution, a rule, a custom — until you can say why it was put there, since the reason may be one you cannot presently see. He pairs it with a Burkean thought that inherited arrangements encode an accumulated wisdom no single person possesses, and he uses the pair to license a presumption in favor of leaving established practices alone and to shift the burden of proof onto anyone who proposes to abolish one. I want to know how far the fence analogy actually carries that weight. Lay out the mapping explicitly — what plays the role of the fence, the builder, the reason for the fence, and the person proposing removal — then separate the disanalogies that matter for his use from those that do not. Among the ones I want addressed: whether institutions have builders with recoverable intentions or arise without any designer; whether they have a single function or many that drift; whether the reason is knowable in principle or lost to history; whether removing an institution is as cheap and reversible as pulling up a fence; and whether a fence that confines people who never agreed to be fenced in changes the default. I also want one test case pushed hard: when I produce an institution whose original purpose is documented and was plainly to exclude a particular group, he reaches for the same principle anyway. Tell me whether the analogy can bear that, or whether it was only ever doing real work where the reason was unknown. Finally, say what his conclusion is worth once the disanalogies are on the table, and whether a better analogy would do the job.

Format the reply as follows:

- The analogy and its use (2–3 sentences).
- Correspondence table (fence-side versus institution-side).
- Where it holds (one short paragraph).
- Where it breaks, ranked by whether each disanalogy matters for his use (one or two paragraphs).
- What the analogy quietly smuggles in (one short paragraph).
- Does the presumption survive (one short paragraph, ending with your verdict).
- A better analogy, if there is one (2–3 sentences).
