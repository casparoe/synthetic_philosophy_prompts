A pattern in arts institutions has long puzzled me, and I would like you to reconstruct the philosophy it assumes. When a painter, novelist, or composer turns out to have done something appalling, museums and orchestras almost never remove the works themselves from the permanent collection or the repertory; the familiar justification is that a work must be judged on its own merits and that the artist's life is a separate matter. Yet those same institutions will cancel a retrospective, shelve a commission, take a name off a gallery wall, or quietly withdraw a piece from a benefit auction, and they do this without any suggestion that the works themselves have gotten worse. Meanwhile, works whose own content is degrading — canvases that traffic in racial caricature, films that sentimentalize the degradation of their subjects — remain on display and in the canon, largely without anyone reaching for these arguments at all.

What theory of artistic value does this combination presuppose? Does it require the autonomist thesis that an artist's moral failings never bear on the value of the work, or can a moralist about the artist explain the same behavior by appeal to reputational and institutional costs that fall outside the work's value altogether? Which rival positions does the practice actually rule out, and which does it merely happen to fit? I am especially interested in the second asymmetry: if objectionable content is simply not treated as a reason for removal, what does that reveal about the operative theory of value, and does it sit consistently with the treatment of the artist? Finally, tell me what would have to change in institutional practice if the presupposed view were replaced by one on which a work's moral defects are genuine defects in the work.
