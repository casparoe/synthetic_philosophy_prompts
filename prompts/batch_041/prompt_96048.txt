I'm doing a philosophy of mind module this term and we've just finished the Dennett stretch -- the intentional stance, real patterns, and his line that asking whether a system *really* believes or wants anything is the wrong question, because the stance is something we adopt for the sake of prediction rather than something we discover to be true or false about the thing. What's thrown me is trying to sqaure that with the later stuff he's been writing about counterfeit people: the claim that a system which can hold up its end of a conversation is a bit like a counterfeit banknote, that we ought to be marking such things as what they are and holding whoever turns them out responsible, and that a world flooded with them loses something we can't easily get back. I don't want a reading list or references -- I know you can't go and look things up, so work with what's in front of you -- I want the two views put up against a real episode.

The episode is Weizenbaum's ELIZA, at MIT in the mid-sixties: a program that did nothing but bounce your own sentences back at you as questions, in the style of a Rogerian therapist, and which somehow got a startling number of people to treat it as though it understood them. The detail that sticks with me is his own secretary, who had watched him build the thing, knew exactly what it was doing and how little was behind it, and still asked him to leave the room so she could talk to it on her own. Weizenbaum was rattled by that, and spent the rest of his career warning against handing such programs to people who need an actual person to talk to.

So map it both ways. Who is doing what here -- Weizenbaum, his secretary, the others in the lab who got their hands on it -- and what does the intentional stance say they were up to? Was she simply mistaken, or was taking the stance towards ELIZA a perfectly sensible thing to do given what it got her, with no seperate fact about ELIZA's mind for her to be wrong about? Then the harder bit: does the counterfeit-people argument need something the stance seems to deny, namely a genuine article for the fake to be a fake *of* -- or can it be read as being about provenance, trust and who answers for the output, in which case does the analogy survive the translation? Tell me which reading makes the proposed remedy work, because ELIZA strikes me as a nasty case for the labelling fix: everyone in that room knew precisely what the thing was and it made no difference whatsoever. And was Weizenbaum offering a third position that neither version of Dennett can absorb -- that the harm isn't a false belief about the machine but a corruption of the practice of talking to people at all? Say where the stance is silent rather than making it answer, and finish with a verdict on whether the episode tells for Dennett's instrumentalism or for his late warning.
