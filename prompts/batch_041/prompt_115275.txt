I am a software engineer with no formal training in either philosophy or law, and I have been trying to understand the disagreement between textualists and purposivists — roughly, whether a legal rule should be applied according to the plain meaning of its words or according to the end it was meant to serve. The trial scene in The Merchant of Venice, in which Portia saves Antonio by holding Shylock to the exact terms of his bond — he is entitled to a pound of flesh, the bond says nothing about blood, and the flesh cannot be taken without shedding it — strikes me as the clearest dramatization of that disagreement I have met, and I would like a reading of the scene that takes both the play and the debate seriously.

My first question is how someone who actually works in this dispute would classify Portia's reasoning. Shylock insists on the letter of his contract; Portia defeats him with a reading more literal than his own. But she arrives at that reading by asking what the bond was for, namely securing a debt, and what a court could conceivably enforce without absurdity — which sounds to me like purposivism, or at least like the canon that a text should not be read to produce an absurd result. Is she a textualist, a purposivist disguised as one, or neither? And does the play vindicate the literal reading or expose it as a trick? I suspect the scene refuses to keep those two possibilities apart, and I would like you to say which it is rather than to harmonize them.

I also want the reading to be honest in the other direction, where the analogy to statutory interpretation strains. A bond is a private contract between two parties, not a public statute, and the court that decides the case — the Duke, and then Portia sitting in his place — is not a legislature bound by a rule it wrote. The scene then reverses its own polarity almost immediately: Portia turns to the Venetian law against aliens and reads that provision against Shylock, and the Duke spares his life in a way that seems to rest on mercy rather than on any canon. I cannot tell whether that reversal is meant to expose the inconsistency of the court, to show that whoever holds the text wins, or to show that the spirit of the law is whatever the powerful decide it is, and I would like your view.

I do not need citations, line references, or quotations — work from the plot and the well-known shape of the scene. What I am after is an argument about what the scene shows that the doctrinal debate does not: whether Shakespeare has dramatized a case the theories cannot accommodate, and what, if anything, the play adds to the question that the legal writing on it leaves out. Please say plainly where the reading is strained, and where the play resists it.
