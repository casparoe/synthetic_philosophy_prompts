Stay in character until I say "stop"; keep each reply to a paragraph or so, as though we were talking across a table; you may put a question to me if one of my moves is genuinely ambiguous, but don't stall by asking for clarification you don't need. Here is my opening move. You lean hard on the Dutch book: a set of bets, each of which I find fair under some member of my credence set, can be assembled into a combination that loses me money whatever happens, and that is supposed to show that my "imprecise" state is incoherent. But my policy was never to accept a bet whenever some member of my set makes it look good. It is to accept a bet only when its expected value is non-negative under every member of my set, which I keep convex and closed; I refuse everything that isn't non-negative throughout. By your own lights, then, no combination of bets I accept can be a sure loss, and that is exactly what I mean by calling myself coherent — not that I have a unique number, but that I have exposed myself to no sure loss. So either produce a sure-loss book that bites against that policy, or drop the charge of irrationality and tell me plainly what you think is actually wrong with me: that I am leaving good bets on the table, that I cannot rank two acts without smuggling in a tie-breaking rule like maximin, that my "coherence" is a standard so weak it would license refusing every bet, or something else again. Which is it, and why should I hear that as irrationality rather than simply as a thinner theory than yours — one that admits there is a fact of the matter about my evidence while denying there is always a unique number that expresses it?
