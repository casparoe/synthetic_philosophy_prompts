I'm in a small reading group on applied ethics and one of the guys there — pro-choice, and honestly a sharper arguer than the rest of us — has basically collapsed his entire position into Thomson's violinist case, and I want to know how much weight that one comparison can really bear. His telling of it: you wake up one morning surgically wired to a famous violinist whose kidneys have failed; the Society of Music Lovers kidnapped you and plugged you in because you're the only match; if you stay attached for nine months he recovers and walks away; you can unplug whenever you like and he dies. His conclusion is that since a right to life doesn't include a right to the use of somebody else's body, abortion is permissible whatever the moral status of the fetus — debate over. He waves off the killing-versus-letting-die worry as irrelevant to that point. He's recently added a second claim that I trust alot less: that the case also shows consent is beside the point, because you can withdraw consent to the use of your body at any moment, so it makes no difference whether the woman got pregnant on purpose or merely accepted a risk — same as it wouldn't matter if you'd volunteered for the experiment and then changed your mind. Someone else in the group thinks the whole thing is loaded from the first sentence.

What I want isn't a summary of the case or a verdict on abortion, it's an audit of the analogy. Start with the correspondence table: what maps onto the violinist, onto me, onto the kidnapping, onto the nine months, onto the Society — and mark which of those mappings are exact, which are loose but harmless, and which are doing load-bearing work while pretending to be innocent. Then the disanalogies, sorted: which ones he can concede because they don't touch his conclusion, and which ones actually threaten it. I have a suspicion that the violinist being a stranger, with a fully formed life behind him, no prior relationship to you, and no causal role of yours in his plight, is smuggling in more than he admits, but I can't tell how much of the conclusion survives once you strip those out. Then tell me whether the stronger consent claim stands on its own or is just riding on the analogy, and what happens to both claims if you swap the stranger for the woman's own child, or make her the one who caused the violinist's kidney failure. And if there's a better analogy for the same conclusion, name it and say what it would cost — what it gives up that the violinist case buys him.
