I am a high school student preparing an argumentative essay for my school's philosophy club on whether manipulation is best defined by the method of influence or by its effect on the person influenced. I need a case that separates the two families of views cleanly, so that the disagreement is visible rather than merely verbal.

The first family holds that an act of influence is manipulative whenever it works by bypassing or short-circuiting the other person's deliberation — by exploiting a known cognitive bias, for instance, or an emotional trigger — irrespective of what is said and irrespective of how the target ends up. The second family holds that an act is manipulative only when it damages the target's judgment: when it leaves them with a belief or a decision they would not endorse on reflection, or one less accurate than what they would otherwise have reached. On this second view, an influence that leaves the target better informed and disposed to reaffirm their decision is legitimate persuasion, however it was produced.

Construct a thought experiment meeting these specifications:

1. A single deliberate act of influence must count as manipulation under the first family of views and as legitimate persuasion under the second.
2. The act contains no false statement, withholds no fact the target has a claim to know, and involves no threat, offer, or deception of any ordinary kind.
3. The person influenced is a competent adult, not a child and not someone specially vulnerable through grief, illness, or addiction.
4. The target's resulting belief is more accurate than the belief they would have reached unaided, and the decision they reach is one they would endorse on reflection.
5. The act works through a route the target is unaware of at the time and would resent having pointed out — even though they would continue to stand by the belief it produced.
6. The setting is ordinary and one a high school student could plausibly encounter; no science fiction, no perfect predictors, no exotic technology; the scenario itself no longer than one paragraph.

Then, after the scenario: for each family of views, identify the feature of the case that triggers its verdict and explain why the other family's trigger is absent. Supply a matched control case, identical in every respect but one, in which the two views agree, and say which feature you changed and why that feature rather than any other is doing the work. Finally, set out how a defender of the second family would try to redescribe the original case so that it is not manipulation after all, and how a defender of the first family would reply.

I do not want a case that announces its own verdict, or one that is merely borderline. The exercise is only useful if both views are applied honestly to the same facts and still come apart, so a case that stipulates its conclusion, or that quietly reintroduces deception, will not serve.
