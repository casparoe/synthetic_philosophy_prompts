In our department's methods seminar, a colleague repeatedly says that there is no probability of a single event, only a probability relative to a reference class, most recently to argue that our effort to calibrate a clinical risk score for individual patients is conceptually confused: the score, she says, is meaningless until you say which class the patient belongs to, and no class is uniquely correct. I am not satisfied that the slogan does the work she wants. Write me a disambiguation: lay out the distinct precise theses that sentence could be expressing—from the trivial observation that observed frequencies depend on how cases are grouped, through the claim that all probabilities are conditional and no unconditional probability exists, to the metaphysical claim that single-case chance is incoherent, to the epistemic or decision-theoretic claim that the choice of reference class is always interest-relative and so no probability is the probability. For each reading, say whether it is true, false, or a platitude, and say specifically which reading, if any, would support her conclusion that our calibration project is meaningless. Do not end by saying it depends on the reading; give me a committed verdict on which reading is the defensible one, which one she probably intends, and whether the slogan, properly precisified, licenses her dismissal of single-case risk estimates. Keep the whole answer focused on this one slogan and this one dispute.
