Consider a choice between a certain good G and a lottery that yields a finite good V with probability p and a bad outcome b otherwise. Two principles about such choices are easy to conflate:

Stake-modesty. For every probability p > 0, there is a finite V such that the certain G is strictly better than the lottery.
Chance-modesty. For every finite V, there is a probability p > 0 such that the certain G is strictly better than the lottery.

Please explain the conceptual issue that separates these two, for a reader who is familiar with the usual treatment of Pascal's-mugging cases but has never noticed the difference.

First, determine which principle follows from expected-value maximization on its own, given only that the bad outcome is worse than G, and which one is incompatible with expected-value maximization once finite goods are allowed to have no upper bound. Make the derivation explicit, and identify the premise each verdict turns on. Say also whether either principle is exactly equivalent to a bound on the value of finite goods, or merely implied by one.

Second, the intuitive case against fanaticism is a case for Stake-modesty. Explain why the examples standardly offered in its favour — a sufficiently small chance of a sufficiently large but finite good — establish only Chance-modesty, and why the slide from one to the other is not a minor slip but the whole substance of the disagreement.

Third, consider three families of repair: holding the value of finite goods to be bounded above; discounting or disregarding probabilities below some threshold; and treating goods of different kinds as not tradeable against one another. For each, say which principle it secures and which structural commitment it surrenders in exchange, naming the commitment precisely rather than gesturing at intuition.

Fourth, examine the threshold family more closely, since it is the most popular and the least stable. Negligibility of this sort is not preserved under conjunction: explain how one and the same prospect, a p-chance of V, can be described either as a single negligible event or as a conjunction of independent events none of which is negligible, and why a rule that turns on the magnitude of individual probabilities will then deliver two verdicts about one prospect. Extend the point to an agent facing many independent lotteries rather than one, and say what this implies about the level at which an anti-fanatic rule must be stated.

Finally, say whether the objection to fanaticism is better read as an argument against expected-value maximization or as an argument about the boundedness of value, and defend the reading you choose.

Throughout, let the quantifier order carry the explanation: the reader should come away seeing that the disagreement concerns which quantity is held fixed while the other varies, and that no further example of a very small chance of a very large good can settle it.
