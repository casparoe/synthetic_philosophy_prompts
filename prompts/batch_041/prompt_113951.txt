I was in a philosophy of maths reading group last night (we've been chewing through the realism/nominalism stuff — Benacerraf's access problem, indispensability arguments, whether mathematical objects are even the kind of thing you could bump into) and someone ended a twenty-minute back-and-forth by saying, in the tone of a man laying down a trump card, that mathematics is discovered, not invented. The argument he'd been having was about whether there's a fact of the matter about the continuum hypothesis: his line was that if CH is either true or false regardless of what anyone can prove, then discovery-talk is vindicated, and the multiverse people who say there's no single set-theoretic universe and no determinate answer are just obviously wrong; and anyway, if maths were invented we could have invented 2+2=5, which is absurd. I've been reading rationalist blogs for the better part of a decade and I've absorbed enough of the sequences to know that a one-liner like that is usually doing about six jobs at once, but I couldn't articualte a single one of them in the moment and it's been nagging at me all week.

What I want is a menu, not a verdict. Lay out the distinct precise claims that "mathematics is discovered, not invented" could actually be making, ordered roughly from the reading so weak that nobody could deny it to the ones that only come out true if you've already signed up to a particular metaphysics. For each, tell me whether it's a platitude, a substantive claim that's defensible, or just false, and what background commitments it silently needs. I'm especially interested in the split between readings that are about ontology (what there is), epistemology (how we come to know it), modality (the necessity of mathematical truths, which I suspect is doing a lot of the illicit work in the metaphor), and practice or language (mathematicians' discovery-talk as a façon de parler, a pedagogical habit, or a social fact about the discipline). Then the part I actually care about: which of these readings, if any, would let the slogan do what my friend wanted — settle whether CH has a determinate answer, or license accepting new axioms like large cardinals on the grounds that they're true rather than merely fruitful — and which of them are just question-begging restatements of the conclusion. Flag any readings that look distinct but collapse into each other, and any I've missed that are actually the ones people usually have in mind when they say it.
