A friend of mine keeps saying that lying and misleading are the same act morally — pushing someone down the stairs versus greasing the stairs so they fall, same thing, either way you're using their own legs. Her line is that the only real difference is who does the inferring: the liar does it for you, the misleader makes you do it yourself, and if anything making someone do the work themselves is worse, not better.

I half agree and half think she's cheating. Can you explain whats actually at stake in the lying/misleading distinction, so I can see where she's right and where she's smuggling something in? Three things I especially want untangled:

1. Is the distinction even a *moral* one, or is it parasitic on a semantic one — on whether the literal content of a sentence is fixed by the words alone, or already includes what any normal speaker in that situation would obviously have meant by them? If the moral verdict flips depending on which of those you accept, the distinction starts to look like a fact about semantic theory wearing a moral costume.

2. A matched pair: same words, same beliefs, same effect on the hearer, and the only difference is that in one case the speaker thinks the words themselves are false, while in the other he thinks the words are true and is counting on the hearer to draw the false conclusion. Same false belief produced either way. Is one worse? Give me a verdict, not a survey of positions.

3. Does the ranking reverse for the really sneaky stuff — where the false belief is carried entirely by a presupposition, or by a headline that's literally true, or by a doctor saying "we did everything we could" when what he means is "we followed the protocol"? Nothing there is false enough to grab onto, whereas a flat lie at least leaves you something to catch. Does that make the implicature version *worse* than the lie? And if it does, doesn't that break the usual story that lying is the worse one?

Related thing I can't fit anywhere: does it even matter whether the speaker was trying to produce a false belief? A false denial in front of ten people who all watched you do it isn't aimed at convincing anyone, and yet we still want to call it a lie. If lying doesn't require an intent to deceive, whats left that separates it from just being wrong about something?

No citations, I don't care who said what. I want the machinery and your own answer.
