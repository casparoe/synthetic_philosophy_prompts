Argument from my metaphysics reading group that I can't decide wether it's a trick or a real result. A friend says it shows there are no actually infinite collections — you can talk about "the set of all naturals" but there's no such completed thing. He thinks Hilbert's hotel describes an impossible object and that an infinite past is impossible too.

(1) For a collection to exist as a single object, its members have to all be there together — a completed whole, not just a rule for generating more.
(2) So suppose some collection is actually infinite: a completed whole with infinitely many members.
(3) Now form the collection of all its sub-collections.
(4) Cantor's diagonal argument: nothing can be matched one-to-one with the collection of its own sub-collections, so that one is strictly bigger.
(5) But the original was a completed whole of exactly the kind (1) insists on, and a completed whole has nothing left out — so nothing bigger can be built out of it. Contradiction with (4).
(6) So (2) is impossible: there are no actually infinite collections, only the potential infinity of always being able to go on.
(7) Bonus: the past can't be infinite, and the hotel is impossible rather than merely weird.

He likes (7). I think (7) is the tell — I'm happy to say the past might be infinite and I think Hilbert's hotel is a fine description of a real possibility. So where does it actually break, and what do I lose by keeping (7) false? If I have to give something up I'd rather keep (1) than (7).
