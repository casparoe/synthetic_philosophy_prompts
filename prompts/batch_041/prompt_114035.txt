In a reading group on decision theory we keep hitting the same wall, and I want a diagnosis rather than another round of argument. The case that stalls us is Death in Damascus: an agent in Damascus learns that Death is coming for him, and that Death's location tomorrow is settled in advance by a reliable prediction of what the agent will do, so that staying in Damascus is evidence Death will be in Damascus and fleeing to Aleppo is evidence Death will be in Aleppo. The causal decision theorists say the agent's choice makes no causal difference to where Death is, so the options are on a par and the case shows nothing; the evidential decision theorists say each option is evidence about where Death will be, and that the case exposes something the causal account cannot handle. My hypothesis is that the real disagreement is about ratifiability — whether an act a theory recommends must keep looking best once the agent has actually settled on it — and that Death in Damascus is a case where no act is ratifiable, so the theories are being asked to do something none of them can do. Is that right? If not, what is the disagreement actually about: the correct theory of rational choice, the interpretation of the case (how reliable the predictor is, whether the agent's credences can be stable, whether the agent can randomize), or two different questions being answered under one heading — one about guiding deliberation from the inside, one about predicting choice from the outside? Give me a diagnosis supported by what each side is actually committed to, and then tell me what would count as progress. Would it take a formal result about self-referential credences, a distinction between the deliberative and predictive standpoints, a decision that one of the two questions is the real one, or an argument that the case is under-described and no verdict should be expected? Please answer in American English.
