I teach the second half of a jurisprudence seminar on the Hart–Devlin debate and I want a handout that does more than list positions. Build me a decision tree that sorts a reader into a position on the legal enforcement of morality, starting from commitments they can state without having read any of the literature.

Constraints:

- Six to nine branch nodes, nine to twelve leaves. Every node must be a question the reader can answer without knowing which position it leads to. No node may name a position or a theorist, and none may be a dressed-up version of the single question whether the law ought to enforce morality.
- Build the space first and place positions in it second. I expect the independent axes to include at least: whether immorality as such is ever a pro tanto reason for state action; whether the reason, when there is one, is the intrinsic wrongness of the act or its downstream effects on a shared moral life; whether the standard to be enforced is the society's actual morality or a corrected, critical morality; whether the reason survives when the conduct is secret and the only complaint is that others would disapprove if they knew; and what instrument is at stake — criminal punishment, civil or regulatory pressure, subsidy or education or symbolic expression, or nothing at all. Some of these axes are independent and some are not. Say which are which, and collapse or drop any axis that turns out to be a proxy for another.
- Each leaf gets a name (or is marked unnamed if nobody defends it), a one-sentence statement of its view, and one sentence on its most awkward commitment. The tree must locate at least: the Mill–Hart line that non-harmful immorality never justifies coercion; a position that admits non-harmful grounds but only where offence is taken in public; a moralism grounded in social cohesion rather than in the wrongness of the act; a retributive moralism that treats immorality as sufficient for criminalization because wrongdoing deserves punishment; a moralism that tracks critical rather than positive morality; a position that permits the state to promote morality non-coercively while forbidding coercion; and at least one leaf nobody occupies.
- Validate every leaf against four cases and report its verdict on all four: (a) consensual incest between adults, conducted in private; (b) public desecration of a revered symbol, intended as contempt, with nobody physically harmed; (c) a practice that no participant regards as wrong but that the majority would condemn if it learned of it; (d) conduct the majority believes is morally wrong but that philosophical opinion almost unanimously regards as permissible. If two leaves return the same verdict on all four, say so and either name the further case that would separate them or state that nothing can.
- Then list the combinations of answers your axes allow that no leaf occupies, and diagnose each one: incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. Be specific about which, and do not treat unexplored as a catch-all.
- Finally, flag any pair of leaves the tree cannot in principle separate and explain the failure — for instance, if two supposedly different grounds for intervention converge once the instrument is fixed.

Keep it a handout rather than an essay: numbered nodes, leaves in a table, prose confined to the diagnostics.
