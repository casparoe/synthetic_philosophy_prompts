I am preparing materials for a graduate seminar on structural explanations of collective failure, and I want a piece of writing I can hand to students as a model of disciplined disagreement. Write a dialogue between two characters: an institutionalist (call her I) and a ratchet theorist (call him T). Both must be fully competent — no strawman, no cheap concession, no talking past each other. Each must respond to the other's actual last point, each must give ground somewhere real, and neither may simply restate a position in new words. The dialogue should end mid-disagreement, with the sharpest unresolved question left on the table rather than a synthesis. Do not cite anything: no authors, no titles, no "as has been argued elsewhere." If a character wants to invoke a precedent, they must describe the case in their own words.

Fix the vocabulary in advance, and have both characters use it consistently. A *multipolar trap* is any situation in which each party's individually rational move, made under the pressure of others making the same move, yields an outcome every party would have preferred to avoid. I holds that the interesting traps are coordination failures: a better outcome exists, is common knowledge, and is unreachable only because no one can afford to be the first to stop. T holds that this description flatters the cases. On T's account the important traps are *ratchets*, asymmetric in time: the step down is cheap, unilateral, and rewarded at once, while the step back up is expensive, requires simultaneity, and is paid for by whoever moves first while the benefit diffuses across everyone. T adds a third category — *grinds* — cases in which no better outcome is available under the current payoff structure at all, so that talk of coordination failure is a category error rather than a diagnosis.

The carrying case, which neither character may abandon, is the workplace norm of answering email within an hour.

In the course of the exchange: I must name a concrete mechanism that could reverse the norm and explain why it would succeed where exhortation has failed, and must say what makes the email case a coordination failure rather than a grind. T must specify what evidence would show the case to be a coordination failure after all, and must say whether he takes the ratchet to be reversible in principle. Each must identify one point at which the other's framework does explanatory work that their own cannot.

Keep the whole dialogue under 1,200 words, and no single speech longer than 175 words.
