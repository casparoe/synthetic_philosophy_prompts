I have been asked to give the short address at a citizenship ceremony in a London borough hall — the presiding officer, a couple of local dignitaries, and about thirty new citizens with their families and friends. I am a philosopher, and the reason I've been asked rather than the mayor is that I have spent a good deal of my working life on the ethics of immigration and I studied with Michael Dummett, who was a lifelong campaigner against racism and who, late in his career, wrote a book attacking the assumption that a state's right to keep people out is simply given and needs no argument. I don't have that book's details at my fingertips and I don't want to misattribute anything to him, so please paraphrase rather than quote: put nothing in quotation marks, and don't invent sentences for him.

What I want is an address that takes the awkwardness of the occasion seriously. A citizenship ceremony is a celebration of people joining a political community whose authority to have kept them out is precisely the thing Dummett thought needed defending and rarely got defended. The easy moves are all closed to me. I can't say that the new citizens are lucky, because that concedes the point at issue; I can't say the border was unjust, because that turns the ceremony into an accusation and embarrasses the presiding officer in front of people who are, after all, becoming citizens of something. I want the speech to hold both facts at once: that admission is not a favour conferred by a rightful gatekeeper, and that joining a polity is a real thing worth doing, which brings obligations along with standing.

Some things I'd like the speech to do structurally, not just mention. First, keep three questions apart that public argument usually runs together: whether a particular person should be let in, who has the authority to decide, and what it means to become a member. A speech that answers the first with sentiment and the third with paperwork misses the middle question entirely, and the middle question is the one that bites. Second, put the burden of proof where I think Dummett put it — on whoever wants to exclude, not on whoever wants to enter — but do this without sounding like a slogan, and without pretending the conclusion follows from anything as thin as the thought that borders are arbitrary lines on a map. I've always found it striking that the same man who insisted in his technical work that truth is not something we can make true by agreeing to it also refused to treat an existing border as settling a moral question merely because it exists and almost everyone accepts it. I'd like the speech to make something of that combination, briefly, and without turning into a lecture on realism versus anti-realism. If the philosophy graduate students in the third row get a flicker of recognition, that is enough; nobody else should feel tested.

The audience is genuinely mixed and the speech has to land for all of it. There will be people in that room whose previous claims were refused, sometimes more than once, and who will hear any defence of the immigration system as a defence of what was done to them. There will be a presiding officer who has to sit through this and then hand out certificates, and I don't want him humiliated. There will be children who are bored. There will be at least one reporter. Write it so that a person who has never read a word of philosophy hears a plain, dignified welcome, and a person who has thinks about the ethics of borders for a living hears an argument rather than a platitude.

Concretely: about seven hundred words, suitable for reading aloud, warm but not saccharine. Avoid the line that we are all immigrants really, and avoid anything that treats the new citizens as guests being welcomed into somebody else's house. End with something the new citizens can actually take up — a thought about what they now get to demand of the place, not only what it demands of them.

Write it as continuous prose, the way one would write a letter. No headings, no bullet points, no bold or italic for emphasis, no numbered lists, no tables. Just paragraphs.
