As a novelist at work on a book about a man who keeps encountering versions of himself, I've been teaching Christopher Nolan's The Prestige in a small writing seminar, and I want a serious philosophical reading of the film through Parfit's teletransporter and his broader argument that personal identity is not what matters in survival. Please write as someone who knows both the film and the personal-identity literature well; begin by brainstorming the scenes and plot points that bear on the question before you commit to a reading.

The crux I care about is this. The film is usually discussed as if Angier's machine were Parfit's teletransporter, but it looks to me closer to Parfit's fission or division case: the man in the machine is not destroyed and rebuilt elsewhere but duplicated, and the one left behind drowns. I'd like you to take that distinction seriously and say what difference it makes. Does Angier's willingness to drown a version of himself each night make sense on Parfit's own account of what matters, or does it betray a lingering belief in exactly the kind of further fact about identity that Parfit denies? What does Borden's twin, who lives one life between two bodies, add to the puzzle? And is there anything the film dramatizes about caring for one's own survival that the philosophical cases smooth over?

Be careful to separate what is settled in the personal-identity debate from what is genuinely contested -- Parfit's reductionism and the claim that identity doesn't matter are live disagreements, not consensus -- and do not present a contested view as if everyone who knows the topic accepts it. Where the film resists the Parfitian reading, say so rather than forcing it.
