There is a style of argument I keep meeting, and I have never found a name for the premise it rests on.

The argument runs like this. Someone wants to establish that one side of a contest is bound to come out ahead, but instead of showing how that side wins, they argue from the shape of the situation alone: suppose the other side had a way of winning; then the first side could have opened with some idle move and thereafter done exactly what the other side would have done, so the other side cannot have had a way of winning. The conclusion is delivered without anyone being able to say what the winning approach actually is. I first noticed it in board games where each player adds pieces of their own colour, and where a piece of your own colour never gets in your way; there the argument seems to me airtight.

What I cannot pin down is what it quietly assumes. It seems to need the thought that having made one move more than you otherwise would have made can never leave you worse off — that the extra move is free, or at worst wasted. And in games where the rules oblige you to move and any move you make loosens your own position, the same reasoning collapses entirely: there the obligation to act is a liability, and the extra move is a burden rather than a gift.

I meet a looser relative of the same reasoning outside games, in ordinary strategic talk: you cannot be worse off for having the option, since you can always decline to use it. Where the other party's behaviour is not itself a response to what you are able to do, that seems to me obviously right. But where others are watching what you are able to do and adjusting accordingly, the extra option can hurt — it can commit you, it can advertise what to expect from you, it can put within reach a move you will not be able to resist.

So my question is about the hidden premise rather than the label. Is there an established name for the assumption that a wider set of options, or one extra move, can never be a disadvantage, and a name for the family of cases in which it fails? And are the two things I have described one phenomenon or two — is the argument that copies the opponent's approach just a special case of more options cannot hurt, or does it need something further, such as a symmetry between the sides or a guarantee that the borrowed move is harmless? What I want is the boundary: how to tell in advance whether the premise holds, what precisely goes wrong when it does not, and whether the failure that comes from the rules and the failure that comes from other people's expectations are the same failure. If neither the assumption nor its failure has a settled name, I would rather have a proposed one with a definition and cases on both sides of the line than a bare term.
