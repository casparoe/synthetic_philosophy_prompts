I'm a novelist by trade — that's the day job — but I teach one upper-division seminar every spring, "Lying, Misleading, and the Ethics of What We Say," and grading is honestly the part of the job I'm worst at. I'm a few weeks out from a deadline on a novel that isn't going well, the dog is asleep on a stack of midterms, and it's late, which is all to say I don't entirely trust my own ear right now. I never know whether I'm grading the argument or the prose. So I'd like a second opinion on one paper before I put a number on it.

Two bits of setup so you know what the students were working with. In week 3 I put our definition of lying on the board: a statement the speaker believes to be false, made with the intention of deceiving. The same week I gave them a term I'd come across in some negotiation research — paltering — meaning the active use of true statements to leave someone with a false impression, as opposed to just keeping quiet about something. Part II of the midterm was 30 marks, 60 minutes, open book. Here it is:

Midterm, Part II (30 marks).

Case A. Rosa is interviewing for a job she badly wants. Asked whether she has ever managed a budget, she says: "Yes — I ran the budget for our department's annual conference." She has never managed a budget. She believes the sentence is false, and she says it in the hope that the interviewer will believe it.

Case B. Dev is interviewing for the same job. Asked whether he can work with SQL databases, he says: "I built the backend for my university's course-registration system." This is true. He knows the interviewer will take it to mean that he can work with SQL databases, and he says it for exactly that reason.

(a) (8 marks) State the definition of lying we used in class and apply it to Case A. Then say whether B is a lie on that definition, and defend your answer.
(b) (10 marks) Some writers treat cases like B — paltering — as morally no better than cases like A. Reconstruct the strongest version of that claim.
(c) (12 marks) Now say what the moral difference between A and B comes to, if anything. If you think the difference is real, say what it rests on; if you think it's empty, say what makes it empty.

And here's what the student handed in:

(a) We defined a lie as a statement the speaker believes to be false, made with the intention of getting someone else to believe it. Case A is a clean example. Rosa believes "I ran the budget for our department's annual conference" is false, and she says it in order to make the interviewer believe it. Both conditions are met.

B is trickier. Dev's sentence is true — he did build the backend — so it doesn't satisfy the falsity condition, and on a literal reading he hasn't lied. But a lie is an attempt to get someone to believe something false, and that's exactly what Dev is doing. He knows the interviewer will conclude he can work with SQL, and he says the sentence for no other reason. I think the honest thing to say is that B is a lie too, in the morally relevant sense, and that the literal truth of the sentence is a technicality about the words rather than about what Dev is doing. Once you see that the intent to deceive is what makes a lie a lie, the line between lying and paltering stops doing any work.

(b) The strongest version of the parity view goes like this. Sentences aren't wrong; people are. What's wrong with Rosa isn't the falsity of her sentence but the way she uses the interviewer — she feeds her a belief she has no business asking her to hold, and she can only do it because the interviewer has to take her word for things. Call that exploiting trust. Dev exploits exactly the same trust: the interviewer can't check the backend claim on the spot any more than she could check Rosa's. He's equally willing to let her walk out with a false belief about his skills. And there's a further strike against him, because paltering buys deniability — Dev can say afterwards that everything he said was true, and if the interviewer feels cheated she can be made to feel that she's the one who jumped to conclusions. Same wrong, plus a way of covering it. If lying is wrong only because of what it does to the person deceived, there's no reason to grade Dev any higher than Rosa. Kant's absolutism about lying to the murderer at the door is basically dead, so we can't say lying is wrong in itself while paltering is just bad manners — lying isn't wrong in itself, so the two cases have to come out the same.

(c) I do think the difference is real, though it's thinner than people want it to be. In B the interviewer draws the inference herself. Dev puts a true sentence on the table; the false belief is her own doing, and she could have asked a follow-up question. That's the sense in which he hasn't asserted anything false, so he hasn't asked her to believe anything false. It also tells us where the real line is: the interesting distinction isn't lying versus keeping quiet — that one's easy, Dev isn't being silent, he's talking — it's lying versus paltering, and what separates them is that Rosa puts a false sentence into the conversation and Dev doesn't. So the difference comes to this: Rosa lies, Dev merely lets the interviewer mislead herself. Whether that gets him off the hook depends on how much a speaker owes beyond the literal content of his sentences. I'd say a fair amount, since Dev meant her to draw the SQL conclusion, but it's still a lesser wrong than Rosa's, because he never said anything untrue.

Can you grade this? I'd like a letter (A–F, +/- is fine), marks out of 30 broken down by part, and then the actual margin comments — point by point, saying which moves earn credit, which ones cost marks, and roughly how much each. I'd rather over-comment than under-comment, so make the comments specific: not "could be clearer" but "this sentence does X, and X isn't enough because Y." If you can also give me a line or two on what a top answer would have done that this one doesn't, that'd help me calibrate the rest of the pile — I won't write that part on the paper. And if the whole thing is sitting on a borderline between two grades, tell me which way you'd go and why.
