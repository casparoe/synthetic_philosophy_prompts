A colleague in economic history is drafting a paper he believes gives Max Weber's Protestant ethic thesis its first genuine test. His design is a natural experiment. In a region where a prince's choice at the Reformation assigned otherwise similar towns to one confession or the other, he finds that towns on one side of the old confessional boundary still display, centuries later, markedly higher rates of self-employment, saving, and small-firm formation, with no corresponding differences in soil, geography, or pre-Reformation economic structure. Since the boundary is arbitrary with respect to those factors, he argues, cultural transmission is left as the residual cause — and this, he says, is the confirmation Weber never had. Assume Weber could read the paper and reply to it at length.

I want your best reconstruction of what he would actually say, not a summary of how the thesis is ordinarily assessed. Please take the following up one at a time.

1. Would he accept the finding as a vindication? His own claim concerned the historical genesis of a particular economic ethos among specific groups in a specific period, not the persistence of behavioural differences between populations today. Does this study test his thesis, a stronger descendant of it, or a different claim that has borrowed its name?

2. Suppose the behavioural difference holds in places where the original beliefs have long since evaporated — the descendants are not observant, and cannot articulate any doctrine of calling or proof of grace, yet they behave as their ancestors did. Would Weber treat this as confirmation, as refutation, or as evidence for something else, such as an ethos that has outlived the meanings which generated it? What does that possibility reveal about what he took a culture to be?

3. Would he recognize the paper's reasoning as causal explanation in his own sense? In his methodological writings, causal attribution in history runs through the notions of objective possibility and adequate causation: one asks, counterfactually, whether the outcome would have been adequately probable without the factor in question, rather than merely more probable with it. Does a natural experiment implement that procedure, or does it substitute a different criterion of causation? Would Weber have regarded a statistically identified effect as a historical explanation at all, or only as raw material for one?

4. A second colleague objects that Weber's thesis was never a causal claim but a claim about elective affinity, and that this renders it untestable in principle, so the new study can neither confirm nor refute it. Would Weber have accepted that description of his own project? What would he say a value orientation is, such that it can enter an explanation without being a cause in the same sense as a soil or a tariff?

5. Finally, imagine he is shown the objection that explanations invoking culture are unfalsifiable because they can always be rescued by positing a deeper layer of values. Does his own way of framing the Protestant ethic give him a reply, or does he share the difficulty?

You need not cite any sources; I am interested in the reconstruction and the reasoning behind it. Close by indicating which of your answers are well grounded in Weber's own commitments and which are speculative, and name the single consideration that would most seriously undermine your reconstruction.
