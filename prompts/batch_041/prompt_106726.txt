A colleague in a perception seminar has constructed an argument that she believes settles Molyneux's question in the empiricist's favor, and I suspect it proves far more than that, but I cannot locate the overreach. I would like you to diagnose it.

The scenario, stated plainly: a person blind from birth has learned to distinguish a cube from a sphere by touch and can name each correctly. She then gains sight. On first seeing the two objects, could she tell by sight alone which is the cube and which the sphere?

Here is the argument, which she calls the sealed-channel argument.

(1) To possess the concept cube is to have a capacity to recognize cubes as cubes — to sort cubes from non-cubes.
(2) Our subject's capacity to recognize cubes was assembled entirely out of tactile experience; what it responds to is a pattern of felt surfaces and edges.
(3) A recognitional capacity formed within one sensory channel responds to the deliverances of that channel; it is not, in itself, a capacity to respond to the deliverances of another channel.
(4) So her capacity recognizes cube-feelings, not cube-lookings.
(5) To recognize a cube by sight, she would have to know that some particular visual pattern is the look of the very thing she recognizes by touch.
(6) But at first sight she has no basis for that identification: the visual pattern does not wear its tactile counterpart on its face, and the correlation between the two has never been experienced.
(7) Therefore she cannot tell by sight which object is the cube. The empiricist answer stands.

Now the part that worries me. Steps (3) and (5) say nothing about the subject's inexperience. They assert that a recognitional capacity is channel-bound, and that crossing from one channel to another always requires an experienced correlation. If that is the real engine of the argument, it appears to license a great deal more than (7).

(8) By (3) and (5), no one can recognize a cube by sight except by way of remembered correlations between looks and feelings — including the sighted adult who has handled cubes for years.
(9) But we routinely recognize shapes we have never touched — the shape of a distant building, a figure in a diagram, a form that exists only on a screen — and we do so on sight, immediately, with no felt correlate in mind.
(10) And if channel-boundness were true, two people who shared a visual experience but differed in their history of touch would attach different concepts to what they saw, so that cube would name different properties in each; it would then be a mystery how they could ever be said to see the same object.
(11) Neither of those consequences seems acceptable.

So either (7) is right and (8) through (10) somehow do not follow, or one of the premises is false. I am fairly attached to the empiricist answer to Molyneux's question — I think the newly sighted really would not know — so I would rather keep (7) than give it up.

Tell me which step is the culprit, why it looked innocent, and what it costs to give it up. If more than one step is defensible, price each: what would a recognitional theory of concepts have to surrender; what would it cost to deny that recognitional capacities are channel-bound; and what would it cost to deny that cross-channel identification needs an experienced correlation. I care less about which answer is currently fashionable than about what each one gives up, and whether any of them can keep (7) intact.
