I have a background in software engineering and no formal training in philosophy or logic, and I have recently been working through Hofstadter's book on Gödel, Escher and Bach. The analogy the book is built around, as I understand it, is that the kind of self-reference Gödel constructs in arithmetic — a formal system that can encode statements about its own formulas and thereby produce a sentence that is, in effect, about itself — is the formal cousin of the self-reference that underlies a mind's awareness of itself. A colleague of mine, who is far more confident about this than I am, has pushed the analogy much harder than I think the book does. His position is that the diagonal lemma, the device that manufactures the self-referential sentence, is essentially a quine — a program that prints its own source code — and that once you see it that way, self-awareness is simply a fixed point: build a system that can inspect and rewrite its own description and you have, in the relevant sense, a system that knows itself. He treats the second incompleteness theorem as a second pillar of the same idea: just as a consistent formal system cannot prove its own consistency, a mind cannot fully know itself, and this is supposed to explain why introspection has limits. The conclusion he draws is practical — that engineering a self-aware system is basically a matter of building the right self-referential loop — and I would like to know whether the formal results carry any of that weight.

Please audit the whole chain rather than either swallowing it or dismissing it. First lay out the correspondences explicitly: what maps onto what across the formal system, its meta-level, the diagonal lemma or quine, the Gödel sentence, the consistency statement, and the mind together with its self-knowledge. Then tell me where the mapping breaks, separating the disanalogies that matter for my colleague's conclusion from the ones that are mere details. I am especially interested in whether the following are genuine problems or red herrings: (a) that the Gödel sentence is true only relative to a standard interpretation, so what the system cannot prove is the unprovability of some statement rather than its falsity, which may mean the self-knowledge on offer is thinner than it sounds; (b) that Löb's theorem appears to say a consistent system can never prove that its own proofs are sound — if a system proves, for some statement P, that the provability of P implies P, then it already proves P — so the blanket self-trust a mind apparently has is precisely something the mapped system cannot have; and (c) that a mind can add axioms or revise its own rules, whereas the Gödel result is always relative to a fixed system, so a mind may resemble the whole ascending sequence of systems (T, then T plus its consistency statement, and so on) more than it resembles any single one of them.

Finally, tell me whether the colleague's conclusion survives: is engineering self-awareness really a matter of constructing a self-referential fixed point, or is the formal result doing none of the work and the analogy carrying all of it? If the comparison is worth keeping in any form, say what the most defensible version of it would be. Where you can, give the correspondences as a table, and keep the assessment concrete.
