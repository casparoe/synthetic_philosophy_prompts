I teach a comparative memory seminar -- Germany and Vergangenheitsbewältigung, Japan and Yasukuni and the textbooks, Turkey and 1915, the American South and the statues -- and I keep running into the same move. Whenever we get concrete about whether some ritual, apology, museum, or curriculum actually does any work, somebody says "never again" and thats supposed to settle it. And it's said by everybody: the Germanist about the memorial culture, the Armenian side about recognition, the student who just came back from the civil rights museum, the guy who wants the Confederate statue gone and the guy who wants it kept but "contextualized." Same four words, completely different jobs.

What I want is a proper disambiguation, laid out as a menu, not you picking the one you like and explaining it. Take "never again" and separate the precise claims it could actually be making, from the near-trivial to the very strong. For each reading I want to know: is it a promise, a prediction, a command, a wish, or just an expression of grief; who is the "we" that's speaking; what exactly is the "it" that's not to happen again (the same act, the same kind of act, any act of that type, anything that led to it); and over what time span and to whom the commitment runs. Then give me your verdict on each -- platitude, substantive and defensible, unfalsifiable, or just false -- and say which ones would actually support the uses I described.

Two uses I care about most. First, someone invokes it to end an argument about whether a memorial, an apology, or a truth report does any good -- as if "never again" answered the question of whether these things prevent anything. Which reading of the slogan, if any, actually gets them that conclusion, and which of the readings are just a way of changing the subject? Second, someone invokes it to end an argument about whether comparison across these cases is even legitimate -- the idea that the German version of the slogan and the Charleston version are the same claim, or that they can't be. Which reading makes them the same, which makes them different, and does the slogan itself decide that or is it the other way round.

Also tell me whether this is really one slogan at all, or a family of them that quietly switches members mid-conversation -- I have a suspicion the switching is doing alot of unearned work and I'd like to be able to say why.

No reading list needed, I know the literature, I just want the conceptual map.
