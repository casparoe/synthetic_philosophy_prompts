I write a humor colum for a small bioethics newsletter and I need something for the next issue. Could you do me a satirical judicial opinion — a fake appellate ruling, deadpan all the way through, no winking at the reader — in a case where a man's advance directive ends up being enforced against the man himself?

Here's the setup I want. He wrote the directive at 45, fully competent, and it's brutally specific: what he'll eat, where he'll live, who's allowed to visit, that he is not to marry the woman he happens to be with at the time, that if he ever becomes "not himself" he gets moved to a particular town and read a particular book once a year. Now he's 70. He has early dementia but he still has good days, and on a good day he can tell you exactly what he wants, and it's the opposite of all of it. He sues. So the court has to answer whose autonomy the law is actually protecting.

The jokes need to come out of the real philosophy and not out of absurdity for its own sake. The doctrine that the earlier, competent self speaks for the later, incompetent one rests on the thought that a directive is an exercise of autonomy. So either the later man is the same person, in which case he has his own autonomy right now and it's at least a tie, or he's a different person, in which case the earlier man has no authority over him at all. I want the majority to walk straight into that fork without noticing, pick whichever horn is convenient, quietly switch to the other horn two paragraphs later, and never admit it. Let the dissent spot the fork and use it, but don't let the dissent come out clean either — it should land somewhere just as uncomfortable, maybe by concluding that the man is two people and the state gets to choose which one it listens to.

A few more things I'd love it to hit, and please don't let anyone in the opinion use the technical name for any of them — it should all come out as ordinary legal reasoning: that we refuse to enforce contracts of self-enslavement, so why this one; that the 45-year-old was deciding under total ignorance of the circumstances he'd be deciding about; and the difference between a directive about a treatment (where we mostly do let the earlier self rule) and a directive about a whole life (where it starts to feel like a dead hand with a lawyer). The target is the doctrine and the genre — the opinion that settles a metaphysical question in the language of "best interests" — not any real judge or real case, and nobody in it should be identifiable as a real person.

Invent the case names and citations and make them obviously fictional (In re somebody, a reporter that doesn't exist). Please don't cite real cases — I can't check them and it'd be embarrassing if one came out wrong.

Format it as one opinion with these labelled parts, in this order:
- Procedural history and facts — about 150 words
- The majority's reasoning — about 300 words
- The dissent — about 250 words
- The order — 50 words or less
Somewhere around 800 words total. The funniest version of this is one where nobody in the document thinks they're being funny, and where the majority reads like something a competent court could actually have written.
