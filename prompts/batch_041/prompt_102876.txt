I'm in a bioethics reading group and last week was the procreative beneficence stuff — the principle that if you're going to have a child, and you can choose between the possible children you might have, you've got reason to choose the one whose life will go best. I came in thinking it's obviously right in the embryo-selection case and obviously overreach by the time people start talking about liberal eugenics, and I came out with no idea where the line is. My friend Dana wrote up this version to push me and now I cant get out of it. I have a response paper due Friday and I'd rather not hand in something that just says "non-identity problem" and hopes for the best, so I need an actual diagnosis.

Here's Dana's argument. (1) If you're going to create a child and you're in a position to choose which of the possible children you'll create, you have decisive reason to create the one whose life will go best, assuming all the candidates would have lives worth living. (2) In embryo selection, the embryos you don't implant never become people, so nobody is harmed by your picking a different one; the reason to pick the best one isn't that the others lose out, it's just that the outcome containing the better-off child is the better outcome. (3) So the principle really says: in a choice that settles which person comes to exist, prefer the option in which the person who comes to exist has the better life. The comparison is always between possible people, never inside one life. (4) Nothing in that formulation cares whether the choosing is done by a technician with a pipette or by a couple deciding when to start trying — conception timing fixes which gametes meet, so it fixes who comes to exist just as much as the pipette does. Dana's slogan for it is "actualize the best person you can." (5) So if you could have waited three months and had a child who'd be a little healthier, a little sharper, a little happier, and you didn't wait because you didn't feel like it, you did something wrong — and by (1), not a small wrong. (6) Same for partners: if you could have a child with someone whose genome would give that kid a better expected life, and you stay with your current partner out of love, you've done wrong by the standard in (1). (7) Same for whether to have kids at all: if you can create a person whose life is worth living and you don't, you're leaving the better outcome on the table. (8) And since (1) says "best," and for any child you could make there's a possible child a bit better than that one, there's no stopping point: every fertile adult is under a standing obligation to be doing whatever maximizes the expected quality of the next person they could make, forever. That last step can't be right, and I don't think Dana thinks it is either — he just wants me to say which step to give up.

The trouble is I don't want to give up (2). I think it's correct that nothing bad happens to an embryo that isn't implanted, and (2) is what makes the embryo case work in the first place. But (2) is also what seems to get me to (8). So: which step do I reject, why did it look fine to me when I read it, and what does rejecting it cost me — in particular, if I keep (2) and drop whatever you say to drop, what am I no longer allowed to say about the couple who declines screening, or about enhancing a kid who already exists? I'd rather keep (1) through (4) and give up (8), if that matters for your answer.
