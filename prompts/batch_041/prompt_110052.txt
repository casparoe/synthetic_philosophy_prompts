I'm running the session on stakes and knowledge next week for a 300-level epistemology seminar — 14 students, one 90-minute block — and I want an activity, not a lecture. What I want them to walk out having felt in their own mouths is that their willingness to say someone "knows" something moved even though the evidence, the reliability, and the truth of what she said were all held fixed, and I want them to have been caught in the act, so the debrief isn't me announcing the phenomenon to them. So: a game with roles, rules, and a fake-currency payoff scheme (tokens, nothing real), built around two knobs I can turn independently. Knob one is the stakes of the person who might know — she's either in a situation where being wrong costs her nothing much, or one where it costs her a lot. Knob two is the stakes of the person doing the attributing — either they have nothing riding on the verdict, or they've got something of their own riding on it, because they'll have to act on it or pay for being wrong. The reason I want both knobs is that the views on our syllabus disagree about which one actually moves the word, and at least one of them says neither does; if the activity works, I should end up with four tables of ballots that contradict each other about cases where nothing epistemic differs, and each table should be able to find its own ballot in the pile. Please don't just hand me the bank cases with a debrief bolted on — they're too well known by now and my students will smell it immediately. Keep the cases mundane and non-academic (platforms, mushrooms, whether a clause is in a contract, that kind of thing), keep the evidence story in the high-stakes and low-stakes versions genuinely identical down to the detail, and build in a second measure that isn't a yes/no verdict: before they answer the knowledge question, have them set each case on a 0-100 "warrant" dial, because in the debrief I want to ask whether the word changed its meaning or whether they just got more careful about acting, and I want them looking at their own numbers when they try to answer that. Don't name pragmatic encroachment, contextualism, or any of the -isms until the last ten minutes — the rules and the payoffs should be doing the work, and I'd rather the naming land as a diagnosis of what just happened to them. One page of materials per table plus a token supply, a timeline for the 90 minutes, the debrief with specific moments from the run mapped onto specific claims, an honest note about the one thing this setup can't reproduce and that I'll have to supply myself, and what predictably goes wrong the first time and how to handle it in the room.

When you answer, lead with the bottom line in one sentence — what the activity is and what contrast it isolates — then give the design, with no preamble and no restating of what I just asked for. And at the end, tell me what would change your mind about the design: what you'd have to see in a pilot run to conclude it isn't isolating anything about stakes at all and is just cuing students to say what they think the professor wants, plus where the reasoning behind the design is weakest. If the honest answer is that a two-knob setup can't seperate the semantic claim from the psychological one, say so instead of papering over it.
