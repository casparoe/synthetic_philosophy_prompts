In the game known as the dollar auction, a one-dollar prize is auctioned off. Bids rise in five-cent increments; when no one bids further, the highest bidder pays the amount they bid and receives the dollar, while the second-highest bidder also pays the amount they bid and receives nothing. Both participants know these rules in advance and may keep bidding as long as they wish.

I want you to argue for the following position: the escalation this game produces is not an instance of the sunk-cost fallacy or of any other defect in the bidders' reasoning, and the common practice of treating the dollar auction as a demonstration of psychological escalation of commitment -- self-justification, loss aversion, the reluctance to waste money already committed -- is therefore a mistake about what the game actually shows.

Your argument should accomplish the following. First, identify exactly which feature of the rules generates the escalation, and show that a bidder who cares only about the money, feels no attachment to bids already made, and reasons correctly forward from the position they occupy would still be drawn upward past the point at which winning is profitable. Second, confront the objection that a rational player simply declines to enter such an auction, and explain why that objection does not restore the psychological explanation. Third, consider a variant in which the losing bidder pays a small fixed penalty -- five cents, say -- regardless of how much they bid, while the winning bidder still pays their own bid; state what your analysis predicts about how far bids will climb past the value of the prize, and explain what that prediction reveals about the mechanism you identified. Fourth, apply the diagnosis to a concrete real-world pattern in which two rivals commit resources they cannot recover to a contest only one of them can win, and say what practical recommendation follows for a party already caught in such a contest if the escalation is structural rather than a matter of bias.

Argue for the position rather than cataloguing considerations on both sides, and where you rely on a game-theoretic claim, derive it rather than naming it.
