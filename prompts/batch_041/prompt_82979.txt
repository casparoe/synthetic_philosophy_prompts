I am a Year 13 student and the outgoing chair of my school's computing society. For two terms we have been building a small chatbot that answers questions from younger pupils about school life — which subjects to take, how to handle a falling-out with a friend, whether something is worth telling a teacher about. It goes live at a launch evening next week, and as chair I have to give the opening address. The audience will be club members, a few of whom read a great deal, plus their parents, our head of computing, the deputy head, and the local councillor whose grant paid for the equipment.

What I cannot work out is what to say about whose values the thing serves, because we never settled it and I do not want to pretend otherwise. We put three candidate value sets to a vote — roughly, the staff's, the pupils', and one the club wrote from scratch — and the result cycled: A beat B, B beat C, and C beat A, each by a couple of votes, so which one had "won" depended entirely on the order we counted in. I voted for the one that lost. The deputy head has asked me not to reopen the argument in public, and keeps saying the bot should simply be neutral; two of my members insist that is impossible, since even a system that refuses to rank values must still choose a tone, a default, and something to say when it has nothing to say, and those choices are themselves values. Worse, when we tried to write down what we ourselves value — kindness, honesty, ambition, not wasting people's time — we found that we disagreed with one another and, on different days, with ourselves.

Please write the address. I would rather it grappled with the substance — whether a machine can hold values that are nobody's in particular, and what would make a decision legitimate for the people who voted against it — than with our club's internal politics. It has to be honest about the disagreement without turning into a seminar, and it has to be something those who lost the vote can stand behind. It also needs to commit: say what the bot will actually do when a pupil asks something that pulls our values apart, such as a pupil who loves a subject her parents are convinced is a dead end, rather than leaving the question open. About four minutes spoken.

Begin with the address itself: no preface, no notes to me, no commentary afterwards, and open with what we are launching and on what terms rather than warming up to it. British English, please.
