I am a professor of philosophy, and I chair the independent review panel that must certify any automated decision system used by our state disability-benefits agency before it may be deployed. On Thursday the panel takes up the agency's request to certify a new eligibility engine, and I need the written opinion I will circulate to the panel and forward to the agency director.

The engine determines eligibility and benefit levels from a claimant's file. Its rule set is published and stable: statutory criteria, a scoring rubric, and a body of interpretive guidance, all individually intelligible. What is not intelligible is their composite operation — no one can say, for a given file, which rules did the work. The agency therefore proposes to discharge its transparency obligation without producing reasons. For each determination it would publish a short certificate: a proof that the output was produced by executing the published rule set on a file matching the commitment recorded for that claimant, verifiable in milliseconds by anyone holding the certificate and the agency's public parameters, and revealing nothing about the file beyond that fact. A claimant who prefers an ordinary written statement of reasons may instead elect a manually reviewed determination, currently running roughly ninety days behind.

Our enabling statute requires that every claimant receive a determination that is "reasoned and contestable," and our charter directs the panel to certify a system only if it affords claimants a genuine opportunity to understand and challenge the grounds of their determinations. Two members have filed written positions. The civil-liberties member argues that a certificate of conformity is not a statement of reasons: it establishes that some computation occurred, not why this claimant got this result, and it gives an appellant nothing to bring to a hearing. The technical member argues that the soundness of such proofs rests on unproven hardness conjectures, and that for succinct schemes of this kind the guarantee is conditional either on a trusted setup that no one outside the vendor can re-verify or on a random-oracle heuristic; that a negligible soundness error is not zero; and that the guarantee covers only a correctly implemented system, which no independent party has audited. The agency's own justification is that producing a reasoned determination is computationally infeasible, since the rule set induces a hard optimization problem.

Please draft the memo. It should recommend certification, conditional certification, or denial; if conditional, the conditions must be concrete enough that the agency can be found to have met or failed them, with dates. Say plainly what the infeasibility claim does and does not establish, given that intractability results are worst-case and asymptotic and tell us nothing about the instances this agency actually sees, and say whether "explainability" here is best understood as a property of the system or of the relationship between the agency and the claimant. State which of the two objections you judge stronger and why the other does not carry the day. Say whether the elective ninety-day alternative makes the loss of reasons consensual or merely formal, what evidence would move you off your recommendation, and whether the panel should retain a power to decertify after deployment. Write for readers with no technical background, and assume no access to outside sources: the memo must stand on its own.
