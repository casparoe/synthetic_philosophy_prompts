I've been chewing on the two-panel induction puzzles that the machine-learning literature usually calls Bongard problems — a set of figures on the left, a set on the right, and the task of articulating the rule that sorts them. There's a feature of the standard format I had never noticed until recently: every figure is classifiable on its own. Once you have the rule, you can point at any single figure and say which side it belongs to. I've been toying with a variant in which that fails, and I'd like your help working out what follows from it.

Call it the ensemble variant. Here the rule is a property of the panel as a whole: whether its members are internally connected, in the sense that you can get from any member to any other by a chain of single-step transformations — one rotation, one recolouring, one added stroke. A toy example: the left panel holds a filled triangle, a hollow triangle, a hollow triangle rotated ninety degrees, and a hollow triangle rotated a hundred and eighty degrees, each neighbouring pair differing by exactly one step. The right panel holds a filled triangle, a square, a filled circle, and a cross, no two of which are one step apart. Both panels draw on the same alphabet of shapes, so the difference lies in the set and not in the ingredients — and the filled triangle appears on both sides, so no inspection of an individual figure tells you where it belongs. At four figures a patient solver can still check every pair; the interesting cases are panels large enough that this becomes impractical.

I care about this because the things I actually want to learn about — the norms of a community, the style of a field, the taste of a research programme — look like properties of ensembles rather than of any single instance. What I want from you are the most important implications of ensemble-level concepts, both for how we should think about concepts and induction and for learning in the wild. I'm not after a literature review; I would rather have your reasoning than a set of citations, and I would rather have a small number of implications argued properly than a long list.

Structure the response as follows:

1. **Restatement** (~80 words): the variant and my example, in your own words, so that I can check we agree on the target.
2. **Implications for the theory of concepts and induction** (~400 words): what this does to the picture of concepts as rules, feature bundles, or prototypes, and to the idea that induction proceeds by spotting the feature that all the positives share.
3. **Implications for learning an unfamiliar domain from a handful of examples** (~300 words): what a person should actually do differently.
4. **Implications for machine learning and few-shot prompting** (~250 words): what it suggests about in-context learning, and about benchmarks built on the standard single-figure format.
5. **Ranked bottom line** (~100 words): the three most important implications, most important first, one sentence each, closing with one sentence on what evidence would make you revise them.
