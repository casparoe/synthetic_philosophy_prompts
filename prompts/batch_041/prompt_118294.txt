I am assembling a set of exercises on the conceivability argument, and I want one that avoids the standard zombie duplication that every model has seen. Construct a thought experiment to the following specification. The target is a creature that is functionally identical to an ordinary human with respect to access consciousness, reportability, global broadcasting, metacognition, and every other functional fact a cognitive scientist would appeal to, yet whose phenomenal character is not absent, as in a zombie, but systematically misaligned with its functional roles: when it occupies the state that plays the pain role, it undergoes the phenomenal quality normally associated with tasting salt; when it occupies the state that plays the red-seeing role, it undergoes the phenomenal quality normally associated with hearing a middle C; and so on, with the misalignment varying across modalities while leaving every report and downstream functional transition intact. Call this the cross-wired qualia case. The case must isolate a single variable, the alignment between functional role and phenomenal character, and it must not simply stipulate that the creature has inverted experiences. Instead, the misalignment should fall out of the stipulated functional facts together with each theory’s own account of what fixes phenomenal character. I want the case to come out as follows: a strong representationalist must say the misalignment is not conceivable once the functional facts are fully specified; a higher-order thought theorist must say it is conceivable but metaphysically impossible; and a property dualist must say it is both conceivable and possible, and may use it to support the conceivability argument. If the case does not generate those verdicts from the theories themselves, revise it until it does, and tell me what you changed.

After the scenario, walk through each of the three theories in turn, stating (a) whether the case is conceivable, (b) whether it is metaphysically possible, and (c) whether it threatens the conceivability argument for dualism. Then give an honest note on which feature of the case is doing the work, what happens if that feature is removed, and how a defender of the conceivability argument would try to disarm the case. Keep the case in the vicinity of ordinary cognitive science: no new physics, no magic, no brain-in-a-vat, and no appeal to perfect predictors or demons. It may be counterfactual, but it should be described in the vocabulary of functional roles, reports, and sensory qualities rather than in the vocabulary of possible worlds alone.
