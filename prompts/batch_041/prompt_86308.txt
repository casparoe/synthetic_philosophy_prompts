I am a doctoral student in epidemiology, and I am putting together the opening session of a reading group on causal inference for people who come to the subject from quite different backgrounds — some from statistics, some from economics, some from medicine. I want to begin with a comparison that forces everyone to separate arithmetic from interpretation, and I have settled on Simpson's paradox and Lord's paradox — the latter being the situation in which two analyses of the very same data, both arithmetically beyond reproach, support opposite conclusions about whether a treatment or group difference exists, because one asks about change while the other asks about the outcome conditional on the baseline. Please compare the two: what each one actually asserts; what kind of object each is — a fact about numbers, a fact about inference, or a fact about estimands; and, the part I care about most, whether they are genuinely two phenomena or one phenomenon seen from two angles. A colleague with whom I have been arguing insists that Lord's paradox is merely Simpson's paradox in disguise, and that once one conditions on the right covariate the apparent conflict dissolves and nothing remains to be explained; I suspect this is wrong, though I recognise the two may share more than I am granting, and I want the case made carefully rather than simply asserted.

To keep the discussion concrete, here is a small table I have invented for the session. In a hospital that treats severe cases, 40 of 100 patients given the new drug survived, against 4 of 20 given the standard treatment; in a hospital that treats mild cases, 90 of 100 patients given the drug survived, against 144 of 180 given the standard treatment. Pooled across the two hospitals, 130 of 200 drug patients survived, against 148 of 200 standard-treatment patients. Use this as a test case: state precisely what it does and does not show; whether it is an instance of Simpson's paradox, of Lord's paradox, of both, or of neither; and what would have to change in the setup for it to become an instance of the other. Then tell me which of the following heuristics is defensible, and why: always adjust for every covariate one has measured; never adjust, because stratification only invites paradox; or adjust only when the covariate plays the right role in a causal structure that has been specified in advance.

Finally, if the two paradoxes do come apart, state as sharply as you can the question that Simpson's paradox leaves open and that Lord's paradox is really about, and say what would count as a resolution of each — and, if you judge that part of the disagreement between my colleague and me is merely verbal, identify which part and why. I do not need references or reading suggestions; I need the distinction itself made exact.
