Somethings been bugging me about the vagueness debate and I'd like a diagnosis instead of a verdict. Here's the pair I keep going back and forth on. The epistemicist says that in a borderline case — this patch is neither clearly red nor clearly not — the predicate either applies or it doesn't, and we simply can't know which; the sorites has a false premise, though we're in no position to say which one; bivalence and classical logic come through untouched; and "borderline" is really a label for our predicament, a fact about us. The supervaluationist says there's no fact of the matter: the predicate is true on some admissible ways of sharpening it and false on others, so the sentence is neither true nor false, while "either it's red or it isn't" still comes out true, because it's true on every sharpening — bivalence goes, classical consequence stays, and "borderline" is a status of the sentence, a fact about the language. Both of them kill the sorites. Both keep classical logic in some form. And the exchange has been running for decades with what looks like the same four moves on repeat. Supervaluationist: the epistemicist has to believe in an unknowable sharp boundary, which is supposed to be absurd. Epistemicist: the supervaluationist's "admissible sharpening" is itself vague, so either there's a sharp line between admissible and inadmissible sharpenings or the account regresses, and the framework makes "there's a last grain that still makes a heap" come out true, which nobody accepts. Supervaluationist: but the epistemicist can't explain why these cases feel unsettled, why there seems to be nothing to find out. Epistemicist: unknowable facts are everywhere — how many hairs are on your head — so why not here. I may be mangling the details, but that's the shape of it.

What set me off was higher-order vagueness. Take a gradient of patches from red to orange, one imperceptible step at a time. Pick one in the middle: it seems neither true nor false that it's red, and it also seems neither true nor false that it's *definitely* red. The epistemicist just says there are unknowable facts about which patches are definitely red too. The supervaluationist has to say which sharpenings count as admissible, and that looks like the same problem one level up. So my hypothesis, offered as a hypothesis: the two sides aren't competing answers to one question, they're answering two different ones — is there a boundary, and how does truth work for a vague predicate — and because "borderline" names an epistemic status on one side and a semantic status on the other, each side's standard objections slide past the other's actual claim. A competing hypothesis is that it's a fight about which data are authoritative: the epistemicist privileges the logic and the fact that nobody can name the false premise, the supervaluationist privileges the judgment that there's nothing to find out, and each explains away the other's datum as noise.

So tell me which it is, or whether the honest answer is that the question is ill-posed. And be careful to separate what's actually settled from what's genuinly contested among people who work on this — don't hand me one side's framing and call it the consensus. Then say what would count as progress: a formal result, a distinction, an experiment on how people judge the higher-order cases, or a reason to think no amount of work will help. Prose only, please — no headings, bullet points, or bold, just write it out the way you'd explain it in an email.
