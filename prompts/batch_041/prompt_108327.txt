I'm a science and ideas reporter — I write a lot about philosophy of mind and cognitive science — and for the past two years I've been quietly collecting material on the knowing-how versus knowing-that fight, mainly because I keep watching the two camps have the same argument at each other in slightly diffrent words. On one side are the intellectualists: Stanley, Williamson, and the crowd who took Ryle's distinction and argued that knowing how to do something just is knowing that something is the case — knowing a way of doing it, under a practical mode of presentation, or however they phrase it that week. On the other side are the Ryleans, the phenomenologists, and the people who study motor control, who think that move is a verbal trick that swallows the whole phenomenon: the whole point of skill, they say, is that it lives in the body, runs without deliberation, and often can't be said at all (that's Dreyfus's line, and roughly where the automaticity people like Fridland have pushed too). They aren't simply talking past each other, but they're not not talking past each other either, and I've got about 8,000 words of notes I can't turn into a peice.

What I actually want from you is the document I wish these two had written instead of another round of conference papers: a joint statement that an intellectualist and a Rylean could both put their names to. Open with a numbered list of what they agree on — about the cases, about what stands in need of explanation, about the psychology that isn't in dispute (nobody denies that skilled performers often can't report what they're doing; the fight is over what that shows). Then a disagreements table: one row per remaining difference, each stated in wording that the side it belongs to would accept as fair — that's the hard part, and I want you to say so honestly when a row can't be written without slanting it. Then the cruxes, and for each one the case, experiment, or argument both sides would agree bears on it, with each side's prediction recorded in advance. I have a hunch about two of them: first, whether the "practical mode of presentation" is doing any real work or is a promissory note that lets the intellectualist keep the word knowledge; second, whether automaticity is compatible with propositional guidance at all — is the proposition still in the loop when the climber actually sends, or has it just become a disposition that the word know is being stretched to cover? If you think part of the fight is purely verbal, say which part, and what they'd have to fix — how they're using propositional, implicit, occurrent versus standing — for it to dissolve.

Two things I'd like folded in, because I think they're where the standard treatment goes thin. First, coaching: I've spent time at a climbing gym, and what a coach actually does is say things like "push through your toes, stop hanging off your fingers" — and the student can recite that sentence back at you while still hanging off her fingers. If the coach is handing over a proposition, why doesn't having the proposition do the job? And if she isn't, what is being transmitted, and in what sense is it knowledge of anything? Second, the stage story from motor learning — cognitive, then associative, then automatic — where the standard telling is that the verbalizable rules fade as the skill takes over. Does that fade-out help the intellectualist (the knowledge is still there, just standing, no longer consulted) or does it expose that knowing that has been quietly redefined into something that costs nothing to have? I don't need reading lists or citations — I can't use them in the piece anyway, and I'd rather you didn't pretend to a literature you can't check — just the argument, laid out so both camps could read it without throwing it across the room. And tell me at the end how confident you are in the main claims you're making, and which single point you're least sure of, since I'd rather hand my editor an honest here's-where-I'm-shaky than a tidy summary I don't believe.
