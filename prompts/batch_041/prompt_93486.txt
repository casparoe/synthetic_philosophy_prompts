I am a fourth-year PhD student in cognitive science, and next term I will be running a single 90-minute session of an upper-level seminar on concepts and categorisation. The group is roughly eighteen students, mostly philosophy and cognitive science majors; they have read the standard material on the classical theory of concepts, on prototype and exemplar views, and a little on family resemblance, but none of them will have encountered the Letter Spirit project or anything like it.

I want to build the session around an in-class activity rather than a lecture. The idea I want them to run into, rather than be told about, is the one that the Letter Spirit project is built on — Hofstadter and McGraw's attempt to get a program to design a complete Roman alphabet in a single coherent style, where the guiding puzzle is what it is that makes every 'a' an 'a'. The tension I care about is between two things that are easy to blur when people talk about letter categories. On the one hand there are the identity conditions of the letter: what has to be true of a shape for it to count as an 'a' at all rather than a 'd', a 'q', or an unrecognisable blob — and whether there is any such set of conditions, or only a shifting resemblance to other things we have called 'a'. On the other hand there is style: the systematic, globally consistent way a particular hand or typeface renders all the letters, something that no single glyph discloses and that seems to be a property of the whole alphabet rather than of any one shape. I want students to feel that these two axes are genuinely separable, and that each is harder to pin down than it looks.

Please design the activity for me. I would like you to give me the rules, the materials (assume paper, pencils, and a whiteboard, and no computers), a minute-by-minute timeline that fits the 90 minutes, and a debrief that maps what happened onto the conceptual territory. A few things I would like the design to accomplish, though I am happy for you to achieve them in whatever way works best:

First, the students should be the ones producing the letterforms, and the puzzle should emerge from the work they do rather than from anything I say up front. Second, at some point a participant should have to extend or match a style on the basis of a single example — the point being that one glyph badly underdetermines the style, and different people will reasonably extrapolate it differently. Third, I want a moment where the group has to rule on a borderline shape and decide whether it is still the letter in question, a poorly executed version of it, or a different letter altogether, and I want that disagreement to be genuine rather than staged. Fourth, the debrief should connect specific moments in the activity to the classical picture of necessary and sufficient conditions, to prototype and exemplar treatments, and to the essence-versus-style distinction, and should be honest about where each of those frameworks does and does not get purchase on what the students just did.

I would also like you to tell me, in some detail, what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this — the failure modes that come from the setup rather than from the students — and how I should handle each of them in the moment or in the next iteration. Finally, be frank about what the activity cannot reproduce and that I will therefore have to supply myself: in particular, anything about the combinatorial search a program would have to perform to generate a whole alphabet at once, any graded or quantitative notion of how well a style holds together across all twenty-six letters, and any functional pressure from legibility or real typographic use that my paper-and-grid version will have abstracted away. If the design admits an obvious scaled-down variant for a group of eight or ten, mention it briefly at the end, but the 90-minute, eighteen-person version is the one I need to be able to run.
