For six years my doctoral supervisor has been the person our department routes its methodological disputes to, and as of last week I am the latest case on her docket. A referee has recommended rejecting my paper on the ground that its central assumption rules out the very mechanism the paper claims to identify, and my supervisor's ruling will decide whether I keep the assumption, rebuild the model, or withdraw the claim. She is away until Thursday, and I would rather walk in with a diagnosis than a plea.

Her rulings, as I have recorded them over the years, are these. A model of falling bodies that ignores air resistance, used to predict fall times for dense objects over a few metres: acceptable; the neglected force is negligible in that range. An ideal-gas model — point particles, no intermolecular forces — used to predict the pressure of a dilute gas: acceptable. A model of a chemical reaction that holds the temperature fixed, used to predict the yield of a reaction run in a water bath: acceptable. A model of price formation in which every trader has identical information, used to explain why prices overreact to news: not acceptable. A model of saving in which households borrow and lend freely at a single interest rate, used to explain why consumption tracks current income: not acceptable. A model of wage determination in which all workers are identical, used to explain the gender wage gap: not acceptable.

In every case she accepted, the model's predictions for its target were accurate and the assumption could be relaxed without disturbing the central result. In every case she rejected, the model could not generate its target phenomenon at all, the mechanism named in the paper was absent from the model, and relaxing the assumption destroyed the central result. Her stated reasons do not line up quite so neatly, though: when she accepts, she talks about how little the neglected factor matters in the range where the model is used; when she rejects, she talks about the model's inability to generate the phenomenon. I have always taken her for an F-twist in the plain sense — that the realism of a model's assumptions is irrelevant to appraising it and only the accuracy of its predictions matters — but her rejections give me pause.

My case is this. I model the evolution of cooperation. The paper's claim is that cooperation is sustained by assortment: individuals interacting repeatedly with the same neighbours. My tractable model assumes that in every period each individual is matched at random with someone drawn from the whole population, which is false (the populations in question really are spatially structured) and which, as the referee says, removes the very mechanism the paper names. Two further facts: calibrated to the field data, the model tracks observed cooperation rates closely; and when I restore neighbourhood structure in a tractable extension, my headline estimate moves by about two per cent, well inside the confidence interval.

Will she tell me the model is unacceptable, or only that my prose is? Which rule is she running? Which rival rules fit her earlier rulings equally well, what does each predict here, and which case — if I could put one more to her — would tell them apart? Lead with your verdict in your first sentence, then give your reasons; no preamble, and please do not restate my question.
