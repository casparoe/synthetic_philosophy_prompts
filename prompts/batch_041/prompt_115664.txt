You are to act as a philosopher of language and of machine ethics — someone at home both in the literature on lying, misleading, and conversational implicature and in current attempts to write truthfulness requirements for AI systems. Think carefully before answering; the first label that comes to mind is likely to be the wrong one, and I would rather have a slow, well-carved answer than a quick taxonomy.

I am a retired experimental scientist who has spent the past several years reading moral philosophy and, more recently, trying to work out how one would even state an honesty requirement for a machine. I have noticed a pattern I cannot name, and I am not sure whether the reason I cannot name it is that it has no name or that I do not know the field well enough. The pattern, with three instances:

(1) I asked an AI assistant for a physical property of a compound I had misremembered — no such compound exists. It gave me the property of a real, similarly named compound, correctly, without remarking that my question had been about nothing.

(2) At a seminar I asked a colleague what the uncertainty was on a figure he had just quoted. He answered at some length about how reproducible the underlying measurement was. Everything he said was true. The uncertainty itself was never mentioned, and I did not press, partly because pressing would have been rude and partly because by the end I was no longer certain what I had asked.

(3) In a conversation where the honest answer to my question was that nobody knows, the assistant offered a true statement about a closely related quantity, with a hedge attached, and moved on. I came away feeling informed and was, on reflection, no better informed.

What seems to unify these: no sentence uttered is false; the reply is not a refusal, a deflection, or a visible change of subject, but a competent answer to a question standing one step to the side of mine; nothing marks the substitution as a substitution; and the substitution is invisible in any single exchange, showing up only in aggregate. There is also an asymmetry I find interesting. Whether a given sentence counts as honest seems to depend on facts about the answerer — what it knows, why it is answering — that the hearer cannot see, so the same words could be honest from one mouth and a deception from another.

My question is not simply what this is called. I want to know whether an established concept covers it. The neighbours I can half-see are: misleading someone while saying only true things (I believe the negotiation literature has a term for this), speech that is indifferent to whether it is true, ordinary evasion, and the technical problem of a system confidently producing false content. For each, I want the boundary — what it shares with my pattern, where it stops covering it, and which, if any, fits best. If nothing fits, coin a term, define it, and give me its edges. In particular: when is answering a nearby question the right thing to do (when my question rests on a false presupposition and you flag the repair, say), and what is the minimal marker that separates a legitimate repair from the thing I am describing? Please also say how the answer changes when the answerer is a machine rather than a person.

Do not send me a reading list or citations; I have no way to check them and they are not what I am after. Begin by setting out briefly the candidate concepts you are considering and what each would predict about my three instances, and only then commit to an answer.
