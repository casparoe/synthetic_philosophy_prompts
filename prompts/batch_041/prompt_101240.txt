I have recently joined a research group that spans philosophy of language and cognitive science, and I am struck by how completely one argument has stalled: whether the content of a thought is fixed by what is inside the thinker's head or by the thinker's surroundings. Both sides know each other's cases by heart, and after a year of weekly meetings nobody has moved. My hypothesis, offered as a hypothesis, is that they are not disagreeing about one thing: the externalist is right, and nearly trivially so, about what fixes the truth or falsity of a belief and the reference of a word, while the internalist is asking a different question, about what content must be like if it is to explain why someone acts as they do. If that is right, the dispute is partly verbal — a tug of war over the word content — and partly two groups answering different questions under one heading. Diagnose it rather than adjudicate it: say what kind of disagreement this really is, in terms of what each side is actually committed to; whether the two questions can be prised apart without loss, or whether the internalist's question collapses into the externalist's under pressure; and what, if anything, would count as progress. To keep this concrete, take a case of my own devising: two people identical inside and out, in surroundings that are physically identical, but whose communities have always applied a certain word differently at the margins, so that one counts a particular borderline specimen as a member of the kind and the other does not, and neither person knows which practice they inherited. Does that case do any work, or does it merely restate the original puzzle?

Give me the diagnosis as a numbered list of exactly five items, each a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion.
