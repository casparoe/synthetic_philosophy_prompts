I am a novelist — four books, all of them written in close third person, all of them staked on the proposition that a reader can be brought to know another mind from the inside — and I have spent the past two years reading philosophy of mind in a semi-disciplined way. I have arrived at a position, and before I write an essay about it I want to know whether it is a position or merely a heap of things I happen to find attractive. I would like you to act as a philosopher of mind with a particular interest in illusionist accounts of consciousness and in the metaphysics of the self, and to give me an audit rather than a survey. Please think carefully. Begin by setting out, in your own words, what each item below most naturally commits me to — the entailments I may not have noticed — and only then start judging how the items sit together. I do not want a reading list or citations; I want the joints located.

Here is where I have landed:

1. I am an illusionist about phenomenal consciousness. I do not think there is any intrinsic felt quality of experience over and above the cognitive and functional facts; the redness of red and the ache of an ache are representations that misrepresent their own character.

2. I am a physicalist. There are no extra ingredients in the world, and nothing about minds requires one.

3. I do not believe in an inner observer. The self is a model the organism builds, not a thing that watches the model; there is no subject distinct from the self-representation.

4. What gives a person moral standing is that there is something it is like to be them. A being with no felt experience could not be wronged in the way a person can.

5. I am drawn to the enactivist thought that experience is a matter of skilled, embodied engagement with a world rather than an inner picture of it.

6. The value of the novel, as I understand it, is that it lets a reader know what it is like to be someone else, and that this is a genuine piece of knowledge and not a pleasant fiction.

What I want from you is a consistency audit, not a summary of the literature. Which pairs among these are actually in tension, and which only look as though they are? Rank the real tensions by severity rather than treating them all as equally bad, and for each one say what would have to give and what giving it up would cost. Then tell me which single revision would clean up the most at the least expense, and be explicit about what I would be buying with it. I would also like you to be honest about your own confidence: where the tension is a matter of contested interpretation rather than outright contradiction, say so, and do not present a live disagreement among philosophers as settled.

Finally, if the list is not really a set of commitments at all — if two items are the same commitment in different clothing, or if I have quietly assumed something I have not listed and the whole audit therefore rests on a premise I never stated — say so plainly. I would rather be told that the task is confused than be flattered with frictions invented to fill the page.
