I am assembling a proposal for a small, philosophy-only research grant and would like you to draft the proposal document itself, roughly 1,500 to 2,000 words. The project would run for one semester, single investigator, no empirical data collection. The subject is escalation of commitment as it shows up in the dollar-auction setup: an auction in which the prize is worth, say, a dollar, bidding proceeds in small increments, and the rule that makes it notorious is that the highest bidder pays his bid and takes the prize while the second-highest bidder also pays his bid and gets nothing. Each bidder, once in, faces the prospect of paying for nothing, and the two of them can bid past the point where the prize is worth anything to either.

My half-formed idea is this, and I want the proposal built around turning it into a real question. Discussions of the dollar auction tend to file the whole thing under one heading: irrational escalation, the sunk-cost fallacy wearing a game-theoretic costume. I suspect that heading is doing too much work, because at least three distinct things can drive a bidder to keep bidding and they are not the same phenomenon. First, a failure of foresight: the agent would have been better off never entering, and in hindsight can see it. Second, a genuine local rationality: once you are in, quitting means paying and getting nothing, so continuing is the better move from where you now stand, whatever you should have done before. Third, a structural transfer: the reason the game is bad for bidders is not any mistake of theirs but that the payoff rule routes money from the bidders to the auctioneer, so the pot is negative-sum before anyone has decided anything, and a pair of perfectly foresighted, mutually transparent agents would still be playing a losing game. I want the proposal to pull these apart and to make available the question the pulling-apart generates: when we call dollar-auction escalation irrational, are we describing a defect in the bidders' reasoning, or are we mislabeling a bad game — a structure that penalizes even agents who reason flawlessly and know each other to do so? Stated so that it has a determinate answer: is there a principled criterion for distinguishing an escalation trap that is an artifact of the payoff structure from one that is an artifact of human psychology, and does the dollar auction fall on the structural side? I am inclined to think it does, and that the standard irrationality verdict survives only for the first of my three drivers, but I hold that loosely and want the proposal to make the case arguable rather than assumed.

Please structure the document as a proposal rather than an essay. It should state the research question precisely enough that one could say what would settle it; explain why the existing treatments of escalation leave the question open, referring to positions and arguments rather than to any particular author's text; lay out the method — what I would actually do across a semester, in stages, given that the work is conceptual and needs no data; say what a successful result would look like and, just as important, what would count as the project failing or turning out to be empty; and give an honest assessment of scope, including whether the question is too large or too small for the time and what I should cut if it is. Assume the audience is a small interdisciplinary review panel that will fund either a sharp negative result or a useful piece of conceptual housekeeping, but not a survey. Do not include a bibliography, reading list, or citations of any kind; I will supply those myself.
