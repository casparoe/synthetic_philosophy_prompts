I'm an undergraduate taking a module on Wittgenstein and the philosophy of language, and I've run aground on the rule-following material. I can reproduce the sceptical paradox in an essay well enough: no fact about my past applications, my training, my dispositions or my inner images determines what I ought to do next, because any finite run of past applications is compatible with continuations that diverge from what I would call the correct one. The trouble is that I can't feel it. Whenever I write it up, it comes out as a form of scepticism I don't actually hold, and I suspect that this is because I've only ever met the paradox in its arithmetic dress — the familiar example of a function that agrees with addition on everything so far and then diverges. I'd like you to write me a short story that does the work my essay can't, so that I can see the thing in a practice rather than in a formula.

Set it in an English parish bell tower, among a band of change-ringers. Explain briefly, as one ringer might explain it to a novice, what a method is: a rule that takes the row currently being rung and yields the next one, so that the band is always applying a rule whose past applications are recorded in the tower's peal book but which has to be applied afresh on every pull. Invent your own method and your own divergence. Do not reuse the arithmetic example, and do not have anyone in the story mention a philosopher, a book, or a paradox by name. The divergence should be native to the practice: a ringer who produces, at a point the band has never previously reached, a row that agrees with every recorded row and with every correction the band has ever made, yet which the rest of the band wants to call a mistake. He should be sincere and entirely convinced that he is ringing the method — the point is that conviction and sincerity settle nothing.

In scenes rather than commentary, the story should do the following.

1. Make it vivid that the divergence is not settled by anything that has happened yet. Show the band hunting for the fact that would settle it — the peal book, the conductor's calls, the old ringer's memory of how it goes, the diagram of the method pinned up in the ringing chamber, the new man's own intention to be following the method — and show each of these turning out to be one more thing that itself stands in need of a reading. The diagram especially: a picture of a rule is not the rule.

2. Give the dispositional answer its best case, and then break it. One character should argue that the new ringer is simply disposed to ring differently, so that there is no real dispute about what the method requires. Have another show why this will not do: our dispositions are finite, and they include our slips, our fatigue and our lapses, so what I am disposed to do cannot be the standard by which my lapses are counted as lapses — and it is exactly the possibility of being wrong that a dispositional account cannot recover.

3. Show the communal answer working, without pretending that it is a proof. The band does not refute the new ringer; it corrects him, again and again, until he rings with them. The reader should feel that this is what following the method comes to, that it is the practice of correction and agreement rather than any fact inside anyone's head, and that this describes how the practice carries on rather than demonstrating that the sceptic was wrong. Let one character — the conductor, or the oldest ringer — gesture at this without turning it into a lecture: the thought that the band does not agree because they all see the same thing, but rather that what counts as seeing the same thing is constituted by their agreeing. Phrase it in your own words; I want no quotations from any text anywhere in the piece.

4. Include the private case. Have one ringer who has been practising alone in a tower with no band, keeping a private notation of what the method really does, and let the reader see that nothing he writes down can distinguish his being right from his merely seeming to himself to be right. Do not resolve his case.

5. End without resolving the paradox. No epiphany, no narrator's aside, no closing paragraph explaining what we have learned. The last scene should be a peal being rung, the band together and the correction happening, so that the reader feels both that the practice is entirely solid and that nothing underneath it has been secured.

Form: roughly 1,000–1,200 words, third person, past tense, one limited point of view — a ringer who is neither the new man nor the oldest one — dialogue-heavy, no section headings, no epigraph, no quotations. A reader who has never heard of the rule-following problem should be able to read it simply as a story about a bell tower; a reader who has should be able to see the moves being made, and should ideally wince at how neatly the band's solution is also its evasion.
