I am a high school student preparing a session for my school's philosophy club on the doomsday argument, and I would like you to write me a decision tree that sorts a reader into a position on it, along with a brief note on how the tree is meant to be used.

Assume the reader arrives knowing only the bare argument: you are one human among all the humans who will ever live, and if that total turns out to be enormous, then it is a remarkable coincidence that you should be as early as roughly the hundred-billionth of them; the improbability of that coincidence is supposed to make a very long future unlikely. Every node of the tree should be a question such a reader could answer without knowing the names of any positions, and each answer should lead to the next question and eventually to a leaf. Each leaf should carry the name of the position, or the label unnamed where no one defends it, and one sentence naming the commitment that position has the hardest time defending.

Please make sure the tree can reach at least the following positions: that the argument is sound and the end is near; that the argument is void because there is no coherent class from which you could count as a random member; that the argument is cancelled by the opposite principle of self-location, roughly that you should expect to find yourself in a world containing more observers; that facts about where you sit in the world cannot be evidence about how large the world is; that the conclusion is an artifact of the prior probability you feed in; and that the argument succeeds but delivers only a shift in credence rather than a date. I would also like branches for two matters that seem to me under-discussed: whether it changes anything that you know the date of your birth, given that almost everyone alive now shares the same narrow band of birth ranks; and whether the argument survives if the total number of future observers might be infinite with some small probability. Treat the choice of reference class — all humans ever born, all observers, all sentient beings — as a branch of its own, and let it lead wherever it leads.

Build the tree from the questions first and locate the positions in that space afterwards, so that combinations no one occupies are visible, and say of each empty combination whether it looks incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. Where two positions end up at the same leaf, flag that the tree cannot separate them. Keep it to roughly eight to twelve leaves, cite nothing, and assume no background beyond the argument itself.
