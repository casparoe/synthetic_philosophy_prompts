I am assembling a small anthology of fiction for a graduate seminar that reads stories alongside problems in decision theory, and I have been unable to find one that dramatizes a particular idea — the one that circulates under the name updateless decision theory — without either turning into a lecture or smuggling in a diagram. So I am commissioning a story. Let me say what I take the idea to be; I am not asking you to explain it, but to build a plot that could only have been built by someone who had absorbed it and then put the vocabulary away. Ordinary decision theory treats your present situation as a premise and asks which act, from here, has the best consequences. The updateless idea asks a different question — which policy you should have adopted before you knew which of the situations your life would put you in — and then refuses to let the answer be revised merely because you have since learned which situation you are in; discovering where you stand is not, on this view, a reason to become a different chooser. Two features of the picture matter for the story. The first is that a policy is not the private property of the moment at which it is chosen: the same policy may be running in other people, in other agents, in earlier or later versions of you, and because each of them is running it, settling on a policy is settling what all of them do. The agreement among them is not produced by anything passing between them; it is produced by their being the same chooser. The second is that your present decision has no special standing merely because it is present, and yours.

The setting I have in mind, though you may substitute a better one, is a chain of way-stations on a hard winter road, staffed by keepers trained at a single school on a single written rule about sharing stores with travellers and with one another — a rule that nothing enforces, since the stations are days apart, there is no traffic in the dead season, and no keeper can ever learn what the others did. Put one keeper at the centre of the story in the year the rule becomes expensive for her: the moment she learns which station she has drawn, and what keeping the rule will cost her there. Give her a companion or a stranded visitor who presses the obvious objection — that a policy cannot bind you once you know you would be better off abandoning it, and that no one will ever be the wiser, so what is the difference between the rule and a mood she has outgrown? — and let her answer be an argument rather than a sentiment. I want the correlation between choosers to do real work in the plot, and I want it to do that work without any channel: no rumour travelling down the road, no letter, no retaliation to be feared, no god keeping score. What she settles on at her station has to bear on what the others settle on at theirs for the only reason the theory allows — that they are the same kind of chooser she is.

Two thousand to three thousand five hundred words, with a title. No epilogue, no author's note, and no character who exists in order to expound the idea; the reader should be able to reconstruct the view from what the characters want and refuse. Keep the metaphysics ordinary — no duplication machines, no simulations, no time travel, nothing that solves the problem by putting two copies of one person in a room. The temptation should be concrete enough, and costly enough, that the objection has real force, and the ending should not be a vindication: I would rather the reader finish unable to say with confidence whether the keeper's reasoning is wisdom or an elegant trap she has built for herself. If the theory's name appears in the story at all, let it be a name someone half-remembers from a course.
