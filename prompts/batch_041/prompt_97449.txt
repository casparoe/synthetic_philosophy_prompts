I teach a final-year undergraduate seminar on Madhyamaka thought and I am drafting the final examination. I need one essay question on Nāgārjuna's tetralemma (catuṣkoṭi) that cannot be answered by reciting the familiar line that Nāgārjuna rejects all four corners — the true, the false, the both, and the neither — and that emptiness therefore is not itself a view. To force candidates past that recitation, the question should give them a small formal apparatus and make them test it against the tetralemma rather than summarize the tradition.

The apparatus, which I will print on the exam sheet, is a stipulative four-valued semantics with values T (true), F (false), B (both true and false), and N (neither true nor false); negation is fixed at B and N, so ~B = B and ~N = N; and conjunction obeys T∧x = x, F∧x = F, B∧B = B, B∧N = F, and N∧N = N. I will note on the sheet that the table is a stipulation and that candidates may challenge it.

The question should require candidates to work out, at minimum: (i) whether the tetralemma is best modelled as assigning these four values in turn to a single predicate, and if so which value a Madhyamika should be said to land on, or whether the whole four-value frame is being dissolved; (ii) whether the both corner is a genuine third value or merely the conjunction of two contradictory assertions, and whether the conjunction table above settles that or begs it; (iii) whether the neither corner amounts to a denial of the law of excluded middle or in fact presupposes it; and (iv) how the classical contrast between a negation that merely cancels a predicate and a negation that denies it while still presupposing its intelligibility — the paryudāsa/prasajya distinction, which I will gloss on the sheet in plain English rather than by name — bears on whether N can be the value of emptiness at all.

Please write the exam question itself, with a one-line rubric telling candidates what is being assessed; a model answer of about 700 words; and a marking scheme built around five must-hit points with marks summing to 25, a one-line note per point on how candidates typically miss it, and an explicit statement of the two moves that separate an A answer from a B answer. The difficulty should come from the crux — whether any assignment of truth values can express a prasajya negation — not from the labels.

End the model answer with a short, non-hedging paragraph on its own weakest joint: name the single argument or consideration that would most plausibly push it to the opposite verdict, and state what that verdict would be. Commit to a verdict in the body of the answer; do not scatter caveats through it.
