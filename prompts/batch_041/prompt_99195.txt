I'm in a graduate seminar on Holocaust historiography, and the argument that will not die is the one between the two standard explanations of the perpetrators. On one account, men like those of Reserve Police Battalion 101 — middle-aged, working-class, drawn from Hamburg, judged unfit for regular military service and hardly selected for ideological zeal — were pulled into mass killing by situational forces: deference to authority, the momentum of gradual brutalization, peer pressure, careerism, and the ordinary cost of stepping out of line; the men were not monsters, and a handful did step out, which is precisely what makes the story about the situation rather than the person. On the other account, the same participation is explained instead by a specific, historically rooted antisemitic political culture that made the killing not merely tolerated but positively willed, so that these were not ordinary men in any recognizable sense but ordinary members of an extraordinary culture, and no situational mechanism is needed once you see the ideology. Both sides work from substantially the same trial testimony and the same battalion records, both concede that a minority refused or shirked, and both can absorb almost any new piece of evidence without being moved, so the seminar keeps restating its priors with increasing force.

My tentative hypothesis is that this is not one disagreement but a collision of two, and that each side is answering a question the other never asked. One question is why these particular men, who were not fanatics, came to kill at all — a question about how participation is even possible for people like us, for which a situational answer seems apt. The other is why the killing took the shape and extent it did — why this unit, this nation, these victims — a question about pattern and variation, for which a cultural answer seems apt. If that is right, then neither account can refute the other, because each is measuring success against a different standard: one asks whether the account supplies a sufficient condition, the other whether it supplies a difference-maker. I have also noticed that the two sides treat different counterfactuals as the decisive test — what would these men have done under a different political culture, versus what would other men have done under this one — and that they may be describing different objects altogether, a particular act versus an aggregate rate of participation.

What I want is a diagnosis, not a verdict, and not a survey of who said what. Is this impasse verbal, substantive, or a case of two distinct questions traveling under one heading? Is some hidden empirical or conceptual question doing the real work — say, the long-running dispute about whether reasons can be causes, or about what an explanation of an individual action, as opposed to a statistical pattern of actions, is even supposed to deliver? And given your diagnosis, what if anything would count as progress: a distinction that splits the original question, a specified comparison that could actually discriminate between the two accounts, or an argument that no progress should be expected here because the participants are not in fact contradicting one another? Ground the diagnosis in what each side actually claims, and tell me honestly whether the dispute is well-posed, merely hard, or something the field should stop treating as a single argument. I don't need a reading list or citations; I want the structure of the disagreement and your reasons for reading it that way.
