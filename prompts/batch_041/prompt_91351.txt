I have no academic background in computer science or philosophy, but I have been trying to understand the debate over whether Solomonoff induction and Kolmogorov complexity give a principled foundation for Occam's razor, and I cannot tell whether the people involved are actually disagreeing about anything substantive. The setup, as I understand it, is this. Assign each possible hypothesis a prior probability that decreases with the length of the shortest program that computes it, and then update those probabilities as data come in. Because shorter programs get more prior weight, the procedure seems to formalize the idea that simpler hypotheses are more likely. A theorem is supposed to show that the choice of universal machine does not matter, because switching machines changes the Kolmogorov complexity of any string by at most an additive constant. Another result is supposed to show that if the true environment is computable, the procedure converges on it with probability one. And yet the debate seems stuck. One side says this is the best available formalization of Occam's razor and a genuine solution to the problem of induction in the limit. The other side says it is either circular or empty: the prior is not unique, the invariance theorem only guarantees agreement up to a constant that can be arbitrarily large, the procedure cannot actually be computed, and real inquiry never begins with a prior over all computable hypotheses. Each side seems to understand the other's points, and yet neither moves.

My hypothesis is that this is not a disagreement about the mathematics but about what a justification is supposed to do. One side is asking whether there is an ideal Bayesian reasoner who would converge on the truth under certain assumptions. The other is asking whether that idealization explains why actual scientists should prefer simpler theories. If that is right, then the two sides are answering different questions under one heading. But I am not sure. Maybe a hidden formal issue is doing the work: perhaps the additive constant is not a technicality but the whole substance, because an asymptotic guarantee says nothing about finite data and every interesting case is finite. Or perhaps the real disagreement is about what simplicity means. I have been distinguishing algorithmic simplicity, which is the length of a program that generates the data, from usable simplicity, which is the length of a description in a language a scientist can actually work with. Solomonoff induction seems to formalize the first. Scientific practice seems to trade in the second. Could the impasse be that one side is asking whether algorithmic simplicity is objective while the other is asking whether it tracks usable simplicity? Or perhaps the sides differ about whether uncomputability is a defect or just a sign that the theory is an idealization, like a frictionless plane.

I want a diagnosis, not a survey. Tell me what kind of disagreement this is, using what each side actually claims. Is it substantive, verbal, or two questions under one name? Is there a hidden empirical or formal question that would settle it? And given your diagnosis, what would count as progress: a new theorem, a distinction between kinds of simplicity, a restriction to computable agents, or an argument that no progress should be expected because the question was ill-posed from the start? I would also like to know which side, if either, is misrepresenting the other, and what the most charitable version of each side looks like. Please do not give me a reading list; I am trying to understand the structure of the dispute, not to write a paper.
