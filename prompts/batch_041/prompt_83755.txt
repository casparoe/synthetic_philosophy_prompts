A friend in my reading group stitched this together from something he half-remembers, and it's been bugging me all week.

(1) Someone has a reason to do a thing only if a sound deliberative route leads from his existing motivational set to a motivation to do it. (2) Sound deliberation can correct false beliefs, sloppy reasoning and means-end incoherence, but it can't manufacture a brand-new terminal end. (3) Now meet Dermot: he knows every fact about the pain he causes, he's perfectly consistent, and nothing in him — no sympathy, no vanity, no wish to see himself as decent — pushes against it. (4) There's no sound deliberative route from Dermot's set to a motivation to stop. (5) So he has no reason to stop. (6) And if he has no reason to stop, then nothing anyone says to him is an argument; it's an attempt to install a desire, and moral criticism of him is just a politer version of that. (7) Same story for the man who doesn't care what happens to his own future. (8) That can't be right, so (1) is false.

Here's my problem. Drop (1) and I owe an account of how a consideration can be a reason for someone it leaves completely cold, and of what makes it bind him — you can't fault a man for failing to be moved by something that, by hypothesis, doesn't touch him. Keep (1) and I'm stuck with (6). My group says (2) is the weak link: Dermot simply isn't imagining what he's doing properly, and if he were, a motivation would show up. That feels like cheating, though I can't say exactly why.

Take a definite line: which step goes, why did it look fine, and what does dropping it cost me? Don't hand me the menu — pick one and defend it. Under 400 words, and in British English.
