I'm a novelist, mostly historical fiction, and I'm working on a weird little book where David Hume gets reanimated in 2025 and has to make sense of the modern science of religion. I've been reading alot of popular cognitive science of religion stuff for background, and I want to predict how Hume would react, not just summarize what he wrote. I know his Natural History of Religion, from 1757, argued that polytheism came first and that religion grew out of hope and fear and the desire to control the future, and that monotheism got magnified through competition between religions. But I'm interested in what he'd do with stuff that came after him.

Here's the stuff I want him to grapple with, and I'll spell it out so you don't have to look anything up. There's the by-product view: religion isn't an adaptation, it's a side effect of ordinary cognitive biases, like our tendency to detect agents where there aren't any, which people call hyperactive agency detection, and our tendency to remember minimally counterintuitive concepts. There's the Mickey Mouse problem: if agency detection just makes us believe in invisible agents, why do we worship gods and not Mickey Mouse or the Easter Bunny? There's the Big Gods hypothesis: belief in moralizing, all-powerful gods helped early humans scale up cooperation and punish free riders. There's theological incorrectness: people's explicit theology says God is omniscient and omnipotent, but when they actually reason about God in real time, they use a much more anthropomorphic, limited agent. There's credibility-enhancing displays: costly public acts that make religious commitment believable to others. And there's predictive processing: the brain as a prediction machine, and religion as a way of dealing with uncertainty and prediction error.

What I want from you is a prediction of Hume's views. Would he see the by-product view as a vindication of his natural history or as a replacement for it? Where would he say the cognitive science is just re-describing what he already said in 1757, and where would he say it's genuinely new? How would he handle the Mickey Mouse problem, would he treat it as a serious anomaly for by-product theories or as a pseudo-problem? Would he accept the Big Gods story about cooperation, or would he think it gets the causal order backwards? And what would he make of theological incorrectness, does it support his skepticism about the rational content of religion, or does it complicate his account of religion as a response to fear and hope? Finally, I'm curious whether he'd think these empirical hypotheses change the philosophical game. He was doing natural history before there was a science of the mind, so would he welcome the lab coats or think they're missing the point? Please write it in American English, and definately don't give me a bibliography or reading list, just the prediction and the reasoning.
