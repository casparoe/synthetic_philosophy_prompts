I am a high school student writing a paper for my school's philosophy club on whether it is wrong to eat animals, and I have been reading an essay by Plutarch, from the first or second century CE, against eating flesh. I do not have the text with me, only the substance of the argument as I copied it down, and I would like help understanding the argument itself rather than the translation. As I have it, Plutarch argues roughly this. Since the earth yields crops in plenty, we do not need flesh to survive, so the killing is done for appetite or luxury rather than necessity. We are also arbitrary about which animals we kill, sparing the savage and dangerous ones and slaughtering the gentle and useful ones. Our bodies are not built for a flesh diet, since we have no claws or hooked beaks and our teeth are flat, and we cannot eat flesh raw without cooking and seasoning it. Animals are not mere things: they perceive, they fear, they struggle, and they have something at least analogous to reason. The first killing of an animal was a kind of crime, and habit has numbed us to it. And if each of us had to do the killing ourselves, with our own hands, most of us would recoil and refuse.

What I cannot work out is which of these steps is doing the real work, and whether the argument survives when some of them are removed. Is the claim about animal reason load-bearing, or does perception alone carry the conclusion? Is Plutarch trying to show that we wrong the animals, or that we degrade ourselves? And is he arguing from necessity, from the character of the eater, or from what the animal is owed?

This matters to me because when I try to extend the argument I get stuck. A friend pointed out that if everyone stopped eating meat, we would still grow crops, and growing crops kills a great many small animals in the fields, whereas a single large animal feeds many people for a long time, so that by some counts a diet heavy in beef might end fewer animal lives per calorie than a diet heavy in grain. I cannot tell whether Plutarch's argument condemns that arrangement or passes over it, and the answer seems to depend on which of his premises is essential.

Please reconstruct the argument as charitably as you can, say which premise supports which conclusion, and tell me what it can and cannot establish about the arithmetic case my friend raised. I would also like you to state how confident you are in each part of your reading, and to single out the one point you are least sure about.
