I am trying to find the principle behind my intuitions about what the criminal law may forbid, and my verdicts will not line up. A competent, terminally ill adult who asks a physician to help him die should be able to get that help lawfully. Two adult siblings who choose a sexual relationship should not be prosecuted. A healthy adult who volunteers for an experiment that will certainly kill him, in order to save thousands, should be permitted to do it. A surgeon who amputates a healthy limb at the competent patient's request should not go to prison, though I am uneasy about it. A consenting adult who contracts to be beaten severely, and is, should be left alone. But a duel to the death between two consenting adults should be a crime; bestiality should be a crime even though I cannot name a victim; and a man who sells a contract on his own life, consenting to be killed for a fee paid to his family, should be prosecuted even though he is competent and willing. The last three verdicts sit badly with the first five, and I suspect the whole list is just the old quarrel between the harm principle and the enforcement of a shared morality showing up in my gut. Is there one principle that keeps them all? My own tentative suggestion is that the state may criminalize conduct when it either sets back the interests of someone who cannot or does not consent, or degrades the meaning of a practice on which the participants themselves depend; but I cannot tell whether that second clause does honest work or merely smuggles in disgust. Test the suggestion against the cases, say which of my cases forces each clause, tell me which verdict, if any, I should give up, and say what the surviving principle implies about a case I have not listed: a televised league in which adults consent in advance to be killed in combat, with the proceeds going to their families.
