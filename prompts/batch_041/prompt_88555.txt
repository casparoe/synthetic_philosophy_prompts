I have been in a reading group on philosophy of mind for a term, and we keep returning to the cluster of ideas around Dennett's three stances without ever, I think, getting to the bottom of it. What I want explained is the conceptual issue that the notion of a real pattern is supposed to resolve. As I understand the proposal — and correct me if this is a caricature — the intentional stance is a predictive strategy we adopt towards a system, and the beliefs and desires it licenses are not mere fictions because the patterns they describe are real patterns: there is a way of describing the data in those terms that is more economical than simply listing the data, and the pattern is there whether or not anyone notices it, so we get a realism about belief-talk that stops short of positing inner causes or inner representations with determinate content. Two things about this I cannot fit together. First, the relativity worry: economical description is economical relative to a vocabulary, and there is always some vocabulary in which almost any data set can be described compactly, so what stops the criterion from admitting a gerrymandered pattern that compresses beautifully and predicts nothing? Second, the stance worry: if pattern-hood is settled by the availability of a compact description, the physical, design and intentional stances begin to look like three description languages competing over the same data, and the question whether a system really has beliefs becomes a question about which description is most economical for us — which sounds like instrumentalism wearing a realist's coat. I want to see what work the appeal to real patterns does that an unembarrassed instrumentalism about belief-talk would not do.

To make the difficulty concrete, here is a case we invented and could not settle. Take a system trained by gradient descent to play a board game. Suppose the most economical description anyone can find of its internal activations uses the vocabulary of what it takes its opponent to be about to do, that this description predicts its moves far better than any description in terms of unit weights and activations, and that it continues to do so when the system is retrained from scratch with different initialisations. Suppose further that a rival description — one that refers to particular weight values and to the system's position within one specific training run — compresses the same data slightly better, but does not survive retraining. Which description, if either, is the real pattern, and what does the answer imply about whether the system has beliefs? And if it turns out that the pattern is sustained only by features of the environment as much as by features of the system, is it still a pattern in the system at all?

I would like the issue set out as you would to someone who knows the standard moves but has never seen them properly assembled: what the criterion is buying, how it is meant to handle both the relativity worry and the stance worry, and whether the intentional stance's patterns are real in the same sense as the design stance's, or whether the stances differ in something other than their ontology. I would rather have your honest uncertainty than a confident survey. Tell me where the account is most likely to break down, and which argument or consideration would lead you to give a different account of what real-pattern realism amounts to.
