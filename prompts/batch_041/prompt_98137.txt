I am putting together a handout for a graduate seminar on epistemic logic, and I want to give my students a decision tree that organizes the responses to the problem of logical omniscience — the fact that if we model an agent's knowledge as the set of possible worlds left open by what she knows, she automatically knows every logical truth and every consequence of what she knows, so that no one can be ignorant of a theorem or fail to see an implication. I want the tree to run from a single innocent starting commitment — that an agent knows a proposition just in case it holds in every world compatible with what she knows — to the full range of positions on whether that idealization is tolerable and, if not, what should take its place. Build it from the logical space rather than from a list of authors: first identify the questions on which the positions genuinely come apart, then locate the positions in the resulting space. Please do not give me citations, reading lists, or attributions; I only want the structure, and I would rather have positions described than named.

Each branch node should be a question that a reader can answer without already knowing where it leads. I think at least these distinctions belong on the tree, though add any you think are missing: whether deductive closure is a requirement or a defect; whether the object of knowledge is a proposition or a sentence/formula the agent must have a grip on; whether the semantics needs impossible or non-normal worlds, or worlds in which a logical truth can fail; whether awareness or relevance is a primitive separate from truth at the worlds; whether knowing is a state or a process that consumes some resource; and whether the agent's mind is one integrated store or a collection of loosely connected fragments. For the concrete cases, use at least one like this: an agent who has before her every premise of a valid argument, correctly and explicitly believed, but who has never drawn the conclusion — every position should have something to say about what she does and does not know there, and about whether iterated knowledge (knowing that one knows, and so on) ever bottoms out.

The leaves I need to see are: the view that simply accepts the idealization as harmless; the view that splits implicit from explicit knowledge by adding an awareness component; impossible-worlds semantics; a relevance-based account that narrows what follows from what; a procedural or resource-bounded account on which knowing is a matter of having an algorithm or a proof; a fragmented-mind account; and a deflationary view on which the problem is an artifact of the modeling choice rather than a fact about knowers. Aim for six to nine branch nodes and six to nine leaves. Where a combination of answers is left unoccupied, mark it and say briefly whether it is empty because it is incoherent, because it is unattractive, or because it is merely unexplored; and if two leaves are not separable by your tree, say so explicitly rather than pretending that the branch meant to divide them does.
