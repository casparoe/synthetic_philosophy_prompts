I am a novelist at work on a book about a man destroyed by a run of decisions that are each defensible when he makes them: no folly, no concealed vice, only the ordinary logic of a person who wants different things at different times. If the trap is not real, the book is a cheat. So before I stage his ruin, I need to know what a genuine theory of diachronic rationality would have to deliver. What I want from you is the specification, not a verdict on my character: the requirements that any adequate account of when one person's choices are rational considered across time, as opposed to rational at a single instant, must satisfy.

Set out the desiderata, each with the case that forces it. I expect the list to cover at least the following, though I want you to add whatever I have missed and to strike whatever is merely the prejudice of one camp.

First, the case in which a person's preferences run in a circle, so that he prefers A to B, B to C, and C to A, and a patient counterparty can therefore relieve him of a sure loss through a sequence of trades he would accept at every step. The account must say what is defective here, and whether the defect lies in the preferences, in the choices, or only in the counterparty's presence; a theory that cannot separate these is of no use to me.

Second, a change of heart that is not an error, a man who once valued ambition and now values peace, with no ground for calling either self the mistaken one. The account must not simply crown the later self, nor the earlier.

Third, the self-binding arrangement made in advance against a later temptation, and its mirror image, the refusal to be bound and the deliberate choice to meet each moment on its own terms. The account must say which is rational, or under what conditions each is.

Fourth, the case in which a reward is offered for merely forming the intention to act later, where the act itself will remain free, so that the prize is plainly worth wanting and yet the intention cannot honestly be summoned for its sake. What must a theory of rationality say about intentions that cannot be manufactured at will?

Fifth, the unravelling of a finite chain of mutually advantageous moves, where backward reasoning appears to strip the whole sequence of its point.

Sixth, a case of my own invention, which I include because I suspect it presses the theory harder than the rest: a broker who coerces no one but instead alters what people want, so that the trades he later offers are, at the time, genuinely rational for them. Is the man pumped, or merely changed?

The account should also respect the distinction between consistency at a moment and consistency over a life; it should remain action-guiding at the moment of choice and not only in retrospect; it should leave room for a person to hold authority over his own ends; and it should not make rational conduct impossible by condemning every sequence that happens to end badly.

Then, two things I most want from you. Flag any pair or subset of these requirements that cannot be satisfied together, and say plainly which single requirement does the most to exclude the familiar candidates. And mark which requirements are non-negotiable and which are the preferences of a particular school. Close with a brief verdict on the main families of answer, the whole-life planner, the time-slice account, and the idealized-advisor picture, measured against the list in a few sentences each, since the criteria are what I need and the verdicts are only a convenience.
