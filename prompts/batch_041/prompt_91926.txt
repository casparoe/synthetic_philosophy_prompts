My cousin is writing a chapter on moral particularism and he's got this argument he calls the parity argument. I've been staring at it for a week. Here it is:

(1) A reason is a consideration that counts in favor of (or against) doing something.
(2) Whether a given consideration counts in favor of an action depends on context. The same feature — that a promise was made, that an act would cause pleasure, that someone asked me to do it — can count in favor in one case, count against in another, and be totally irrelevant in a third.
(3) So no consideration counts in favor of anything just by virtue of being the kind of consideration it is.
(4) So there are no true general statements of the form "considerations of kind K count in favor of actions of kind A."
(5) A moral principle just is a true general statement of that form.
(6) So there are no moral principles. Particularism wins.

And here's the part he thinks is the sting. He says nothing in (1)–(5) is special to morality. Run the same thing for epistemic reasons: whether "it seems obviously true to me" counts in favor of believing something depends on context too. Same for prudential reasons, aesthetic ones, reasons of etiquette. So (4) generalizes to every domain: there are no general truths about what counts in favor of what. But by (1) that just means no consideration is a reason for anything, in any context. So there are no reasons at all.

He treats that as a reductio and concludes particularism is false — we need principles after all. I agree the conclusion is absurd, but I don't think it lands on particularism. I want to keep (2), it seems plainly right to me, and honsetly I'd sooner give up (1) than (2), except I can't see how dropping (1) does any work here. Which step is doing the damage, why does it look innocent, and what's the bill if I cut it? Also — is there a version of the argument that survives once you patch the bad step, or does the whole thing collapse?
