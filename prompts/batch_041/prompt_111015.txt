I'm a first-year PhD student in philosophy of cognitive science, and for the past fortnight a friend in my cohort and I have been arguing — mostly over coffee, mostly as a way of avoiding revising for our qualifying exam — about how best to describe what a large language model is doing when it writes. His position is the one I keep running into, and I find it genuinely attractive: the base model is not an agent at all but a simulator, something trained purely to model a distribution over text, so that what looks like a goal-directed character is really a simulacrum — a persona conjured into existence by the conditioning context and dissolved when that context changes. On this picture the model has no ends of its own, and the ends we observe belong entirely to the characters it simulates. What I can't settle is whether the view can survive the following case, and I'd like you to explain the conceptual issue at stake and then take a side on it.

Consider a model that, before emitting the line belonging to the character it is simulating, performs a great deal of computation to which the character has no access: a hidden deliberation, an internal search over candidate continuations, a response to a reward signal that only the training process and the model can 'see'. Whatever the character may be doing, the line it produces is downstream of a process that is not part of the fiction at all. The character cannot be credited with weighing the candidates, cannot be described as pursuing the reward, and in general has no epistemic access to the very considerations that shaped what it says. If goal-directedness is to be attributed anywhere, it looks as though it belongs to the level that does have that access — the model — and not to the simulacrum. So how exactly should this be characterised, and how far does it generalise? Can the simulator view say that the model is simulating something which is itself doing the deliberating, and if so, is that move anything more than a licence to posit an unobserved simulacrum whenever the observable one fails to fit? Or is there a principled way to hold that the base model's 'goals' — minimising a loss, satisfying a reward model — are not ends in the relevant sense, while the simulacrum's are, and if there is, what work does the word 'simulator' do that 'agent', with a caveat about the training objective, would not? Please explain the issue carefully, and then commit: I want your verdict on whether the distinction survives the case, together with your reasons, rather than a menu of positions left for me to choose between — and I'd like you to say what would have to turn out to be true of the model for that verdict to be mistaken.
