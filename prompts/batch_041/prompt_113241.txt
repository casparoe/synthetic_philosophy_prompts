Please give a careful explanation of a conceptual issue that arises in spectrum arguments against the transitivity of 'better than'. Take the familiar structure: a long chain of options, each differing from its neighbour by a step that intuitively makes no difference to which is better, while the two endpoints plainly do differ in betterness. Three diagnoses of what has gone wrong are available, and I want them separated sharply:

(a) 'better than' is transitive but vague — somewhere along the chain there is a genuine threshold, but its location is indeterminate;
(b) 'better than' is precise but intransitive — the chain really does run A better than B, B better than C, and so on, without the first option being better than the last;
(c) the intermediate pairs are not ordered at all — they are on a par, or incomparable, rather than one being better.

Explain what actually separates these diagnoses, and in particular why the intuition that no sharp line can be drawn anywhere does not by itself favour (b) over (a): what further premise is needed to get from 'no detectable boundary' to genuine intransitivity?

Then assess a model I have in mind. Let 'better than' be defined as 'differs by more than a threshold', where the threshold is not a fixed constant but a vague or context-sensitive parameter that shifts with the alternatives being compared. Explain how such a model yields intransitivity across the chain while leaving each single step's comparison determinate, and say whether the resulting relation can still support a coherent ranking or a defensible choice rule.

Finally, consider whether intransitive betterness is a defect in its own right — for instance, whether an agent with those preferences can be exploited through a sequence of pairwise trades, and whether that worry is an independent objection to the spectrum conclusion or merely a restatement of the conviction that betterness must be transitive.

Say how confident you are in what you claim, and single out the point you are least sure of.
