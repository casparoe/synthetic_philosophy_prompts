I'm a novelist — my last book was about a family of lighthouse keepers, so I'm comfortable with alot of wind and bad weather — and I've been trying to write a short story that puts the adaptationism fight at its center without it turning into two people arguing in a seminar room for twelve pages. Here's the setup I keep circling back to: a mid-sized natural history museum is closing its Hall of Human Origins, and the woman who's run it for thirty years (call her Vera) has built her whole career on the idea that nearly everything we've got — the whites of our eyes, our weakness for music, the way we cry at movies — is there because it did something useful for our ancestors. Her former student turns up at the closing gala, and he's got a different story. He thinks a lot of those things are just what you get for free when you build an organism that way: leftovers, side effects, the stuff that comes along for the ride. He has this little set piece about the triangular bits of stone you're forced to fill in when you put a dome on top of a square room in a church — nobody designed them, the geometry just makes them happen — and he uses the word spandrel about four times too many, and he thinks a good chunk of what Vera calls human nature is that. I'd love for the story to be in real dialogue with the Gould and Lewontin critique without ever being about it, if that makes sense.

What I want is for neither of them to be a strawman, because I've been arguing about this with my brother-in-law at barbecues for two years and I've noticed that both sides sound dumb when the other person summarizes them. So Vera should get her licks in — she should point out that you can invent a just-so story about why something is a leftover just as easily as about why it's an adaptation, and that his side seems to buy itself a free pass from ever having to say what would count as evidence; and he should get his — that noticing a trait is useful right now tells you basically nothing about why it showed up, and that the human chin (she brings up the chin, he's ready for it) is his favorite case because it looks like a feature and isn't one, it's just what falls out when two growth processes meet. And then I want the ending to muddy the whole either/or rather than settle it, because the thing that actually keeps me up at night is that a leftover can get used for something later and then start getting shaped, and once that happens the clean split between the two categories starts to look like a filing system somebody made up. Maybe that's what the museum is for — I don't know, I'm still figuring it out.

Mechanics, such as they are: no citations, no one quoting famous papers, no footnotes, no lecture-y exposition — let it come out sideways, through the packing crates, the donor who wants his name on something, the weather, whatever Vera does with her hands when she's losing. Around 2,500 words. And please don't have either of them win.
