I'm in a seminar on privacy — it's cross-listed with the law school — and something's been bugging me all week. We keep talking about how the internet is supposed to give people control over their privacy, but when I actually look at the machinery it seems like four completely different ideas wearing the same word. There's the browser setting that sends a little flag to every website asking it not to track you; as far as I can tell basically nobody ever honored it and everyone has quietly given up on it. Then there's the cookie banner, where every site has to ask me to click accept before it drops a tracker on me, and that gets held up as the gold standard of doing privacy right. Then there's the fact that some browsers just block third-party trackers by default, without asking me anything at all, and that also gets called doing privacy right. And then in a different seminar I'm pretty sure I heard that the census now adds deliberate random noise to its published numbers so that no individual person can be picked out of the data, which sounds like a fourth thing entirely — like privacy is a property of the statistics rather than of anybody's information.

Could you reconstruct what each of these practices presupposes about what privacy actually is — what the harm is, who's supposed to be responsible for preventing it, and whether it's a right or a preference or something the architecture should just handle on my behalf — and then tell me whether they're genuinely in conflict or whether a couple of them are just different means to the same end? I'd really like the commitments each practice strictly needs separated from the ones that are just along for the ride, because my suspicion is that the cookie banner would survive almost any theory of privacy you threw at it, while the please-dont-track-me flag only makes sense under one quite specific one, and the census noise thing doesnt make sense at all under the theory the consent people seem to be working with. If I'm right that the default-blocking approach presupposes something pretty different from the consent approach, what would actually have to change — in the law, in how the technology is built, in what companies are obliged to tell me — if we took that one seriously instead of the consent one? And do any of these treat privacy as a collective or social good rather than a personal one, because the census thing kind of smells like that and I can't tell if that's a real position or just me pattern-matching.
