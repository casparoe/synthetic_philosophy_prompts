I chair the ethics consultation service at a mid-sized teaching hospital, and I would like you to take on the role of a philosopher of practical ethics who is unusually good at building protocols — someone who knows the literature on moral dilemmas, moral residue, and the distinction between a hard choice and an impossible one, and who can compress it into something a committee can run in forty-five minutes without having read any of it. Our service receives roughly twenty consultations a month. Most are ordinary: questions of capacity, surrogate disagreements, discharge planning. But a growing share arrive with the same framing — the requesting team says there is no right answer here, or both options are wrong, or we cannot win either way. Within about forty-eight hours, usually before a family meeting, we owe the chart a one-page opinion; the attending then acts on it, and nobody is bound by what we say. The committee is six people: me, a bedside nurse, a chaplain, the hospital's lawyer, a community lay member, and the clinician who requested the consult. We have no power to decide anything; we can only say what we think and, if we are honest, either shield or fail to shield the person who has to choose. Our problem is that when a case gets hard we have exactly one move. Someone says it is a genuine dilemma, everyone nods, and the note concludes that either course is defensible. Sometimes that is the truth. Sometimes it is how we avoid a call we could have made, and sometimes it is a factual dispute or a policy gap wearing philosophical clothes.

Please write me a procedure the committee can run on every consultation, in order, that sorts the case into one of a small fixed set of verdicts — something along the lines of genuine dilemma, hard choice with a better and a worse option, resolvable conflict, policy gap, and misuse of dilemma language to dodge responsibility — and specifies for each verdict what we must produce. That means: when we owe the decision-maker cover and when we should instead name a preferred course and defend it; when we should send the case back for facts; when the real answer is that the institution must declare a value it has never declared; and, in the cases we do call dilemmas, what is owed afterwards to the option that was not taken — an apology, compensation, follow-up with a bereaved family, a change to the policy that manufactured the trap. Two constraints. First, the procedure must not presuppose that genuine dilemmas exist. One colleague argues that a genuine dilemma is incoherent, because obligations that cannot be jointly fulfilled would not both be obligations, and he will veto anything that treats the existence of dilemmas as an assumption; the chaplain holds that the residue people feel after choosing is evidence the dilemma was real. I need a procedure both can sign, which means it must be able to certify a case as a dilemma without settling the metaphysics, and it must be explicit about which of its steps are conceptual commitments rather than observations. Second, it must terminate in one of the fixed verdicts. If the honest output is that it depends, then the procedure has failed, and I would rather you tell me that than paper over it. Some consults come at two in the morning from a resident who needs an answer before an operation, so say which steps can be skipped under that kind of pressure and which cannot.

Start by laying out the distinctions you intend to rely on, and why the ones you discard are less useful, then give me the procedure as an ordered list of questions, with what counts as a sufficient answer to each and where the procedure stops. Then run it on three cases. The first: a family asks that a diagnosis be withheld from a patient who has said he would rather not be told, and then the family itself asks the nurses direct questions about his prognosis, so the team feels caught between honesty, the patient's stated preference, and the family's request. The second: one remaining course of an expensive therapy and two patients who both meet the criteria, a thirty-four-year-old with a reversible condition and a long recovery ahead, and a seventy-one-year-old who is the sole carer for a grandchild with a disability; the team insists there is no right answer, and I suspect the procedure will tell me that my committee should refuse to decide the case and instead force a policy the hospital has spent years avoiding — an answer I will not enjoy defending upstairs, so do not soften it. The third: a patient discloses in confidence that a relative who drives a school bus has been drinking before work; the relative is identifiable, the risk is real but not imminent, and the patient forbids any disclosure. Add a few lines on a fourth kind of request, one that arrives mainly because the attending wants cover against a threatened lawsuit. Finally, tell me where the procedure will misfire or be gamed, and say plainly what it cannot do. I have no interest in citations or a reading list; reason it out from what you know, and think carefully about the cases where the classification itself is the contested question.
