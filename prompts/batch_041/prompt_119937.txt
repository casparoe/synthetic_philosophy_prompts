I am assembling a proposal for a nine-month interdisciplinary project — philosophy with a historical component — supported by a small internal grant, and I would like you to draft it for me. The subject is moral revolutions: the fairly abrupt collapse of entrenched norms such as aristocratic dueling, footbinding, and Atlantic slavery. The existing discussion is usually organized as a three-cornered contest among arguments, interests, and honor. On one side, moral argument is credited with changing what people believed was permissible. On another, material interest is credited with doing the work, as in the older economic reading of British abolition associated with Eric Williams. On the third, the claim — most closely identified with Appiah — is that what actually moved people was not a new belief about permissibility at all but a new sense of what was shameful, contemptible, or beneath the dignity of a person of standing; on that view, dueling died because it became unmanly rather than because it became wrong.

My half-formed thought, and the thing I want the proposal built around, is that this contest is at least partly spurious, because the three candidates are not answers to the same question. Each may be doing a different kind of work: one explaining how a change became possible at all, another explaining why it happened when it did rather than a generation earlier or later, and a third explaining why it stuck once it had happened. If that is right, the real question is what becomes of the rivalry between the three accounts once the questions are separated, and whether there is a defensible criterion for saying that in some particular case one of them, rather than the others, bears the explanatory weight.

So the proposal should turn that into a single question with a determinate answer. I take it the question is something like whether the honor explanation is a genuine rival to the argument and interest explanations or occupies a different explanatory slot, and if it is a rival in some cases and not in others, what it is about a case that makes the difference. State the question in whatever form you think is sharpest, but it has to be the sort of question that could in principle be answered wrongly, and you should say what a correct answer would look like.

Then say why the existing positions leave it open. I do not want a literature survey; I want the specific gap. My own sense is that each of the three accounts is usually advanced as a sufficient cause of the same event, and that nobody has said what would count as decisive evidence for one over the others, so the debate has been conducted as a horse race in which every case can be claimed by everyone. If you think that diagnosis is wrong, say so and give the better one.

Then the method. This should be conceptual work with case studies rather than archival history, and you should say why that division of labor is the right one for a nine-month project and for someone without the languages or the archives. What I want is a framework that separates the three accounts as answers to distinct questions — possibility, timing, and durability, or whatever set of slots you think survives scrutiny — and that supplies a criterion for explanatory weight, most naturally some counterfactual test: holding the other factors fixed, would the norm have survived? Be honest about whether such a counterfactual can be given any evidential content in cases where the only evidence is common historical knowledge, and if it cannot, say what weaker criterion we should settle for.

Two things I want imported into the analysis, because the debate as I know it does not use them. The first is preference falsification, Kuran's idea that people routinely misrepresent their private preferences under public pressure, so that public and private opinion can diverge sharply and a norm can collapse suddenly once the falsification unravels; this suggests a fourth possibility, that public conduct changed first and dragged private belief along behind it, which would complicate the argument story rather than merely adding to it. The second is the tipping or threshold model of norm cascades associated with Schelling, and alongside it Elias's account of the civilizing process, in which declining tolerance for interpersonal violence and the state's monopolization of force do the explanatory work. I would also like the project to press a distinction between the content of a norm and its enforcement: honor may function as an enforcement technology that operates independently of what anyone believes, in which case an honor-driven revolution could change conduct thoroughly while leaving the underlying moral judgment untouched. That seems to me the sharpest test of the honor account, and I want the proposal to say how one could tell whether it holds.

For the cases: dueling in Britain, which is honor-rich and interest-poor; British abolition, which is the interest story's strongest ground; footbinding; and, as a control, a norm of the same family that did not collapse, with honor killing being the obvious candidate. The control case is what I care most about, because it is where the three accounts should come apart if they are really distinct; if the framework cannot distinguish a revolution from a non-revolution, it has failed. You may add or substitute cases, but keep the number small enough that a nine-month project can handle them.

I also want the proposal to be candid about failure. Tell me what result would show the project's central distinction to be empty — for instance, if the three explanatory slots turn out to collapse into one another, or if the criterion of explanatory weight cannot be applied to any real case and every case ends in a shrug that all three contributed — and tell me what you would cut if nine months turns out to be four.

Finally, the deliverable: I think it is a framework paper plus two case studies, but you should say what the deliverable should be and what a useful result would look like to a historian who is skeptical of philosophers doing history. Do not give me a bibliography, a reading list, or citations of any kind; refer to positions and lines of argument in your own words and I will attach the references myself, and do not invent titles or dates.

Write it as continuous prose, roughly a thousand words, with no headings, no bullet points, no bold, and no tables — the way you would write a letter to a colleague.
