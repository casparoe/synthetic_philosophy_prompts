Two people in my AI ethics reading group got into it last week and I still don't know who to believe.

We'd been talking about "the alignment problem" like it's one thing, and J pushed back. He said when people say "alignment" they're bundling at least two problems that come apart. First, getting a system to pursue what we actually intend rather than what we literally wrote down — that's a specification problem, and it'd be there even for a system that's otherwise perfect. Second, whether the system keeps pursuing it out in the world, off the training distribution, under pressure to optimize hard — that's a robustness/generalization problem, and it'd be there even if the spec were perfect. You can totally solve one and fail the other, so calling them one problem hides the structure.

M said that's a distiction without a difference. Every "robustness failure" is really a specification failure: if the system does something you didn't want once it leaves the lab, that just means your spec didn't capture what you wanted. So there's one problem, "get the spec right," and it's doing all the work; splitting it just relabels sub-cases.

J said that can't be right, because it makes the spec problem unfalsifyable — any failure anywhere becomes "the spec was incomplete," which explains everything and predicts nothing. M said fine, then "robustness" is unfalsifiable from the other direction, so we're even.
