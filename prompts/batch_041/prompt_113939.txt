I'm a novelist, and I need a case built to order for the moral crux of my next book. The subject is commemoration, and the scenario must be designed so that three accounts of what to do with a contested public monument come apart cleanly. On an endorsement account, on which a public monument is the state's continuing assertion that its subject was worthy of honor, the verdict must be removal. On a reparative account, on which what matters is the monument's present effect on the people who live with it, the verdict must be retention — this is the harder direction for that account, and I want the case to earn it rather than stipulate it. On a historical-record account the case must come out genuinely neutral: the record has to survive either way, so that this account cannot quietly settle the question. The facts about the honoree's misdeeds are stipulated and uncontested, so no verdict may turn on historical uncertainty, and the retention verdict may not rest on any residual sympathy for the honoree or on an obviously bad rule about property, donors, or procedure; it must fall out of a principle an ordinary reasonable person would accept in general. Keep it realistic and small: one town, one object, no national politics, no exotic devices, under 400 words, and not a Confederate general, since that case is worn smooth. Then, for each account, state the verdict and the premise doing the work; name the single feature of the case that makes the endorsement and reparative accounts diverge, and say what becomes of the case if that feature is removed; and finally tell me how a committed endorsement theorist would try to defuse the case, and whether the disanalogy they would point to is real or ad hoc. The point is to test whether my ending follows from a view or is only a contrivance.
