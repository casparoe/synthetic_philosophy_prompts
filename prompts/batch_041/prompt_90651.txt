I host a small philosophy of science reading group, and we have spent three sessions re-arguing the same point about whether the sciences really latch onto genuine divisions in nature or whether that whole way of talking is a piece of rhetoric that a sober theory of classification should drop. I would rather stop re-litigating it and build something that sorts us by what we actually believe. Please construct a decision tree that begins from a commitment everyone in the room already accepts — that at least some scientific classifications, say the periodic table or the classification of the chemical elements, are more objective, or track the world more closely, than something like the Dewey decimal system or the alphabetization of a phone book — and leads, through a sequence of branching questions, to named positions on natural kinds. Each internal node should be phrased as a question a student can answer without already knowing which position it leads to, and each should isolate one genuine axis of disagreement rather than smuggling in a label. The axes I want you to work with include, at minimum: whether the objectivity of a classification is a claim about mind-independent structure in the world or instead a claim about the reliability of the inferences and predictions that the classification licenses; whether a kind needs something like an essence or real definition or can instead be held together by a cluster of imperfectly correlated properties with no single feature necessary; whether the question of whether something is a natural kind is really a question about language and concepts or about the world; and whether the kinds we care about in biology, psychiatry, and the social sciences deserve the same account as the kinds of physics and chemistry, or a separate treatment — and if separate, whether that is a difference in degree or in kind.

Build the tree out of the logical space first — the questions and the combinations of answers they permit — and only then locate the actual positions in it, naming each leaf (or marking it 'unnamed' if you think no one has defended that combination) and adding one sentence on that position's most awkward commitment. Please make sure the instrument is anchored in concrete cases rather than abstractions: I want the element/periodic-table case in there, the biological species case, the case of a folk category that fails to line up with any real division (the familiar example of fish excluding the lobe-finned relatives of land animals is one we always end up on), race, and at least one case where the classification is contested for practical rather than theoretical reasons, such as a psychiatric diagnosis. Where two positions come apart in principle but your tree cannot separate them, flag the pair explicitly rather than papering over it, and where a combination of answers seems to be empty, say whether you think that is because the view is incoherent, unattractive, or simply unexplored. Aim for twelve to sixteen leaves, keep the whole thing usable as a one-page handout for students with a semester of philosophy of science but no metaphysics, and remember that the point is to sort us by what we say about the cases, not by which label we happen to like — so no node should simply ask whether someone is a realist or an anti-realist about kinds.
