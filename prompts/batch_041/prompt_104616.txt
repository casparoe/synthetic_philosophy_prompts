Write a fictional specialty coffee roastery's monthly tasting-notes insert — the card that ships with a subscriber's curated flight of four coffees — in the full register of the genre: florid, confident, faintly liturgical, heavy with the vocabulary of stone fruit, jasmine, and the wet aroma of a forest floor. Roughly 700 words. The house can be called whatever you like, but its head roaster is a sincere qualia realist: he has read Dennett's thought experiment about the two coffee tasters who disagree over whether the coffee changed or they themselves changed, and he has concluded, in good faith, that it does not touch him. The comedy should arise from the genre's own logic rather than from invented absurdity. Every note he writes in praise of a coffee's inner character must, read literally, report either a relation (to another bean, to a season, to a remembered cup) or one of his own dispositions (what the taste moves him to do, pair, or reach for) — never an intrinsic, non-relational quality of the experience. He should go on insisting that he is telling you what the coffee is like, while the notes go on doing something else.

Give the insert four entries, each collapsing in a different way, and let the philosophy stay implicit in the structure. One coffee's notes should be wholly comparative, so that its supposed inner character turns out to consist of its differences from other coffees, which is to say of relations. One should consist of the roaster's reactive dispositions — the coffee that makes him want to sit down, that he reaches for when it rains — rather than of the taste itself. One should be a coffee whose allegedly ineffable character he communicates perfectly well by training a new hire to pick it out against the house's numbered flavour wheel, showing that the vocabulary is public, taught, and shared. One should be a private, incorrigible-seeming episode that a colleague, tasting the same cup at the same bench, flatly denies — leaving the reader to ask what the roaster's certainty about his own experience was ever worth.

Thread through the card a running box headed something like 'From the roaster's desk', in which he narrates his quarrel with his co-founder: one of them no longer enjoys the house's flagship blend but insists the coffee is unchanged, while the other still enjoys it but is convinced that his own palate has altered; neither can tell, from the inside, which of them is right, or whether they are even disagreeing. Have him describe, in the same cheerful tone, a slow recalibration of his palate he undertook over several months, and note without seeing the point that he cannot distinguish what it did to the taste of the coffee from what it did to him.

Aim the satire at the practice of the tasting note — the genre's pretence that it reports an inner, private, ineffable given — and not at any actual roastery, roaster, or coffee. Keep it deadpan throughout: no narrator stepping out to explain the joke, no footnotes, and no naming of any philosopher or theory anywhere in the piece; the philosophy must be legible only in what the roaster says and in the way his own words undo him. A reader who has never heard of qualia should find it a funny piece of food writing; a reader who knows the four properties commonly ascribed to them — that they are ineffable, intrinsic, private, and directly apprehensible in consciousness — should be able to point to the exact line in each entry where the realist's position quietly refutes itself. Close with a subscription renewal coupon or satisfaction guarantee that, with the same straight face, offers to refund the subscriber should the coffee change or the subscriber change, without ever promising to tell which of the two has happened.
