I teach a graduate seminar on the philosophy of statistics, and I want to use the current editorial regime in the empirical sciences as a case study, so I would like a careful reconstruction of the philosophy of inference that the regime presupposes. Take the following four features, which I believe are standard. (1) A substantive claim is publishable only if the analysis reaches p < 0.05; a result that misses the threshold is treated as no result at all rather than as evidence of absence. (2) Some journals now accept papers on the strength of the design alone, provisionally before the data are collected, and undertake to publish them whatever the data show, including a null result. (3) Direct replications of published findings are seldom published, on the ground that they lack novelty. (4) The same journals increasingly ask authors to report effect sizes and confidence intervals and to stop describing results as significant.

What conception of evidence, of the hypothesis under test, and of the aim of inference does this combination presuppose? In particular: does (1) commit the field to reading a p-value as a graded measure of evidence against a null, or does treating 0.05 as a fixed decision threshold commit it instead to the view that a test is a rule for acting with known error rates rather than a scale for weighing evidence — and can those two commitments be held together, or are they the residue of two incompatible programs? Which of the four features are strictly presupposed by the conception you reconstruct, and which are institutional conveniences that no conception of evidence requires? The coexistence of (1) and (2) strikes me as the sharpest puzzle: (2) appears to presuppose that the evidential value of a study is fixed by its design and is independent of the direction of its result, which (1) denies. Which of the two is the anomaly?

Then: if we replaced the presupposed conception with a frankly Bayesian one, or with a likelihoodist one, which of the four features would have to change and which could survive? And is the replication crisis evidence that the presupposed conception is false, or only that it was never seriously implemented? Reconstruct the implicit theory, separate what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely accommodates, and say where the practice is underdetermined, so that several philosophies of inference would license it. State how confident you are in each claim you make, and identify the point you are least sure of.
