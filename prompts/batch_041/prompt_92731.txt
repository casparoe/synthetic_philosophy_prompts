"Mises argued that socialism can't work because without private ownership of the means of production there are no prices for capital goods, so planners have no way of knowing the opportunity cost of using a machine one way rather than another -- they're just guessing. Lange's answer was that a central planning board doesn't need private ownership: it can set prices for producer goods, watch for shortages and surpluses, and adjust them by trial and error until supply equals demand, exactly the way a competitive market does. Managers are told to minimize average cost and push output until marginal cost equals price. So Lange grants that prices matter but denies they require private property or real markets -- his economy is fully administered, with no markets at all. Mises and Hayek are basically making the same argument, except Hayek adds that the information planners would need is dispersed. Hayek's reply to Lange was that the board would have to solve millions of simultaneous equations and gather data on every consumer's preferences, which is computationally impossible; so the debate was really about computing power, and since computers can now do that arithmetic, Hayek's objection doesn't have much left. In the end Mises and Hayek both backed off and conceded that a socialist economy could in principle allocate correctly, which is why the debate moved on to incentives."
