Ellsberg's two-urn setup: Urn A holds 50 red and 50 black balls. Urn B holds 100 balls, each either red or black, but the mix isn't disclosed. You win a prize if the ball you draw matches the colour you bet on. Asked to bet on red, most people choose Urn A; asked to bet on black, most people again choose Urn A. But if you prefer A for red you must think A gives a better chance of red, i.e. that B gives a worse chance of red, which means B gives a better chance of black; yet you also prefer A for black, which says A gives the better chance of black. Since the chances of red and black within an urn have to sum to one, no assignment of probabilities makes A better on both colours, so the pattern can't be squared with expected utility maximisation. What's really going on is that people are risk-averse about the unknown urn: Urn B is the risky one because its odds aren't known, whereas Urn A is safe because its odds are 50/50. Ambiguity aversion, as this is called, is really just a strong form of ordinary risk aversion, a dislike of uncertainty about outcomes; it's the same thing that makes people take a sure $50 over a 50/50 shot at $100. So the paradox refutes expected utility theory as an account of rational choice, and the rational response to the example is simply to be indifferent between the two urns, since by symmetry they must come to the same thing; anyone who prefers one has made a mistake.
