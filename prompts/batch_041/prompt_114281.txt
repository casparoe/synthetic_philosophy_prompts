I am teaching a graduate seminar on the question of what life is, and I want to use Mary Shelley's Frankenstein as the literary centerpiece. I do not want a summary of the novel or a general essay on the dangers of playing God; I want a philosophical reading of the creature as a borderline case in the debate over definitions of life. Use four candidate accounts: (1) the metabolic account, on which a living thing is a self-maintaining chemical system that sustains itself far from thermodynamic equilibrium by taking in energy and matter; (2) the evolutionary account, on which life is whatever belongs to a lineage capable of Darwinian variation and selection; (3) the autopoietic account, on which a living system is a network of processes that continuously produces and maintains its own components; and (4) the cluster account, on which life is a loose, disjunctive cluster of properties—metabolism, reproduction, evolution, responsiveness, self-maintenance—with no single essence, so that borderline cases are expected rather than embarrassing. Give me exactly five numbered points, each a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion, in American English, and do not quote from the novel; describe scenes in your own words. The five points should do the following work: say which account, if any, the creature satisfies and where the others strain or fail; treat the creature's demand for a companion as a test of the evolutionary and cluster accounts, asking what would change if a second creature existed; identify the novel's refusal to describe the moment of animation and say what that omission implies about the definitional project; examine Victor's study of decay and the boundary between life and death and say whether the novel commits him to a metabolic, a vitalist, or an agnostic position; and close by saying what the novel contributes to the philosophical debate that a taxonomy of definitions cannot.
