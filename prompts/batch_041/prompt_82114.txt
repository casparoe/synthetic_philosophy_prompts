The Darwinian inversion and the Turing inversion are usually filed as two instances of a single move: in each, something that looked as though it required comprehension — the fit of an organism to its world; the execution of a rule by someone who follows it for a reason — turns out to be produced by a process that comprehends nothing, so that comprehension, wherever it does show up, comes after the competence it was supposed to explain rather than before it. That is the whole content of the slogan about competence without comprehension, and it is normally asserted as a package deal: Darwin and Turing, the same trick run twice.

I want the package taken apart. Begin by drawing a distinction, in your own terms, between two ways an inversion can handle the comprehension it displaces. It can genuinely end the regress, by bottoming out in a level whose operation can be described completely without any appeal to what that level gets right; or it can merely relocate the regress, by presupposing a comprehension somewhere the inversion itself does not touch. State the distinction sharply enough that it could be applied to a case neither of us has in mind. Then say how it cuts across the two inversions: whether each falls cleanly on one side, or whether the distinction splits each inversion internally, so that part of the move ends the regress and part of it only moves it. Be explicit about the residue in each case — the thing the inversion must leave standing, unexplained by the inversion itself, if it is to do any work at all — and say whether that residue is a genuine limit or just the next item in line to be inverted away.

Then test the distinction on a third case that is neither biology nor computation. A market allocating resources without any participant comprehending the allocation is the obvious candidate, but pick whichever case makes the distinction work hardest, and say whether it lands on Darwin's side, Turing's side, or somewhere the distinction was not built to reach.

Close with a verdict: which of the two inversions is the deeper one, what turns on the answer — in particular for what we should expect of systems that are competent without comprehending anything, and for whether the comprehension of the people who build such systems is load-bearing or incidental — and what would have to be true for your verdict to be wrong. No appeals to the historical record for support, and no hedging about what various commentators have said; I want the structural claim, defended.
