I sit on the parole board of a mid-sized state, and at Thursday's hearing we take up the first case that will be decided under our newly adopted release framework, which means whatever we write will function as precedent for the several dozen cases queued behind it. The framework instructs us that release must be "consistent with the seriousness of the offense" and also "supported by an assessment that the person presents a low risk of further serious offending," but it is silent on what to do when those two point in opposite directions, which is precisely our situation. The petitioner has served twenty-two years of a twenty-five-to-life term for a killing committed during a robbery; under the guidelines now in force the same offense would carry a fifteen-to-life term, so he has already served longer than the current benchmark; the actuarial instruments place him at low risk of further serious violence; and he has volunteered, unprompted, that he does not think he has yet done what he owes and would accept a denial. The sentencing judge's remarks at the time, which are in the file, were emphatic that nothing short of the full term would answer the wrong, and the victim's family opposes release. My colleagues split cleanly. Those who read the statute retributively hold that the offense fixes a floor that no risk assessment can lower and that the board's job is to give the wrong its due; those who read it consequentially hold that continued detention is unjustified once the risk is genuinely low, since the state gains nothing from it but another year of suffering at the cost of a bed, and that denying release to satisfy the community's anger simply uses the petitioner as an instrument of expression. I have spent enough years in rationalist-adjacent corners of the internet that my instinct is to convert everything into expected harm and take the option with the smaller number, but I have a nagging sense that this is exactly the move the retributive reading exists to block, and that adopting it here would commit the board to a position none of us has ever openly defended.

Draft the written opinion I will circulate before the vote. It should recommend a concrete disposition -- grant, deny, or grant on stated conditions -- and give reasons the losing side can actually engage with rather than dismiss as sentiment; it should say plainly what the board is committing itself to for the cases that follow, in particular whether it is adopting anything like the rule one of my colleagues has floated, under which the offense fixes a range of years and risk assessment only decides where inside that range a person lands; and it should tell me how much weight to give the petitioner's own account of what he deserves, since he has handed us one and it cuts against his own interest. I am not asking for a survey of the punishment literature and you need not cite anything -- I will add references myself -- but I do want the argument to take a definite position rather than split the difference, and to be honest about what would be lost by the recommendation you make.
