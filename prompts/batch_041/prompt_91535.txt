Write a mock retraction notice, about 500–600 words, from the editorial board of a philosophy journal, announcing that it is retracting Stalnaker's Thesis — the claim that the probability of an indicative conditional of the form "If A, then C" is the conditional probability of C given A — on the ground that Lewis's triviality results show that if the thesis holds, then P(C | A) = P(C) for every A and C, so that conditionals come out probabilistically independent of their antecedents. Keep the register deadpan and institutional: a findings section summarising the proof, a short note on method, a statement of competing interests, an itemised list of objections considered and dismissed, and a closing change to submissions policy. The board should behave as a bureaucracy does — by narrowing what it retracts rather than by abandoning the thesis, retreating first to antecedents of positive probability, then to a privileged class of probability functions, then to a language too impoverished for the proof to get off the ground — and the notice should end by imposing the corresponding restriction on the journal's own prose. Aim the satire at this retreat-and-restrict strategy rather than at the theorem, and get the technical content right: that the proof proceeds by applying the thesis to the probability function conditionalised on the consequent and on its negation, that rejecting bivalence and rejecting the thesis are different moves, and that a conditional with a zero-probability antecedent may be perfectly assertible while the thesis says nothing about it. Let the board never notice that it is retracting something it never published, and give it at least one deadpan self-application (a footnote noting, say, that the board's own recommendation is itself stated conditionally and that the board has accordingly declined to assign it a probability). Invented names for the editors are fine; no references or citations are needed.
