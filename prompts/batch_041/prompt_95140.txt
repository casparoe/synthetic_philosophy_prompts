tort law works out causation with the but-for test: a doctor's negligence counts as the cause of a death only if the patient would've survived had the doctor not screwed up. but when the odds are murky -- patient maybe had a 40% shot anyway -- a lot of courts carve out a separate injury, loss of chance, and award damages for the lost 40% instead of for the death. what does that whole practice presuppose about causation? does the but-for test need creatures to have real causal powers, the doctor's act actually making a difference, or is it enough that things just happen in regular patterns? i ask because ive been reading about occasionalism (al-Ghazali and the Ash'arites, later Malebranche): nothing created ever really causes anything, God produces each event on the occasion of the one before it, and what we call a law of nature is just God's habit of doing it that way. on that picture, is the but-for test even coherent, and does loss of chance come out better or worse? tell me what the practice strictly needs versus what it merely fits with, since i suspect its underdetermined and several theories of causation would license it.
