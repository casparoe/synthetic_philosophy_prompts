I'm in the final year of a philosophy degree and I'm writing the long paper for my epistemology module, where the assigned topic is the relation between luminosity and the KK principle; our tutor has asked us not to recycle the examples we discussed in seminars, so I want to build the central case myself and then interrogate it. The definitions we were given were these: a condition is luminous just in case, whenever it obtains, the person whose condition it is is in a position to know that it obtains; and the KK principle is the claim that if you know that something is the case, then you are in a position to know that you know it. Every example we saw of luminosity failing had the same skeleton — a long series of very small changes, none of which the subject notices, together with a principle to the effect that if you know something in one case you must also know it in any case that is similar enough, so that knowledge gives out somewhere along the series while the condition itself does not. My complaint is that this makes the failure depend on there being a series at all, and I would like a case that runs in the opposite direction: a condition that genuinely is luminous, attached to a proposition the subject genuinely knows, where she is nevertheless not in a position to know that she knows it.

I want the engine of the failure to be structural rather than marginal. Specifically, the step from the condition to the proposition should be ampliative, in such a way that the reliability of that step could only be established by relying on the very belief at issue, so that the second-order claim inherits a circularity which the first-order claim does not; margins, similarity, and gradual change should play no part in the story at all. Constraints: no deception, no brains in vats, no fake barns, no drugs, no neurosurgery, no demons; the subject should be an ordinary adult on an ordinary afternoon; the scenario itself should be given in a single paragraph and should contain no philosophical vocabulary; and nothing in it may simply stipulate that the second-order belief is unjustified, since the unjustification has to fall out of the structure. After the scenario, do the audit. State the condition, say why it satisfies the definition of luminosity given above, say why the first-order belief still counts as knowledge, say precisely why the second-order claim fails and whether anything can be recovered at the third level. Then tell me which single feature of the case is doing the work, and what becomes of the case if that feature is removed. Finish by saying how a defender of KK ought to attack it — by denying the first-order knowledge, by denying that the condition is luminous, or by arguing that the circularity is benign here — which of those is the strongest line, and why.
