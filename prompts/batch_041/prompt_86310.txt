Okay so I've talked myself into a position and I want you to try to break it, not just nod along.

The money pump is usually told as the killer argument against cyclic preferences: you prefer A to B, B to C, and C to A, so someone can walk you around the loop taking a small fee at each step until you're broke and holding nothing you like better than what you started with. I think the real lesson is way bigger than the textbook case. My position is this: diachronic rationality just is a matter of not being exploitable. If there's a sequence of trades, each one you'd accept on your own terms at the moment it's offered, that leaves you worse off by your own lights with nothing to show for it, then you're irrational -- not just unlucky or out-talked. The fees dont have to be money, the loop doesn't have to be a cycle in the strict sense, and the trades can be spread over years with your tastes shifting in between.

So: what would count against that? I want the actual defeaters, sorted from fatal to merely awkward, and I want to know which ones a defender of the view would themselves concede rather than just bat away.

Things I'm already suspicious about, so tell me if they're as damaging as they feel:

- Foresight and commitment. If you can see the pump coming and precommit to refuse the trades, are you still irrational? Because if the argument only works on naive agents, maybe it's an argument about naivety, not about rationality.
- Exploitability vs. actually being exploited. Someone can be exploited by an extortionist without any defect in their preferences -- paying the blackmailer looks like the rational move. Does that show exploitability is the wrong thing to fix on?
- Pumps without cycles. Time-inconsistent tastes, or the dial case where each tiny turn of the dial is worth it and the whole trip isn't -- those look pump-able even with acyclic preferences. If so, my thesis is broader than the money pump argument, which means it needs support that argument can't give it.
- Cycles that can't be pumped. Are there preference structures that are cyclic but un-exploitable in any environment a real agent is ever in? Counterexample or footnote?
- Circularity. Does the pump only work if you already accept that rational agents don't take sure losses? If so, what independent reason is there for that?

Also, give me an honest read on whether the position has been qualified into unfalsifiability. A defender can always add clauses -- the agent has to be naive, the trades have to be offered together, no commitment devices around, the losses have to be foreseeable -- and at some point it collapses into "an agent who is irrational in this particular way is irrational," which risks nothing. Is that whats happening here, and if it is, is that a real problem or just the normal price of a conceptual thesis?

And what does my view predict that the rival view doesn't -- the rival being that rationality is a per-moment, per-choice business and exploitability is at most a symptom? If the two make the same predictions everywhere, say so.

Personally I think the resolute-choice move is cheating and I'd rather lose the argument than use it, so weight that. Skip the who-said-what, just give me the arguments.
