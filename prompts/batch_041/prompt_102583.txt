Over the past year I have watched several people I know be disciplined, and in one case effectively ruined, by loose coalitions of strangers, and I have found myself approving of some of these campaigns and recoiling from others without being able to say what distinguishes the ones I accept from the ones I do not. I do not need another essay on whether cancel culture is real or whether it is a moral panic. I need the specification, and I need it pitched so that a person actually deciding, in a particular case, whether to join a sanction or resist it could use it. Write out the desiderata that any adequate theory of informal social sanction as punishment must meet: the cases it has to get right (private criticism, a consumer boycott, dismissal from a job, the refusal of a platform, organized ostracism, and the pile-on that far exceeds what the original offense warranted); the distinctions it must not collapse (punishment versus disapproval, versus the mere withdrawal of association, versus self-protection; the sanction versus the wrong that provoked it; intended versus foreseeable harm); and the constraints it must respect (that no one is in charge, that the rules are unwritten and applied after the fact, that sanctions accumulate, and that standing to punish is itself contested). Give the consideration behind each requirement, say which are non-negotiable and which are the prejudices of a particular camp, identify the requirement that does the most to exclude the leading positions, and tell me which pair of requirements cannot be jointly satisfied. My own suspicion is that fair notice or proportionality and genuinely decentralized enforcement are incompatible, but I want your judgment rather than mine. The criteria are what I am after; a brief scorecard of the main positions can follow.
