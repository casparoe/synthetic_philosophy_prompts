I retired recently from a career in the physical sciences and have taken up philosophy as an amateur; among other things I run a small interview series, and my next guest is a philosopher of physics who defends what is usually called quantum immortality. Her claim, as I understand it, is this: if she repeatedly submitted to a device that kills her in one branch of the wavefunction and spares her in another, she should not expect ever to experience her own death. Some successor of her always survives; the death is undergone by a counterpart she is not; and she treats that as a first-person certainty rather than a matter of luck.

I want eight to ten questions that press her on the substance of this, each with a follow-up keyed to the reply she is most likely to give, and I should like the whole answer in British English. Specifically, I want her pressed on the following.

- Measure. After n rounds the successor who lives carries a measure that has halved at every round — the very figure the Born rule would have assigned to her survival. Does she take measure to be probability, and if not, what work is the word expect doing in her claim? Have a follow-up ready for the reply that measure is not probability for the person living through the branches.

- Identity and self-location. What makes a successor in another branch her rather than a copy of her? The familiar analogy with a person dividing into two equal offshoots, neither of which is the original, may not survive the fact that the living offshoot's measure dwindles towards nothing. I should like her to say whether her argument requires that survival consists in there being some descendant who is hers, rather than in her being any one of them.

- Action and insulation. If the argument works, it should work for every lethal hazard, so that she should expect to survive each fatal illness and to reach an implausible age, while apparently having no such expectation. Is that a confusion about measure, or evidence that the doctrine is unfalsifiable?

- Learning nothing. The counterpart who dies cannot update on her death, which makes the reasoning look anthropically suspect. Why is it not simply vacuous to say that I never experience dying?

- What would count against the view? I want a closing question that obliges her to name evidence or argument that would make her abandon it.

Please keep every question content-driven; nothing that invites her merely to restate the position.
