Next month I'm presenting at our department's rationality-and-conflict workshop. The room is mostly formal game theorists (signaling models, trembling-hand refinements, that whole toolkit is second nature to them), plus two security-studies people who've read far too much Cold War history, and one moral philosopher who works on just-war theory. My thesis: the usual story of brinkmanship has the causal mechanism backwards. Brinkmanship is always told as a strategy of deliberately running a risk of catastrophe -- you make the bad outcome somewhat likely, so the other side backs off. I want to argue that what actually does the deterrent work isnt the risk that you'll choose to fire; its the deliberate destruction of your own ability to choose otherwise. You don't raise the probability of a bad outcome, you make the retreat option disappear. I've been calling it "commitment by demolition." And here's the twist I want to defend: the same feature that makes it work is exactly what makes it invisible to expected-utility models, because those models keep treating your future choice as still a choice, just with a probability attached to it.
