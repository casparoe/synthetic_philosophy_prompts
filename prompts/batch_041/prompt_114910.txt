Alright, here's what I want to think through. Al-Ghazālī's occasionalism — the view that God is the only genuine cause, and that what we call natural causes are just God's habit of pairing one event with another, so the fire doesn't do the burning, God creates the burning when the fire is there. I want you to drop him into a pile of debates that only exist after his time and tell me, as concretely as you can, what he'd actually say. Not "he'd find it interesting" — positions.

A few things to hit:

1. Quantum mechanics. Take both flavors seriously: the one where the indeterminacy is irreducible, and the one where the apparent randomness is really deterministic underneath (hidden variables, pilot-wave style). Would either be an ally to him? Would the no-go results showing you can't get the correlations out of local hidden variables change anything for him, or is he immune to all of it because he never thought events needed to be law-governed in the first place?

2. The causal exclusion argument. The wory is roughly: if the physical is causally closed and does all the causing, then anything else you posit as a cause — the mental, the divine — gets squeezed out as either redundant or just a different description of the same thing. Does his occasionalism have its own version of that problem, with God and physical causes both claiming to do the same work? If it dodges the problem, does it dodge it honestly, or by quietly changing the subject about what "cause" means?

3. Humean regularity — causes are nothing but constant conjunctions. Would he sign on as an ally, since he also denies any real power in creatures? Or is that a rival view in disguise? Say exactly where they part.

4. Someone hands him a modern interventionist account — X causes Y if wiggling X changes Y, the kind of thing you draw with arrows between variables. Is that a genuine account of causation for him, a useful fiction, or a category mistake?

Then the main thing: his bottom line on whether his view is exposed to anything at all. What would he concede counts against him, what would he wave off, and is there any conceivable scientific or philosophical result that should make him drop it — or is the view built so that nothing could?

Ground rules: no reading lists, no citations, I don't care who said what in the secondary literature. Write it in American English. Commit to a reading — I'd rather have a bold reconstruction with the extrapolation flagged than a survey of "scholars disagree." If his position depends on which flavor of occasionalism you attribute to him (plain Ash'arite vs. his own refinements), say so and pick one. And yeah, I know this is anachronistic as hell, that's the point — handle the anachronism seriously instead of winking at it.
