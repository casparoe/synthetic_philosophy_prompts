I teach the closing module of a third-year course in formal epistemology and decision theory, and I am drafting the take-home paper for the final examination. I have set a question on the two-envelope paradox every year, and the scripts have become nearly interchangeable: students reproduce, with small variations, the received line that the switching argument treats the unknown amount as at once a fixed quantity and a random variable, and that no proper probability distribution makes the two candidate amounts equiprobable, and then they stop, having said nothing that would survive a change in the numbers. I want a question for which that recitation is the beginning of the answer rather than the whole of it — one where the student has to carry the diagnosis into a case that the textbook treatment does not cover off the shelf.

I want the question built on the following scenario, stated in the paper in these terms. Before the game begins, the host announces her procedure: she will toss a fair coin, and on heads she will seal ten dollars in one envelope and twenty in the other, while on tails she will seal twenty dollars in one envelope and forty in the other. She shuffles the pair, the player draws one at random, opens it, and only then chooses whether to keep it or exchange it for its partner. The question should oblige the student to do three things in order. First, for each amount the player might see, decide whether the optimal move is to keep or to exchange, and give the conditional expected gain or loss from exchanging at that amount. Second, compute the expected value of the best available policy and compare it with the expected value of the two constant policies, always keep and always exchange, so that the student discovers, and must explain, that the constant policies are exactly tied with one another while the amount-conditional policy is strictly better than both. Third, tie this back to the original paradox's step in which the other envelope is said to hold twice or half the observed amount with equal probability: say precisely what that step presupposes about the player's prior over the amounts, what becomes of it once a prior is specified, and what a prior would have to look like for the step to hold at every amount. I would like a short final part asking the student to say what, if anything, remains paradoxical once the case has been worked through — whether the residue is a defect in decision theory, a lesson about the value of conditioning on one's own observation, or nothing at all — and I want the model answer to be scrupulous in separating the settled arithmetic from the contested interpretation, so that a student who presents a contested reading as the consensus, or the arithmetic as if it were in dispute, loses marks in proportion to how badly the two are conflated.

Please return the response in three named sections, in this order: The question (the paper as it should be printed, about two hundred words); Model answer (roughly six hundred and fifty words); and Marking scheme (the specific moves a first-class answer must contain, the marks each is worth out of twenty, and, for each move, one line on how students characteristically go wrong on it). Where a move turns on a numerical result, give the number. Keep the whole thing self-contained: no citations, no reading list, and nothing that presupposes the student has read any particular paper.
