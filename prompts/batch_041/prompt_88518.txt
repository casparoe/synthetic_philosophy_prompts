I have just finished a first course in game theory, and the one stipulation I cannot stop worrying at is the common prior assumption — the requirement, built into the very definition of a Bayesian game, that every player's beliefs be obtained by conditioning a single shared prior on that player's private information. My instructor waved it away as a bookkeeping convenience, a device that lets one write down types and beliefs compactly and then get on with the analysis; a classmate in the same course maintained that it was a substantive claim about how reasonable people must be related to one another, and that the interesting disputes in the surrounding literature are really disputes about whether to keep it. I have come to think the instructor was wrong and the classmate only half right, and I would like you to make the case I have not yet been able to make myself.

Please argue, as forcefully and precisely as you can, for the position that the common prior assumption is a substantive and controversial idealization rather than a neutral convention — that it encodes a particular picture of disagreement, on which any difference of opinion between rational agents must trace back to a difference in information, and thereby rules out in advance the possibility of two equally well-informed and equally rational agents who nonetheless persist in disagreeing. I want the argument to engage the strongest line of defense rather than a caricature of it. The defender will say that the assumption is empty, since any profile of beliefs can be represented as arising from a common prior over a sufficiently rich space of states, so that nothing is genuinely being assumed. Take that reply seriously and show what it costs: say whether the move really rescues the assumption or merely relocates the substantive commitment somewhere less visible, and what work the enriched space is then doing.

I am not looking for a survey of the literature, and I do not need citations — I would rather have one tight argument that a skeptical and technically competent reader would have to work to resist. It is fine to concede that the assumption is often a useful modeling choice; I am not asking you to claim that game theorists should stop using it, only that its status is that of a contestable idealization rather than a free lunch. If the strongest version of the position turns out to be narrower than it first appeared, say so and argue for the narrower claim.
