I am a high school senior working on an independent-study project in decision theory, and I have gotten stuck in what I think is a productive way. I began with the St. Petersburg game, a fair coin tossed until the first head, paying two dollars raised to the number of the toss on which the head lands, and then I found a 2004 paper in Mind by Harris Nover and Alan Hájek that introduces a nastier relative. As I understand it and have been working with it, their game is this: a fair coin is tossed until the first head, and if the head lands on toss n the player receives negative one to the n, times two to the n, divided by n dollars, where a negative amount means the player pays. Working the expected-value series out myself, the term for toss n has probability one over two to the n and payoff negative one to the n times two to the n over n, so the terms of the series are 1, negative 1/2, 1/3, negative 1/4, and so on. It converges, but only conditionally, which means that by the rearrangement theorem the payoffs can be reordered to make the sum come out to any number whatsoever, or to positive or negative infinity, without the game itself changing at all. I have also been using two further cases as tests, both of which I constructed myself and both of which I want any theory to survive: a variant in which every payoff is increased by exactly one dollar, so that in every possible outcome the player is one dollar better off than in the original game and the expected value now exists and equals one minus the natural logarithm of two; and a second variant whose payoff is negative one to the n times two to the n, whose expected-value series has partial sums that stay bounded but oscillate forever rather than diverging to an infinity or settling on a limit.

What I want from you is not a solution to the paradox but the specification that a solution would have to meet, meaning the desiderata for any theory of rational choice that claims to handle cases where expected value is undefined. Please give me the requirements list: the cases it must get right, the distinctions it cannot collapse, the mathematical facts it has to remain consistent with, and the ordinary practical demands it must not quietly abandon. For each requirement, give me the consideration or case that motivates it, say whether it is non-negotiable or merely a preference of one camp, and say which single requirement does the most work in ruling out the theories people actually propose. I especially want your view on whether some subset of these requirements is jointly unsatisfiable, since if two of them cannot both be met then that is itself a result, and I would much rather hear it stated than have it papered over. In particular I am suspicious of three demands that seem to pull against one another: that the theory always return a verdict rather than falling silent, that its verdict depend only on the payoff table and not on the order in which the outcomes happen to be listed, and that the verdict be computable by an ordinary person in a reasonable amount of time. I would also like you to address whether the requirement that dominance be respected, which my one-dollar-improved variant is designed to test, can be satisfied without letting the well-defined expectation of the improved game license a verdict about the original one.

Do not send me to the literature and do not give me citations; I have no internet access, and what I need is the criteria rather than a bibliography. At the end, tell me what would change your mind: which requirement, if it turned out to be dispensable or to be a prejudice of one particular camp rather than a genuine demand on any adequate theory, would most alter the list you have given me, and where in your reasoning you think you are most likely to be wrong.
