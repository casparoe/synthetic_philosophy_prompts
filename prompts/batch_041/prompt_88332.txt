I'm building a small library of cases for a project on the epistemics of collective belief, and I need one more. Put it in the information-cascade / rational-herding territory, but make it earn its keep.

What I want: a scenario, realistic and no longer than one paragraph, in which a run of ordinary people each rationally disregard their own private evidence and just copy whoever went before, so the group ends up confidently wrong about something that actually matters. Constraints:

- Everybody is a competent Bayesian. No stupidity, no panic, no social pressure, no one being payed off, no one lying or bluffing. Each choice has to be the choice you'd make if you were them.
- No urns, no colored balls, no abstract "two states of the world" machinery. Give me people doing something — a hospital adopting a treatment, a village planting a crop, a hiring committee, whatever you like. One paragraph.
- The cascade has to lock in by the third decider, and I want to see why the third decider is not being dumb: lay out what each of the first three knows and believes, and show the update.
- Then I want the twist. Give me a second version of the exact same story in which every observable thing stays fixed — same people, same order, same actions, same public statements — but the private evidence is no longer conditionally independent: the early signals are correlated through some shared source (same flawed training, same supplier's brochure, same regional rumor, you pick). Same behavior, different epistemic situation. Then explain what actually changes, whether the agents in version two are still rational if they know about the shared source, and what a Bayesian who has figured out the correlation should do differently. Tell me whether version two collapses back into version one once you remove that shared source, and which single feature is doing all the work.
- Don't stipulate the verdicts. If the case only "works" because I told you the crowd is wrong, it's no good — the wrongness has to fall out of the structure.
- Finish with how a defender of the "herding is basically benign, committees and markets aggregate information fine" line would attack the case, and where that attack is weakest.

One more thing on the write-up: keep the settled and the contested separate. Don't sell me the correlation point as the consensus of the people who work on this if it's actually a live fight, and don't hedge the standard independent-signal result as if nobody belives it. Flag which claims are textbook and which are up for grabs. American English, please.
