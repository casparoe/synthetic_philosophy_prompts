I have no academic training and have never taken a course in economics, so what follows is my own reconstruction, in my own words, of an argument I keep running into. I am setting it out because I am stuck on it and would like to be told where my own words fail me.

The argument is the case against running an entire economy by a single planning body. Here is the strongest version of it I could build.

(1) To use a resource well, someone has to compare what is given up by using it one way with what is gained by using it another way.
(2) That comparison requires a common measure of how much the different uses matter to people.
(3) Such a measure is produced by trade, by people actually giving things up for other things.
(4) A planning body has, by definition, replaced trade with instructions.
(5) So no such measure is available to it.
(6) So it cannot make the comparison in (1).
(7) So it cannot use resources well, and its economy must do worse than one organized through trade.
(8) So a planned economy must fail.

I cannot accept (8), and not only because it feels too strong. The trouble is that (1) through (4) seem to apply just as well inside a single firm. Managers decide how much of the firm's money goes to one product rather than another, how many people to put on one job rather than another, which project to kill. Inside a firm there is no trade and there are no prices; there are instructions. So by (1) through (7) a firm should be unable to use its resources well and should do badly. But some firms use their resources very well, and so do armies, hospitals and households, none of which has an internal market. Either the argument is wrong somewhere, or a firm is quietly drawing the missing measure from somewhere outside itself, and if it is, I suspect that somewhere is exactly what a planning body for a whole economy would lack. I would like that suspicion tested rather than confirmed.

I have also heard the reply that a planning body could simply propose prices, watch what happens, and keep adjusting them until what is produced is what people want, so that it would have the measure after all. I cannot tell whether that reply works or whether it only appears to work because it has quietly assumed the very thing in question.

And I would like to know whether any of this turns on modern computing. As I have written it, the argument is not about how much arithmetic there is to do; it is about where the knowledge comes from in the first place. But I may have written it that way only because I do not understand the other way of putting it.

So tell me which step to give up, and what giving it up costs. I would most like to keep (1), and I would be sorry to lose (7), but I have no idea whether those are the ones that should go. Do not lay out the positions people have taken and leave the choice to me. Give me your own judgment, committed: say what you think is actually wrong here, and what it implies for the firm, for the household, and for whether an economy could be planned, computers or no computers.
