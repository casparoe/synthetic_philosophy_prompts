Been reading up on futarchy — the scheme where you don't vote on policies at all, you vote on what counts as doing well (some single number for the whole country), then you run conditional prediction markets, one on "welfare if we adopt policy X" and one on "welfare if we don't," and whichever price is higher gets implemented. A couple of crypto projects have actually run versions of this, usually with something crude like their own token price as the metric.

The bit that nags me is the split. The metric itself is settled by plain old voting, politics as usual, and only then does everything downstream get handed to markets — as if the empirical questions were the only ones you can be right or wrong about. What does that split presuppose? Does it need a real fact/value line, or is it just that you can't write a contract that pays out on "the good" and so values get fudged into a number by other means? And does the mechanism quietly need things it never says out loud: that the metric can't be gamed by the very traders who profit from moving it, that a conditional price is a forecast and not a cause (your position helps decide which policy gets enacted, which helps decide the payout), that welfare is one scalar comparable across people and policies, that the winning policy actually gets implemented as written rather than quietly watered down?

Tell me which of those the practice strictly presupposes, which it merely fits well with, and which a rival arrangement — ordinary voting on policies, or a scheme that lets markets pick the metric too — could satisfy just as easily. Where it's underdetermined, say so, and say whats inconsistent if anything is.
