I'm a backend engineer with no formal training in philosophy, and I've somehow become the person who runs the monthly lunchtime reading group at work. Last month we did one of the popular Stoicism books — the kind that boils the whole thing down to the idea that you can't control what happens, only how you respond — and it went badly: half the room said that's trivially true, the other half said it's obviously false, and I realized none of us had actually read any Epictetus. So for the next session I've started the Enchiridion, in an old public-domain English translation whose diction is stiffer than I'm used to, and I'm stuck on the opening chapter. I'll paraphrase it rather than quote, since I don't want to garble the wording of a translation I can't vouch for: Epictetus divides everything into two classes — what is up to us, namely our opinions, aims, desires, and aversions, and what is not, namely the body, property, reputation, and office — and then draws the striking conclusion that if we confine our concern to the first class, nothing external can obstruct or compel us, while if we misclassify, we are left frustrated, resentful, and dependent. Later, in the line everyone repeats, he says that what disturbs people is not events but their opinions about events. I can follow every sentence and I still can't say what the claim actually is. Read as a claim about causal power it looks false — I don't choose my opinions the way I choose where to put a semicolon, and intrusive thoughts, panic, and ordinary self-deception all look like judgments I don't author. Read as a claim about what I'm answerable for it looks trivial. And then there's the piece the modern books quietly drop: the Stoic still prefers health to sickness, and a runner can train well without guaranteeing a win, which hints at either a third class of things that are partly up to us or a distinction between what I can secure and what I can only aim at.

What I want is help reading this passage charitably and precisely enough that I could walk a room of engineers through it without either flattening it into the modern slogan or overclaiming on Epictetus's behalf. Concretely: is the division meant as a claim about causal control, about what is attributable to me as an agent, or about what is properly the object of my concern and desire — and does the text itself settle which? Does the difference between an impression that arrives unbidden and the assent I give it do real work here, or is it a patch that makes the view unfalsifiable? How should I understand the preferred indifferents so that preferring health isn't simply a contradiction of the opening division? And is the popular modern move of adding a third, partial-control category a legitimate repair of Epictetus or a distortion — and if a repair, a repair of what, exactly?

Please explain the concepts and the argument rather than the scholarly literature; I don't need reading lists, citations, or a survey of competing interpretations, and I'd rather have one carefully worked reading of the passage, with the places where it is genuinely vulnerable marked as such. Concrete cases would help — a deploy that fails because of a flaky dependency, a chronic illness, an intrusive thought I'd rather not have — provided you say which of them the view can absorb and which it actually struggles with.
