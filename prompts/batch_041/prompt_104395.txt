I've been trying to work through the puzzle of weakness of will on my own, without any academic background in it, and I keep hitting a snag I can't resolve. Psychologists and economists treat someone as irrational when her preference reverses as the moment of choice nears — she ranks the larger, later reward above the smaller, sooner one while both are distant, then ranks them the other way once the sooner one is at hand (what's often called hyperbolic discounting). But the older philosophical puzzle seems to demand more than a change in ranking: that she judges, all things considered, that she ought to do one thing, and then does another instead. My question is whether these are one phenomenon under two descriptions or two genuinely different things. Concretely: does the reversal in the discounting case count as weakness of will at all, if what flips is only her ranking of the options and never her considered verdict about what she ought to do — and if it doesn't, what would a case look like in which the ranking holds steady but the verdict is flouted, and is that even psychologically possible, or does flouting a verdict always drag the ranking along with it? I'd like the explanation to locate where the real disagreement lies, and to separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who study this, rather than presenting any one side's position as the consensus.
