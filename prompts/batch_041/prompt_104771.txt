I run a grad seminar that's half philosophy, half econ students, on epistemic game theory, and I'm putting together the final. I want one essay question on common knowledge of rationality that they can't just answer by reciting the "everyone knows, and everyone knows that everyone knows, and so on" mantra and then declaring victory. The move I've watched alot of them fumble is the jump from common knowledge of rationality to the backward-induction conclusion in something like a finitely repeated Prisoner's Dilemma or a centipede, and I want the question to plant itself right on that jump.

Three things I'd like it to force out of them:

1) Get them to state common knowledge the two standard ways -- the fixed-point / self-evident-event way and the iterative "all finite orders of knowledge" way -- and say why those two turn out to coincide in the usual partitional setup. I want to see whether they actually know why, or just that it's "the same thing."

2) The meat: why does "each player is rational, and each knows the others are rational, and each knows that..." look like it should already hand you defection at the first move, and where's the gap? I want them to locate the extra assumption the backward-induction argument needs beyond common knowledge of rationality, and to notice the self-undermining flavor of it -- that the certainty you'd need at the later nodes is hard to square with the game ever reaching them. And I want them to see that how they answer depends on how they're defining 'rationality' in the first place, acting on your beliefs versus your strategy actually being optimal, since that fork is where the whole debate lives.

3) A short applied bit: a two-player coordination story where the only communication is a message that might not get through. Have them explain why each player merely "almost knowing" -- high probability at every finite order -- that the other got the message is not common knowledge, and why that can flip whether they coordinate at all. I want them to resist the intuition that if the probability is tiny it barely matters.

No citations anywhere, they can't look anything up, so keep it to concepts. And a one-liner per sub-part on how students typically get it wrong.
