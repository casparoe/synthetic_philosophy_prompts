I have no academic training in philosophy, but for the better part of a year I have been reading about how we talk about machines and minds, and I keep circling one question I cannot settle on my own. Please take the role of a philosopher of mind who also knows how real machine-learning systems are actually built and audited — someone as comfortable with the metaphysics of belief as with the guts of a deployed production system — and think carefully and slowly before answering. Before you construct anything, spend a few lines brainstorming the design space of the case I describe below, so that I can see the candidate scenarios you considered and rejected and why you rejected them. My question is whether it can ever be right to say of a piece of software that it believes something, when the software plainly contains no sentence, symbol, table entry, or stored state anywhere inside it that says that thing.

What I want from you is a constructed case, built to a demanding specification, plus an honest audit of whether the case actually meets it. The specifications are these. First, the scenario itself must be one paragraph, realistic in the way a workplace is realistic — no brains in vats, no uploading, no science fiction, no hypothetical superintelligence; just an ordinary deployed system and the ordinary people who run it. Second, the case must make the attribution of a belief to that system predictively indispensable: forecasting its next outputs by assuming it holds a certain belief and pursues certain goals must beat forecasting them by walking through its actual mechanism step by step, and it must do so even for inputs nobody has ever fed the system before. Third, and this is the part I care about most, the case must make the belief attribution genuinely underdetermined, so that the same observed behavior is optimally predicted by at least two mutually incompatible ascriptions of what the system believes, and nothing in the behavior itself can choose between them. Fourth, the belief so ascribed must not be stored anywhere in the system — not in a table, not in a prompt, not in any weights or memory that could be pointed to — and this must fall out of the mechanism you describe rather than being stipulated by either of us. Fifth, after the scenario, introduce one small and plausible change to the system — a logging feature, a cache, a second model running alongside the first, whatever you judge best — and say whether and why it breaks the tie between the two ascriptions.

Then do the following work on your case. State the two incompatible ascriptions precisely, and for each one say what it would predict about a specific intervention that has never been tried. Walk through what three positions would say about your case: the instrumentalist who holds that the belief talk is merely a predictive strategy with no further question to ask, the realist who holds that a successful belief ascription must be made true by something inside the system, and the skeptic who holds that if no internal state answers to the ascription, we should stop saying the system believes anything at all. Tell me plainly which feature of the case is doing the work — I suspect it is the underdetermination, but tell me if I am wrong — and what happens to the case once that feature is removed. Finally, take the position your case treats worst and write the strongest reply its defender could give, saying exactly which step of the construction they would attack and whether their attack is about the mechanism or merely about the vocabulary. Please write in American English, and keep the scenario itself to one paragraph even though the analysis after it may be as long as it needs to be.
