I've been working through Hume on miracles and I want to know what he'd actually say about a modern case. Here's his track record on the ones he looks at: the healings and exorcisms reported among ignorant, uneducated peoples, he rejects. The ones tied to a particular religious faction, he rejects, partly because each religion's miracles are attested in a way that would equally confirm its rivals, so they cancel out. And when he states his general rule, it's that testimony could in principle establish a miracle if believing the testimony to be false would itself be more of a miracle than the event it reports -- but he thinks in practice that never happens, and the scales always tip the other way.

Now the new case: a severed limb grows back, over a few days, in a modern hospital, caught on video, documented by a team that includes several openly hostile skeptics who were trying to debunk it, with thousands of independent witnesses, no religious group that stands to gain anything, and no rival miracle claims from a competing faith. Whats his verdict?

I care less about the yes/no than about the rule he's actually running. Is it a flat "no testimony can ever do it," or the weighing rule, or something about the uniformity of nature meaning the scales can never tip no matter how good the evidence gets? Seperate out which rules fit everything he says, say what each one predicts about my case, give me your best guess at which he'd endorse, and tell me what further case would actually force the choice.
