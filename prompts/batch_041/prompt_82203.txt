ok so I'm third year, and I'm taking this course on memory, history and ethics thats cross-listed with the history dept, which is why the room for our end of term workshop is so weird. Next week I have to present my paper there and I want to walk in with a Q&A prep sheet instead of just nodding at everything and saying 'good point'.

The line I'm defending: everyone treats the duty to remember as basically a duty to commemorate -- keep the day, hold the ceremony, tell the story again. I think thats a misreading, at least of Margalit. What he's tracking is a duty of upkeep: an obligation on institutions to keep the past retrievable -- archives that don't get quietly destroyed, records that stay open, a curriculum that leaves a trail back, so that someone who asks later can actually find out. Call it the retrievability reading. If thats right, then forgetting-as-not-commemorating isnt the enemy of remembering-as-retrievability. You can cancel the ceremony and still be under the duty, and Rieff's complaint lands mostly on the ceremony rather than on the upkeep. So my move is: take Rieff seriously without giving up the thing he's attacking.

The wrinkle I'm adding, and its the part I'm least sure of: I want the duty to bind only where theres still uptake -- roughly, where theres someone whose standing to be wronged by the loss hasnt expired. So the duty is grounded in thick relations rather than in the value of the record itself, and when the last person with standing is gone the duty doesnt just coast on inertia; it converts into something weaker, a general scholarly or curatorial interest, which is a different thing and doesnt get the same authority. My test case is a village whose last fluent speakers died in the 1970s -- do the recordings still carry an obligation, or only an interest? I need to be able to say which, and I half suspect my answer is gerrymandered.

The room: my prof, who is a Margalit person, sympathetic but precise. Six or seven history grad students plus two history faculty, who are allergic to memory as a category -- for them the honest unit is the archive and the footnote, and a duty to remember sounds like a licence for bad history with good intentions. Two people from the law faculty who do transitional justice work and will come in hot, because for them the duty is load bearing. And one person from my own dept who does philosophy of language and will find a way to make the whole thing about reference.

What I want: the objections I'll actually get, in their vocabularies, so that the historians' version and the transitional justice version sound like different objections even where they overlap. Order them by how much damage they do to the paper, not by how likely they are. Say which ones I should just concede up front, give me a spoken reply for each (90 seconds, I dont get more), and flag where the reply is thin. And tell me the one question I'm hoping nobody asks -- I'm pretty sure I know what it is and I'd rather see it written down.

No citations or reading lists please, I know the stuff for the course, this is just about being ready in the room. Dont be nice about it.
