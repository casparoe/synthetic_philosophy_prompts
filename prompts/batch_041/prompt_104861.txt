I've been TAing a 300-level social choice theory course this term, and next week I've got an 80-minute section with 18 students to introduce Arrow's impossibility theorem. They can read a simple proof and they know what a preference ranking is, but when I've taught it before they nod along and then treat the theorem as a fun fact about voting rather than a wall they've actually hit. I want a runnable in-class game that makes them feel the force of it before I name it. The room has movable chairs, a whiteboard, and I can bring index cards, a timer, and a bag of candy. My rough idea is a constitutional convention for a student club that's hiring one of three finalists for a paid coordinator role; each student gets a hidden role card with a ranking of the three candidates and a one-line reason tied to a personal stake (one candidate will fund their committee, another will cut it, etc.), and they earn points if their top choice wins, fewer for second, none for third, with points buying candy at the end. The class has to agree on a decision rule that takes everyone's rankings and produces both a winner and a full ranking, and that they can all defend as fair, then run it. I want them to propose pairwise majority first and hit a cycle, then reach for a rule that always returns a ranking, and then I want to spring stress tests where a fourth candidate is added or one withdraws and their rule flips the order of the two remaining candidates even though nobody changed their mind about those two. I'd also love a short bonus round where the order in which pairwise votes are taken changes the winner, so they feel that even majority rule is not neutral. The whole thing should be arranged so they run into these walls themselves, not so I announce them.

Can you design the activity? I need the rules, the materials, the payoff scheme, a minute-by-minute timeline for the 80 minutes, the role cards or a way to generate them, and the exact stress-test profiles I should have ready. The debrief should map each moment onto the conditions in Arrow's theorem without assuming they remember the names, and should be honest about which part of the theorem the activity cannot reproduce and that I'll have to supply myself (the full generality, the formal proof, the fact that it holds for any number of voters and alternatives, and the precise role of unrestricted domain). Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle it: I'm guessing they'll try to switch to score voting or approval voting, someone will suggest letting a chair break ties, and at least one student will insist the cycle is just a badly chosen set of preferences. And please don't make it a trivia quiz about real-world voting systems; I don't want them recalling Ireland or Australia, I want them building something and watching it break. No citations or reading lists needed.
