I retired from a career in biochemistry a few years back and have been reading philosophy ever since, mostly the political stuff, since for forty years I never once asked whether anybody deserved anything. A younger friend in my reading group handed me this argument and I've been chewing on it for a week. I'm pretty sure it proves too much. I can't find the joint.

(1) For you to deserve some good thing that came to you — a salary, a prize, a place at a good school — you have to deserve the things that produced it: the effort, the talent, the character that did the work.

(2) You didn't choose your talents or your character. They were handed to you, by your genes and by the house you grew up in, and nobody asked you first.

(3) Same goes for the effort, if you made any. The capacity to work hard, to concentrate, to sit still, to not be ground down by a bad year — that was handed to you too.

(4) So every time you try to trace a reward back to something you deserve, you find another thing you got for free. The chain never bottoms out in you.

(5) Therefore nobody deserves anything. Not the salary, not the medal, not the praise.

Here's what bothers me. If (5) is right, it isn't only the good stuff. Nobody deserves blame either. Nobody deserves to be resented, or fired, or locked up, or shunned, or simply trusted less. And nobody deserves to be believed — not my friend, not me while I'm making this argument, since my reasoning is as much a free gift of genes and schooling as anything else.

Someone in the group said this is just the old luck point and the fix is to seperate luck you could have avoided from luck you couldn't. That doesn't seem to touch it. I'm not trying to equalize anything. I'm asking what makes a person deserve a thing at all, and the argument seems to answer: nothing, ever.

So — which step do I give up, and what does giving it up cost me? I'd rather keep (5) than swallow (6), but I want to know what I'm paying. Also tell me whether it matters that the argument eats itself, or whether that's a parlor trick; I can't tell why it would be one, but it smells like one. And in my old line of work nothing ever deserved anything — a protein folded because of its sequence — so I keep wondering whether that's relevant or just a habit of mind I should drop.
