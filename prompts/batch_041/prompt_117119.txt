A colleague has drafted an encyclopedia entry on Bertrand's paradox, and I have been asked to sign off on it before it goes to press. His gloss runs roughly as follows. The principle of indifference — assign equal probability to cases you have no reason to distinguish — is refuted by the paradox. Ask for the probability that a random chord of a circle is longer than the side of the inscribed equilateral triangle, and indifference returns three answers: 1/3 if you randomize over the chord's endpoints, 1/2 if over a point chosen uniformly along a radius, 1/4 if over the chord's midpoint in the disk. One quantity cannot bear three probabilities, so the classical interpretation of probability collapses. The lesson is that objective probability exists only where we have frequencies; where we do not, any assignment is a subjective choice. Specifying the physical procedure does dissolve the puzzle, since the three procedures amount to three different experiments, but that concession shows only that probability claims are relative to a procedure — subjectivism with extra steps. I want a definite verdict rather than a survey of positions: go through the summary clause by clause and tell me what should stand, what a philosopher of probability working on this topic would wince at, and what is simply wrong. In particular, does his closing subjectivist conclusion follow from anything true in the summary, and is he right that specifying the symmetries of the experimental arrangement merely relocates the appeal to indifference rather than answering the puzzle?
