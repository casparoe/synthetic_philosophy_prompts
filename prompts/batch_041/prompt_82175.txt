I sit on the research-methods committee of a mid-sized institute, and I am trying to draft our evidence policy without smuggling in philosophical commitments I could not defend if challenged. The policy currently has four clauses, and it is the combination that troubles me.

(1) Registration. Before data collection, investigators must lodge a dated protocol naming the hypothesis, the primary outcome and the analysis. Any analysis departing from the lodged protocol must be reported as exploratory.

(2) Novelty. In judging whether a study supports a hypothesis, we discount results drawn from data the hypothesis was constructed to fit, and give full weight to results it did not anticipate. Hence our modelling group insists on out-of-sample validation, we distrust a backtested trading rule however well it fits the history, and we ask of any striking fit between theory and data whether the theory was built around those data.

(3) Bayesian analysis. Our statisticians are Bayesians. They report posterior probabilities obtained by conditioning on the data, and their arithmetic returns the same posterior whether the data reached them before or after the hypothesis was written down.

(4) Forecasting. Our in-house forecasting tournament pays out on a proper scoring rule, which rewards a forecast of a given accuracy identically whether it was lodged before or after the outcome was knowable — and yet we disqualify any forecast lodged once the outcome was in fact known.

Clause (3) appears to make (2) pointless, and (4) contains what looks like the same contradiction in miniature: the scoring rule is indifferent to when the forecast was made, the eligibility rule is not. I want to defend the whole package, but I cannot presently say what it presupposes. I am told the underlying difficulty is a familiar one, but the formulations I have read all concern a single agent who already knows the data, and none of them addresses an institutional package of this shape.

So: what view of evidence, and of the relation between evidence and the timing of belief, does this package commit us to? Address in particular (a) whether novelty is a genuine epistemic requirement on evidence or merely a device for detecting the human vice of fitting hypotheses to data already seen — and if the latter, what that implies for the scrupulously honest investigator who happened to know the data beforehand, whose posterior is, by clause (3), identical to that of a colleague who did not; (b) whether (2) can be made consistent with (3) by relocating novelty from the data to the process that produced the hypothesis, and what that relocation costs — in particular, whether the resulting view remains a view about what supports what, rather than merely about which researchers to trust; (c) what (2) implies for evidence that is old for one investigator and new for another, or old for the community and new for a newcomer; and (d) whether the demand for novelty presupposes that a hypothesis can be confirmed by data it was not designed to accommodate, and if so whether that is a substantive thesis about confirmation or a definitional stipulation in disguise.

Tell me which clause, if any, I should abandon, and what abandoning it would cost. Distinguish what the package strictly presupposes from what it merely fits comfortably with, and say where it is philosophically underdetermined — that is, where several incompatible theories of evidence would license exactly this policy.
