For my school's Model United Nations conference, I have been assigned to a crisis committee on secession and self-determination, and I would like a decision procedure I can actually run during committee: a checklist of questions to ask in a fixed order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, the point at which the procedure returns a verdict of not legitimate, and the point at which it must stop and hand the judgment back to the delegate because the remaining question is one of values rather than facts. I will have to apply it repeatedly and under time pressure, to several regions in the same session, and defend each verdict to the committee and the chair, so it needs to give a consistent standard rather than a fresh intuition each time.

Some background on how I think the problem should be framed, since I have no deeper training than this. Philosophers disagree about the ground of the right to secede. On one view, a group has a right to secede whenever a clear majority of its members prefers independence, so the right is in principle available to any group at all. On the other view, the right is remedial only: a group may secede when independence is the remedy for a serious and persistent wrong — conquest, the violation of a prior autonomy agreement, entrenched discrimination — and not otherwise. I want a procedure that keeps this disagreement visible rather than quietly assuming one side of it, and that can tell me when the choice between the two views actually decides a case and when it does not.

I also want it to handle the problems I keep running into that make the simple version of the question collapse. First, the boundary problem: when we ask whether the people of a territory have a right to secede, whose preference counts — the majority within the territory that wants to leave, or the majority of the existing state that wants to keep it, and does it matter which borders we use to define the group in the first place? Second, recursive secession: if a region may leave because its majority prefers to, must it in turn allow its own sub-regions or minorities to leave on the same ground, and if not, what does that inconsistency reveal about the principle? Third, the gap between a legitimate grievance and a legitimate claim: a region may have a real complaint about how it is treated without that complaint being sufficient to license breaking up the state.

The procedure should be usable in the few minutes I have to prepare a position, by a delegate with no background in philosophy beyond what I have just described, and it must not require me to look anything up. Where a step genuinely cannot be made mechanical — for instance where serious enough or persistent has to be judged — say so plainly rather than smuggling a value judgment into a test that looks factual.

Then run the procedure on three cases and give me its verdict in each: Scotland's 2014 independence referendum, Catalonia's 2017 independence referendum, and Quebec's 1995 referendum. At least one of the three should come out in a way I will probably find uncomfortable; do not soften the procedure to avoid that, and tell me which verdict you expect me to resist and why.

Please take a definite position rather than laying out options: commit to one procedure, commit to verdicts on all three cases, and give your reasons. Where you are unsure, say which specific step your confidence rests on.
