I'm a third-year undergraduate currently taking a graduate seminar in formal epistemology and philosophy of probability. Our unit on imprecise probabilities ended with a lecture on dilation, and I'd like to write my final paper on it, but I'm not yet confident that I understand what the phenomenon actually shows. Please act as an expert in imprecise probability and Bayesian epistemology and give me a careful, self-contained explanation, pitched at someone who is comfortable with conditionalization and credal sets but has never worked through a dilation example in detail.

Here is the case I've been working with, which I take to be the cleanest way to see the phenomenon; correct me if I have any of the details wrong. Two coins, C1 and C2, are each known to be fair, but nothing is known about their dependence: any joint distribution over the four possible outcomes is admissible, provided each marginal is 1/2. Before any tosses, my credal set for "C1 lands heads" is the single point 1/2. I then learn that C2 landed heads — an observation that appears to tell me nothing whatever about C1, since every hypothesis in my credal set already agrees both that C2 is fair and that C1 is fair. Yet conditionalizing each member of the credal set on that observation widens my credal set for C1 to the entire interval from 0 to 1. If I instead restrict the admissible joint distributions to those with non-negative correlation between the coins, the widening is partial rather than total.

Please explain the following.

First, the mechanism. Precisely why can conditioning on something that looks uninformative about C1 widen rather than narrow my credal state for it? What do the members of the credal set disagree about, such that the disagreement is invisible before conditioning and visible afterwards?

Second, the contrast with precise probability. Why is dilation impossible for a single probability function, and what does that contrast show about what has gone wrong (or not gone wrong) in the imprecise case? I want to be able to say clearly whether the agent has become less well informed, more well informed, or neither.

Third, what I take to be the conceptual crux: whether dilation is a genuine epistemic phenomenon or an artifact of a bad notion of irrelevance. Lay out the candidate precise definitions of "Y is irrelevant to X" — for instance, invariance of the marginal for X across the credal set; invariance of the conditional distribution of Y given X; agreement among the members on the conditional probability of X given Y; and any others you think belong on the list. For each, say whether dilation comes out as paradoxical, as a triviality, or as neither, and say which notion you think a credal state ought to be committed to.

Fourth, the fallout. What does the phenomenon do to each of these claims: that acquiring more evidence never makes an agent's doxastic state less committal; that information never has negative value; and that an imprecise probabilist can be a straightforward conditionalizer? Where dilation is treated as a serious objection, what exactly is the objection, and what are the strongest lines of reply? I'm also curious whether it gives any reason to interpret a credal set as a set of admissible models rather than as the doxastic state of a single agent.

Before you begin, please brainstorm: list the possible diagnoses of the phenomenon, say what each predicts about my two variants (the full and the partial dilation), and then commit to and defend the one you find most defensible. Think carefully and go slowly, and flag every step where the answer depends on a technical condition, such as whether the credal set is required to be convex.
