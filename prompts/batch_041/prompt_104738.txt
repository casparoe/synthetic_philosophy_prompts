I run the science side of a small foundation that gives multi-year grants to research groups, and I keep having the same fight with my board. A group's project stops producing anything genuinely new and just keeps patching itself -- every time a result comes back negative they bolt on another assumption and carry on -- and the board wants to know how we decide whether that's dogged persistence or a dead end. I know philosophy of science has a framework aimed right at this, the one about research programmes with a hard core you protect by policy and a belt of auxiliary assumptions you're allowed to revise, and a distinction between revisions that lead somewhere new and revisions that only save the theory from a counterexample. But I can't hand my board a philosophy book, and honestly half the useful content is in the fine distinctions that get lost when you wave at it in a meeting.

So: turn that framework into a decision procedure we can actually run at a renewal meeting. Some constraints. The committee is eight people, about half are working scientists and half are donors with no technical background, so nothing can depend on reading the group's whole literature or on judging the theory's ultimate truth -- we can't settle whether they're right, only whether the programme is still going somewhere. We get one 90-minute slot per renewal, we have the group's own outputs plus two external letters, and the chair has to sign off, so I need a place in the procedure where the answer is genuinely a call for the chair and not a fact we can look up. Give me the questions to ask, in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each one, where the procedure stops with a no, and where it hands the decision to a person because it's turned into a values question about risk appetite rather than a question about the science. And please keep what the framework actually says -- I don't want a generic innovation checklist wearing its clothes.

Then run the procedure on three cases so I can see it work. One: a materials group that staked out a specific, falsifiable prediction a few years back and a completely separate lab has now confirmed it, though nothing else about their core picture has moved. Two: an immunology lab that's our flagship grant and whose PI is a friend of the foundation -- they've had four failed predictions in a row and each time the fix is a new auxiliary assumption that only ever gets tested by them. Three: a young programme that hasn't had time to make a novel prediction yet, where I honestly can't tell whether we're looking at a dead end or just an early stage. At least one of these should come out as a no that I won't like, and I want you to say so plainly. Also tell me where the procedure can't decide and is just dressing a judgment call up as a step, and what it can't do at all -- in particular, someone on my board will always say that defending a core against awkward evidence is just normal science and nothing to worry about, so tell me what that objection gets right and where the procedure should still push back.
