I sit on the program committee of a small interdisciplinary workshop series on the ethics of accountability, and we have funding for roughly six philosophers, legal theorists, and moral psychologists, part-time for two years, to work on standing to blame. The familiar territory has been well worked: the hypocrite who blames, the complicit blamer, the complaint that someone lacks the standing to object, and the dispute over whether standing is a condition on the appropriateness of blame, on the blamer's right, or on something else. I want the next phase to go somewhere less trodden, so take the following provisional frame seriously before you answer.

Treat standing not as a single thing but as three separable strands. First, a permission: the blamer is not barred from performing the act. Second, an epistemic entitlement: the blame is credible and carries weight as moral address or testimony, so that the target and bystanders have reason to take it seriously. Third, an exposure: in blaming, the blamer becomes answerable and can be met with counter-blame, demands for consistency, or an accounting of her own record. These strands can come apart. One might be permitted to blame while having no epistemic entitlement to be heard, or hold the entitlement while laying oneself open to nothing in return.

Given that frame, give me a defended research agenda rather than a survey. Which open questions about standing to blame most deserve the next two years, and in what order? I care more about the reasoning than about the list. For each candidate, explain why it is upstream of the others, how tractable it is for a small interdisciplinary group rather than for a single subfield, how neglected it currently is, and what argument or result over the next couple of years would move it up or down the ranking. Then identify the overrated questions: ones that look central but whose resolution would change little, or that are really terminological disputes dressed up as substantive ones. Finally, tell me where the three-strand frame is likely to be doing illegitimate work, and what would show that it is the wrong cut. We have a standing interest in whether any of this bears on the design of institutional accountability procedures, but I do not want that interest to crowd out questions whose payoff is purely conceptual.
