I'm a fourth-year PhD student in cognitive science, and in three weeks I give a departmental colloquium talk defending a thesis about Hofstadter's careenium thought experiment — the one in which a frictionless table is covered with myriads of tiny magnetic marbles (he calls them simms) that careen around, clump transiently into larger structures (simmballs), and are supposed to be the analogue of symbols in a brain, so that the interesting question becomes which direction the pushing runs: do the simms push the simmballs around, or do the simmballs push the simms? My thesis is that the clump-level description earns a genuine causal role rather than a merely convenient explanatory one — that when we say a symbol shoved the substrate, we are making a claim about difference-making that survives the standard reductionist rejoinders — and that this has a concrete upshot for interpretability and cognitive modeling, namely that symbol-level states should be treated as legitimate targets of intervention rather than as lossy summaries we could in principle throw away. My argument has four moves: first, an interventionist account of causation that permits variables at any level of description so long as they are difference-makers under intervention; second, a toy simulation in which clamping a clump (by adding a weak external field that stabilizes it) shifts downstream clump dynamics in ways that no tractable computation over individual marble trajectories would have handed me; third, the claim that this is not merely predictive intractability but a case of a higher-level regularity doing real work, because the clump state is what the system's own regulatory machinery is sensitive to; and fourth, the upshot that "it's just the weights" is not an answer to a question about what causes what.

I need a Q&A prep sheet, because this audience will not be gentle and I would rather rehearse the actual room than a generic hostile philosopher. The audience is roughly: two philosophers of science who work on causal explanation and mechanisms, both interventionists, who will want to know how a simmball is supposed to be manipulable independently of its constituent simms and who have the exclusion problem at their fingertips; three computational neuroscientists who do circuit-level work and will say the clump level adds no predictive content and that my toy model was built so that it couldn't; one dynamical systems person who will deny that simms and simmballs constitute two levels at all, since clumps are metastable, graded, and partitioned by my convenience; one connectionist who thinks the word symbol is smuggling classical cognitivism back in through the window; and one metaphysics student who will ask whether simmballs are objects with determinate boundaries and therefore whether they can be causal relata in the first place. Two of the neuroscientists are friendly and will ask the question that rescues me if I answer it well; the philosophers are the dangerous ones, and the dynamical systems person is the one most likely to make my second move look naive.

Please draft the prep sheet: the eight or so objections I will actually get from this room, ordered by how much damage they would do rather than by the order they'll be raised, each phrased the way that particular objector would phrase it and tagged with who raises it and how likely it is, followed by a two-minute spoken reply, an explicit note on whether I should concede, hold, or split the difference, and a sentence on where my reply is soft — the point I should not let them reach. Include the concessions I should make in my opening remarks so the easy objections never get raised, and a short section distinguishing which objections attack the toy model and which attack the thesis, so I don't burn my time defending a simulation that isn't load-bearing.
