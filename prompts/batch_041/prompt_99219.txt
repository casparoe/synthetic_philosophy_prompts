I'm the TA for a second-year seminar on cooperation and moral disagreement, and I'm partway through grading the midterm. The students were told they could consult any materials but had to argue in their own words, and that we cared about the argument rather than about name-dropping. I'd like to return the exams with written comments by the end of the week, so I need a careful read rather than a quick impression. The question and one student's answer are below.

Question. In this course we have treated moral trade as the idea that two agents who disagree about value can still both come out ahead by exchanging concessions — each giving up something it disvalues relatively little in return for the other's giving up something it disvalues relatively little, with each party scoring the exchange by its own lights. This is supposed to be distinct from two neighbors: moral compromise, in which the parties converge on a middle position neither fully endorses, and moral offsetting (sometimes called indulgence), in which an agent pays to cancel out a wrong it is itself committing. Consider a concrete case. R assigns overwhelming weight to reducing animal suffering and little weight to preserving built heritage; S assigns overwhelming weight to preserving built heritage and little weight to animal suffering. R agrees to spend several weekends cataloguing an archive for a heritage society; S agrees to go vegetarian and donate part of each month's income to an animal charity. Neither changes its fundamental priorities. Write an essay that (i) states the condition under which this exchange is a genuine moral improvement for both parties rather than a mere redistribution, (ii) explains what, if anything, keeps such trades from collapsing into the sale of moral permission, and (iii) addresses the objection that R, by cooperating with S, becomes complicit in S's — from R's view — badly mistaken valuation of heritage. End with your own assessment of whether the trade should go ahead.

Student's answer. On my reading, the condition for a genuine moral improvement is that each party surrenders something it values less than the other party values it. R values a few weekends of archival work less than S does, and S values a vegetarian diet less than R does, so each walks away, on its own scale, with more than it gave up. That is why the exchange is not mere redistribution: the total moral value, summed across both parties' own scales, is larger after the trade than before.

Nor is the trade a compromise. In a compromise, R and S would each move partway toward the other's position — R conceding that heritage matters somewhat, S conceding that animals matter somewhat — and settle somewhere in between. In the trade, neither party abandons its priorities: R still regards heritage as nearly worthless and S still regards animals as barely counting, yet they transact. The disagreement remains fully intact, which is the point.

Whether the trade is permissible seems to me to depend on whether the parties are approximately right about the moral facts. If S is simply mistaken about how much animal suffering matters, then R is handing over real moral value in exchange for something that is only valuable in S's eyes, and the trade should not go ahead. Only when both parties' valuations track the truth can the exchange actually raise the moral total rather than just moving value around.

The complicity objection, I think, fails. R does not endorse S's valuation of heritage, and complicity requires sharing the value in question; since R shares nothing of the sort, R is not complicit. R is simply paying S in S's own currency, which leaves R's own commitments untouched.

Finally, this is plainly not offsetting. In offsetting an agent pays to neutralize a wrong it is itself committing; here neither R nor S is doing anything wrong that stands in need of neutralizing. The archival work is not a penalty and the donation is not a fine. The trade is therefore a straightforward mutual benefit and, in my view, should go ahead.
