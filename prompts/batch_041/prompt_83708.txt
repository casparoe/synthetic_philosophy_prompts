I'm a third-year PhD student in philosophy, and on Saturday I have to give the eulogy at my grandfather's funeral. He was devout in the old way — daily Mass, the rosary, no interest whatever in arguments for God — and the room will be his parish and my aunts and uncles, plus a few people from my department who know perfectly well that I'm not religious. I've spent two years on Wittgenstein's later remarks on religious belief, and what I've taken from them is that his faith was never a hypothesis about how things stand: demanding his evidence, or offering mine, would have been a category mistake rather than a disagreement. That is a fine thing to think and a hard thing to speak from. The ordinary consolation — that he is in a better place, that he is at peace, that he lives on in us — would have me asserting what I don't believe; saying flatly that he has ceased to exist would have me asserting a hypothesis where there wasn't one. So I want the eulogy built on a distinction I've been chewing on: between asserting something and saying it with someone — joining in his words because they were his, without claiming them as my own. Where that works, the religious sentences in the speech should be his, handed back; where it doesn't, the comfort has to come from describing what he did with those words and what it was like to be near that, not from any claim about where he is now. Please write it, about six minutes spoken, and don't explain Wittgenstein to a room of mourners: if the idea is in there at all, it should be doing work rather than being announced.
