I'm a novelist — a few books out, nothing you'd have heard of, and I teach a workshop on Tuesday nights to pay for the boiler. I've got a problem that I genuinely can't tell if its a plot problem or a life problem, because at the moment it's both, and I keep flipping between them.

I live in a converted warehouse, fourteen flats over a dead printing shop. The landlord wants out, and a group of us want to buy the building as a co-op before some developer turns it into short-term rentals. The catch is who decides. A committee of five of us — self-selected, basically the ones who kept showing up to the Thursday meetings — wrote a draft set of rules and stuck a copy in every mailbox, and the draft says only the eight owner-occupied flats vote on whether to go ahead, on the grounds that they're the ones with equity in the building. The renters are not thrilled. One family's been in 3B since about 2004, a woman runs a ceramics studio out of the ground floor, and there's a couple subletting 6A who are somehow the most organized people in the whole building. They say they're the ones who get evicted if it goes to a developer, they're the ones who've been patching the roof, and anyway we five wrote those rules in a group chat. And here's where my head starts to spin: whoever wins the vote in November gets to write the co-op charter, and the charter says who's a member, and members vote on who else gets to buy in, and on whether tenants get voting rights later. So the vote about who votes is being run by the people the current rules say get to vote, which is us five and the eight owners who are mostly also us five. My partner thinks I'm overthinking it. My friend Ravi says obviously everyone affected should get a vote. My dad says obviously only the people on the hook for the mortgage. Both sound airtight until I push on them — if affected is the test, the developer's affected, the neighborhood's affected, the guy who delivers the flour to the bakery downstairs is affected; if on the hook is the test, the renters are on the hook for the rent and the subletters are on the hook for the sublease, and my dad just says "that's different" and changes the subject.

Meanwhile the novel is a small town on the coast where the year-rounders are voting on whether to let the summer people and the families who moved in over the last decade vote on a buyout of the fishing rights. Same shape, I'm fairly sure, and I've written three versions of that scene and thrown all of them away. So what I want from you isn't "be more inclusive, meetings are hard." I want the actual arguments. Is there a principled way to draw the line about who belongs in the decision, or is every answer just whoever's holding the pen deciding who counts? How do people who think about this for a living deal with the fact that the vote about who votes has to be organized by somebody who's already decided who counts — is that a real contradiction, or just an awkward fact you get past and how? And if you had to hand my character (retired union rep, sharp, tired, allergic to sentiment) the strongest version of each side for one scene, what would you have her say? I'd like to know what I actually think before I have her say it.
