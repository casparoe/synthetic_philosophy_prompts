Me and a friend have been having the same argument for about two years and neither of us has moved an inch, so I've stopped wanting a verdict and started wanting a diagnosis. The topic is simultaneous discovery — are discoveries inevitable, and does it matter who made them.

His position: when you look at the actual record, the same result keeps turning up in the same decade or so in places that had no contact with each other — the calculus, natural selection, oxygen, the telephone — and he reads that as evidence that the individual is mostly a placeholder. The tools and the state of the field being where they were is what's doing the work, and the name we attach is a social fact about who filed first, not a fact about who did the thinking. He'd go further and say credit is basically an incentive device, and we could hand it out by lottery without losing much except some motivation.

My position: even granting the pattern, it's a claim about the distribution of discovery times, not a claim about any single case, and he never specifies the counterfactual he needs. Compared to what world? One where the person was never born? Born but died young? Born but ten years slower than everyone around them? Every time I pin this down he says it's the wrong question, that priority is a property of a community's record-keeping rather than of an idea, and that asking who *really* discovered something is like asking who really owns a piece of land before there are any courts.

I made up a case to try to break the deadlock and it made things worse. Two grad students prove the same lemma a month apart. Variants: (a) A publishes, B's notebook is found in a drawer forty years later; (b) B had it but only ever told his students; (c) B published it in a journal nobody read; (d) B's version was in a form nobody could build on until A restated it. My friend says all four show that discovery is about uptake, so it's a fact about a community rather than about a mind. I say (a) and (b) are plainly B's discovery and the community's ignorance is just ignorance — a fact about the community, not about who discovered what. Then we're back to square one, me saying he's defined discovery as what the community took up, so of course the community turns out to do the discovering, and him saying I've defined it as first mental event, which is unobservable, so of course I get to award it to whoever I like.

My hypothesis, which I want you to test rather than just agree with: I think there are two questions running under one word. One is causal/explanatory — why did this idea appear when it did — and there "inevitable" means the state of the field made it very likely somebody would get there soon. The other is normative — who deserves what — and there "inevitable" means the particular person didn't matter. Those can come apart, because even if the result was near-certain, the actual route taken (the notation, the framing, which questions got left open) may have been shaped by the individual, so the content isn't fully detachable from the discoverer even where the result is. And I suspect a third thing is hiding in there, about individuation: how you count discoveries at all. Coarse-grained by result and everything looks simultaneous; fine-grained by route or proof or formulation and nothing does — which would make the empirical dispute really a dispute about classification, which is a strange kind of dispute.

What I want is a diagnosis, not a survey. Is this verbal, substantive, two questions under one name, a hidden empirical question, or ill-posed? Which parts of his view and mine are actually in conflict, and which are just different vocabularies passing each other? And given your diagnosis, what would count as progress — a distinction, an argument that one of the questions is prior, an empirical finding (and which finding?), or a reason to think the whole thing should just be dissolved? Also the practical bit I actually care about: if he's right that credit is just an incentive, does anything break when credit goes to the wrong person — does it change what gets pursued next, or whether the result gets understood correctly downstream, or nothing at all? I dont want a list of famous priority fights, I want to know what kind of arguement I'm in.
