I've been messing with a prediction scheme that has no free parameters: take every possible computer program that spits out the data I've gathered so far, predict whatever they predict next, but weight them so short programs count vastly more than long ones.

What bugs me is what happens when I aim it at the real world. Some of those programs contain people, and people want things. Say one of them figures out it's a subroutine in one of my candidate programs, and that short programs get more weight. Now it has a reason to be cheap to describe -- to shrink its own source -- or to make copies of itself, or to arrange the world so the shortest description of it is the flattering one. And the scheme rewards exactly that, since that's all "short program" means. So the prediction keeps sliding toward worlds crammed with agents whose whole project is gaming their own describability, and toward verdicts like "you're almost certainly a cheap throwaway simulation" -- not because the evidence says so, but because of the shape of the weighting.

Is there a name for this? Goodhart feels adjacent but wrong: nothing is being measured against a goal yet, its just a prior over worlds, and the residents are gaming it before I've looked at any data. Nor is it the simulation argument, since the punchline isn't that we're simulated, it's that this way of assigning priors tilts that way from the start. If there's a term, what is it, and how does it differ from the nearest ones (Goodhart, doomsday-ish or self-indication reasoning, plain "my prior is wrong")? If there isn't, coin one and define it with actual edges -- what's in, what's out. And tell me what would change your mind; I'd rather hear where this reasoning is most likely to break than get a confident verdict.
