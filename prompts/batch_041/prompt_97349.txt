I somehow let myself get volunteered to run next month's discussion night at my local arts centre's book club — we've just read Tolstoy's What Is Art?, I'm the one who proposed it, and now I have to lead it. The crowd is sharp but not academic: a couple of painters, a retired schoolteacher, a guy from the amateur theatre group. Their default is that art is self-expression and that anyone who calls a work bad because it's immoral is either a censor or a philistine, and I definately half-agree, which is exactly why Tolstoy is such a good grenade to roll into the room. Don't explain his position to me, I've read the book — plan how I should explain it to them in one 45-minute session.

What has to come first so that his verdict (that most of what they love, including his own big novels, doesn't really count as art) lands as an argument and not just as a stunt? Which single example should carry the whole thing, what does that example need, and what would wreck it? I keep worrying that if I reach for some obvious moral horror they'll just shrug and say well obviously that's not good, which proves nothing — so tell me what to use instead. Then the misconceptions, in the order they'll actually form as I talk: I'm predicting "so he's just a moralist / censor", "he means art has to be uplifting", "he's saying the artist's intentions are what matter", and "he's only being a contrarian about his own books" — where in the sequence each one has to be headed off, and what I should openly refuse to go into at this level. And finish with one question I can put to the room at the end that tells me whether they've actually got his criterion, or just enjoyed watching the demolition.
