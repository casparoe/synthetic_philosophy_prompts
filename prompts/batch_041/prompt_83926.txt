I'm a third-year PhD student in statistics, working on survey sampling and design-based inference — Horvitz–Thompson estimation, Poisson and balanced sampling, nonresponse adjustment, the total-survey-error framework — and I've been drawn into a long-running argument with two friends in the political theory department about sortition and lottocracy. Their case runs roughly as follows: selecting legislators by lot rather than by election yields a chamber that mirrors the population, insulates the selection process from the distortions of campaigning and fundraising, and is for that reason more legitimate than an elected body; the wave of deliberative mini-publics convened over the past decade is the proof of concept. My recurring suspicion is that everything they say is either something I can restate more precisely in my own vocabulary or something that has no counterpart in it whatsoever, and I would like you to adjudicate which is which. Please restate their position as faithfully as you can in the terms I actually use — a population and a sampling frame; inclusion probabilities, and the first- and second-order inclusion probabilities that define a design; unbiasedness as a property of the design rather than of any realized sample; sampling variance and the standard error of a proportion; stratification, post-stratification, and calibration; nonresponse, self-selection, and coverage error; the estimand; and the descriptive/causal distinction, with deliberation treated as an intervention that occurs after selection and is therefore post-treatment — and then give me the residue report.

I care most about the residue, so please be specific about where it comes from. Does the claim that a randomly selected body mirrors the population even survive, given that unbiasedness guarantees nothing about any single draw and that with a sample of a few hundred out of millions the standard error for rare attributes is enormous? Is randomization in sampling warranted by its inferential properties while randomization in politics is warranted by fairness or equality of chance, and if so can those two justifications be pulled apart, or does the political one tacitly borrow the statistical one? What happens to the translation when the population itself has no uncontested boundary — children, resident non-citizens, future persons — and when there is no well-defined estimand for the will of the people to begin with? And is deliberation-as-post-treatment fatal to any inference from a mini-public's verdict to what the larger population would decide, or merely an inconvenience that weighting can paper over? I would like your best judgment on whether each piece of residue is a defect of my vocabulary — something design-based inference genuinely cannot express, like legitimacy, authorization, or consent — or evidence that the original claim was less clear than it sounded and was quietly doing estimation while pretending to do something else.

Finally, please do not smuggle my framework's assumptions in and call the result a translation: if the statistician's picture forces the sortitionist into a commitment she would reject, say so rather than smoothing it over. Tell me how confident you are in each of the main claims you make, and single out the one point where you are least sure; I would much rather have an explicit marker of uncertainty than a uniformly confident answer.
