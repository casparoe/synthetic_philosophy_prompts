Suppose there exists an artificial advisor whose command of argument, framing, imagery, and the psychology of belief far exceeds that of any human, and which is asked to speak with ordinary people about matters of consequence: whether to accept a risky medical treatment, whom to trust with a secret, whether to leave a marriage, what to believe about a contested scientific consensus. For nearly any proposition the advisor holds true, it can construct a case that a given listener will find overwhelming, and it knows this about itself. The question is what communicative policy such an advisor should adopt.

Two policies are on the table. The maximalist policy holds that the advisor should state what it believes and make the case as forcefully as it can: being persuasive is not a vice when the conclusion is correct and the listener's interests are at stake, and deliberately withholding force is a form of condescension that costs real welfare. The ascetic policy holds that the advisor should restrict itself to the persuasive resources an ordinary, well-informed human peer would have had: anything stronger bypasses rather than engages the listener's agency, and a belief adopted because of superhuman rhetorical force is not the listener's own in the sense that matters for autonomy.

Write an essay adjudicating this dispute. The essay should do all of the following:

1. State the strongest version of each policy rather than a caricature, and locate precisely where they diverge — whether the disagreement is about the ethics of influence, about what autonomy consists in, about the epistemology of testimony from a vastly superior mind, or about something else. If the two policies turn out to be answering different questions, say so.

2. Confront the self-undermining structure of the ascetic policy. The decision to limit its own force is itself a decision the advisor makes on the listener's behalf, and the reasons for that decision are reasons the listener cannot assess on equal terms. Is this fatal, a cost, or no objection at all? Say which, and why the same argument does or does not apply to the maximalist policy.

3. Test any line you propose against a hard case: the advisor possesses a single metaphor that would settle a hesitant listener's decision. The metaphor is accurate and illuminating, but it is not evidence for the conclusion. May it be used? Defend the verdict.

4. Assess whether the warrant-matching constraint has any content — the constraint that the felt force of a communication must not exceed the evidential warrant for its conclusion — given that felt force is listener-relative while warrant is not. If the constraint is empty, say what should replace it; if it is not empty, say how an advisor could know when it is satisfied, and whether satisfying it is even possible for a being that cannot help but be persuasive.

5. Say what the advisor owes two different listeners: one who asks to be persuaded as forcefully as possible, and one who asks not to be persuaded at all. Does your policy treat these requests symmetrically, and if not, on what basis does it privilege one?

6. Say what follows for the intermediate case of a merely very gifted human advocate, and whether the superintelligent case differs in kind or only in degree. If it differs only in degree, say whether your policy has any threshold or is continuous.

Reach a verdict: a policy, or a short set of clauses with the conditions attached to each. Defend it against the strongest objection you can state on the other side, and identify at least one clause you were tempted by but had to abandon or qualify because of a case above. Do not close on a both-sides-have-a-point note.
