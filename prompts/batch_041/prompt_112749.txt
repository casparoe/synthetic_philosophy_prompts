I run a monthly pub night, and for the past year we have closed the evening with a game I invented and have since grown slightly ashamed of enjoying. I hold up a twenty-pound note and auction it off, with three rules: bidding opens at 50p and rises in 50p steps; the two highest bidders both pay me what they bid, though only the winner gets the note; and the auction has no closing time — it ends only when nobody has raised for a full minute, so there is never a moment at which a bidder can simply wait the thing out. Last month two friends paid me £31 for a £20 note. A third friend, who did a term of economics before dropping out and who has never once placed a bid in twelve months of this, tells me the whole thing is just the sunk-cost fallacy in a party hat and that anyone who bids is being taken for a ride. I do not think he is right, and I have been trying to say why without much success, so I want to rehearse the defence against someone who will actually fight back rather than nod.

Please play that third friend for me — sharp, not a strawman, happy to grant a small point when it is a good one but never the main one, and in character until I say "stop". Neither of us is allowed to name a book, an author, a paper or a famous experiment; the argument has to turn on the structure of the game and on what it is rational to do inside it, as though we had both worked it out ourselves. Keep each reply to exactly five numbered points, one or two sentences apiece, with no introductory line and no summing-up at the end, so it lands as five separate punches rather than an essay. My opening, as the auctioneer: my friend keeps saying the bidders should have stopped somewhere, but he can never say where, and I take that to be a defect in his theory rather than in my friends — at every stage of that auction a bidder was choosing between a small certain loss and a slightly larger one that at least kept the note within reach, and no rule I have ever heard can tell him to prefer the certain loss. If the correct move in my game cannot be named, the game is not a trap but a mirror; and a man who has never put 50p on the table has, I would say, the easiest seat in the house.
