OK, I've been arguing about affirmative action for years and I want to pin down what I actually think, because I suspect my gut is doing something my stated reasons aren't. Here are my verdicts on a bunch of cases. Tell me what principle (or principles) they add up to, and which of my verdicts it makes me give up.

1. Norway style: large companies have to have boards that are roughly 40% women and 40% men, and if a company won't comply it can essentially be dissolved. Verdict: fine, even good. Nobody is being denied a job they would have got, the rule binds the institution rather than ranking people against each other, and the punishment falls on a company, not a person.

2. A US state university doing holistic admissions, race one factor among a dozen, no numbers, no set-asides. Verdict: fine, I guess. Noticeably less fine than #1, and I can't tell you why.

3. Same university, but now there's a fixed set-aside: exactly 20% of seats reserved for one racial group, with a lower cutoff for that group. Verdict: not fine. This is where I get off the bus.

4. India: state medical colleges reserve somewhere around half the seats for backward castes and tribes, hard numbers, separate and lower cutoffs. Verdict: fine, and I feel pretty strongly about it. Which is awkward, since it looks exactly like #3.

5. Inside that system, the creamy-layer idea: families from the beneficiary group above a certain income don't get the benefit. Verdict: fine, and I'd want it stricter, because the same handful of families seem to soak up the seats year after year.

6. Same medical colleges, but the reservation is now for a religious minority rather than a caste. Verdict: uneasy, probably not fine, even though that minority is genuinely poor and genuinely discriminated against.

7. Legacy admissions at a US university, alumni kids getting a leg up. Verdict: not fine, and honestly it bothers me more than #2 does, even though #2 is the one everybody fights about.

8. A legislature with seats reserved for a group that's a minority nationally but a majority in one region. Verdict: fine. A legislature is supposed to look like the country; that's the entire point of a legislature.

9. The same move applied to a fire department: a hiring quota. Verdict: not fine, even if the group is underrepresented. Something about the job being about the job.

10. A quota written with a 25-year sunset clause that then gets renewed forever. Verdict: less fine with every renewal, though I wouldn't scrap it.

11. A group I'm not part of gets the benefit, and I notice I'm much more confident about my fine verdicts when my own family isn't in the pool. I'm reporting this because I think it's evidence about me, not about the cases.

The principle I keep reaching for: permissible when the good is a position of power or a seat in a body that governs everybody (boards, legislatures), impermissible when it's a scarce individual good being allocated among competing individuals (college seats, jobs). That gets #1 and #8 and then dies on #4, which I'm sure about. Second try: permissible when the group's exclusion was total and is ongoing, and the group is something you're born into and can't leave, caste or race, rather than something chosen, religion or language. But #6 is a birth group too and I don't like it, and I'm not sure caste is the only birth group in India I'd feel fine about.

So: what principle actually captures these? State it precisely, tell me which clause is doing the work in each case, tell me what it says about cases I haven't listed, say a quota for a group that's currently overrepresented in the good being handed out, or a quota that's filled by the same families every generation, or a quota in a country that never had the exclusion the quota is meant to remedy, and then tell me honestly which of my eleven verdicts has to go. If the honest answer is that no principle gets more than eight of them, say so and tell me which two or three I should give up.
