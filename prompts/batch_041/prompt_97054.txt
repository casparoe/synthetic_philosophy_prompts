I'm putting together a two-year research fellowship for a single philosopher of science working on dispositions, and I need help deciding what belongs at the top of the agenda. Assume the standard conditional analysis and its familiar counterexamples are already on the table: finks are triggering conditions that remove the very disposition they trigger; masks are conditions that block a disposition's manifestation while leaving the disposition itself intact; mimics are outcomes that resemble a disposition's manifestation without the disposition being present; and antidotes are conditions that neutralize a mask. What I want from you is not a survey of that terrain but a defended ranking of the open questions that remain, ordered by what is genuinely upstream of the rest.

To keep this from collapsing into recitation, work with a framework I'll stipulate rather than assume. Classify every interfering condition along two axes: whether it operates on the stimulus side or on the manifestation side, and whether it acts by removing the disposition, by interrupting the causal pathway from disposition to manifestation, or by producing an outcome that merely looks like the manifestation. I'll call that third kind a mirror, and I'll treat it as distinct from both a mask and an antidote. Now add a less standard proposal: that some dispositions might be guarded, in the sense that their stimulus conditions include the absence of any interfering condition whatsoever. Consider whether guarded dispositions rescue the conditional analysis by making its conditional trivially true, or whether they instead expose a circularity that was always there — and treat that question as a live candidate for the agenda rather than a settled matter.

With all of that in view: which open questions should this fellowship prioritize, in what order, and why? For each, tell me why it is upstream of other work, how tractable it is for a philosopher working alone without empirical collaborators or a lab, how neglected it is relative to its apparent importance, and what argument or result over the next two years would move it up or down the ranking. Be explicit about the criteria you are using and about the trade-offs among them, since importance is relative to the kind of effort available. Also tell me which question looks central but is in fact a dead end, and why. Finally, say whether the two categories I introduced — mirror and guarded disposition — generate open questions of their own, or whether they turn out to be notational variants of the standard ones, and let that judgment feed back into the ranking. I care far more about the reasoning than about the list.
