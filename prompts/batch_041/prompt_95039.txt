i sit on the clinical AI governance committee at a three-hospital network. Thursday we have to sign off on our public finding about the death back in March on 4-West, and on a standing rule that's been kicking around since. Short version of the case: a sepsis-alert model scored a 68-year-old man low for about nine hours while his vitals drifted the wrong way, nobody called the rapid response team, and he arrested the next morning and died. The vendor's own docs flag this exact pattern — post-op, on beta blockers, so the heart-rate signal is blunted — as a known weak spot. The ward nurse told the reviewer that she'd stopped checking the score closely because it had been right for months and the override pathway takes six clicks and a phone call.

The committee is split. Half want the finding to name somebody — the attending, the nurse, the vendor — on the theory that a finding with no responsible party teaches nobody anything and gets us shredded in the press. The other half says nobody could have known, so nobody is at fault, full stop, and we should just publish the list of fixes. I think both are missing something, and I've been trying to work out what. My line is that we've been treating "responsible" as one thing when it's at least two: being blameworthy, and being answerable — on the hook to give an account and put it right, which is seperate from blame. So my proposal is that the finding refuses to blame any individual (I think the knowledge condition genuinely fails for the nurse, and probably for the attending too) but places answerability somewhere explicit: a standing "gap-bearer" role, held by a named person in the CMO's office, whose job is to answer for what our AI-assisted decisions do in the cases where no individual can be faulted. Paired with a rule: no AI-assisted clinical decision without a named human who was in a position to have overridden it, where "in a position to" gets spelled out as having the information, the time, and the standing to say no — not just being logged in.

What I need from you is the memo I'll circulate Tuesday. I dont need case law or citations, I can add those; I want the argument itself. Be straight with me about where it's weak — I can already hear someone saying the gap-bearer is just a scapegoat with better PR, and I'm not sure they're wrong.

Lay it out in these sections, in this order:

- Recommendation (about 200 words): what the committee should adopt, with a concrete date for the follow-up review
- Why I won't name an individual (roughly 250 words)
- The case for a fixes-only finding with no answerability assigned, put as strongly as you can make it (roughly 250 words), then why I still think we need the gap-bearer (roughly 150 words)
- What the rule looks like in practice (roughly 250 words): the conditions on "in a position to", and one thing we could check at 90 days to tell whether the named human is real or decorative

Finish with a short note on how confident you are in the argument overall and which single step you're least sure about.
