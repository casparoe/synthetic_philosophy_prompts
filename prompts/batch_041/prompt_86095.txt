I'm a novelist, and for the better part of a month I've been trying to write a dinner-party scene in which two of my characters fall out over the centipede game, and I've stalled because I cannot tell which of them has the better of it. I would like you to adjudicate their exchange for me, move by move.

The version they were arguing about was one Ines had sketched on the back of a menu: a hundred steps, belonging to the two players in turn. The player whose step it is may take, which ends the game, or pass, which hands the decision to the other. Taking on step k pays the taker k+1 and the other k−1; passing adds one to both sides of that ledger, so that taking on step k is always exactly one unit better for the taker than passing and letting the other take on step k+1. If both pass on the hundredth step, the game ends with a single unit apiece. Whoever takes at the end takes far more than the first-step taker does, and so does the other player; the longer the thing runs, the better it goes for both of them.

What followed, more or less verbatim, was this.

Tobias: "It isn't a paradox; it's a proof. On the hundredth step the player on the move compares taking, which pays her 101, with passing, which pays her one. She takes. Knowing that, the ninety-ninth step is the same choice one level down, and it unravels all the way back to the first. Two rational players, each aware of the other's rationality, end the game on the opening move. That most of us don't is a fact about us, not about rationality."

Ines: "Your proof needs each player to be certain, at her own step, that the other will take at the next one. Nothing in those numbers delivers that certainty. It's an extra premise, and it's doing all the work."

Tobias: "It isn't smuggled in; it's derived. The hundredth step requires no assumption about anyone's rationality beyond a preference for more money over less. Every step before it follows by substitution. That's induction from a base case, not a petition."

Ines: "Induction needs the base case to arrive. You need the player at the first step to be certain that the other is still playing by your lights ninety-nine moves later. That isn't a base case; it's a forecast about someone else's future reasoning, and a rational player revises forecasts when evidence comes in. When your partner passes on step one, the sensible inference isn't 'how strange' but 'so she isn't the player my argument assumed.' There's a worse wrinkle. Your conclusion is that she takes on step one. If she does, no later step is ever reached. So the whole chain is about positions your own conclusion guarantees will never occur, and it's the payoffs at those positions that drive the answer."

Tobias: "Every game-theoretic argument must say something about positions that don't occur; that's what off-path reasoning is. And the alternative you keep gesturing at — the two of them passing and sharing the large pot — is not an equilibrium. The last player would take, and everyone before her knows it. It's the prisoner's dilemma with a longer arm: the outcome that's better for both isn't available to two people who each look after themselves, and nobody calls the prisoner's dilemma a paradox."

Ines: "The analogy fails precisely where it matters. In the prisoner's dilemma, defecting is dominant — better for you whatever the other does. Here, taking on step one is not dominant; it's better only if your partner would otherwise take soon. If she's the sort who passes, passing on step one is better for you than taking. So your conclusion is conditional on a belief about her, and the belief is one your own reasoning eats. Let it be common knowledge that there is even a small chance she's the sort who passes for the pleasure of it, and passing becomes the best response; the unraveling collapses. A conclusion that turns on a probability being exactly zero isn't a theorem about rationality; it's a theorem about a knife's edge."

Tobias: "Then you aren't disputing the logic, you're disputing the model. What you've actually shown is that 'rational' is doing two jobs in this conversation. In the thin sense — take the action that maximizes your payoff given what you believe — the unraveling is right, and the people in the laboratory who pass are simply not maximizing. In the thick sense — do what a sensible person would do with the situation in front of her — the unraveling is absurd, and the passers look sane. The centipede is the cleanest case I know where the two senses come apart. You're welcome to the observation; it just isn't an argument against backward induction."

Ines: "I'd put it the other way round. The thin sense isn't a sense at all until you say what beliefs it's maximizing against, and the theory has nothing to say about where those beliefs come from. That's not a defect of the centipede; the centipede is where it becomes visible. People pass because they read the other player's moves as evidence about her, which is exactly the reasoning the thin sense leaves out. So the puzzle isn't that backward induction is false. It's that a player who is rational at every step can end up worse off than two players who aren't."

I'm not asking you to point me toward the literature; I'll take the reasoning on its merits. What I need is a verdict. For each of the eight moves above, tell me whether it is right, wrong, or right only in a qualified sense, and why. Be explicit about which moves are claims about the game as Ines specified it, which have quietly changed the subject to some variant of it, and which are really claims about the word "rational" rather than about the game at all. Tell me what each of them is missing that the other sees, and what both are missing. Finally, if you had to say who is more nearly right overall, say which of them and on what grounds — and tell me whether the dispute is genuinely unresolved or whether one of them has simply made an error. The scene turns on that last question, so I would rather have a definite answer than a both-sides flourish.
