I chair the earned-release panel of a state corrections department. Under our statute, a prisoner serving an indeterminate sentence may be released early only if the panel finds that he "has addressed the conduct that produced his offense" — a phrase the legislature left undefined and that we have been applying ad hoc for two years. My staff have now proposed a rule they call the own-reasons test: earned release would be granted only where the prisoner can state, in his own words, a reason to desist that he would still accept if release were off the table — not "I want to go home," not "the program said so." Two of my five members object, and their objections point in opposite directions. Our general counsel argues that a reason can bind a person whether or not he accepts it: if the law's reasons are good ones, the prisoner's rejection of them is no answer, and the own-reasons test merely rewards the well-socialized while punishing everyone the system has already failed. Our outside consultant argues the reverse — that a reason a man does not accept is not a reason for him at all, but a demand dressed up as a fact, and that conditioning release on avowed motives turns the panel into a sincerity tribunal it has no reliable way to be. (She calls the disagreement the difference between reasons internal to a person's motives and reasons external to them.) Both objections cannot be right. We vote in ten days and I expect it to be 3–2.

Two cases are pending before us. Prisoner A carries the lowest risk score our instrument produces, has completed every program available to him, and says in every interview that he is only doing it to get out and that the victim provoked him. Prisoner B carries the highest score on the same instrument, has completed nothing, and says — credibly, in the view of two members — that he understands what he did and will not do it again. Whatever rule we adopt, both are decided under it.

Draft the short written opinion I will circulate before the vote. Recommend one rule — the staff proposal, one of the two objections, or a modification of either — and give me the operative sentence the panel would actually adopt, the reasoning behind it, and the honest case for the position you are rejecting. Two constraints. First, the rule has to be one a hearing officer with no philosophical training can apply at a table in twenty minutes, and it must say what to do with a prisoner who is articulate but whose record is bad, and with one whose record is good but who says nothing at all. Second, tell me the cost plainly: which prisoner the rule releases whom we would rather keep, or keeps whom we would rather release, and do not pretend the rule escapes that. Take a definite position — I do not need a survey of the options — and where the argument runs out, say that it runs out, and tell me what to do anyway.
