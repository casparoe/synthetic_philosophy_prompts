I work on both normative ethics and formal social choice, and I would like a translation done properly rather than flatteringly. Take the core of Scanlonian contractualism and restate it in the vocabulary of aggregation theory: individuals as bearers of complaints; the moral status of an act as a function of the profile of complaints the act generates; the test for an act as whether any principle that would permit it could be reasonably rejected by someone as a basis for informed, unforced general agreement; and the restriction that the reasons which may be invoked in rejecting a principle must be reasons addressed to each person individually rather than to the aggregate, which I will call the individualist restriction, rendered as an informational constraint on that function in the sense in which social choice theorists speak of the informational basis of a rule. Reconstruct the apparatus in those terms, then run it against the cases it is meant to be judged by: one person's severe complaint set against the mild complaints of very many, and an act that benefits some while unavoidably burdening others.

The question I care about most is whether the reconstruction is consistent. Contractualism appears committed both to comparing the seriousness of one person's complaint with another's and to refusing to let the verdict turn on a sum or a balance of complaints taken across persons. State those two commitments as constraints, namely comparability of complaint strength and a non-additivity condition capturing the refusal to trade one person's loss against another's gain, and tell me whether any aggregation rule satisfies both together with the conditions the view plainly needs: a unanimity-like condition, anonymity across persons, and independence from alternatives that are not at issue in the act under assessment. If no rule satisfies them all, say which constraint contractualists in fact abandon, and whether abandoning it costs them the individualist restriction.

Then the residue report, item by item, with a verdict on whether each residue is a gap in the formal apparatus or an unclarity in the contractualist formulation: the first-personal standpoint of the person who must live with the act; the difference between the strength of a complaint and the number of people who hold it; and the reciprocity of reasonable rejection, which is addressed to others who are themselves seeking agreement rather than applied to a candidate principle as a filter. If you conclude that complaints are better modelled as judgments about the rejectability of principles than as weights, say so, and tell me whether the aggregation problem then belongs to the theory of aggregating judgments rather than to the theory of aggregating preferences. Do not reconcile the two frameworks by importing one framework's assumptions into the other; if the translation succeeds only because of that, say so plainly.
