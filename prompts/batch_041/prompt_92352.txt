I'm a hobbyist machinist, no college, I just read philosophy blogs at night while parts cool off. I keep running into this argument about whether someone knows something, where one side says it depends on the standards in play in the conversation where you say it, and the other side says it depends on how much is riding on it for the person doing the knowing — what they'd lose if they were wrong. I understand basically everything through tolerances: nothing is ever dead flat, you pick a tolerance off the print, and a part is good or bad only relative to that. So restate both sides in my vocabulary — tolerances, specs, go/no-go gauges, fits, inspection reports, the decision to ship — as faithfully as you can, and then give me the residue report: what translates cleanly, what I had to fudge, and what doesn't come across at all. Two things I specifically want you to press on. First, in my world "in tolerance" is never the same as "good" — my gauge can be off, the part can still fail in service, the tolerance itself was picked by someone who guessed. Is that the same thing as the philosophers insisting knowledge has to be actually true, or does my way of talking just not have a counterpart for that? Second, I suspect my vocabulary quietly splits "is it in tolerance" from "should I ship it," and that the whole point of the stakes side is that you can't split those. If so, tell me whether that split is a defect in how I think about my own work or whether it's showing me the debate is mushier than it sounds. And if some part of what the philosophers care about just has no expression in my terms, I'd rather you say so plainly than improvise a translation.
