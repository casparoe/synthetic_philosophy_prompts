Until I retired I spent thirty-odd years running a lab in immunology, and I've been spending my mornings since then working through philosophy on my own — mostly ethics, and lately the older Christian writers. I got hold of Augustine's line that every lie is a sin, no exceptions, not even to save someone's life, and honestly my first reaction as a scientist was that this has to be a mistake: it looks like exactly the kind of absolutism you'd adopt if you'd never had to weight competing harms. But the more I poke at it, the less I can say what a good theory of lying would even look like, so before I start arguing with him I want the spec. Give me the requirements any adequate theory of lying has to meet: which cases it must get right, which distinctions it can't collapse, which findings from other areas it can't contradict — with the case or consideration behind each requirement, and a view on which of them are non-negotiable and which are just the prejudices of one camp. If some subset of them can't be satisfied together, say so, because I suspect that's where the real argument lives, and tell me which single requirement does the most to eliminate the popular theories. I don't know the landscape well, but I gather the standard one is that a lie requires an intention to deceive, and I'd like to see that and its rivals scored against the list, briefly.

The cases I keep getting stuck on, in case they're useful: whether it still counts as lying when nobody could possibly believe you (my granddaughter's routine where she solemnly announces she's a dinosaur, or propaganda so crude it fools no one); the doctor who tells a dying patient he'll be fine; the misleading-but-technically-true answer, since I once watched a colleague be scrupulously truthful in a misconduct hearing in a way that left the panel with entirely the wrong impression, and my gut says that was a lie even though every sentence was true; bluffing at poker; the researcher who quietly drops the runs that didn't work and reports only the ones that did, which is probably where I most want the theory to have something to say about my own former trade; and the case where the speaker himself believes the falsehood, so there's no attempt to mislead anyone who doesn't already share the false belief. I'd also like to know whether the theory has to treat lying to a machine or lying to a child as the same kind of act as lying to a competent peer, or whether the whole category comes apart once you drop the assumption of an audience that can be taken in. And where does Augustine's ban actually land against the list — does it survive, and if it fails, which requirement kills it?

Please write this as plain continuous prose, the way you'd write a long letter — no headings, no numbered or bulleted lists, no bold, no tables. American English. I don't need a reading list or citations; I just want the requirements and the thinking behind them.
