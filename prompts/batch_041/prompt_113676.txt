When a hunger crisis becomes severe enough to be called a famine, the word is no longer assigned by a government or a newspaper; it is assigned by a scoring system. The Integrated Food Security Phase Classification, run by a partnership of UN agencies, NGOs, and national governments, grades an area on a five-step scale — minimal, stressed, crisis, emergency, and finally catastrophe/famine. The top grade is not a matter of ordinary judgment. It is triggered when three published thresholds are met in the same area: at least a fifth of the population in extreme food consumption gaps, at least thirty per cent of children wasted, and a crude death rate above two deaths per ten thousand people per day (four per ten thousand for children). Where the evidence points that way, a Famine Review Committee of outside experts is convened to verify the analysis; the process is consensus-based, and the committee's mandate is to reason from technical and scientific criteria alone, disregarding the political interests of any government involved. The instrument measures food consumption, livelihoods, nutritional status, and mortality. It says nothing about who caused the crisis, and it was not built to.

So the practice has a distinctive shape. The word is applied to a population's condition, at a moment fixed by a threshold, on evidence of nutrition and death, by experts instructed to set politics aside — and it is applied by agencies that, in their own public statements, insist that contemporary famines are man-made, and describe a famine declaration as an admission of collective failure.

Consider the obvious rival account: the argument associated with Sen that famines are failures of entitlement rather than of food supply, that people starve amid available food because they lose the ability to command it, and that the great famines of the modern era — Bengal, Ireland, the Great Leap Forward — were political events with authors rather than natural disasters.

I want an account of what the classification practice presupposes about what a famine is.

1. Reconstruct the implicit theory. Does the practice presuppose that a famine is a state of a population rather than an act or a process? That it is an outcome — measurable in bodies and body mass — rather than a relation between people and their command over food? That it has a determinate onset, and therefore a date, fixed by the crossing of a line? That causation is not part of the concept, so that a drought famine and a blockade famine count as the same kind of thing at the same severity? That it is a technical fact rather than a normative judgment? Say which of these the practice actually commits to and which it only suggests.

2. Separate what the practice strictly requires from what it merely fits comfortably with. Could a thoroughly political theory of famine — famines as crimes — be implemented with exactly this instrument, using the thresholds as a trigger for response rather than as a definition of the thing?

3. Say where the practice is underdetermined: which rival theories of famine would license the same five phases and the same numbers.

4. Then examine three asymmetries and ask whether each is a presupposition or an artifact: the label arrives only after deaths have accumulated, so a famine that is prevented never becomes one and the instrument records only failures; the top phase is named catastrophe/famine, quietly distinguishing extreme destitution from famine proper; and the death rate counts deaths from epidemic disease as famine deaths. One strand of famine research, based on fieldwork in Darfur in the mid-1980s, argued that the mortality spike was overstated and that famine is better understood as a slow process of destitution and displacement — so the worst famine might be one that never crosses the threshold at all. Does the practice have the resources to answer that, or does it simply define it away?

5. Finally, describe what a practice built on the rival view would look like — classifying loss of entitlement rather than outcomes, permitting a famine to be declared in advance, attaching attribution to the label — and what such a practice would cost in credibility, speed, and political feasibility.

I am not asking for a survey of the literature or a reading list; reason from the description above.
