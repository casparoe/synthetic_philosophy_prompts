I write about science and ideas for a living — a weekly column, a long read every month or so — and for the last three years I've also taught a ten-week evening class at the university's adult-ed place. The class is called Clever Cases, and it's basically ninety minutes a week of making people argue about thought experiments. Best thing in my diary.

Final's in three weeks. I need one essay question: 90 minutes, 20 marks. I have to write the model answer and the marking scheme myself, because the department won't pay for a second marker. The class is mostly final-year undergrads plus two or three master's students, and the trouble is that they can now recite the greatest hits on demand — trolley, Mary's room, the Chinese room, Twin Earth — so any question built on one of those is a memory test and nothing more. I want a question about how a case like that is *built*, and I've written one of my own for them to chew on. It's below. Please don't go looking for it in the literature, its not there — I made it up on a train.

THE QUIET VALLEY

In a valley in the Andes, the water has carried a mineral for a hundred years that quietly knocks out the top two octaves of everyone's hearing. It's painless, it's gradual, and nobody has ever noticed. Birds, kettles, violins, the squeak of a bike brake — the villagers hear all of it, they'd tell you; they've never once felt that something was missing. Then a visiting audiologist tests them and reports that their hearing stops dead at about two kilohertz. Every bird they have ever loved has been singing notes they cannot hear, and every one of them has been like this since birth. Some of the villagers shrug: the valley sounds like the valley and we love it. The audiologist says: you don't know what you're missing, and that's exactly the point — a person can be missing something and never know it. Whose verdict do we trust?

What I want from you:

1. The exam question. It has to be unanswerable by saying "intuitions aren't evidence", because everyone in that room can already say that and I'd be marking the same script twenty times. I want them to have to say what an intuition from a case like this *could* be evidence about and what it couldn't, to give me both a debunking story and a defence, and to notice whether the debunking story they reach for ends up eating itself.
2. A model answer, about 700 words, written the way a very good student would write it under time — not like a lecture.
3. A marking scheme that actually seperates a first from a 2:1. Name the specific moves each script has to make, not "shows understanding".

No names, no "as X argues in Y". If a script cites scholarship I can't check, it shouldn't earn anything, and I don't want students guessing at who said what and being rewarded for it.

Give me the marking scheme as a table with these four columns, and make the marks add up to 20:

| Move | What a first-class script does | What a 2:1 script does instead | Marks |

Then, after the table, a JSON object with exactly these fields:

- "recitation_traps": a list of the three or four canned answers my students will reach for, each an object with "what_they_recite", "marks_ceiling" and "one_line_fix"
- "the_one_thing_every_script_misses": a sentence
- "hardest_move": the single move in the scheme most scripts will bottle, in a sentence

Keep the whole thing tight enough that I can read it on the bus.
