Here's the thing I want you to chew on. We say a group has common knowledge of something when everyone knows it, everyone knows everyone knows it, and so on up. But in real small groups there are two states of affairs that get lumped together under that label. Call them thin and thick common knowledge. Thin is when the whole tower of mutual knowledge is propped up by a single public event: you all heard the same announcement, and if the announcement hadn't happened — or if anyone started doubting it — the tower would collapse. Thick is when the tower holds because each person has independent, private grounds for thinking the others are on board; you'd each still believe it even if the public announcement were retracted. Assume, for the sake of the exercise, that most functioning teams run on thick rather than thin, and that thin is what you get from a memo, a vote, or an all-hands meeting.

Now give me the most important implications of that thin/thick distinction for how a small group should actually run its decisions. What happens to the tower when one member leaves? Is it common knowledge of rationality that does the work here, or common knowledge of something else — goodwill, stubbornness, pride?
