For a comparative-philosophy seminar I am reconstructing the dream material in the Zhuangzi as a formal argument, and the reconstruction I have built is both airtight and monstrous. In the butterfly episode the narrator dreams he is a butterfly, wholly absorbed in that life and unaware of being himself, then wakes and is unmistakably himself; he reports that he cannot tell whether he was a man dreaming he was a butterfly or is now a butterfly dreaming he is a man, and then, oddly, adds that there must after all be a distinction between the two, which he calls the transformation of things. Elsewhere in the same chapter a speaker observes that while dreaming one does not know one is dreaming, that one can even dream of trying to interpret a dream, and that only after a great awakening would one see that all of this is a great dream, while fools take themselves to be awake; he then remarks that he and his interlocutor are both dreaming, and that in telling his interlocutor so he is dreaming as well. (Earlier in the chapter someone else asks how we can know that what we call knowing is not not-knowing.) Here is my reconstruction:

(1) While dreaming, one takes the dream for reality; from the inside there is no experiential mark separating dream from waking. (2) If two hypotheses fit the whole of one's present experience equally well, one cannot know which is true. (3) The hypothesis that I am now awake and the hypothesis that I am now dreaming fit my present experience equally well. (4) So I cannot know that I am awake. (5) Every belief I hold about the world beyond my own mind depends for its justification on my not now dreaming. (6) So none of those beliefs is justified. (7) My present reasoning is no exception: I cannot know that it is not the reasoning of a dream. (8) So I cannot know anything at all — not that (2) is true, and not that this very argument is sound.

My instructor says the reconstruction either begs the question or refutes itself and will not say which; one friend insists the trouble lies in (2), another in the slide from (7) to (8). I want a diagnosis rather than a slogan. Tell me which single step I should give up, why it looked compelling in the first place, and what giving it up costs me elsewhere in the reconstruction — and say whether the regress in the great-awakening passage, where the speaker's own charge that his interlocutor is dreaming is itself a dream, is a separate difficulty or the same one in another guise. I would rather keep (1) and (3) than (2), if that matters. Please answer in no more than 350 words, in two paragraphs at most, leading with the step you would give up and its price before you defend the choice.
