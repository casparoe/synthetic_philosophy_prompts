Her line: our moral beliefs are basically the output of natural selection shaping us to be good cooperators, and since selection only "cares" about fitness, not about tracking independent moral facts, our moral beliefs would look pretty much the same whether or not any moral facts existed. So realism is in trouble. Fine, I don't even disagree with that part. But then she said the fun part: this whole line of attack is a problem *for realists specifically*, and if you're a non-realist — a constructivist, an expressivist, whatever — you just shrug, because you were never claiming your moral beliefs track mind-independent moral truth in the first place. She thinks the debunking argument is basically a free win for anti-realism. That's what we fought about.

Here's my pushback, and I want you to tell me if it holds up. Most versions of this argument are *epistemic*: the claim is that selection gives us bad evidence or an unreliable faculty. But there's a nastier version that I think she's ignoring. Selection didn't just shape our moral *beliefs* — plausibly it shaped the concepts and the reference-fixing machinery too. If the concepts themselves were built for coordination rather than for latching onto anything determinate, the worry isn't "are our moral beliefs reliable?" but "do our moral terms even succeed in being about something, such that there's a fact of the matter to be right or wrong about?" And if THAT version goes through, I don't see how anti-realism escapes, because the constructivist and the expressivist also need their moral vocabulary to be determinate enough to be about anything at all — a constructivist procedure that takes "the moral truths are whatever comes out of procedure P" as input still needs the concepts going into P to be stable. So I think the escape hatch is narrower than she thinks, or maybe not there at all.
