So I read this pop-science piece about Hofstader — the Gödel, Escher, Bach guy — and I want to check it before I repeat any of it at a dinner party, because the last time I paraphrased a book I'd only read about, I got burned.

The gist was: Gödel showed that in any formal system strong enough for arithmetic there are true statements the system can't prove. Hofstadter's big idea, supposedly, is that this is the mathematical shape of self-consciousness — just as the Gödel sentence talks about itself and escapes the system, the "I" is a symbol that points at itself and can never be fully captured by the brain that produces it. That's the strange loop. The piece then says that since no machine can prove its own Gödel sentence, no machine could ever be self-aware, and that this is why Hofstadter thinks the self can't be computed at all — which is also why he's a hero to people who think AI will never work. It ends by saying that for Hofstadter the self is basically an illusion, a convenient story the brain tells itself, so there's nobody really home and the "I" doesn't actually do anything.

The Bach fugues and the Escher hands are, I gather, just further illustrations of the same self-referential pattern, which is at least why the book is called what it's called.

Then a friend of mine added his own spin: he says if we ever build a chatbot that can say "I am a chatbot" and reason about its own source code, we've built a strange loop and therefore a self, and the only remaining question is whether it suffers. That seems way too fast to me, but I can't say why.
