My friend Priya is an editor at a small nonfiction publisher and she's stuck on something I couldn't help her with. The manuscript she's handling has maybe 400 checkable factual claims in it -- dates, names, numbers. She's gone through every one of them and she's confident in each, like 99% on the ones she's least sure about. But the house rule is that before a book ships she has to sign a line at the front saying the book contains no factual errors.

Here's what's tying her in knots: an outside fact-checker definately told her the manuscript contains at least one error, but won't say where -- they don't want to bias her search. So she's convinced that every individual claim is fine and that the book as a whole is wrong somewhere.

Her colleague says she obviously shouldn't sign -- you can't swear a book is clean when you believe it isn't. Priya says thats ridiculous, because she'd happily bet on any single line, and on these grounds she'd never sign off on anything ever again.

She asked me who's right and I gave her some useless answer about how everyone knows what that signature really means, and she wasn't having it. Can you actually sort this out? Is she being genuinely inconsistent, or is the colleague running two different things together, and what should she actually do -- sign, refuse, or something in between? One more thing she keeps flip-flopping on: does it matter that the errors are probably clumped rather than scattered? The whole book was stitched together from about five source documents, so if one of those is off then a whole cluster of claims goes down with it. She thinks that might change something but can't say what.
