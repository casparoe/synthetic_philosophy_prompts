I teach an upper-level seminar on philosophy of history and narrative ethics, cross-listed with film studies, and I am writing the take-home final. The course has centred on the ethics of dramatizing real lives, and I have been pressing a distinction I call the two-ledger view. The first ledger records obligations to the living: descendants, communities, archival custodians, and audiences who may be misled. The second ledger records obligations to the dead considered as subjects of their own lives rather than as raw material for a story. My students keep collapsing the second ledger into the first. They say that because the dead cannot be harmed, talk of owing them anything is either a metaphor for what we owe the living or a category mistake. I want an exam question that forces them to confront whether the second ledger is irreducible, and that cannot be answered by reciting stock phrases about artistic license or historical accuracy.

Please write one essay question, a model answer of about 700 words, and a marking scheme for 25 marks. Build the question around a concrete but invented case: a filmmaker dramatizes the life of a real, recently deceased scientist; invents a clandestine romance that never happened; and gives the scientist a speech endorsing a political position she in fact opposed. The scientist’s granddaughter objects; a historian objects; the filmmaker replies that the film is not a biography but a work of art and that the dead cannot be defamed. The question should ask students to assess that reply using the two-ledger view and to say what would have to be true for the filmmaker’s reply to succeed. I want the question to turn on a further distinction I have been calling the narrative remainder: the residue of a life that survives the collapse of the second ledger into the first, and that may be either a moral residue or merely an aesthetic one. The model answer should take a stand rather than summarize positions; it should distinguish harms to the living from a putative wrong to the dead, and it should engage at least one serious objection to the idea that the dead can be wronged—for example, that wrongs require a subject who can be made worse off. The marking scheme should separate an A from a B answer by naming the specific moves each must contain: whether the student identifies the difference between counterfactual damage to a person’s interests and the violation of a narrative trust, whether they can explain why the dead person’s inability to experience humiliation does not by itself settle the question, and whether they can state what a posthumous wrong would have to be if it is not a harm to the dead person’s experience. Include five must-hit points, the marks each is worth, and a one-line note on how students typically get each wrong. No citations are needed; the case is fictional, and I care about the argument, not the bibliography.
