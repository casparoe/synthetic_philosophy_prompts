In discussions of consequentialist cluelessness, a common move is to invoke the butterfly effect: a butterfly flapping its wings in one place can set off a tornado far away, so any small act may have enormous and unforeseeable consequences, and therefore the consequentialist can never know what she is bringing about. The analogy is used to support a strong conclusion — that consequentialism is not merely difficult but practically impossible, because the consequences of our choices are epistemically inaccessible. I want an audit of how far the analogy actually holds.

Begin with an explicit mapping: what corresponds to the butterfly, the wing flap, the initial atmospheric conditions, the sensitive dependence, the tornado, and the causal chain between them? Then identify where the mapping breaks. In particular, distinguish two things the cluelessness charge might be: a causal claim, that we cannot trace the downstream causal consequences of an act, and an evaluative claim, that even if we could trace them we could not rank or compare the resulting states of affairs. Which of these, if either, does the butterfly effect actually illustrate? Does the analogy import features from chaotic systems — determinism, a fixed underlying dynamics, sensitive dependence on initial conditions — that the ethical case may not have? And does the strong conclusion survive once those disanalogies are on the table, or was the analogy carrying the argumentative weight?

Finally, if the butterfly effect is a poor fit, propose a better analogy for cluelessness — one that captures what is actually supposed to be unknowable — and say what it would and would not establish.
