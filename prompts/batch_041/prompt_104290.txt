A buddy in my reading group hit me with this and I can't find the exit. He says Condorcet's jury theorem actually refutes itself, and here's how he lays it out:

(1) If each of n voters is independently more likely than not to get a yes/no question right, then the chance the majority gets it right climbs toward certainty as n grows — the error probability falls off exponentially in n.
(2) So a big enough electorate is right with probability as close to 1 as you like.
(3) So on any factual question the majority has voted on, the rational move is to just adopt the majority's answer as your own — you literally can't do better than a near-certain source.
(4) But a voter who does that has stopped forming an independent judgment; they're just copying the majority.
(5) So in any electorate where people are rational enough to apply (3), the votes aren't independant anymore, and that's exactly the assumption (1) needs.
(6) So the theorem only applies to crowds that don't understand it, and the more rational the crowd the less it applies. As a defense of democracy it's self-undermining.
(7) Worse, feed the crowd the question "should you defer to the majority?" If they answer no, then by (3) you shouldn't defer to them — contradiction. If they answer yes, then by (3) you should defer, which is just the same move again. It eats its own tail.

He's been calling the whole thing the deference ratchet, which I think is just him being cute. Anyway: (1) looks bulletproof to me and I'd hate to lose it. (6) and (7) are the fun part and honestly I want them too. So where's the actual mistake, if there is one? My gut says (3), but why does (3) look so good if it's wrong, and what does giving it up cost? Because rejecting (3) feels like saying a near-certain information source shouldn't be believed, which sounds insane. Also I don't think (5) is as strong as he thinks — is the independence assumption really that all-or-nothing? Give me the diagnosis, in American English, and please don't just tell me to go read the literature on jury theorems.
