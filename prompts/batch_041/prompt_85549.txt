I'm taking a course on the history of ethics (we've done Plato and Aristotle and are just into the 18th century), and for the response paper I want to do an actual case analysis instead of the usual look-how-quaint-Franklin-was essay. The case is Franklin's own run at moral perfection as he tells it in the Autobiography, and I want to treat it as an episode with a method in it, not just a punchline. The method: thirteen virtues, each with a one-line precept, a little book with a page per virtue ruled in red ink into seven columns and thirteen rows, a black spot recorded for every fault found at the evening review, and a week's strict attention given to one virtue at a time, moving down the list only when a line came clean -- temperance first, on the theory that a cool head makes the rest easier, then silence, then order, thirteen weeks to a course and four courses to a year, with the garden-weeding analogy (don't try to pull all the weeds at once). Then the parts I actually care about. Order is the one that breaks him: he says it gave him the most trouble, and his own explanation is almost sociological -- a man who disposes of his own time, a journeyman printer say, could manage it, but a master who has to receive people at their own hours can't, and anyway his memory was good enough that he never felt the inconvenience of having no method, so he had no early habit to lean on. He gets so little traction that he nearly gives up, and that's where the axe story comes in: the man who asks the smith to grind the whole surface bright, tires of turning the wheel, and decides he likes a speckled axe best -- which Franklin says is what something pretending to be reason kept whispering to him, that this extreme nicety was a kind of foppery in morals, and that a benevolent man should keep a few faults to keep his friends in countenance. And the ending: he never got there, but says he was a better and happier man for trying, and he mentions that the list began at twelve and became thirteen only after a Quaker friend told him he came across as proud and overbearing, so humility was bolted on last -- after which he admits he can't claim the reality of that virtue, only the appearance, and that he'd probably be proud of his humility if he ever managed it.

What I want is that episode run through the ancient thesis that the virtues come as a package. Socrates in the Protagoras won't let courage be seperated from wisdom, and Aristotle holds that the virtues are inseparable because each one requires practical wisdom and you can't have any one of them without the others (with the doctrine of the mean making each virtue a matter of hitting the right amount in context rather than following a rule). Franklin's method looks like a clean test, because it's serial and atomistic: thirteen separate habits, installed one at a time, each with a precept short enough to check off, failure recorded as a spot on a grid rather than as a defect of character. So: which parts of the episode does the unity thesis actually explain, and where does it misdescribe what happened? Does his report that the early virtues went fine and Order alone defeated him support the thesis or embarrass it -- on a strong reading, if the virtues really are one thing, shouldn't the whole course have collapsed at once? What does the thesis make of humility arriving last, as a repair job after a social complaint, when Aristotle would say you can't attach a virtue at the end because it needs the others to be exercised at all? And is Franklin's own diagnosis of the Order failure -- that it depends on whether your work lets you dispose of your time -- an unwitting concession to the other ancient point, that virtue needs external conditions and not just will, in which case the experiment fails for reasons that have nothing to do with his character?

Two things I'd like pushed on specifically. First, the self-undermining bit: if humility is the virtue that forbids thinking too well of yourself, and Franklin's evidence for having it is a technique for winning arguments by seeming modest (dropping certainly and undoubtedly for I conceive, I apprehend -- he says it worked, he carried his points more often), is that a counterexample to the unity thesis, a virtue you can fake that still delivers the good it's supposed to deliver, or is it just Aristotle being right that you can have the habit without the virtue? Second, the foppery worry and the speckled axe: does that anecdote show him quietly revising the ideal, or only losing his nerve, and can the unity thesis even describe a person who stops at a speckled axe -- someone with fragments of virtue -- or does it have to say he simply lacks virtue full stop? Run it both ways please, theory on the episode and episode on the theory, and tell me where the theory is just silent rather than forcing it to speak.
