I work on AI governance, and over the past year the singleton hypothesis—the idea that a single decision-making agency could come to dominate world affairs, whether a single AI, a world government, or a hegemonic coalition—has become a live concern for me rather than an abstract one. In a recent seminar on international relations, I was struck by the thought that the Concert of Europe might be the closest historical analogue we have. I would like you to analyze the Concert of Europe as a case study for the singleton hypothesis. The Concert was the system of great-power congresses and consultations that emerged after the Napoleonic Wars, involving Britain, Russia, Austria, Prussia, and later France, and it aimed to preserve the balance of power and prevent revolution until it broke down around the Crimean War. Was the Concert a singleton in the relevant sense, or was it something else—a coalition, a regime, a balance-of-power system? What does the singleton hypothesis say about the strategic situation the great powers faced, and what does it imply about the rationality of their efforts to maintain a concert system rather than a true singleton? What made the Concert stable for as long as it was, and what made it collapse? What does the case show about the conditions under which a singleton can arise and persist? Does it support the pessimistic view that singletons are inherently fragile, or the optimistic view that they can be stable? Finally, what does the Concert reveal about the singleton hypothesis itself—does the analogy force us to revise the definition of a singleton, or does it show that the hypothesis is too vague to be useful? Please be disciplined about the mapping: identify the relevant agents, the decision-making structure, the mechanisms of enforcement, and the sources of instability. Where the theory is silent or the analogy breaks down, say so rather than forcing the fit.
