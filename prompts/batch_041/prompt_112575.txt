I'm putting together the centrepiece for a little zine my evolutionary-anthropology reading group is doing on signalling, and I want it to be a spoof op-ed. The voice is an invented management consultant — give him a pompous name and a bogus affiliation — who has read one too many pop-science glosses on Zahavi's handicap principle and now believes, earnestly and at exhausting length, that the lesson of the peacock's tail is that institutions should make their credentials as wasteful as possible. He's writing in the business pages of a broadsheet, so it's deadpan, no winking, no authorial nudge, and he should sound like a man who thinks he's being brave. The humour has to come from the biology being *right*, because my reading group will howl if it isn't: he understands that a signal only stays honest if the cost is differentially borne — cheaper for the genuinely high-quality signaller, ruinous for the bluffer — and he knows the difference between an index, which can't be faked because physics won't let you, and a handicap, which can be faked but isn't worth it. He knows all of that, and he still talks himself into something genuinely alarming.

Concretely, I'd like him to argue that the ideal hiring process is one that strong candidates find merely tiring and weak candidates find ruinous, so firms should be designing ordeals rather than tests, and that a year of unpaid slog is not an inefficiency but the whole point. He should raise the obvious objection himself — why not just measure the skill directly? — and bat it away with something that's recognisably in the spirit of the theory (a test that's cheap for everyone tells you nothing about who'll stay, who's committed, whose quality is real) rather than with a howler. Where I want him to overreach is the policy conclusion: that because the waste is doing informational work, the state should subsidise it, and that a society that maximises the cost of its credentials is simply a society that has understood evolution. He should also, once, mistake a mere index for a handicap and get away with it rhetorically. Around 600 words, a headline plus the body, British English, and hard cap it at 650 — I want it tight. By the end the reader should feel the real question (is a stable, informative, wasteful equilibrium any kind of defence of the waste?) without the consultant ever suspecting he's made the case against himself.
