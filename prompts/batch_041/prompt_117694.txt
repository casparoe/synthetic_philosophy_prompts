I'm writing an essay on Nietzsche's Genealogy for a seminar and I'd like you to poke holes in the draft before I hand it in. Heres the gist.

My claim is that the book gets misdescribed as a debunking argument, and that this wrecks the reading. Read as debunking, the First Essay says our concepts good and evil were invented by the weak out of ressentiment — a kind of imaginary revenge, the way someone who's been passed over at work stops caring about the standards of the place and invents his own scoreboard — and that this disreputable origin shows the concepts have no authority. Which is roughly what the contemporary evolutionary debunkers do: the causal history of a belief is supposed to undercut its justification. Then the objection writes itself — you can't get from where a belief came from to whether it's true, so Nietzsche's critique is a genetic fallacy.

But I don't think that's the shape of the book. The First Essay isn't showing that slave morality is false; it's tracking a revaluation, the noble good/bad being displaced by good/evil, and the whole point is to raise the question of the value of our values, which is a different question from their truth. And that's why the Third Essay matters more than people admit: the ascetic ideal isn't just monks being gloomy, it's what gave suffering a meaning, and its heir is the will to truth itself — science included — so the book ends up turning the genealogical knife on Nietzsche's own practice. If that's right, the genetic fallacy objection misfires, and the real question is whether the will to truth can survive its own genealogy.

Where I'm shakier. I say in the draft that the Second Essay treats bad conscience and guilt as the same thing — the internalised cruelty of a man who can no longer discharge his aggression outward just is the feeling of being guilty. And I close by saying Nietzsche wants a return to master morality, which is why the book gets read as dangerous.

So: where am I flat out wrong about the essays, and is my Third Essay bit doing real work or just repackaging the standard "it isn't a debunking argument" line? I'd rather be told the first than flattered on the second.
