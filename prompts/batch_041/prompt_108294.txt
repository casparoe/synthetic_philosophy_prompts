Since 2019 I have held the lay seat on the clinical ethics committee at a mid-sized teaching hospital — I am a science journalist by trade, and I took the seat because I wanted to see how the sausage gets made — and over the past two years the reproductive endocrinology division has begun sending us embryo-selection consents at a rate nobody planned for. It used to be one a quarter, almost always a single-gene condition with a clear clinical severity. It is now four or five a month, and a growing share are requests to rank an entire batch of embryos by a polygenic score — for adult height, for risk of a common disease, occasionally for a behavioural or cognitive trait — and to implant the embryo at the top of the list. We have no framework. The written policy says the committee defers to the treating physician's judgement of medical necessity, which does no work at all when the trait in question is not a disease, so we relitigate the whole thing from first principles in a room with a two-hour agenda, a clinician who has already told the patient that something is probably possible, a chaplain, a disability studies scholar and me. Decisions get documented on a one-page form that the chair signs and the division chief must countersign; we get twenty minutes of pre-reading and about half an hour of discussion per case, and none of us is a philosopher.

What I want is the strongest version of the principle I find most attractive, brought down to earth. Julian Savulescu and those who follow him defend what they call procreative beneficence: roughly, that parents choosing among the children they could actually bring into existence have a reason — on his stronger reading, an obligation — to choose the one expected to have the best life, where the traits that count are those bearing on how well that child's life goes, and where all-purpose goods such as health, intelligence or memory get their weight from their usefulness in whatever life the child ends up wanting. I find this compelling and I cannot defend it in the room, largely because I can never tell whether I am applying the principle or applying my own taste with the principle's vocabulary. So please convert it into a procedure we can actually run: the questions in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops with a yes or a no on its own authority, where it must hand the decision back to a person because the question is one of values rather than facts, and which distinctions have to survive the translation into a form my chaplain and my disability studies colleague can both apply. Tell me plainly which positions in the underlying disputes the procedure quietly commits us to — in ordinary language, no citations, and nothing that requires the reader to have read the literature — and where it will misfire.

Then run it on three requests, in each case giving me the answer the procedure yields rather than the answer you think I want to hear: (a) five embryos, no disease indication, a polygenic score for adult height, both prospective parents well below average height, and a request to implant the tallest-scoring one; (b) two deaf parents, both raised in signing families, who ask us to select an embryo carrying the variant for their form of deafness and to implant that one; (c) a batch in which every embryo scores poorly on the trait at issue, with the parents asking to proceed with the least-bad one and the clinician supporting them. At least one of the three should land somewhere I will find hard to accept; where that happens, say why the procedure lands there rather than softening toward my preferences, and what I would have to give up in the principle itself to get the verdict I want.

Give me two sentences of bottom line first, then the rest as a single JSON object with exactly these fields. bottom_line: string. procedure: an array of objects, each with step (integer), question (string, phrased so it can be read aloud), sufficient_answer (string, what would satisfy it, including what to do when the evidence is a weak polygenic score), if_not_satisfied (string, naming either the next step number or the terminal outcome: approve, refuse, or refer), kind (one of empirical, conceptual, value), and who_decides (string, the person or body who must settle it if it is not a fact). cases: an array of three objects, each with case (string), assumed_facts (array of strings), verdict (one of approve, approve_with_conditions, refuse, refer), confidence (number between 0 and 1), decisive_step (integer, the step number that settles it), reasoning (string, no more than 150 words), what_would_flip_it (string), and dissent (string, the strongest objection a committee member would raise and how the procedure answers it, if it can). commitments: an array of objects, each with disputed_question (string) and position_the_procedure_takes (string). simplifications: an array of objects, each with distinction (string), kept_or_dropped (one of kept, dropped), and cost (string, what is lost by that choice). failure_modes: an array of strings, each naming a case type where the procedure will give a wrong or unusable answer and what a committee should do instead.
