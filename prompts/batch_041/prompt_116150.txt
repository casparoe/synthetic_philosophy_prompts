I have spent the last month in a reading group on philosophy of mind that keeps returning to the divide between original and derived intentionality, and I have noticed that my credence in the distinction swings wildly depending on which example is on the table. So I want a single number with its derivation rather than another survey of positions. The proposition I want priced is this: there is a determinate, non-conventional fact of the matter about which representational states are original and which are merely derived, a fact that does not depend on anyone's finding it useful to interpret the system in question, and that places the internal state of Dennett's two-bitser (the coin-operated machine whose internal state registers genuine quarters and rejects slugs, and which would register something else altogether if that same state had instead been wired to a lamp or to a different mechanism downstream) in a genuinely different category from a human being's belief that there is a quarter in her hand. To be precise about what I am not asking: I am not asking whether the distinction is useful, nor whether we can usually tell the cases apart in practice, but whether one of them has its content in a way the other does not.

Please decompose the question rather than reporting a bottom line. I take the terms to be something like these, though you should adjust the list if you think I have mis-carved the space: first, whether content is interpretation-relative all the way down, so that original intentionality is not a further fact about the system but a courtesy we extend to systems we find it convenient to treat as believers; second, conditional on there being more to it than stance, whether the extra ingredient can be specified in austere naturalistic terms (selection history, causal-informational covariation, counterfactual tracking under the right conditions) or whether it requires something like a first-person or phenomenal ingredient that no functional story can supply; third, whether the two-bitser intuition is a fair test or an artifact of the machine's simplicity, so that the sense that its state has no content of its own would evaporate once we gave it memory, learning, several downstream consumers of the same state, or an evolutionary history that made coin-checking adaptive; fourth, whether the question is well-posed at all, that is, whether intrinsic versus derived marks a real joint rather than a verbal one that dissolves once each side says what it means by representation; and fifth, if the distinction is real, whether it is binary or a matter of degree, which matters because a degree view can grant Dennett nearly everything he wants while still refusing the framing he is arguing against. Give me a rough probability for each term with one line of justification, tell me how the terms combine into the target number, and then identify the term the final number is most sensitive to.

For calibration, I put myself at about 0.3 and the colleague I argue with most, who reads the intrinsic/derived distinction as tracking something like the presence of a subject of experience, at about 0.8. Tell me whether our disagreement is located mainly in the first term or the second, that is, whether we disagree about the metaphysics of content or only about what would ground the distinction if it exists. Then tell me what would actually move your own number, and by how much: a working teleosemantic account of the two-bitser's state, an argument that the state already has content once its history and its role in the machine's economy are specified, or a demonstration that the distinction cannot be drawn without circularity. Finally, apply your number to the case I care about most, a large language model trained on text and then fine-tuned on human feedback: is its aboutness original or derived, or is the question at that point just a way of asking how much we care about the difference?
