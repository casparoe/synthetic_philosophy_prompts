I am a professor of philosophy of science, and I sit as the outside member on the advisory board of our university's natural history museum, which is installing a permanent gallery on the history of life. The gallery ends in a timeline wall running from the Archean to the present, and the final panel is still blank. The board must settle its contents before the fabrication deadline in April. Our curator proposes that the present be labeled the Anthropocene, and wants to know where on the wall to place the boundary marker. Our two geologists object that the Anthropocene was rejected as a formal epoch in 2024 — the rejection was carried by a small expert vote within a subcommission of the International Commission on Stratigraphy and then confirmed by the International Union of Geological Sciences — so a museum that labels the present an epoch would be teaching something the responsible body declined to ratify. A third group on the board wants the panel to avoid the term altogether and speak only of the Holocene and the Great Acceleration.

Please draft the short written opinion I will circulate before we vote. It should recommend a concrete decision — the label, the date the boundary marker represents, and the explanatory text beneath it — with a clear time frame for the designers, and it should give the strongest opposing consideration an honest hearing rather than dismissing it.

The board keeps circling one underlying question, and I would like the memo to take a position on it: is the onset date a matter of discovery or of stipulation, and may a museum legitimately adopt a term that the naming body has rejected, especially when the geological union itself said on announcing the rejection that the term would go on being used by scientists and the public to describe human impact? Please engage the actual state of the debate rather than treating it as closed. Rival proposals date the onset to about 1610, on the dip in atmospheric carbon dioxide recorded in Antarctic ice that followed the collapse of Indigenous populations in the Americas and the regrowth of forest on abandoned land, or to the late eighteenth century. The working group's own case, rejected in 2024, placed the boundary in the mid-twentieth century and pointed to plutonium fallout from atmospheric nuclear tests preserved in the sediments of Crawford Lake in Ontario. Others argue that the Anthropocene is not an epoch at all but an ongoing geological event, in which case the designers' request for a single boundary point may rest on a mistake. Bear in mind too that stratigraphic rules distinguish a boundary fixed by a physical reference point in a rock sequence from one fixed by a stipulated date, the device used for boundaries older than roughly 630 million years, and say what each convention would imply for a display that has to fit on one panel.

Please write the memo in British English.
