Several jurisdictions now decide bail, parole, and sentencing conditions partly by entering a defendant's attributes into a statistical model that returns a recidivism score, where the score is an estimate drawn from how people with those attributes have behaved before; the same courts, in the same proceedings, insist that each defendant be judged as an individual rather than as a member of a class. Insurers do something structurally similar with applicants, and it provokes little objection. What does this practice presuppose about the reference class problem: that a determinate probability attaches to a single person at all, that the model's chosen variable set rather than any of the many other classes the person falls into fixes that probability, or only that some class-relative estimate is good enough to decide on? Which rival views does it rule out -- for instance, that single-case probabilities are meaningless because probabilities are frequencies in a population -- and which does it leave open, since a propensity view, a credence view, and a purely decision-theoretic reading of the score might all license the same procedure? Distinguish what the practice strictly requires from what it merely fits comfortably with, say whether its treatment of criminal defendants and of insurance applicants rests on one theory or two, and describe what would have to change if the presupposed view were abandoned in favour of the claim that only classes, never individuals, can be the proper objects of these estimates.
