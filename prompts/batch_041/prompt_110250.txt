After thirty-five years as an experimental chemist, I have spent the last four reading philosophy of language and literary theory, and I have run into a question I cannot get a grip on, largely, I suspect, because my instincts are those of someone who spent a career assuming that when two people disagree about what an instrument is measuring, at most one of them can be right.

Here is the case I keep turning over. A novel is published and read for fifty years as a satire of a political movement; reviewers, teachers, and ordinary readers all take it that way, and nothing in the text visibly resists that reading. The author dies, a sealed archive is opened, and the letters and drafts make it plain that the author intended the book as a sincere and admiring defense of the very movement readers took it to be mocking, and that the author foresaw the satirical reading and was content to let it stand.

I want your credence in a proposition I will try to state so that it has a truth value. Stipulate that an interpretation is correct just in case it is the reading a competent reader ought to adopt. Then:

(P) In the case described, the correct interpretation is the one fixed by the author's actual communicative intention, and the circumstance that competent readers took the work otherwise does not make their reading correct.

Give me a number between 0 and 1, then decompose it: the sub-questions the figure rests on, a rough probability for each with one line of justification, how they combine, and the resulting value. I expect the decomposition to include at least these: whether there is a determinate fact of the matter about what a competent reader ought to adopt, as opposed to interpretation being relative to a community or to a purpose; whether the meaning of a work is closer to what a speaker means on an occasion or to what the words mean by convention; whether publication transfers interpretive authority from author to audience; and whether the case is even coherent, since everything the author did publicly — the genre signals, the tone, the decision to publish and not correct — might itself constitute the intention, in which case the supposed gap between text and intention closes.

I would also like you to adjudicate a distinction I have invented, because I cannot tell whether it survives scrutiny: between (i) the author's actual intention, including intentions never articulated and possibly misreported; (ii) the intention the author would have had under full reflection and full information; and (iii) the intention a competent reader could in principle recover from the text alone. Tell me whether (ii) and (iii) are genuinely distinct from (i) or collapse into it or into each other, and if the trichotomy is sound, give each a probability of being the standard that fixes the correct reading.

My own number for (P) is about 0.7, though I notice I may simply be importing a chemist's prejudice that the facts settle matters. Tell me your number; which term in your decomposition the final figure is most sensitive to; whether my 0.7 and your figure are more likely to diverge over the first sub-question or the third; and what single argument or piece of evidence would move you furthest.
