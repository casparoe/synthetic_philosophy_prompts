I've spent the last few years reporting on metric-driven institutions — hospital systems, universities, police departments, platform companies — and I've come to hold a sharper version of Goodhart's law than the one usually invoked. On my version, proxies don't degrade because measurement is imperfect; they degrade because a metric that allocates scarce resources becomes a prize, and prizes go to whoever is best positioned to game them. The law's scope is therefore exactly the scope of competition for resources: where allocation isn't competitive, I claim, the effect doesn't arise. Tell me what would count against this. What would have to be true for it to be false, which observations or arguments should move me, and how would you rank them from fatal to merely awkward? I'd also like to know what my version predicts that the loose formulation doesn't, which of these defeaters a defender would concede rather than wave away, and whether the appeal to competition has quietly made the claim unfalsifiable — and if so, whether that is disqualifying for a law of this kind. The most decisive thing I could be shown is a well-documented case of a proxy degrading under intense optimization in which no one was competing for anything.
