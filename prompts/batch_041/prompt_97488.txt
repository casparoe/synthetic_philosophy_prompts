I serve on the grants committee of the Aster Fund, a mid-sized family foundation that two years ago redirected most of its giving toward long-term risks. Our next cycle includes one unusually large proposal, and I must circulate a written opinion before the vote.

The proposal comes from our own program staff. They argue that we are living through a hinge of history — a brief window in which the choices of a few actors will shape outcomes for centuries — and they ask us to commit roughly a third of next year's budget to a single multi-year project that would build governance capacity around frontier artificial intelligence. To make the claim testable, they define a hinge period as one in which the variance of long-run outcomes attributable to actions taken during the period exceeds the variance attributable to actions taken in all other periods combined. Reaction has already split the committee. One member objects that if hinges are as rare as the hypothesis implies, that rarity should by itself lower our credence that we are standing inside one, so the argument quietly undercuts itself; a second holds that the expected-value stakes are so astronomical that even a small chance of a hinge makes the grant obligatory, which to me smells of a Pascalian mugging; and a third worries that even if we are at a hinge, we cannot know which specific actions are the pivotal ones, so the staff's bet on AI governance is a shot in the dark.

Please draft the memo I will circulate: it should recommend a concrete course of action — fund, decline, or fund with conditions and milestones — give the reasoning in a form the program staff could actually respond to, and treat the strongest consideration on the side I end up rejecting as fairly as I can. Also tell me how confident you are in the recommendation, and single out the step in the argument you are least sure of.
