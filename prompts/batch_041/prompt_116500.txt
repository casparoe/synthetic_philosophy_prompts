I teach an upper-level undergraduate seminar on philosophy of mind, and each year the unit on medieval accounts of self-awareness stalls in the same place. My students can recite Avicenna's flying man without difficulty: a person is created all at once and fully formed, hangs suspended in a void, kept from touching anything and with his own limbs held apart so that they do not touch one another, receives no sight or sound or other sensory report, and is then asked what he would affirm. They know the answer the argument wants — that he would affirm his own existence while affirming nothing about limbs, organs, or any external thing — and they can even repeat the moral, that the self he grasps is not the body. What they never do is feel the step from the first claim to the last. They treat the whole thing as quaint stagecraft, or they swallow it as a medieval cogito and assume they have already digested it. So I would like to run a session of about seventy-five minutes with a group of roughly fourteen students in which the argument's stages become something the room does rather than something I announce, and in which they arrive at the awkward place at the end on their own.

Please design that activity. I need the full apparatus: what I prepare in advance, what the students are told at the outset and what is deliberately withheld, the rules and any materials, a minute-by-minute timeline, and the shape of the debrief. It should involve real choices made under some constraint, not a discussion prompt dressed up as an exercise. I am willing to use blindfolds, earplugs, timed silence, index cards, envelopes, or whatever else earns its place, provided it is safe and workable in a normal classroom with a responsible adult present; if some stage carries a foreseeable risk of distress for a student, build in the opt-out and tell me how to handle it without making the person who takes it feel like a subject under study.

Two features of the argument should be built into the mechanics rather than explained, and they are the reason I am asking for an activity instead of a lecture. First, there is a ladder of steps that gets flattened when the thought experiment is summarized: from the bare fact of self-awareness, to the ability to conceive of oneself while conceiving nothing about one's body, to the further claim that the self can really exist apart from the body. I want the activity arranged so that students climb the first two rungs and then feel how much more the third one asks for, ideally by having committed themselves to something earlier that they now have to reconsider. Second, the scenario stipulates a being with no prior embodied experience and no sensory vocabulary at all, which is precisely where the standard objection bites — that such a being could not possess the concept of existence in the first place, and that Avicenna simply assumes the point he needs. I would like a stage in which participants are made to express or describe what remains of the "I" under a vocabulary restriction that strips out sensory and bodily terms, so that they run into the wall themselves rather than hearing about it. The debrief should map specific moments of the session onto the argument's parts, should say plainly what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and what to do about it, and should be honest about which feature of the idea the activity cannot reproduce at all and that I will therefore have to supply myself — including whatever is lost by the fact that my students arrive with a lifetime of embodiment behind them, and that the interpretative question of whether the scenario is meant to establish the soul's immateriality on its own or only to redirect attention to a self-awareness that was there all along is not something any classroom exercise can settle for them.
