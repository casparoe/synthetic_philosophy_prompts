I teach a graduate seminar in formal epistemology, and last night a colleague and I fell into a long, increasingly ill-tempered argument about Dutch book arguments that neither of us managed to win. We agreed completely on the arithmetic and disagreed about everything downstream — about what the existence of a sure-loss book actually shows, and whether the usual slogan (something to the effect that a bookie can make you lose whatever happens) does any real work or is just a memorable picture standing in for a much narrower theorem. I want to turn that unresolved argument into the take-home exam question for my students, because I suspect it will cleanly separate the ones who understand the logical skeleton of the argument from the ones who can only recite the slogan. I need you to act as an expert formal epistemologist and philosopher of probability and help me build the whole assessment. Think carefully before you write, and start by brainstorming the structure of the argument and the objections to it, then commit to a design.

Here is the case I want the question built around, and I want you to use exactly these numbers so the students can't retreat into generalities. An agent has credences P(A) = 0.4 and P(B) = 0.4 for two mutually exclusive, jointly non-exhaustive events A and B, and assigns P(A or B) = 0.9. The question should require students to do three things in sequence: first, construct explicitly the set of bets a bookie would offer, state who pays what to whom up front, and show by cases that the agent loses a fixed sum no matter which of A, B, or neither occurs; second, take a defended position on whether the existence of that book already establishes that the agent's credences are irrational, engaging seriously with at least one objection of the kind that says the theorem only demonstrates that these numbers cannot serve as the agent's betting odds on both sides of a wager without a sure loss, and that this is a claim about odds rather than about credences, resting on auxiliary assumptions (linearity of utility in money, willingness to buy and sell at one's fair price, the events being well-defined and bettable) that a reasonable agent may reject; and third, say precisely what would have to be true of an agent — about her utilities, her willingness to bet, or the status of her doxastic attitudes — for the Dutch book to have no force against her at all.

Please give me the finished exam question, a model answer of about 600 words, and a marking scheme out of 20 marks. The model answer must commit to a verdict rather than surveying positions and leaving the reader to choose; I want to see someone actually land on a view about what the Dutch book does and does not prove, and defend it against the strongest objection. The marking scheme should name the specific moves a top answer has to make and the specific errors that should cost marks — for instance, treating the sure-loss result as automatically establishing irrationality, or conceding the objection so completely that the theorem is left with no normative force whatsoever. Please do not ask for citations, sources, or reading lists anywhere in the question or the model answer: my students sit this without materials and I don't want the answer to hinge on who said what.
