Ok I've been going down a frame-problem rabbit hole and I've built this arguement that looks watertight to me and also seems to prove that no finite agent can ever act on purpose, and I can't find the loose thread. Here it is. (1) For an agent to rely on its prediction of what an action will do, that prediction has to be complete — it must not be silently missing some fact that the action actually changes. (2) A prediction is complete only if, for every fact about the world, it either says the fact changes in such-and-such a way or says the fact stays as it was. (3) The number of facts about the world is unbounded; just think of all the ways you could redescribe my greenhouse — the pump, the vent, the nutrient solution, the humidity in every corner of the room. (4) So any complete prediction has to fix the value of unboundedly many facts. (5) A finite agent can only represent finitely many facts. (6) So no finite agent can form a complete prediction of any action. (7) And an agent that can't form a complete prediction can't know that its plan will bring about its goal rather than some side effect it never considered. (8) So no finite agent can act deliberately. (9) But finite agents plainly do act deliberately sometimes — me, and the little controller that runs my greenhouse. I'd rather keep (9) and (1) than give either of them up. So which step do I reject, why did it look fine in the first place, and what do I have to pay to give it up? I keep hearing people separate the metaphysical frame problem from the representational one, but I can't see how that touches any of my steps. Be concrete, and answer in British English.
