I run the ethics consult service at a mid-sized hospital. We get maybe two calls a week, and a lot of them arrive pre-labeled by whoever paged us: 'this is a genuine moral dilemma, there's no right answer here.' Sometimes that label is doing real work — it's a way of saying stop hunting for the clean answer and back us up. Other times it's a way of dodging a decision that actually does have a right answer, or a way of laundering a choice someone has already made. The label changes what we tell the team, so it matters. If it's a real dilemma, we tell them they're permitted to go either way, that nobody gets to second-guess them for it, and that they'll owe something to the option they don't take. If it isn't, then we owe them a recommendation, and they don't get to call the outcome a tragedy.

So I want a procedure the on-call consultants can actually run on a case in about twenty minutes — most of us are clinicians with a bit of ethics training, not philosophers, and the call usually comes at 4pm with the family in the room. I don't want a literature review. I want the tests in the order you'd run them, what counts as passing or failing each one, where the procedure stops with a verdict of genuine dilemma, where it stops with a verdict of hard-but-not-a-dilemma, and where it stops by handing it to a human because the remaining question is values rather than facts.

A few things I'd like the procedure to actually use, since they seem to be the load-bearing bits of the philosophical debate and I keep half-remembering them:

- The argument that if you ought to do A and you ought to do B, then you ought to do A-and-B, and if you can't do both, that's supposed to be impossible — so a real dilemma would have to be a case where the usual machinery breaks. I want that turned into something a consultant can test, not just cited.
- The residue idea: a genuine dilemma is supposed to leave something behind even after you've chosen — guilt, the sense that you still owe the person you didn't choose, an apology or a making-good that isn't just optics. If a case produces no residue at all once it's resolved, does that tell us it wasn't a dilemma?
- The difference between me not knowing which option is right and there being no fact of the matter about which is right. I suspect a lot of our 'dilemmas' are the first and get dressed up as the second.
- The difference between a prima facie pull toward each option and an all-things-considered requirement to do both, and whether that distinction survives contact with a real case or just moves the problem around.

Then run it on three cases so I can see it work, including at least one where the procedure tells me something I won't like:

1. An ICU nurse who is pretty sure a senior colleague is impaired on shift, and who is also the only person who can cover the shift if she reports him.
2. A family insisting on continued aggressive treatment for a patient whose documented values, as best anyone can reconstruct them, point the other way, where the team is split.
3. A case where two patients need the one remaining bed and the criteria everyone agreed on give a clear answer, but the losing patient is a child.

For each, I want the verdict, the specific step that decided it, and — for the ones that come out as genuine dilemmas — what we actually tell the team to do about the residue, since I have no idea what a hospital is supposed to do with that.

Two more things. Be honest about which of your steps are judgment calls wearing a procedure's clothing, and tell me where this thing is most likely to misfire on us. And since I'll be teaching this to consultants who'll trust it, say how confident you are in the procedure as a whole and flag the single step you're least sure about — if it's one I'm going to lean on, I'd rather know now.
