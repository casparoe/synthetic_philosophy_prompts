I work in a public-health forecasting unit, and we publish a weekly estimate of the effective reproduction number for a seasonal respiratory virus. The institution requires that every output be probabilistic: each analyst's judgment enters the pipeline as a point estimate with a credible interval, and the dashboard carries an action threshold, so that if the median estimate stays above one for two consecutive weeks the unit formally recommends reinstating a mitigation measure. What puzzles me is how we handle inputs. When a new data stream arrives — a wastewater concentration, a sentinel-clinic count, a seroprevalence survey — the published estimate is revised by plugging the new numbers into the model, which treats them as the observed data and carries a noise term for them. In one sense, then, nothing is ever treated as certain, since every reading enters through a likelihood. In another sense everything is: the reading is taken as given, as something to condition on rather than something we hold a degree of belief about. When a reading is ambiguous — an assay whose field sensitivity is disputed, a survey with a suspicious age profile — we do not discount it partially; we either accept it whole or drop it from the update, and we have a standing rule that only streams above a quality threshold may enter at all. A colleague defends this by saying that a number you don't trust is not evidence, and that if you are unsure about a reading you should simply build that uncertainty into the model. Meanwhile I have been reading about updating by a weighted average over the possible values of an uncertain observation — what the literature calls Jeffrey conditionalization, or probability kinematics — and I cannot tell whether our practice is a legitimate instance of ordinary conditioning or a different thing wearing its clothes.

What I want from you is the theory of evidence this practice presupposes. Does it commit us to the view that evidence arrives as certain propositions, and that any uncertainty about a reading is either relocated into a likelihood, absorbed into a model, or else disqualifies the reading from being evidence at all? Which rival accounts does it quietly rule out — in particular the account on which evidence can be something you are only, say, seventy per cent confident in, and on which you update by taking a weighted average of your conditional credences rather than conditioning on a proposition? Distinguish what the practice strictly requires from what it merely happens to do. If a shop that conditions on a likelihood and a shop that uses the weighted-average rule would be behaviourally indistinguishable whenever the reading can be redescribed as a certain proposition (the meter read 0.83), then is our quality-threshold rule doing any philosophical work, or is it engineering that no theory of evidence would notice? Are the parts of the practice consistent — we insist readings are uncertain enough to need a noise term, yet treat them as inputs rather than as propositions with credences; we demand a full predictive distribution, yet act on a threshold rule over its median? Where is the practice philosophically underdetermined, in the sense that several incompatible theories of evidence would license it just as well? And finally, suppose we adopted the weighted-average rule and abandoned the quality threshold: what would actually have to change — in the pipeline, in what we publish, in the audit trail, and in what we would have to know before any update could be made at all — and what does the rival view fail to tell us about where the input credences themselves come from? Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restatement of my question.
