I spent the better part of thirty years as a physicist — condensed matter, then a long stretch in instrumentation, nothing cosmological — and since retiring I've been reading a lot of philosophy of religion and philosophy of science. I've got stuck on the fine-tuning argument and I can't for the life of me work out why the debate never moves. I'm not asking you to tell me who's right. I want a diagnosis of the disagreement itself, because I suspect it isn't one disagreement.

Here's the shape of it as I see it. One side says the constants that make stars, chemistry and long-lived structures possible sit in a narrow band, and a narrow band out of an enormous space of possible values is antecedently improbable, so a designer is confirmed. The other side comes back at them in what look to me like three quite different ways. Some say there is no well-defined 'space of possible values' in the first place: you can only say the life-permitting region is small relative to the total if you've already chosen a measure over the constants, different measures give different answers, and so the premise is either meaningless or smuggled in by the back door. Others accept a measure but say observer selection does all the work — we could only ever have found ourselves in a life-permitting universe, so the fact that we do is no evidence of anything. And a third lot just say a deeper theory will eventually fix the constants, multiverse or otherwise, so it's premature to read design off an unfinished calculation.

My hunch, and it's only a hunch, is that this is at least three disagreements wearing one coat, and that the one people are actually having depends on which objection they've latched onto. So what I'd like is this: tell me what kind of disagreement it really is. Is the load-bearing issue the measure problem, which sounds like a formal question that could in principle be settled by a theorem? Is it the treatment of observer selection, also formal but maybe not settleable? Or is it something underneath — whether a designer is even a live hypothesis, which looks to me like a starting point neither side can argue for? And are the two sides even answering the same question — one asking whether the fine-tuning needs an explanation, the other asking whether it is genuinely surprising?

Then the part I really care about: given your diagnosis, what would count as progress? A distinction, a formal result, a change of question? Would a measure everyone accepted, or a theorem showing the likelihoods don't depend on the measure, actually end it — or would both sides just relocate and carry on? And if you think there's a good reason no progress should be expected here, say so plainly and tell me why. I'd rather have an honest account of which parts are genuine technical disputes that could in principle be settled and which are just different places to start from.

Also, please write your answer in British English.
