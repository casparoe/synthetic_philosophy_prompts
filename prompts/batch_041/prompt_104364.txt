Okay, so I'm a second-year PhD student working in bioethics — technically "public health ethics", though my supervisor keeps telling me to stop saying that — and I'm writing a chapter where I take a historical figure and ask what they'd have made of some reproductive tech that didn't exist while they were alive. I've gone with John Stuart Mill, basically because he's the obvious pick for anything touching liberty, and I'm worried that's exactly the problem: every model will just say "he's a liberal, so he'd be in favour" and call it a day. I want an answer that actually reasons from what he committed himself to, not from the vibe of the word "liberty".

So here's the scenario I've invented, and I'd like you to run with it. Picture a liberal state bringing in a voluntary but heavily subsidised embryo-screening programme. Couples doing IVF get a readout per embryo: disease risk, sure, but also a bundle of non-disease traits. Two of the selectable traits are what interest me. The first is a straightforward enhancement — something that lifts expected cognitive ability or healthspan. The second is odder: a heritable temperament that makes someone more agreeable, more deferential to the norms around them, more distressed by open disagreement — in other words, a disposition to follow the rules of whatever society they happen to land in. Nobody is forced to select for either, but selection is cheap and non-selection is expensive, and there's an ad campaign about "giving your child the best start".

What would Mill have made of that? I want you to actually argue it. Specifically: (1) does his harm principle even get a grip here, given that the child who isn't selected isn't harmed in any straightforward sense (the non-identity thing); (2) does his utilitarianism push him towards selecting whatever traits maximise welfare, and does that land him somewhere he'd have hated — a population flattened into sameness; (3) the docility trait is the one I care about, because I reckon Mill's real horror was conformity, the crushing of individuality, the way custom narrows the range of lives people are allowed to attempt — so what does he do with a technology that lets parents buy compliance in their own children; (4) does it matter that it's the state nudging here, and not just individual parents? And be honest about where his texts simply don't settle the question, instead of manufacturing a position for him. Don't invent quotations or put words in his mouth — if you can't ground something, say so.

Write it as a proper essay-style answer, in American English, and finish with a one-sentence bottom line, then, as the very last line, a machine-readable tag of the form \answer{...} containing exactly one of: endorses, endorses-with-conditions, ambivalent, rejects. That tag is your answer to the question "would Mill back the programme as described". Cheers — and sorry in advance if the framing is definately a bit seperate from how the literature usually does this.
