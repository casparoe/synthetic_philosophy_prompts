Write a short story of roughly 1,500–2,000 words about a translator working under a formal constraint that cannot be satisfied without loss. The story should be literary and restrained, told in close third person, and should dramatize its ideas through scene and argument rather than explaining them.

Premise: In a fictional city, a translator is commissioned by a state cultural office to produce the single authorized translation of a short, celebrated lyric by a long-dead national poet. The office attaches one non-negotiable condition: the translation must reproduce the poem's formal apparatus exactly — its rhyme scheme and, above all, an acrostic formed by the first letters of the lines, which in the national tradition spells out the name of the poet's lost beloved. The translator's target language is built so that the acrostic and the rhyme pull in opposite directions: any word that preserves one wrecks the other. This is the engine of the story, but two further pressures should build on it. First, as the translator works, she discovers that the acrostic was not written by the poet at all — it was inserted generations later by an earlier translator patching a damaged manuscript, so the form everyone treats as sacred is a fossil of someone else's compromise. Second, the target language forces choices the source left open — every verb must fix a gender, a level of formality, or the speaker's basis for knowing — so that even the most literal rendering quietly asserts things the original never said.

Let the story move through concrete scenes: the translator at her desk, a meeting with the ministry official who insists the acrostic *is* the poem, a rival translator who argues for abandoning the constraint, a late-night decision. Include at least one moment where the translator drafts an actual line and weighs two candidate renderings against the constraints, closely enough that the reader can verify the trade-off for themselves. You may invent the poem, the languages, and the institutions, but keep them internally consistent and specific.

The questions the story should make the reader feel, without ever stating them as theses: What is a translation faithful to — the source text, the poet's intention, or the tradition of reading that has grown around the text? If the revered constraint is itself an artifact of a prior act of translation, is preserving it fidelity or idolatry? And if the target language cannot avoid adding meaning, what would a neutral translation even be? Do not close with a moral. Let the translator make a choice, and let the reader feel what it costs.
