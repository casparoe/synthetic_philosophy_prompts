I'm in a political theory seminar on precommitment and we keep landing on the same image. The prof says the constitution is the people tying themselves to the mast, and last week a guy in section used it to defend a state law that deliberately makes its own carbon tax politically painful to repeal — his line was that the whole point of a Ulysses contract is that you can't untie yourself later, so the difficulty is the feature not the bug. I can't tell how many seperate claims 'tie yourself to the mast' is being made to carry, or which one his argument actually needs. Lay them out as a menu: that preferences genuinely shift across time so the later me can't be trusted; that the earlier me has authority over the later one; that something only counts as binding if undoing it is literally impossible; that the mast (something external, not willpower) is what does the work; that a binding is legitimate only when it protects a preference the later self still endorses; that the whole picture presupposes a single continuous self that may not exist. For each, say whether it's trivial, substantive, or just false, and then tell me which reading actually gets him from 'the people bound themselves' to 'stop complaining that it's hard to amend' — and whether any of them survives the fact that a people, unlike Ulysses, can't really be said to have one will in the first place.
