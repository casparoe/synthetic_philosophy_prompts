I teach a third-year undergraduate seminar in philosophy of the social sciences — eighteen students, one ninety-minute session — and the week on social kinds keeps falling flat. The students can tell me that race and gender are socially constructed, and they can tell me that generational labels like Boomer and Gen Z are marketing categories rather than natural divisions, but neither performance involves any thinking: they arrive knowing which answer I want and they hand it over. What I want instead is a session in which they run into the difficulty themselves, and I've come to think the difficulty is best located in a piece of arithmetic bookkeeping that they can be walked into without knowing any statistics. Here is the thing I want them to collide with. A claim about a generation is a claim about people born in the same window of years, but in any single survey we observe age, not birth year; across several surveys we observe age and calendar year, and birth year is nothing over and above the difference between them — so the three quantities are not three independent observations but one identity, and a gap between Gen Z and Millennials on some attitude can be redescribed, with no change whatever in the data, as a gap between the young and the old (an aging effect) or as a gap between the 2010s and the 1990s (a period effect). A cohort story, an aging story, and a period story can each fit the same table exactly. The usual fix is to assume something extra — that aging effects are smooth and gradual, that some particular period left no trace, that the oldest cohort is untouched — and it is the assumption, not the data, that does the work. That is what I want the room to discover: not that generational talk is false, but that it is underdetermined, and that the loud disagreements between people who make it are usually disagreements about hidden assumptions rather than about facts.

Design me the activity. It has to be runnable in ninety minutes with eighteen students in a normal seminar room, with no lecture beforehand: the idea must arrive through the structure of the materials and the incentives, not through anything I say until the debrief, and nobody should hear the words identification problem or age-period-cohort until then. All teams must work from the same table, and I want the disagreement to be manufactured rather than merely hoped for: if the only thing separating two teams' conclusions is an auxiliary assumption, then arrange for different teams to be quietly handed different assumptions and to believe, for as long as possible, that the table is doing the work rather than the card. Materials should be printable and self-contained, and I want the actual content, not a description of it — the numbers in the table, the exact wording on any cards or instruction sheets, the token values, the wording of the prompts I read aloud. Use invented figures and label them as illustrative; I do not want fabricated real survey results, and I do not need citations, references, or further reading anywhere in your answer. Keep the stakes in points or tokens; nothing should cost anyone money. Give me the timeline in minutes, the roles if there are any, who says what and when, the payoff scheme, and then the debrief, with an explicit mapping from particular moments in the activity to the particular philosophical claims I should be drawing out: the difference between a category that is merely useful for prediction and one that is a kind; what kindhood would have to consist in here — genuine clustering in the data, a shared causal mechanism that produces and maintains the cluster (a formative historical event, something like what philosophers of science call a homeostatic property cluster), projectibility to new cases and new populations; and the possibility that generation is a real entity in some thinner, historical sense even if the demographic label is arbitrary. A colleague of mine in the sociology department thinks the whole complaint is overblown, since some cohort effects are real and measurable; I want to be able to give that case its due in the debrief without pretending my activity has shown it false.

Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it, including the two failures I most fear: that the group collapses into so it is all made up and stops reasoning, and that two or three sharp students spot the hidden assumptions and announce the trick before the others have felt the pull of the table. Say how to handle each, and be frank about the feature of the question the activity cannot reproduce and that I will have to supply myself. Finally, tell me where your design is most likely to fail, and what would change your mind about whether it works at all — what you would need to see in a pilot run to be convinced that the participants' insight came from the structure of the activity rather than from confusion about the numbers or from my own nudging.
