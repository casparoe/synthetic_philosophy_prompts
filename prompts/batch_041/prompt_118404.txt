I'm a software engineer with no formal training in philosophy, and I'm preparing an internal seminar on what I've been calling competence without comprehension. I'd like you to build its centrepiece for me: a tiered ranking of candidate examples.

The idea, as I've reconstructed it, is really two moves that are usually lumped together. The first is Darwin's: biological design, which looks like the product of foresight, is explained by blind variation plus selection, with no foresight anywhere in the process. The second is Turing's: intelligent-looking behaviour is explained as the output of a machine that simply follows explicit rules, so that its performance can be as good as if it understood what it was doing while it understands nothing. In both cases something we would ordinarily treat as evidence of comprehension is produced without it.

For this exercise, treat an example as a strong instance of competence without comprehension to the degree that all three of the following hold:

(i) Competence — the behaviour reliably achieves something that counts as success in its domain, not merely something predictable.
(ii) Absence — the system that produces the behaviour has no grasp of what it is doing or why; it does not represent the goal and select its behaviour in light of it. (I expect the arguments will be about what counts as a grasp, and whether one can be non-propositional.)
(iii) Non-relocation — the case does real explanatory work, rather than simply moving the comprehension somewhere else (into a designer, a programmer, a legislator) and leaving it there as the unexplained source of the competence.

Here is the list I want ranked:

1. A production compiler's optimizer, which rewrites code into a form the source author did not anticipate and often cannot easily read back.
2. The vertebrate eye, explained as the product of natural selection.
3. Affinity maturation in the immune system, where antibody-producing cells mutate at a high rate and the variants that bind an invader best are the ones that proliferate.
4. A chess engine with a hand-written evaluation function and alpha-beta search, playing at grandmaster level.
5. A reinforcement-learning agent that learns a game by self-play from the rules alone, with no human games and no hand-coded heuristics.
6. A market-clearing price that summarises information no individual participant possesses — the phenomenon Hayek described.
7. A large language model producing a fluent and factually accurate summary of a long document.
8. A body of common-law rules accumulated from individual court decisions, with no legislator having designed it.
9. A slime mould in a maze settling into a tube along the shortest route between two food sources.
10. A human expert's immediate, confident judgement in a familiar situation — the kind of case Hubert Dreyfus pressed against rule-based models of expertise.

Please do the following:

1. Sort the ten items into four tiers, best fit to worst fit, and order them within each tier.
2. For each item, give one or two sentences justifying its placement, saying explicitly which of (i), (ii) and (iii) it satisfies or fails.
3. Tag each item as Darwinian (blind variation and selection is doing the work), Turing-style (rule-following execution is doing the work), both, or neither, and flag any item where those two tags pull in different directions.
4. Name the two items whose placement the definition above least settles, and say precisely what makes them hard.

No need to cite any literature — I care about the reasoning, not the references.
