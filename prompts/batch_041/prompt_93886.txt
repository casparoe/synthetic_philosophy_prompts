I have no background in economics or philosophy, and I have been trying, on my own, to work out whether the following pattern has a name.

Several people compete for a single thing, and the competition is arranged so that you must keep paying in order to stay in it. What you have already paid is gone whether or not you win; the other side keeps raising, so you raise as well; and by the end the total that all of them have spent is larger than the thing was ever worth. Asked at the outset, every one of them would have preferred that no one had entered. What puzzles me is that at each separate step, continuing looks sensible: the earlier money is already lost, so the only live question is whether the next small payment is worth the chance of the whole prize, and usually it is. I have watched this in two people bidding each other up at an auction, in a boundary dispute that became years of legal fees on both sides over a strip of land worth less than the fees, and in a strike where the company and the union together lost far more than either could have won.

I have heard of something called the dollar auction, and I suspect it is part of what I am circling, but my cases do not all fit it. In some of them, everyone who took part pays, not merely the two highest bidders. In some, the prize is not a fixed sum at all, because the fight itself consumes it, so the thing being fought over shrinks as the fighting goes on. And in some, the only value of winning is that the other party loses.

So what I would like is a proper carving rather than a label. Is there an established concept, or a small family of related ones, for this structure? Which one fits best, and how exactly does it differ from the dollar auction, from the sunk-cost fallacy, and from what is called escalation of commitment? I have a suspicion that the sunk-cost fallacy is the wrong frame, because that one seems to be about an individual reasoning badly, whereas here each party is responding correctly to the incentives the situation hands it — but I cannot tell whether that is a real distinction or merely a polite way of excusing the behaviour. And I would like to know which single feature is doing the work: that everyone pays, that the prize is fixed, that the individual payments are small relative to the prize, or something else — and which of those features, if removed, would let the trap dissolve. A name alone will not satisfy me; I want to know where the edges of the thing are.
