I spent most of last night in a long, increasingly heated argument with a close friend about the so-called paradox of deontology, and I would like to convert the wreckage into something disciplined. The dispute began with a case we both found harder than the textbook trolley: whether I may break a promise to you in order to stop five other people from breaking theirs, given that the promise is the very kind of thing that the prohibition on promise-breaking protects. She insisted that constraints are not to be understood as aiming at any outcome, that the whole point of a constraint is that it holds even when more of the same would otherwise occur, and that demanding a reason why the constraint does not simply minimize its own violation is already to have conceded the game to a teleological picture. I pressed that this looked less like an answer than a refusal to give one, and that if she was right then a great deal of ordinary moral reasoning, which plainly does care about how much of a bad thing happens, would have to be reconstructed from scratch. I left convinced that the "paradox" may be an artifact of an assumption we both carried in without noticing — that any theory organized around a value must be answerable to how much of that value the world ends up containing — and that if you set that assumption down, the puzzle stops resembling an inconsistency and starts resembling a category mistake. But I do not trust the thought yet: it may prove too much by dissolving genuine puzzles elsewhere, or too little by leaving the original sting exactly where it was, and I cannot tell from the armchair which.

What I would like from you is a short research proposal — a prospectus for a second-year paper, around eight thousand words over a single semester — built around the half-formed idea above. Please state the governing question precisely enough that it has a determinate answer, something like whether the apparent self-defeat of deontological constraints is a genuine structural problem for the theory or an artifact of importing a goal-maximizing framework into a view that has no such goal, and, if the latter, precisely what the deontologist must concede for the dissolution to go through. Explain why the standard responses leave the question open rather than settling it, lay out the approach you would take and why that approach rather than the obvious alternatives, describe what a successful result would look like and what would count as the project failing, and give an honest and realistic scope for the time available — including what would have to be left out. You need not supply citations, a bibliography, or a reading list; refer to positions and arguments by their content and I will attach the references myself.
