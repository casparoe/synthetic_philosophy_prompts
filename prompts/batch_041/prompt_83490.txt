My friend and I spent most of yesterday evening arguing about vagueness, and we ended the evening at an impasse. I would like you to audit his account of the two positions rather than take my side, because I am not confident that my objections were any better than his.

He put it roughly as follows. "Epistemicism is the view that vagueness is ignorance. On that view a word like 'bald' really does have a hidden sharp meaning: there is a fact of the matter about how many hairs separate the bald from the not-bald, and the only reason nobody has settled it is that we have not built a machine sensitive enough to find the line. With better instruments, or a large enough computer, we could read the fact off the world. Supervaluationism is the more honest option. It says that where a case is borderline there simply is no fact of the matter, so borderline statements are neither true nor false; the view treats them as taking a third, intermediate value, halfway between true and false, in the way fuzzy logic does. Because it says that 'either he is tall or he is not tall' is not true, it gives up the law of excluded middle — that is the price it pays. In exchange it gets to say that 'true' for a vague term means 'true under every admissible way of making the term precise'. The trouble is that the notion of an admissible precisification is itself vague: the line between the cases that are definitely in and the cases that are merely borderline has no sharp edge. That is fatal, and it is why the field has drifted back to epistemicism, which has no such difficulty."

Much of this strikes me as right and much of it as wrong, and I cannot separate the two. I came away inclined to think supervaluationism the more sensible view, largely because I dislike being told that there is a fact of the matter that I could never in principle reach — but that is a temperament, not an argument.

The case we kept returning to was a small one from our climbing club. For two years we have logged every session in a shared spreadsheet with a column headed "proper session". The only written guidance is a line someone added in the first week — a session counts if you were on the wall for at least an hour — and in practice people log forty-minute sessions after a hard set of routes, or when the gym was crowded, without any sense of having falsified the record. Nobody has ever gone back to amend the rule, and there is no committee to appeal to. Neither of us could say whether a particular forty-minute entry in that spreadsheet was a proper session, and I suspect our disagreement about the spreadsheet was the same disagreement one level down.

Please go through his summary sentence by sentence and tell me what someone working on vagueness would accept, what they would wince at, and what is simply wrong — marking especially the places where he has collapsed two claims that a view keeps apart, and the places where he has stated a familiar objection as though it were a refutation. Say what a defender of each position would reply, and whether his closing verdict survives once the summary is corrected: does supervaluationism collapse under higher-order vagueness, and does epistemicism genuinely escape that difficulty? Then bring it back to the climbing club: what would each view say about that forty-minute session, and does the answer turn on any of the points where his summary went astray? I would rather have the corrected version of each disputed point than a verdict on who won the argument.
