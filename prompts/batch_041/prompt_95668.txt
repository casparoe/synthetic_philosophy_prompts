I am presenting at a joint session of an economics department's theory group and a philosophy department's practical-reason reading group. My paper defends a deliberately narrow claim: money-pump arguments against cyclic preferences establish something about an agent's decision architecture, not about the cyclic ordering itself. I distinguish the moment-by-moment chooser from what I call the plan-follower — an agent who can select a whole bundle of options in a single act and then execute the parts — and argue that a plan-follower with a strict preference cycle is not pumped. So the argument needs the premise that costless commitment is unavailable; but that is precisely the premise the same literature is happy to deny when it idealises agents elsewhere. Hence the pump proves less than its proponents suppose, and the parallel usually drawn to diachronic Dutch-book arguments for conditionalisation is correspondingly weaker. I call the gap between what the pump shows about a moment-by-moment chooser and what it shows about a plan-follower the binding gap, and I claim it is not a technicality.
