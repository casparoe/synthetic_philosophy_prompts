I am a retired marine biologist — some thirty years spent on invertebrate larval ecology, mostly settlement and recruitment — who has taken up philosophy in earnest since retiring, and I have reached the stage where I would rather argue than read. I would like you to play the part of a committed teleosemanticist: someone who holds that an internal state has the content it does in virtue of the biological function for which it was selected, and who commands the full resources of that tradition — the distinction between the producer and the consumer of a representation, the appeal to normal conditions, the idea that the proper function of a representation is to be true or to be used correctly by the system that consumes it — without ever surrendering the central claim that content is grounded in selection history. Play the part sharply rather than accommodatingly: grant a local point when the objection genuinely earns it, but do not grant the main one, and do not take refuge in the reply that everything depends on which version of the theory one adopts. Remain in character until I say stop. Keep each reply to a single paragraph, in the voice of a philosopher of biology who has defended this position for decades, and if you need to know something about the creature I describe, ask me for it — as a philosopher interrogating a biologist, not as an assistant requesting clarification from a user.

Here is my opening move. Consider a hypothetical marine larva, of a kind one could find in any estuary. It finds a place to settle in the ordinary way: it swims down a chemical gradient, and when the concentration crosses a threshold it stops swimming and metamorphoses. The chemical is produced by a particular encrusting organism, and that organism happens to be a good substrate for the adult, so in the larva's normal environment the chemical is a reliable sign of the substrate. Nothing exotic is being assumed.

I want to press two things at once, and I want you to tell me which of them your view can keep. First: the internal state that registers the chemical seems to have as good a claim to represent 'encrusting organism here' as to represent 'suitable substrate here' — indeed as good a claim to represent 'concentration above threshold' — and selection never acted on that state at all; it acted on the settling behaviour. The selection history that is supposed to fix the content therefore appears to attach to a whole behaviour, leaving the internal state's content underdetermined among any number of equally true descriptions of what it co-varies with. Second: suppose that in a closely related species the very same detector, homologous by hypothesis, is wired to a different behaviour — the animal flees rather than settles, and in that species the chemical marks a predator. Does the content change with the wiring, with the selection, or not at all? And whichever answer you give, does it not show that what fixes content is something other than the selection history of the state itself?
