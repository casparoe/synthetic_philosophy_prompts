I have no academic background and no training in philosophy, but for about two years I have been working through a puzzle on my own, and I have reached the point where I can see two defensible answers and cannot tell whether the people who give them are actually disagreeing or merely talking past each other. I would like you to write the document that would settle that.

The puzzle is this. There is a well-known line of reasoning — the simulation argument — that runs through three options: civilisations almost always die out before they become technologically mature enough to run simulations of their ancestors; mature civilisations can run such simulations but choose not to; or we are almost certainly living in a simulation. Set the third option aside as a possibility worth taking seriously, not a certainty. Now set beside it the case for what is often called fanaticism about the far future: the thought that because the expected value of the long-run future is astronomically large, we should be willing to accept almost any certainty of a modest good in exchange for even a tiny probability of an astronomically large one. My question is whether the first of these thoughts should make us less willing to accept the second. Does the simulation argument dampen fanaticism about the far future — and if so, how much, and through exactly what step? I can sketch an argument for damping and an argument against, and I cannot tell which of them is doing real work.

Please write the joint statement that a genuine, good-faith adversarial collaboration between two people could produce and that both would put their names to: one who holds that the simulation argument does dampen fanaticism about the far future, and one who holds that it does not. I want the claims they can both sign — about what the simulation argument does and does not establish, about what fanaticism actually asserts, about what it would even mean for a consideration to dampen it; then the precise statements of where they still differ, each phrased the way its own side would want it phrased; then the cruxes, the sub-questions such that settling one would move at least one side; and, for each crux, the case, argument, or thought experiment that both sides agree would bear on it, together with each side's prediction about how it comes out, written down in advance. If any part of their quarrel turns out to be about words rather than substance, say which part and why.

A few guesses of my own, which you should treat as proposals rather than instructions, and which you should discard if they are confused. I suspect one crux is whether the simulators' behaviour is independent of what we decide — whether a simulation is more like a sealed experiment whose operators never look at the results, or more like a study designed precisely to observe how its subjects respond. I suspect another is whether the value of a simulated life counts the same as the value of an un-simulated one. I suspect a third is whether the argument should be run from inside the simulation or from the standpoint of whatever world might contain it. And for the concrete case, I offer this one, which I made up: a civilisation runs ancestor simulations only as a way of stress-testing its own decision theory, feeding each simulated civilisation a different philosophy of risk and recording which ones survive. Both sides should be able to say what they predict about whether our choices matter in that setup.

Two further requirements. First, hold the probability of being simulated fixed at some specific figure — say one half — and state whether the disagreement survives at that value, so that the two sides cannot simply be disagreeing about the number. Second, do not give me citations, references, or a reading list; I have no way to check any of them and they would not help me.

Please begin directly with the joint statement, with no preface, no commentary, and no restating of my question. Let its opening sentence be the bottom line: one sentence stating what the two signatories now agree the dispute really comes down to. Then a numbered agreed-claims section, then a disagreements table with a row for each crux, giving each side's wording, the case or argument both accept as bearing on it, and each side's prediction. Around a thousand words.
