Two of my friends got into an arguement at a dinner party last month and it's still bugging me, mainly because I couldn't tell who was winning. I write code for a living and never took a philosophy class, so I defnately don't have a way to settle these things myself.

The setup: a guy we both know turned down a job that would've been a big deal for his career so he could keep being the main person caring for his dad. Someone at the table said obviously that's the right call, and that's what set them off.

Dee's position: when what you do costs somebody else, you have to be able to answer for it from outside your own life. She says it's fine for him to put his dad way ahead of strangers, but the honest way to describe that is that he has a permission to be partial -- impartial morality lets you weight your own people much higher, and that permission is what makes the choice defensible. If he can't say that out loud, he's just doing what he wants and dressing it up.

Marcus said that's exactly the picture that's wrong. He brought up a philosopher named Williams who apparently had an example about a man with a choice between pulling his wife out of a burning building and pulling out strangers, and he pulls her out. Marcus's version: if the guy has to stand there thinking "am I allowed to favor her," something has already gone wrong -- not because the thought is wicked, but because the whole point of being her husband is that you don't need the permission slip. He says Williams called that extra thought one thought too many.

Dee said that can't be the complaint, because the thought doesn't have to be one he actually has. Nobody runs that check while the house is burning. It's a background fact about what makes the act defensible if somebody challenges him afterwards. If that's all Williams meant, he's attacking a thought nobody ever has.

Marcus said no, it's not about a thought that occurs, it's about the shape of the reason. On Dee's picture the wife's claim is one unit sitting in a pile of equal units, and the husband gets to overweight it because there's a rule saying spouses may. That's a different reason than "she's my wife," and reasons can differ even when the act is identical. He said it's like a program that gets the right output because of a check in a branch that never executes -- same output, still the wrong program.

Dee said fine, but then your view grades two people who do the exact same thing -- both save the wife, both let the strangers die -- differently based only on what's going on inside their heads, and the people who died drop out of the story completely. She said it gets worse: the person who actually agonized, who felt the pull of the strangers and saved his wife anyway, comes out looking worse on Marcus's view than the person who never even noticed them. That seemed backwards to her. She'd rather have the second guy as a friend.

Marcus said it's not about how much you suffer or how pure you are. It's that "she's my wife" is supposed to be where the deliberation stops, not a move inside a bigger calculation, and the agonizer is fine by him as long as his deliberation stops there too. He's not saying you're never allowed to think about the strangers.

Dee said that is just psychology. Same act, same bodies, different inner monologue, and you want to grade them. And she said she still doesn't buy that "she's my wife" is a reason at all -- if someone asks him why he let those people die, that isn't an answer, it's a refusal to answer. She said Williams basically wants a conversation-stopper and calls it integrity.

Marcus said she's got the direction of the justification backwards. It isn't that he loves her and therefore gets a license; it's that his life -- the thing he's actually living -- is where his reasons come from in the first place. Strip that out and you're asking him to treat his own life as one item in a pile, which nobody can actually do. He said Williams used the word integrity because the alternative splits the person off from what they're doing.

Dee said then it proves way too much. By that logic nobody ever has to justify anything, because every act is part of somebody's life somewhere. And she said none of it tells her anything about the caregiver case they started with, which is what she actually wanted to know.

Then they stopped and went back to the cake. Can you sort this out? I don't care who won the dinner -- I want to know which of the specific moves each of them made actually hold up and which don't. In particular: is Marcus right that the objection is about the shape of the reason rather than a thought you actually have, or is Dee right that this makes the whole thing about what's going on inside someone's head? Is "she's my wife" a reason, or a refusal to give one? And does Dee's agonizer point land, or does Marcus have a reply to it that isn't just restating himself? Also tell me what both of them are missing, and whether the caregiver case they started from is even the same kind of case as the fire one, or whether they've been talking past each other the whole time. Last thing: the compiler analogy -- is it doing the work Marcus wants, or does it break somewhere? Same question for Dee's "the output is the same" comeback.
