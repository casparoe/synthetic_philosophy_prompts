I'm a retired marine geochemist — 35 years of sediment cores and nutrient budgets, and since I packed it in I've been reading philosophy in a fairly disorganised way, mostly decision theory and bits of ethics. I've ended up on a regional council that advises on a shared groundfish stock, and the recurring fight there is unilateral restraint: our boats could cut effort by about a fifth, the stock would very likely recover, but any skipper who does it alone just eats the loss while the other fleet cleans up. A young economist on the council keeps saying this is a prisoner's dilemma and that we should "be superrational", which sent me down a rabbit hole. As I understand it (and I'm probably mangling it) the idea — Hofstadter's, I've only read about it secondhand — is that when a situation is symmetric and the other party is the sort of agent who'd reason their way to the same answer you're reasoning your way to, you shouldn't evaluate your options by asking what's best given what they'll probably do, but by asking what happens if everyone who reasons like you makes the same choice; and that this is meant to bite in one-shot, no-reputation settings where defecting is dominant. That's not the same as "be nice", and it's not tit-for-tat in a repeated game. I half-believe it and half-think it's smuggling in a wish that the world is tidier than it is.

What I actually want is a procedure I could run at the council table, on paper, in about twenty minutes, without a blackboard: an ordered list of questions, what counts as a good enough answer to each, where the procedure says cooperate, where it says don't, and where it stops and hands the thing back to us as a judgement call — either a values question or a question about how much we trust our own model. It has to survive contact with the ugly parts: the other side isn't a copy of me (different country's fleet, different debts, different discount rates), the set of "everyone who reasons like me" is a population I get to define and could define dishonestly, and there's a genuine chance I'm just the sucker. Please build in one or two tests I can actually apply, and be warned that I've been leaning on a crude one I call the same-recipe test — would they have picked what I'm picking if they'd followed my reasoning from the same facts? — and I suspect it's doing far too much work, that it needs splitting into seperate questions, and that I need something that catches the case where my "reasoning" is really just me wanting to feel good about cutting my catch. I'd also like the procedure to say something about the weird asymmetric case where only one side can even see the problem.

Then run the finished procedure on three cases and give me a definite answer each time, including one where it tells me to do something I won't like: (a) our council's unilateral 20% effort cut, where the neighbouring fleet has no such council, no shared scientific advice, and a much heavier debt load; (b) an anonymous referee report — I've been asked to review a paper by someone I'm fairly sure is working the same seam as a former student of mine, and I can write a soft report that helps the author or a harsh one that helps my student's odds, and no one will ever know wich I wrote; (c) a data-sharing pledge among six labs, where five have signed and the sixth is by far the biggest, so its participation basically decides whether the whole thing works at all. And please commit: don't hand me a framework plus a list of "it depends" — pick the version of the procedure you'd actually defend, say why you chose those questions over the obvious alternatives, and be upfront about where you expect it to misfire, or where the honest answer is that the idea doesn't yet tell us what to do.
