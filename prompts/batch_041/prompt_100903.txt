I'm a third-year philosophy student who has somehow been handed the discussion slot of our philosophical logic course next week: twelve students, seventy-five minutes, one room, and the instructor's only brief that by the end they should understand why a serious person might take dialetheism seriously. I want to get there through an activity rather than a mini-lecture. A friend and I spent an hour in the corridor last term arguing about whether "true contradiction" is even a coherent thing to say, and the argument went nowhere because neither of us could point to anything we had actually felt; I suspect the view only becomes tempting when you are the one holding the pen and being forced to write both values in the same box. So I'd like you to design a runnable game or structured exercise — rules, materials, incentives, a timeline, and a debrief — that lets the group feel the pull of the paraconsistent move from the inside, and that names "dialetheism" only after they have already made the move themselves.

There is one concrete thing I'd like the activity to build toward, because I don't want it to collapse into the plain Liar, which everyone in that room will have seen a hundred times. I want the group to end up with a small loop of index cards, an odd cycle, where each card declares the next one false and the last declares the first false again — a configuration for which, as they will discover, there is simply no way to assign each card exactly one of true and false. I then want them bound by two further rules that pull in opposite directions: a principle of non-contradiction (no card may be marked both), and an explosion rule (from any card and its negation you may write down any card you please). The intended squeeze is that keeping both rules leaves them stuck, and that the only way to carry on is to relax one of them — and I want them to feel in their hands that relaxing non-contradiction, and letting some cards be both true and false, is far more livable than relaxing explosion. If it helps, the debrief can connect what they did to the three-valued system usually called LP, whose third value is "both" rather than "neither," and to the contrast with a Kleene-style logic whose third value is a gap; but please don't let the labels do the work — the point is that they should run into the need for a glut before they hear the word.

Please write it up under these headings, in this order, with roughly these lengths: Overview and learning goal (about 150 words); Materials and setup (about 200); Rules of the game (about 500); Timeline for the seventy-five minutes (about 250); What will predictably go wrong the first time and how to handle it (about 250); Debrief (about 400); What this activity cannot reproduce and the facilitator must supply (about 200). Assume no real money and no equipment beyond paper, pens, and index cards, and assume the group will be willing but skeptical.
