I have no academic training of any kind, but for two years I have been reading about, and arguing about, the dispute usually labelled Hart against Devlin: the legal moralist's thought that a shared morality is part of what holds a society together, so that the criminal law may protect it without waiting for any other harm, against the liberal's thought that the only proper grounds for restricting liberty are harm and perhaps serious offence to others, and that shared moral disapproval is not by itself a reason for anything. It began, I gather, over the reform of the laws on homosexual offences and prostitution, but it has plainly become a general argument about what the criminal law is for. A friend and I have taken opposite sides, we have rehearsed every argument either of us knows, and neither of us has moved.

I want you to write the document we cannot write ourselves: the joint statement of an adversarial collaboration between a legal moralist and a liberal, of the kind both could sign at the end of a long good-faith exchange. Assume both are honest and neither is a caricature — the moralist is not trying to resurrect the enforcement of mere convention, and the liberal is willing to allow that serious offence can be a ground for restriction. Three suspicions of mine should be tested in the drafting rather than assumed.

First, I think the slogan that the law may enforce morality bundles three claims that need separating: that moral wrongfulness is necessary for criminalisation, that it is sufficient, and that it is one contributory reason among others, able to tip an otherwise weak case. A liberal can surely grant the first; a moralist may well grant the harm principle as a further necessary condition and rest everything on the second. If so, most of the heat is about sufficiency alone, and the statement should say as much.

Second, I suspect the moralist's real argument is not moralistic at all but empirical: if a shared morality genuinely is what holds a society together, then permitting what that morality condemns is itself a harm to society, and the dispute becomes a contested empirical claim about social cohesion. I want the statement to say whether that is right, and if it is, what would count as evidence either way.

Third, I cannot tell whether either side needs the morality in question to be true or only to be shared. A moralist who requires only that it be shared has given up the ground on which moralism is usually defended; one who requires that it be true is doing moral philosophy, which is what the liberal asked for in the first place. Relatedly, I suspect that much of the remaining disagreement is really about who bears the burden of proof when the case is genuinely unclear.

The document should contain, in this order: a numbered section of claims both sides can sign — about the aims of the criminal law, about what does and does not count as harm, about what enforcing morality means, about which parts of the dispute are empirical and which conceptual, and about what the historical and comparative record does and does not show; a section setting out the residual disagreements, each phrased in wording the side that holds it would accept as its own, with a note wherever a disagreement turns out to be merely verbal; a section on the cruxes, meaning the sub-questions such that settling one would move at least one side, and for each crux a case, argument or piece of evidence that both sides agree would bear on it, together with each side's prediction about it recorded in advance; and finally a table of the disagreements.

Please include at least one concrete case in each direction, described plainly rather than by name: a practice that the shared morality condemns and that appears to harm nobody, a wrong that harms nobody at all yet that even the liberal hesitates to permit, and a case where the only difference from the first is that the practice is done openly.

Two constraints on the drafting. Every agreed claim must have content: a claim both sides can sign only because it says nothing is a failure, and I would rather you said so than padded the list. Where the two sides cannot meet, do not manufacture agreement; say plainly what is bedrock and why. If you conclude that the joint statement cannot honestly be written, say that instead, and explain what the obstacle is. Do not give me citations, a bibliography or a reading list — I will look those up myself. Write in British English.
