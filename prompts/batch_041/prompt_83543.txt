I'm TAing for a second-year epistemology course this term — the prof's whole thing is skepticism and the structure of epistemic support — and I'm definately drowning in midterm marking. One of the questions was about Moore's proof of an external world: the one where he holds up one hand and says that's a hand, holds up the other and says that's another, and concludes that at least two external things exist, hence that there's an external world. He also insists he knows the premises even though he can't prove them, and says that doesn't stop it being a perfectly good proof. In lectures the prof laid out two ways of diagnosing why the proof doesn't give the skeptic what he wants. Strategy A: the proof fails because knowledge doesn't close over known entailment — you can know the mundane premise, and know that it entails the anti-skeptical conclusion, and still not know the conclusion. Strategy B: closure is fine, but warrant doesn't transmit — your warrant for the premise already presupposes that the anti-skeptical conclusion isn't false, so you can't get warrant for the conclusion by inferring it from the premise. The question asked students to (a) reconstruct the proof as a valid argument and be precise about what the conclusion actually is, (b) explain both diagnoses and say what each one does and doesn't give up, and (c) say what work Moore's concession about not being able to prove the premises is doing — whether it makes the proof circular, and whether the proof is best read as an argument or as a move in a dispute about who has to prove what. The prof also warned people off reciting stuff about the problem of the criterion, which half the class did anyway.

Here's the answer I'm staring at. Student wrote: "Moore's proof is: (1) here is one hand [right hand held up]; (2) here is another [left hand held up]; so (3) at least two external things exist; so (4) an external world exists. The conclusion is the existence of external things — Moore isn't just saying he believes in an external world, he's claiming to know the premises, and since the inference is valid he knows the conclusion too. Notice the hand-waving isn't an argument for (1), it's how he states (1): he isn't offering evidence for his hands, he's asserting it while pointing. Strategy A says the inference from (1) to (4) is no good because knowledge doesn't close over known entailment: you can know you have hands, and know that if you have hands you're not a handless brain in a vat, and still fail to know you're not a vat. What A gives up is closure, which is a real cost, since closure is what lets us extend knowledge by deduction in ordinary cases. But what A is really denying is the entailment in that conditional: the move from having hands to there being an external world is at best an inference to the best explanation, not a strict entailment, since the vat story is a different 'relevant alternative' rather than a consequence of (1). Strategy B is subtler. Here we keep closure — if you know (1) and know that (1) entails not-BIV, you can know not-BIV. What B denies is that your warrant for (1) is the kind of thing that can be handed along. Your warrant for (1) is perceptual, and perceptual warrant for mundane claims only gets going if the world is roughly as it appears, so the anti-skeptical conclusion is a precondition of your being warranted in (1) at all. So the inference is circular in a subtle way: you're using (1) to establish something that has to be in place before (1) is warranted. B accepts closure but denies transmission, and since it keeps closure it doesn't really cost us anything except some awkwardness about how to describe the warrant for everyday beliefs. Finally Moore's concession. When Moore says he can't prove (1) and (2) he's conceding that they're not certain — that he has ordinary defeasible grounds for them rather than proof — and that's why the proof doesn't refute the skeptic. It's also why he shifts into talking about what counts as a proof: proof doesn't require proving your premises from scratch, so the burden is really on the skeptic to say why the everyday premises are the ones that need support. On balance I think B is the stronger diagnosis, since it doesn't cost us closure the way A does."
