So I've been messing around making my own Bongard-style puzzles — you know, six boxes on the left, six on the right, figure out the rule that splits them — and I keep running into something that I think breaks the usual story about what these puzzles are actually testing. Here's the one that got me going: on the left I put a triangle and a pentagon, then a square and a square, then two triangles; on the right a triangle and a square, a pentagon and a square, then three triangles. The rule I actually used was parity of the total number of sides — left boxes always add up to an even number, right boxes to an odd one. But as a decoy I also made every left box have its two shapes touching and every right box have them separated, except for one box on each side where I broke that. Both of my friends found the touching/separated rule straight away and both got kind of annoyed when I said the "real" answer was the arithmetic one, and honestly I'm not sure I was right to say that — the touching rule fits everything except the two boxes I deliberately broke, and I only know it's the decoy because I made the thing.

Anyway, what I want from you is an arguement for a position I've been half-convinced of: that a Bongard problem doesn't really test concept induction at all, it tests something closer to mind-reading. Solving one is mostly about inferring what a designer would plausibly have had in mind, which means "simplest rule consistent with the examples" is the wrong objective, and the whole genre only works because solver and designer share a big pile of conventions about which features are worth noticing and which are throwaway noise. So please make that case as hard as you can: the real skill is modelling the puzzle-maker, not generalizing from examples, and a solver who lands on a genuinely different but equally consistent rule hasn't made a mistake so much as failed to guess the designer's taste — the two-broken-boxes thing is exactly the kind of move a real concept doesn't license but a person does. Don't just gesture at this, give me an actual argument with the strongest counterpoints you can think of addressed, and if you can say what it implies for the standard claim that humans are amazing few-shot concept learners while machines aren't, even better. No reading suggestions or citations please, I just want the argument.
