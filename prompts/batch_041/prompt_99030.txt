I retired some years ago from a career in the physical sciences and have been teaching myself political philosophy since, and I keep encountering a summary of Sen's liberal paradox that I am not equipped to assess. It was given to me roughly as follows: "Sen proved that no society can be both liberal and Pareto-efficient. His example uses two people and a single copy of a notorious novel -- a prude who would rather nobody read it and a reader who wants to read it. Liberalism says each person should decide what happens in his own private sphere, so the reader settles whether he reads and the prude settles whether he reads; the Pareto principle says that if everyone prefers one outcome to another, society must prefer it too; and these commitments chase one another in a circle. The theorem therefore shows that liberal rights and the Pareto principle are incompatible as soon as people hold preferences about how other people live -- it is really a theorem about busybodies. In a well-ordered society, where people mind their own business, the paradox is a curiosity of the mathematics rather than a live problem for liberalism, and Sen's own conclusion was that we must give up the Pareto principle."

I would like a careful audit rather than a verdict. Go through the summary claim by claim and tell me which parts a social choice theorist would endorse, which are loose but defensible as popularization, and which are outright misstatements. In particular, I cannot tell how strong the "liberalism" assumption in the theorem actually is, what the Pareto condition precisely asserts, or whether the summary has quietly substituted one kind of result for another. Then address the proposed dissolution: is it settled that the paradox depends on people having preferences about other people's lives, or is that one contested response among several, and what do the standard responses actually concede in order to escape the result? Finally, tell me whether the summary's practical conclusion -- that the paradox is a technicality and that the Pareto principle should be sacrificed -- survives your corrections, and what a defender of Sen's result would say the theorem does and does not show. Assume I know no formal notation and no names beyond Sen's, and give me the corrected account in plain prose I can keep beside the original.
