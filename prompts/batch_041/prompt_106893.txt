My friend is a public defender and we ended up arguing about this till 1am after she'd had a rough day in court, and I still can't sort out who was right. Here's the thing that was eating at her. In a criminal trial the state has to prove every element of the offense beyond a reasonable doubt, and if there's a gap anywhere in the chain of reasoning -- one inference that doesn't quite follow, one link missing -- the defendant walks. No shortcuts, no "come on, it's obvious," the whole case has to stand on its own two feet. But in the very same courtroom, the judge can simply announce that some fact is true and the jury is told to accept it without a shred of evidence, and the law instructs juries to presume that a letter properly addressed and mailed actually arrived, and that public officials did their jobs correctly, and so on. She had a case last month where the judge just took it as given that a particular block was a "high-crime area" and that was that, no proof, no witness, nothing. So on one side we demand an unbroken chain of reasons going all the way down, and on the other side we just stop and call it common sense. Her take is that this proves the legal system doesn't actually believe in proof at all, it just believes in whatever lets it reach the verdict it wants, and the "beyond reasonable doubt" stuff is theater. I think that's way too cynical but honestly I couldn't articulate why it's wrong, which is annoying me.

What I want from you is the theory of justification this whole practice is secretly committed to. Is the law a foundationalist -- there are just some basic facts that need no support and that's the end of it -- or is it a coherentist that accepts whatever fits smoothly with the rest of the body of common knowledge, or does it quietly give the infinitist the point and just cut the regress off for practical reasons while pretending that's not what it's doing? And does the two-facedness (merciless in one spot, permissive in another) actually make it inconsistent, or can a single theory cover both faces without strain? Please separate what the practice strictly has to presuppose from what it merely happens to fit, and tell me where it's underdetermined -- i.e. where several rival theories would each license the exact same courtroom behavior. Then tell me whether a system that abolished all of this, that demanded proof of absolutely everything including the roundness of the earth and today's date, would be more intellectually honest or just paralyzed, and if you swapped the practice for a cleanly infinitist one, what would actually have to change on the ground -- who'd be doing what differently.

Give it to me in this shape: "The practice, as I understand it" (short, like a paragraph, just so we're on the same page about what's actually happening in court), then "What it presupposes" (the main event -- the implicit theory of justification, this should be the longest part), then "What it merely fits" (the rival theories it's compatible with but doesn't strictly need), then "If we swapped it out" (what changes under a different view), and then "Bottom line" (two or three sentences, whose side should I be on, mine or hers, and why). Keep it tight, you don't need to write a treatise.
