I run the model-review desk at a state environmental agency, and we keep getting computational models—fishery stock assessments, airshed models, groundwater contaminant transport—whose assumptions everyone knows are false: well-mixed reservoirs, constant dispersal, no adaptive behavior, linear dose-response, stationarity. My staff have science backgrounds but not modeling PhDs, we have about a week per review, and the agency director signs off on permits. We were trained to ask whether assumptions are realistic, which is a nonstarter; modelers invoke the F-twist, saying assumptions need not be realistic and predictive success is what matters, and we have no way to adjudicate. As a longtime reader of rationalist blogs and forums, I want a reusable assumption-audit procedure with explicit stop conditions, not a list of virtues. Give me the questions to ask in order about each false assumption, what counts as an adequate answer, when a false assumption is disqualifying, when it is acceptable, and when the decision must go to a values panel because it depends on what we are willing to get wrong. Build it around a distinction I find useful: load-bearing falsehoods, where the false assumption does the predictive or explanatory work and cannot be relaxed without changing the result; scaffolding falsehoods, where it simplifies the derivation but the result is robust to relaxing it; and convenience falsehoods, where it is there because the modeler was lazy or the data are missing. Say how to tell which kind you are dealing with and be honest about when the distinction collapses. Then run it on two cases: a climate-economics model that assumes a single representative consumer with perfect foresight, and an epidemiological model that assumes a well-mixed population and no behavioral response. Include one case where the procedure tells me to reject a model I would like to approve, and say what the procedure cannot do—where it is just a checklist for making a political judgment look technical.
