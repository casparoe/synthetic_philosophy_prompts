I'm a science and ideas reporter and Ive been poking around a story about whether ordinary people should even try to do good on a big scale. I keep running into an argument that some philosophers call cluelessness: anything you do has causal ripples that spread out basically forever, you can't possibly trace them, so comparing the expected consequences of two options is hopeless. Even for a committed consequentalist, the whole maximize-the-good project seems to dissolve into a shrug.

What I need from you is the most important implications of that, and I want you to go downstream with it, not just hand me the obvious so-consequentialism-is-impractical punchline. In particular: what does it do to the whole enterprise of ranking charities by cost-effectiveness? Does it give you a serious reason to prefer rule-based or virtue-based ethics over act-based ones? And does it hand people an excuse for moral paralysis, or is paralysis itself unjustified under cluelessness?

Two things to weave in. A source of mine insists on a distinction between not knowing what your action will cause and not even knowing what would count as a better outcome; she calls the second one deep cluelessness and says thats the one that really does the damage. Is that distinction pulling any weight, and does it change the implications? Separately, a finance friend of mine says the fix is simple: just maximize expected value over an extremely long horizon with a near-zero discount rate. Does that actually dissolve the problem, or does it just relable it?

Please write it in American English, and keep the jargon light — my readers are smart but theyre not philosophers.
