I have been reading rationalist blogs and their comment sections for close to twenty years, and I have come to think that the debate over whether numbers count in rescue cases is nearly always staged in its most sanitized form. I would like an essay that takes a genuine position on the controversy, and that earns the position by pressing it against a case the sanitized form omits.

The controversy, in brief: you can save either one person or five, and you cannot save all six. One side holds that the five have the stronger claim simply because five lives outweigh one, and that any refusal to aggregate is arithmetic anxiety dressed up as respect for persons. The other side — the view usually associated with John Taurek — holds that each person has a claim not to be sacrificed for the sake of others, that such claims do not sum, and that the fitting response to a forced choice among strangers is something like a lottery, granting each of the six an equal chance of being saved. I want you to adjudicate, but not on the familiar ground of trolleys and transplant surgeons.

Consider this variation instead. The five you could save are not five unrelated individuals; they are near-duplicates of one another, cloned from a single template moments ago, each now psychologically indistinguishable from the others and each about to begin a separate life. The one you could save instead is an ordinary, unremarkable individual. Does the anti-aggregative case survive this change, or does the near-identity of the five undermine the distinctness of persons on which it rests? Then reverse the asymmetry: the one is a person who, if spared, will shortly be duplicated into five, while the five are ordinary strangers. Say what each case shows about whether the anti-aggregative view is really about numbers at all, or only about the separateness of the claimants.

Weigh the strongest form of each side — including the best defense of the lottery, and the best reply that the lottery itself treats persons as interchangeable — and end with a verdict you are prepared to defend: whether numbers count, in what sense, and what the duplication cases reveal about why. Write for a reader who already knows the standard moves, so do not spend space rehearsing them, and do not cite any literature; make the argument yourself. Aim for roughly a thousand words.
