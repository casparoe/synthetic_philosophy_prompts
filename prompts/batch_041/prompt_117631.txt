I sit on my department's animal ethics committee (I'm the grad student rep, so mostly I read forms and nod), and I keep going in circles about what our approval process actually assumes. The form asks us to justify that the species we're using can experience pain or suffering, and in practice everyone writes the same three things: it has a central nervous system, it has nociceptors, it responds to analgesics and shows wound-directed behaviour. That's the whole test. Meanwhile two floors down there's a physics group doing god knows what to trapped ions and they fill in no forms at all, and last term we voted on whether to extend our process to octopuses and crabs, and someone asked about the insects in the fly lab and about the human brain organoids in the stem cell group, and the meeting basically dissolved without deciding anything. So what does this practice actually presuppose? Not what it would be nice for it to presuppose — what it commits us to. That sentience requires a nervous system, or only that a nervous system is the only place we can currently read it off? Those are different claims and I want to know which one the practice needs, and whether the CNS line is doing metaphysical work or just epistemic work.

Then my flatmate, who has read too much Philip Goff and now thinks the lights are on everywhere, says that if experience is fundamental then our form is arbitrary, because the vacuum has as much claim to matter morally as the mouse does. My other friend thinks that's backwards: she says panpsychism makes the ethics narrower rather than wider, because whatever flickers at the bottom of physics isn't valenced and doesn't add up into anything that can have a bad time, so nothing down there is a patient at all. I'd like you to take both of them seriously and tell me which is right, or at least what each would have to assume to get off the ground. And here's the test case we argued about for an hour: a colleague proposed replacing the CNS requirement with something like — a system counts if it has some internal state that it acts to hold within bounds, a state that things can go better or worse for, such that its behaviour is organized around keeping that state in range. Does that criterion sweep in the thermostat, the bacterium, the single cell? Does it sweep in anything in fundamental physics? Is that a reductio or is it the correct answer?

So: reconstruct the implicit theory of the current practice, tell me what it strictly presupposes versus what it merely happens to fit well with, tell me which live views it quietly rules out (panpsychism, functionalism about minds, whatever else), and then tell me concretely what would change if we adopted the panpsychist picture or the functionalist picture instead — which experiments would suddenly need review and which wouldn't, and whether a form built on either picture could be administered by actual people on a Tuesday afternoon. I don't need reading lists or citations, I need the argument.
