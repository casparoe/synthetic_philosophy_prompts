I am a novelist, and the book I am writing turns on a question I did not expect to care about: what it is for a name to name someone. My protagonist discovers, late in life, that the name she has carried since birth was chosen by her parents for its sound alone, with no thought of the great-aunt who bore it a century earlier, and she becomes obsessed with whether her name touches that woman at all or merely resembles hers. To get the philosophy right I have been reading an introduction to the philosophy of language, and I have also been listening to a friend who took the subject for a term and is very sure of himself. Between them I have assembled a picture, and I would like to know how much of it I can build on.

The picture runs as follows. Kripke's central achievement was to show that proper names are rigid designators, which means that a name attaches to its bearer not through any description the speaker associates with it but through a causal chain stretching back to an original act of naming — a baptism. Because the name fastens onto the object directly, the identity of Hesperus with Phosphorus is necessary: it holds in every possible world, since both names designate the same planet wherever they designate anything at all. Yet astronomers established it by observation, so it is a necessary truth that can be known only a posteriori. This overthrows the older descriptivist view, on which a name is a disguised definite description and such an identity would be merely contingent; and it shows, more broadly, that the meaning of a name simply is its bearer — there is no separate sense doing any work — so that there are no analytic truths to be found about natural kinds, only a posteriori necessities. My friend draws the moral for me: this is why you may know everything there is to know about a person and still not know whom the name picks out, and why a name is at bottom a bare tag, carrying no descriptive content whatever.

Some of this I believe, and some of it I suspect has been smoothed flat in the retelling. What I want from you is an audit, claim by claim. Which parts would a philosopher working on reference and modality accept as stated? Which are the ordinary simplifications a good popularizer is entitled to make? Which are conflations or overstatements — I have a persistent feeling that rigid designation and the causal theory of reference are being pressed into a single thesis here, and that the claim about meaning being identical with reference is being made to carry more than the view can bear. Tell me, too, what a defender of the view would say my book and my friend have got wrong. And then, because it governs my last chapter, tell me whether the inference I want actually survives: my protagonist reasons that since the identity of Hesperus with Phosphorus is necessary and independent of any description, she can arrive at her true name — the one that genuinely designates her — by reflection alone, without any empirical inquiry into her family. Does that follow from the parts of the picture that survive your audit, or have I been leaning on the parts that do not? Finally, if you can, say what the causal-chain element, taken on its own, tells me about my protagonist's case, where the name was coined afresh rather than inherited.
