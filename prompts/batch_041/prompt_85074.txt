I belong to a reading group that has spent several months on Dutch book arguments, and we have reached a standoff that I would like diagnosed rather than adjudicated. The material we have worked through is: the classical sure-loss argument, on which credences that violate the probability axioms leave you open to a set of bets each of which you would accept and the whole of which guarantees you a loss; the diachronic version of that argument, offered in support of conditionalization; and the later reformulations that drop the betting setup altogether and try to show that non-probabilistic credences are accuracy-dominated, that is, that whatever happens they will be less accurate than some probabilistic alternative, as measured by a proper scoring rule.

Two factions have formed and neither has moved in months. The first holds that the reformulations settle the matter. An argument that shows your credences to be inaccurate rather than merely unprofitable is an argument about your credences as credences, and the fact that it can be run without mentioning money or bookies shows that the classical objections have been answered. The second holds that nothing has been settled: the reformulations replace one contested premise (that you will bet at your own odds, and that money is linear in utility) with another (that gradational accuracy is what credences are for, and that it is to be measured by a scoring rule of a certain kind). On this view the argument either shows only that a non-probabilistic state is defective by the lights of a standard the probabilist has chosen for himself, or it shows something weaker still, that such a state is not the best state by one measure among many. Each side regards the other as begging the question.

My hypothesis, offered as a hypothesis, is that the impasse is not between a pragmatic and an epistemic reading of the argument, but between two different things the argument is taken to establish. On one reading, a sure-loss result is supposed to expose an inconsistency in the state itself, a failure of the credences to be representable as a single probability function, which is a structural defect in the state. On the other reading, it is supposed to expose exploitability, a fact about what happens when the agent meets a bookie, which is a defect in the agent's situation rather than in her state. The classical arguments are exploitability arguments that are routinely read as inconsistency arguments, and the accuracy reformulations are inconsistency arguments that inherit the rhetorical force of the exploitability ones. If that is right, the parties may never have been disagreeing about the same claim.

What I want is a diagnosis with reasons: is this a substantive disagreement about what makes credences rational, a verbal disagreement about the word 'epistemic', a disagreement about which cases count as evidence for a norm, or two groups answering different questions under a single heading? Is my hypothesis right, or is the real fault line elsewhere — for instance, is the accuracy reformulation only as strong as a disputed assumption about which scoring rules are admissible, in which case the disagreement is a formal one that further formal work could settle; or is it a disagreement about what a vindication is required to do, in which case no theorem will touch it? Given your diagnosis, tell me what would count as progress: a distinction, a formal result, a change of question. Be concrete about the shape such a result would have to take — for example, whether one could show that exploitability and structural incoherence come apart, so that a state might be immune to sure loss while still failing to be a probability function, or the reverse, and what that would do to each faction's position. Do not survey the literature or hand me a reading list. Commit to a diagnosis, defend it against the strongest version of the position it rejects, and tell me what argument or result would make you abandon it.
