I am in a small reading group on the philosophy of mind, and we keep circling the same exchange. Two members are deflationists about phenomenal consciousness: they hold that experience will eventually be accounted for in the way biology accounted for life, and they press the point with an analogy that has become the group's shorthand. Vitalism, they say, was a research programme resting on the conviction that living things contained something over and above their chemistry — a life force, an élan vital, a special ingredient that no mechanical account could ever capture. Then biochemists worked out metabolism, replication, and regulation, and the conviction simply evaporated: nobody had to explain the life force, because it turned out there was no life force to explain, and the mystery had been an artefact of treating a label for a cluster of capacities as though it named a further thing. Consciousness, they conclude, stands in exactly that position, and the hard problem will dissolve in exactly that way; anyone who insists that felt experience is a further fact over the physical is a vitalist about the mind, defending the last mystery for the sake of the mystery. A cousin of the analogy gets used in the same breath: water is wet even though no individual molecule is, so we should not expect phenomenal properties to show up at the level of neurons while still counting them as nothing over and above the physical.

What I want is an audit rather than a verdict. Lay the correspondences out explicitly — what plays the role of vitalism as a doctrine, of the life force, of the biochemists' successes, of the residual explanatory gap that the vitalists kept pointing at, and of the holdout who refuses to be convinced — and then tell me where the mapping breaks. I am particularly interested in whether the force of the analogy depends on features of the life case that the consciousness case may not share: that life was fixed functionally, by capacities such as growth, reproduction, and self-regulation, so that a mechanism for those capacities automatically counted as a mechanism for life; that the vitalists were making a claim that could be tested by intervening on organisms and predicting what happened; that there were rival research programmes with measurably better track records; and that nothing seemed to be left over once the capacities were explained. Say which disanalogies bear on the use my friends are making of the analogy — licensing the conclusion that the hard problem will dissolve — and which are merely decorative. If what is really doing the work is not an analogy but an induction from the history of science, namely that mysteries have a good record of dissolving so this one probably will too, I would like that separated out and assessed on its own terms.

Finally, apply the same treatment to the wetness cousin: what would have to be true of phenomenal properties for the water case to be a guide, and does the disanalogy that sinks the vitalism comparison, if any, sink this one too. End by telling me whether the deflationist conclusion survives once the mapping has been repaired, what the strongest version of the deflationist's real point turns out to be — perhaps something about who bears the burden of explanation rather than about whether experience exists — and whether there is a better analogy available for either side.
