my prof summarized Kripke on origin essentialism in a lecture last week and I wrote it down pretty much verbatum, hoping someone can tell me how much of it to trust:

'Kripke's argument is that your origin is part of your identity, so you could not have come from a different sperm and egg. If you try to imagine yourself born to different parents, or as the product of a different fertilization event, you are really imagining a counterpart of yourself -- a person who resembles you in lots of ways but is not you. The same goes for objects: this table could not have been made from a different hunk of wood. What makes the argument work is that it is conceptual analysis: Kripke is not discovering a fact about the world, he is spelling out how our concepts of a person and of a possible world work. So origin essentialism turns out to be a truth about how we talk, not a metaphysical thesis. Critics object that this makes the argument circular, since it has to assume we are already identified in the premise before it can conclude anything about what is essential to us.'

I found this convincing at the time, but a friend in the seminar said the part about it being "about how we talk" gets Kripke exactly backwards. Can you do an audit? Which claims would a philosopher who actually works on this accept, which would they wince at, and does the "about how we talk" conclusion follow from the rest, or is it a separate claim that needs its own defence? And is the table example really doing the same work as the sperm-and-egg case?
