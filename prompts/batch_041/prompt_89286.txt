Last month I spent most of an evening arguing with a friend who works on machine learning, and I have not been able to let the argument go. His position is that the symbol grounding problem has been solved in practice if not on paper: systems trained on enough text and images end up with symbols that mean something, and the philosophical worry was always an artefact of looking only at toy systems. Mine is that he is answering a different question from the one the problem was posed to ask. What struck me afterwards is that we kept sliding past each other, and that the slide has a structure worth writing up.

I want to turn my side of that argument into a research proposal, and I would like you to write it. The core idea, still only half-formed, is this: discussions of grounding run together two questions that should be separated. The first is genetic — what causal history must a token have had for it to carry the content it does, that is, what makes it the case that this symbol rather than another was acquired from that object or interaction? The second is sustaining — what makes it the case, at a given moment, that a symbol has the content it has, that is, what features of the system's current organisation and its ongoing coupling with the world fix its meaning now? My suspicion is that the standard families of answer each address one of these and are then read as though they had addressed both, and that most of the familiar objections — the ones about symbol manipulation without understanding, and the ones about causal chains that are too deviant or too long — are really aimed at the sustaining question while the theories that provoke them are offered as answers to the genetic one.

Write me a two-page proposal for a one-year fellowship on this. It should state the question so that it has a determinate answer rather than reading as an invitation to take sides; say precisely why the existing positions leave it open and not merely unresolved; and set out the method — what I would actually do, and in what order, across twelve months. I expect the method to be largely conceptual: constructing cases in which the two questions come apart, testing whether the standard theories can be restated so as to answer the sustaining question without collapsing into a functional-role account, and working out what each separation costs. Say what a successful outcome would look like and, separately, what would count as the project failing — I would rather the proposal be honest about the possibility that the distinction is real but makes no difference to any live dispute. Give a realistic scope for a year, and say which parts I should expect to leave for later work.

Do not include a bibliography or a literature review by citation; refer to positions and arguments by description and I will supply the references myself. I will be the one defending it, so write it in the first person, and let the friend's objection stand as the thing the proposal has to answer.
