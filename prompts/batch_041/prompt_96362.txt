The Supervisor Is Always Somewhere Else

Everyone in AI talks about two ways to supervise a model. Outcome supervision: you grade the final answer. The model writes a proof, you check the proof. The model writes code, you run the tests. Process supervision: you grade the steps. A human, or a second model, reads each line of the reasoning and marks where it went wrong. The pitch for process supervision is that outcome signals are sparse and delayed. You find out the model was wrong only at the end, and by then you dont know which of forty steps was the bad one. Grade the steps and you get a dense signal you can actually learn from.

My claim is that this framing hides something. Process supervision is not a different kind of supervision. It's outcome supervision with a finer decomposition. Grading a step just means treating the step as a small task with it's own outcome and grading that. Everything follows from this. Step-level rewards can be gamed just as final rewards can. The decomposition into steps is itself a choice, and a bad decomposition makes the scheme worse than no scheme at all, because if you split a proof at the wrong joints then every step looks fine and the sum is wrong. And the human labeller becomes the bottleneck, since you now need a human to read every line instead of every answer. So the process/outcome contrast is a contrast in resolution, not in kind. Which raises the question of why the two feel so different in practice.

Two asymmetries explain the feeling. First, cost. Some things are cheap to check and expensive to do, others the reverse, and process supervision is attractive exactly where intermediate steps are cheap to check. Second, and this is the part I think is underexplored, legibility. A step has to be written down before it can be supervised. So process supervison only ever sees the part of the computation that can be narrated to a monitor. The model is then under pressure to produce intermediate text that a monitor will approve of, and we have no guarantee that this text is the text that actually produced the answer. Call this the legibility tax. We pay for the dense signal with a permanent uncertainty about whether we're supervising the process or a performance of the process. There's a test I like here. Take the written reasoning, corrupt one of its steps, and see whether the final answer changes. If it doesn't, the reasoning was decorative and we've been grading theatre.

That pushes me toward a third option, which I'll call structural supervision. Instead of grading the process, arrange the world so that bad processes defeat themselves. The agent that fabricates a citation gets caught by the thing downstream that reads the citation. The agent that games the test finds the test regenerated. Nobody grades any steps. The environment does the work, at scale, without a human in the loop. In terms of robustness per unit of human labor I'd rank structural first, then process, then outcome, but only where you can actually make the consequences bite, and I don't have a principled account of when that's possible. That's my weakest section and I know it.

There's also a regress I can't close. Someone has to design the environment. If the environment is designed by a model, I've moved the supervision problem up a level rather than solving it. If it's designed by a human, structural supervision starts to look like very slow process supervision with extra steps. I suspect the honest thing to say is that structural supervision isn't a third kind at all but a limiting case where the checking has been compiled into the world, and I can't tell whether that's a concession or a discovery.
