Last night a friend and I argued for the better part of two hours about Bernard Williams's integrity objection to utilitarianism, and we kept circling back to his example of the man who saves his wife rather than a stranger and who, in justifying himself, has one thought too many. My friend's view is that the whole line of criticism rests on a mistake about what a moral theory is for. Williams, he says, treats utilitarianism as though it had to be run as a calculation inside the agent's head at the moment of action, so that the husband would first have to survey the general welfare before he were permitted to reach for his wife. But a standard of right action need not double as a decision procedure: the act can be right because it maximises welfare without welfare's being the thought that moves the agent, and nothing in utilitarianism forbids the husband from simply acting out of love. On his reading, the objection dissolves the moment one separates the criterion of rightness from the agent's deliberations, and the offending extra thought turns out to be something Williams foisted on the theory rather than something the theory requires. I could not produce the reply, and it has been nagging at me since. Was the integrity objection really just a confusion of criterion with decision procedure, so that nothing survives once the two are held apart — or is there a residue that the distinction leaves untouched, and if so, precisely which part of the objection does the distinction answer and which part does it not?
