I'm a graduate student in a formal epistemology seminar, and we've just finished a unit on updating on uncertain evidence. A classmate floated an objection that I can't shake, and I can't tell whether it's decisive or whether it quietly cheats. I'd like you to identify which step fails and tell me what it costs to give it up.

Here is the background as I understand it. When an experience doesn't hand you a proposition you are certain of, but merely shifts your credences across a set of mutually exclusive and exhaustive alternatives, the standard prescription is to hold your conditional probabilities given those alternatives fixed and average them against your new unconditional probabilities for the alternatives. In symbols: if the alternatives are E1, E2, and so on, and your new unconditional probabilities for them are q1, q2, and so on, then your new credence in any proposition X is the sum over i of P(X given Ei) times qi. The conditionals are supposed to be carried over from your prior unchanged, and the qi are supposed to be supplied by the experience rather than chosen.

The argument my classmate gave:

(1) The averaging rule is the correct account of learning from uncertain evidence.
(2) The rule takes as input a partition of alternatives and a set of new unconditional probabilities over them, and apart from requiring that the old conditionals be carried over unchanged, it imposes no constraint on which partition or which new probabilities these are.
(3) Neither the partition nor the new probabilities is itself the output of any rule of rationality; they are simply whatever the experience delivers.
(4) For any proposition X and any target credence t for X falling between the smallest and largest of the values P(X given Ei), there is a choice of the qi that makes the rule output t.
(5) So an agent who wants to end up at credence t in X can always describe her transition as an application of the rule, by selecting a partition and new probabilities to suit.
(6) Hence the rule is satisfied by every possible transition from one credence function to another.
(7) But a purported constraint that every transition satisfies constrains nothing.
(8) Therefore there is no rational constraint at all on how one may revise belief in the face of uncertain evidence, and the entire apparatus of uncertain-evidence updating is empty.

I'm confident (8) cannot be right, and I would rather not abandon (1). My suspicion is that the trouble lies somewhere in (3) through (5), but whenever I try to say precisely what is wrong I find myself simply restating (2) with more emphasis. Where does the argument go wrong, and if I repair it by rejecting the step you name, what must I give up or add in order to keep the averaging rule in business?
