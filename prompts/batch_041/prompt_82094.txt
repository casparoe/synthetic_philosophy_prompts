He talked it through with a genetic counsellor and came away convinced there's nothing he'd actually do differently either way: no treatment, no prevention he isn't already doing, no plans for kids, insurance already sorted, no relatives he'd be warning. So for him it's pure news. It would tell him a fact about himself and change nothing he does.

Two people in our group chat have now been going at each other about whether he should open it. It's run for several rounds and I've copied it out as faithfully as I can.

(1) Jonah: this is exactly the case the theorem is about — Good's, I think, the value-of-information one — the one that says the expected value of free information can never be negative for a rational agent, because whatever you'd do after looking, you could have done anyway without looking. So "I'd rather not know" can only mean one of two things: either the information isn't really free (you're paying for it in anxiety), or you intend to act on it badly. Neither is an argument against looking. The first just means the price is your anxiety; the second means the problem is you.

(2) Marta: that's a theorem about a very particular idealised agent. It assumes your utility depends only on what's true in the world, not on what you believe about it. Ravi's utility is obviously going to depend on what he believes — that's what "pure news" means. If the news is bad, he's worse off, full stop. The theorem doesn't say otherwise; it quietly assumes away the thing that's in dispute.

(3) Jonah: but "I'm sadder now" is a cost. And if it's a cost, then the information wasn't free — he paid in sadness. So the theorem survives; all it tells you is that "I don't want to know" is a claim about the price, not a counterexample. And it's a price worth paying, because knowing tends to make you better at navigating the world in ways you can't foresee from inside your current ignorance. He might be wrong that nothing is actionable — that's exactly the sort of thing people are systematically wrong about.

(4) Marta: two problems. First, the theorem's step — "you can always do what you would have done" — assumes you'll actually do it. Ravi isn't a perfect maximiser. If he looks and then spends a decade in a doom loop about it, then for him the information was bad, and a theorem about ideal agents has nothing to say about him. And you can't patch that by saying "just put the doom loop into the utility function", because then the theorem becomes a tautology: information is good for agents for whom information is good.

(5) Jonah: no, that patch isn't a tautology, it's the substantive claim that the only thing that matters about information is what it does to your actions. And on the practical question, if Ravi is the sort of person who'd doom loop, the fix is to become someone who doesn't, not to stay ignorant. Building a mind that can look at bad news without falling apart seems like the whole point of the whole project. Refusing to look in order to protect a fragile self is precisely the failure mode we were all supposed to be against.

(6) Marta: and I'm saying that's a values claim wearing a theorem as a costume. "Become someone who isn't hurt by bad news" may not be possible for everyone, and the cost of trying is itself real. Also, the theorem is about a single decision. Ravi is deciding something with a thirty-year horizon, and the one-shot framing is doing a lot of quiet work in your arguement.
