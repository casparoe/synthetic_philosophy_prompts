So I finally read The Buried Giant (the Ishiguro one, with the mist and the dragon) and it's been bugging me for weeks and I want to think it through properly instead of just nursing vague feelings about it.

Quick reminder of the setup in case it's been a while for you too: post-Arthurian Britain, everyone's got this fog over their memory that they call the mist, nobody can hold onto the past for long. Axl and Beatrice, an old couple, set off to find a son they can barely remember. Turns out the mist isn't natural at all. Arthur had a she-dragon called Querig parked in a lair and her breath is what does it, and the whole point was to make everyone forget the massacres Arthur ordered against Saxon villages after the peace deals. Axl himself was Arthur's envoy back then and helped broker those deals, and he's forgotten his own part in it. Wistan, the Saxon warrior, kills the dragon to bring the memories back, and says the giant that was buried is stirring now, meaning the old hatreds come back and there's going to be a war. And poor Gawain, who everyone thought was just failing at his one job of killing the dragon, was actually guarding it on purpose, to keep the peace. Then at the end the ferryman rows Beatrice over to the island and Axl just walks off down the shore.

Here's what I keep circling. I've read about two positions on this stuff. One is that communities have some kind of duty to remember their wrongs and their dead, that forgetting is a betrayal of the people you owe it to (Margalit, I think?). The other is that all this remembering and memorializing does more harm than good, that it keeps old hatreds alive and peace sometimes requires letting the past go (Rieff, In Praise of Forgetting, I'm fairly sure that's the title). I want to say both, depending on the day.

But the thing that stops me from just applying either one to the book is this: nobody in the book forgets anything in the sense Rieff means. The mist isn't people slowly letting go of old wounds, it's a spell, dosed out by a dragon, arranged by a king, so that a massacre stays unpunished and unremembered. So the fog isn't forgetting, it's being kept from remembering — nobody's deciding anything, it's done to them. And if that's right then the book isn't a test case for the pro-forgetting side at all, it's a story about a cover-up. Except... is it? Because the peace is real, Saxons and Britons genuinely live side by side without killing each other, and Wistan killing the dragon does seem to bring the slaughter back. So maybe the book is saying the only kind of forgetting we ever actually get is the adminstered kind — there's no clean, chosen, dignified letting-go, there's just the question of who's holding the fog machine. That's the thought I want you to take seriously or shoot down.

I also want you to say something about the ending. The ferryman judges couples on whether their love is deep enough for them to stay together on the island rather than being kept apart forever, and it's their own memories of each other that seem to be the evidence, and theirs are shot through with fog. Then he can only take one at a time, and she goes first, and Axl walks away without a word. It feels to me like the book is doing something there about memory as the thing that keeps you attached to the people you love, not just to history — like the real duty isn't to remember events, it's to be able to remember the person, and the mist costs them that too.

So: read the novel through the duty-to-remember vs. case-for-forgetting debate. Where does it actually dramatize the real disagreement — and remember Rieff's side isn't stupid, the victims are dead and the living still have to live together — and where does it cheat by making the forgetting a magic spell instead of a choice, so the reader never has to feel the pull of the pro-forgetting side at all? Does Gawain have a real argument or is he just a tragic enabler? Is Wistan the villain, or the one person honest about what remembering costs? Does the book land on a side, or is it just showing there's no clean way out? What I really want is the thing the book shows that neither of those two positions can quite say in argument form, if there is one. And if there isn't, say so, and tell me which scene is the one that won't fit the reading — the scene where the honest critic has to admit the book resists.
