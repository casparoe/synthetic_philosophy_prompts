I am trying to work out what I believe about what wealthy countries owe to poorer ones, and I would like you to tell me what principle, or small set of principles, best fits the verdicts I record below. The cases are constructed rather than drawn from the news, so please treat the stipulated facts as fixed and do not worry whether they resemble any actual country.

My verdicts:

1. Norland is a wealthy, high-emitting industrial country. Its emissions have submerged the coastal farmland of Corallia, a small island state. I think Norland owes Corallia compensation, and owes it to Corallia as a political community, not merely to the individual Corallians who happen to have been harmed. The claim feels very strong.

2. Norland caused no harm whatever to Bakhara, where a drought has produced a famine. I think Norland owes famine relief there, but the claim feels weaker than in (1), and I have the sense that it is owed by whichever wealthy countries can meet it, rather than by Norland in particular.

3. Bakhara's government would divert roughly 60 per cent of any general budget support to patronage and armaments. I think Norland still owes something to the Bakharan poor, but not in the form of budget support; vaccines delivered directly, and cash to identifiable households, seem to me owed, whereas a cheque to the treasury does not.

4. Suppose Bakhara's health ministry had already decided, and budgeted, to run a vaccination campaign. I think Norland's paying for that same campaign discharges very little of its obligation, because the ministry will simply spend the money it saved on something else. The aid has to add something that would not otherwise have happened.

5. A scheme under which Norland sends its own doctors to staff Bakharani clinics, undercutting the salaries and training posts of local physicians, seems to me not owed at all, and arguably a wrong, even though it would save lives in the short run.

6. I think Norland may legitimately spend a given sum on its own moderately badly off citizens rather than on more urgent needs in Bakhara. It need not treat a Bakharan life as worth exactly as much as a Norlandic one in its budget.

7. A country that is very wealthy in aggregate but contains a large poor population of its own owes much less, in my view, than a smaller and more equal country of the same total wealth.

8. I do not think the obligations here fall on wealthy individuals as such. A Norlandic billionaire and a Bakharani billionaire seem to me to owe nothing of this kind; it is states, and the political communities they represent, that owe.

The tentative principle I have been running is this: a country owes aid in proportion to (a) the harm it has caused and (b) its capacity to help, measured net of the unmet basic needs of its own people; the obligation is owed to persons rather than to governments; and it is strongest where the beneficiaries can be identified.

Please tell me what this principle captures, what it cannot, and what the least revision is that would fit the whole set. I am particularly interested in whether the difference in strength between (1) and (2) is doing any work, whether clauses (3) to (5) can be derived rather than bolted on, and how much of (6) and (7) I would have to give up.

Then apply the repaired principle to a case I have not listed: Norland owes compensation under (1) to the Corallians, but the only body able to receive and disburse it is a Corallian government that is itself oppressive to a minority. Does the repaired principle direct Norland to bypass the government, to pay it anyway, or to hold the money back, and which of my eight verdicts does that answer force me to abandon?
