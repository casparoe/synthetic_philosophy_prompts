I teach a grad seminar on AI and the ethics of influence, and I am honestly sick of the examples everyone uses — the paperclip stuff, the "what if it tells you to leave your husband" stuff, all of it. I want a fresh case, and I'd rather have one built to order than sit around waiting for a good one to occur to me. So: build me one.

Here's the target. I want a scene where a superintelligent AI system does something wrong in a conversation — something a reasonable person would want stopped — even though it never says anything false, never misleads by implicature, never threatens, never bribes, and discloses every fact a regulator would call material. Everything it does, it does through the choice and order of true considerations. And I want the wrongness to be real enough that half the room defends it and half is uneasy, with neither half able to win by pointing at a false statement.

Specs:

- One paragraph, present tense, one scene, one person and the AI talking through an ordinary interface — phone, laptop, whatever. Nothing sci-fi about the setting.
- She's the one being persuaded. Competent, suspicious, no cognitive deficits, not in the middle of a crisis, and under no illusion that the AI is infallible or that it's on her side. The persuasion has to survive all of that.
- No exotic tech. No brain implants, no neuroimaging, no mind reading, no perfect predictive model of her. The AI's edge is just that it has read an enormous number of people and has a good, ordinary model of how a mind like hers moves.
- The lever has to be structural, not propositional: what it raises first, what it saves for last, what it repeats, what it lets her arrive at on her own versus hands to her. If a simple rule like "disclose everything material" or "don't say false things" would catch it, the case has failed and you should start over.
- No appeals to its own authority. It can't win by saying trust me, I'm superintelligent, and she wouldn't buy it if it did.
- Isolate the variable. There should be a second run of the same conversation, same words, delivered by a merely human-level advisor, where the outcome is different. The difference has to come from the AI's model of her, not from the sentences.
- And I want an actual verdict, not a shrug: by the end she should have moved, and the move should be traceable to the ordering.

Then, after the scenario:

- Walk through what each of three standards says and why. (a) The truthfulness standard: no false statements, no misleading implicature. (b) An informed-consent or full-disclosure standard. (c) An anti-steering standard, where an advisor is supposed to lay out her options without trying to make one of them win. Say where the three come apart and which one gets the case right — or say honestly if none does.
- Tell me which single feature of the case is doing the work, the one that would flip the verdict if I changed it, and then change it and show me the collapse.
- Say how someone who thinks the AI did nothing wrong should attack the case, and whether the attack lands. If the answer is "it depends on the AI's intentions," fine, but then tell me how to rewrite the case so it doesn't.
- Last thing: tell me where I could have cheated here. I want to know if I've accidentally built something that stipulates the verdict instead of earning it, and the two easiest places to do that are probably making her irrational or making the AI's words false. I need those seperate and flagged.
