And here's what the student turned in:

"Simon's target is the picture of choice you get from expected utility theory: the agent is handed a complete list of options, with their payoffs and probabilities, and just picks the highest number. Simon's point is that in any real situation the option set isn't handed to you — you have to search for it, and searching costs time, effort, and missed opportunities. So you can't optimize, because to know which option is best you'd have to have already looked at all of them, and looking is not free. What the agent does instead is carry an aspiration level, a threshold of 'good enough', and search until something clears the bar, then stop. That's satisficing. The threshold is basically fixed by the agent's habits and by what it's used to getting, so someone accustomed to bad options will settle for less than someone with higher standards. Simon's image for this is the ant on the beach: the ant's path looks wildly complicated, but the complication is in the beach, not in the ant — the ant is running a simple procedure in a messy environment.

The reason this isn't just 'people are dumb' is that Simon isn't making a claim about error. He's saying the standard model is the wrong idealization: it tells you how a mind with unlimited computation would choose, and then counts every departure from that as a failure. For a finite agent in an uncertain world, spending more time computing the optimal choice can leave you worse off than a crude rule that fires fast, and sometimes the crude rule is actually more accurate. That's what Simon's distinction between substantive and procedural rationality is for: substantive rationality asks whether the choice was optimal given the situation, procedural rationality asks how the choice was actually arrived at. A choice can be substantively suboptimal and procedurally perfectly sensible, and for Simon the science is on the procedural side.

Gigerenzer's fast and frugal heuristics are the continuation of this. Take-the-best, the recognition heuristic, and friends are algorithms that deliberately ignore information, and Gigerenzer's claim is that in uncertain environments they can outperform regression models, so that less information can lead to better inferences. That's Simon's point with a sharper formal edge: the heuristics are ecologically rational because they're fitted to the structure of the environment instead of to the fiction of an omniscient chooser. Simon's own work with Kahneman and Tversky on availability and anchoring is the direct ancestor here, showing that people lean on quick rules rather than full computation — though Gigerenzer cares more about when the rules work than about when they misfire.

If all that's right, then satisficing doesn't really break with expected utility theory at all. It's just optimizing once you add a search-cost term to the objective function, which is presumably why economists were able to absorb it into standard price theory without much fuss. The real disagreement is about which idealization is more useful, not about whether agents are maximizing something."
