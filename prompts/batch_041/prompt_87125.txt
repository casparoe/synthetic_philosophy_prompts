My mother had a stroke last year, and her neurologist told us she has a condition called pain asymbolia: she says a stimulus hurts, can tell us where it is and roughly how intense it is, and yet shows no distress, does not pull away, and sometimes laughs while it is being applied. I have since learnt that she is not a one-off curiosity — there are people born unable to feel pain at all, people who feel pain they do not mind, and people whose pain is vivid but entirely fails to move them — and that philosophers have long disagreed about why pain is bad, and whether its badness lies in the sensation, in our aversion to it, or in what it does to a life. Before I read any more of that literature, I want the specification rather than the theories. What would a theory of the badness of pain actually have to get right? Give me the desiderata: the cases it must accommodate, the distinctions it must not collapse (sensation against affect, unpleasantness against unwantedness, the badness of a pain against the badness of a life containing it), and the clinical findings it must not contradict, with the case or consideration behind each requirement. Tell me which of these are genuine requirements and which are the parochial demands of one camp; flag any pair you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied, and say which single requirement does the most to eliminate the popular accounts. Then, briefly, how the main families of view fare against the list — pain as bad in itself, pain as bad because unwanted, pain as bad because of what it does to us — keeping the weight on the criteria rather than the scorecard. Please write your answer in British English.
