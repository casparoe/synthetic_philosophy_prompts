I run the numbers and sit on the board of our family business — about 25 garden centres across two states, big enough to have a real board and a real payroll, small enough that a bad call actually hurts. The thing that eats my time is this: every few weeks someone (a supplier, the trade magazine, my uncle who goes to the industry conference every year) tells me that everyone in our line of business is now doing something. Moving to subscription plant boxes. Dropping peat-based compost. Going cashless at the tills. Adding click-and-collect. Killing a whole product category. And then the question lands on me: are we doing it or not. We meet about monthly, we usually have a day or two to decide, nobody on the board has any stats training including me, and these calls are expensive and hard to unwind once we've retrained staff and rejigged the tills. My cousin did a masters in econ and when I described all this she said it sounds like an 'information cascade' and told me to go read up on it, but I don't want a reading list or names of papers, I want something I can actually run in a meeting. What I've picked up on my own, and I don't know if it's right: the people who move first seem to be carrying most of the actual information, and everyone after them is mostly just looking at the queue ahead of them; a crowd agreeing isn't the same as a crowd that checked; and there seem to be two totally different reasons a person follows a crowd — one where they think the crowd knows something they don't, and one where they just don't want to be the only one blamed if it goes wrong — and those two probably ought to lead me to different conclusions, but I can't work out how.

So what I want from you is a procedure my board can run on one of these proposals: the questions to ask in order, what counts as a good enough answer to each, which step stops things with a flat no, and which step hands the decision back to a person because it's turned into a question about what we value rather than a question about what's true. Please don't just tell me to 'trust the crowd' or 'be a contrarian' — the whole problem is that I can't tell which situation I'm in, so the procedure has to help me tell. It should also be honest about which steps are real tests and which are just judgment calls wearing a lab coat, and about where it's going to misfire on us.

Then run it on three proposals: (1) our whole sector plus the competitor twenty minutes down the road has moved to a subscription box model, and we're deciding whether to; (2) every grower we buy from has stopped using a particular pesticide that we still use, and the pressure is on to drop it too; (3) every garden centre in our region put prices up about 8% this spring and we're deciding whether to match. Be warned, on that last one I'm already leaning no, mostly on gut, so if the procedure says follow I want you to say so plainly and tell me what it's assuming about me that I might be wrong about. Give it to me as tables so I can print it and hand it round: first a table for the procedure itself with columns Order, Question, What counts as a clear yes, What counts as a clear no, What to do if it's murky, and Real test or judgment call — and second a table for the three cases with columns Case, Which steps actually decide it, Verdict (follow / don't follow / can't tell from here without spending money or time), How confident (high, medium, low), and The cheapest thing that would change the verdict. After the tables, finish with a JSON object with these fields: "signs_we_are_in_a_cascade_and_not_a_real_consensus" (list of short strings), "cannot_settle" (list of the things the procedure is just not able to answer), "expected_misfires" (list), "answer_the_asker_will_dislike" (one string), and "first_question_to_ask_next_time" (one string).
