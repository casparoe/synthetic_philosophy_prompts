I've been drafted as the note-taker for a reading group that has spent the term on Moore's paradox, and my job now is to turn our scattered complaints into something usable: a specification of what any adequate account of the absurdity of Moore-paradoxical utterances would have to deliver. I want the desiderata themselves, not a survey — each requirement stated plainly, paired with the case or datum that motivates it, and, where relevant, tagged as non-negotiable or as a preference of one camp. The requirements we keep circling are these: the account must explain why the omissive form (asserting p while disavowing belief that p) strikes us as absurd even though it can be true and even though its two halves do not contradict each other; it must handle the commissive form, in which the speaker asserts p while avowing the belief that not-p; and it must say why the absurdity persists when the belief-clause is swapped for a knowledge-clause, so that asserting p while disavowing knowledge that p is likewise out of order. It also has to respect the asymmetries, since the absurdity evaporates in the third person (someone else may consistently hold p while denying my belief in p), in the past tense (I may coherently assert p while noting that I did not believe it earlier), and in various hedged or retrospective constructions, and it has to distinguish the absurdity both from outright lying and from a genuine contradiction, on the one hand, and from mere hedging or surprise, on the other.

Beyond that core I want the requirements that come from the edges, because that is where our disagreements live. Should the account locate the absurdity in the sentence-type, in the act of uttering it, or in the norms governing assertion — and if the last, which norms (truth, belief, knowledge, justification)? Must it extend to non-assertoric moods, such as guessing, supposing, or hoping, and to second-person address? Must it cover graded cases, like asserting p while disavowing confidence in p, and the whether-form, in which one asserts p while professing not to know whether p? And must it apply to a subject who lacks the very concept of belief, or to a system that produces the offending string without any beliefs at all — the case I keep getting stuck on is an automated assistant that outputs that the library closes at six and then adds that it does not believe the library closes at six. Does the account even get to call that absurd, or does its machinery presuppose a believer of a kind the system is not? I also want you to flag any pair of these desiderata that you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied — I have a nagging sense that the demand for one mechanism covering both the belief-version and the knowledge-version pulls against the demand that the absurdity be traced to the fact that asserting expresses belief, and I would rather have that tension stated than papered over. Finally, tell me which single requirement does the most to eliminate candidate accounts; a brief paragraph on how the expressivist and the content-based families fare against the list is welcome, but keep the weight on the criteria, since the criteria are the deliverable and I will do the scoring myself.
