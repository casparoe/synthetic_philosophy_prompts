I chair a standing research-integrity committee at a mid-sized university — seven voting members, we meet monthly, and every meeting ends with a written decision: a short list of findings plus a single verdict that our charter says must be derivable from those findings. The stakes are real (careers, retractions, occasionally a report to a funder), and our general counsel has warned us that a decision whose findings don't actually support its verdict is vulnerable on appeal, so logical consistency is not just a nicety for us. A colleague from the philosophy department sat in on one meeting and remarked that we seemed to have reinvented something that has a whole formal literature behind it, but she was no help beyond that. Here is the problem we keep hitting: each of us can be individually consistent, and we can still end up with a majority for every finding and a majority against the verdict those findings are supposed to entail — or, just as bad, a verdict that nobody can derive from the findings we actually adopted. I do not want a lecture on social choice; I want a procedure I can run in the room, with the order of the steps, the decision points, and an honest marking of where I am making a judgment call rather than consulting a rule.

Please write that procedure. It has to cover: how we should decide, case by case, whether to aggregate our votes finding-by-finding and then deduce the verdict, or to vote on the verdict directly and back-fill the findings; how to treat abstentions, recusals, and a member who appears to be voting on the findings in order to steer the verdict rather than because she believes them; who is entitled to set the agenda — that is, which propositions count as findings and which count as the verdict — and why that choice does more work than it looks like it does; and what to do when the two aggregation methods disagree and neither side will move. Mark clearly which steps are genuine procedure and which are values or judgment dressed up as procedure, say where the procedure simply cannot settle the matter and a person has to own the decision, and keep in mind that I am the chair, not a specialist, and that I have to be able to defend the whole thing in writing afterward.

Then run the procedure on three cases. First, a case where the finding-by-finding and verdict-direct methods agree, so that I can see the procedure staying quiet when it should. Second, a case where they come apart: a majority finds that the data were mishandled and a majority finds that the mishandling was material to the paper's conclusions, yet a majority votes against retracting the paper, with a third question — whether to notify the funder — hanging off the retraction. Third, a case where the procedure yields an outcome I will not like, perhaps because a recusal or an abstention swings it. For each case, give the vote tally, the outcome under each method, and exactly what the procedure tells me to do.
