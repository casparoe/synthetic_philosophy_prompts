I am a junior and I run my school's philosophy club. Next Thursday I have forty-five minutes and a whiteboard to introduce the frame problem to about a dozen members who have read no philosophy and no computer science; last week one of them insisted the whole issue is obsolete, since a model trained on the internet has never needed anyone to write down which facts stay true when something changes. I do not want the explanation itself; I want a plan for giving it. What has to be established first, and what depends on what, so that the problem arrives as a discovery rather than as a puzzle someone invented? Which single example should carry the whole session, and what must it have and lack to do that work? Which misconceptions will they form — I expect at least that it is only about computers being slow, that it is the same thing as the halting problem, and that it concerns robots rather than thinking generally — and at exactly which point in the sequence should I head each one off? Does comparing it with the halting problem help or mislead? How should I treat my friend's objection without settling it for them, and what should I openly decline to explain at this level? Finally, give me one question to ask at the end that would show whether the idea landed.
