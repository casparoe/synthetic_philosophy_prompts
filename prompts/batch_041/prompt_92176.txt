Here's what's been bugging me. In alot of countries with conscription, if you're drafted you can get out of it on grounds of conscience: you say you can't in good conscience fight, and instead of fighting you do alternative service — sometimes something like being a medic, i.e. you're still plugged into the war effort, just not doing the killing. Fine. But the same states will put you in jail for refusing to pay the slice of your taxes that pays for that war, even when your reason is exactly the same conscience. They generally won't let you out of fighting just because you think this particular war is unjust — the objection has to be to war as such. Meanwhile those same states often do let doctors and nurses refuse to take part in abortions on conscience grounds, and they'll happily punish you for sitting down in a road to protest a law even if you calmly accept the arrest and pay the fine.

So: what theory of political obligation is that whole pattern presupposing? Concretely — does it presuppose that the state's authority over you depends on what it's asking you to do, so that killing is different in kind from paying? Or does it presuppose the opposite, that the demand binds you whatever its content, and the exemptions are just concessions bolted on the side? Which of the candidate grounds of obligation can actually generate this pattern, and which does it quietly rule out? And is the pattern even internally consistent: is there anything principled separating the conscience exemptions from the refusals it won't tolerate, or is the line drawn by something else entirely — what's administratively easy, what the army actually wants, who has a lobby? Be careful to seperate what the practice really needs to presuppose from what it merely happens to fit with, and tell me where it's underdetermined, i.e. where several quite different theories would license exactly the same set of rules.
