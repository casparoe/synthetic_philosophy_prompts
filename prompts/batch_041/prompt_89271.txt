---

**The Regress Pump: Why Resoluteness Does Not Close the Money-Pump Argument**

*1. The standard pump.* A money pump is a finite sequence of trades, each of which the agent prefers to the status quo, that returns her to her starting point with less money. The familiar version exploits a cycle in her preferences: if she prefers A to B, B to C, and C to A, an exploiter can walk her around the cycle, charging a small fee for each swap she currently wants, and leave her where she began but poorer. The usual moral drawn is that rational preferences must be acyclic, or at least transitive.

*2. A pump that needs no cycle.* A pump does not, however, require intransitivity. It is enough that the agent's preferences change over time in a predictable way. Suppose there are two states, A and B, besides money. At the outset she prefers A to B. Once she is in A, her preferences reverse and she prefers B. Once in B, they reverse again. An exploiter need only charge her a small fee for each switch she currently desires; she pays, twice, and is returned to her starting point, lighter by the two fees. At every moment her preferences over the two states are transitive and acyclic. What the exploiter exploits is not a cycle within a single preference ordering but a reversal across time. I will call such an agent myopic: she chooses according to the preferences she holds at the moment of choosing.

*3. The standard defense.* An agent who foresees the reversal can defend herself by being resolute. She adopts a plan and refuses the trades that would undo it; if she can bind herself, or merely keep faith with her plan, the exploiter gets nothing. The diachronic pump is therefore usually taken to show not that diachronic preferences must be acyclic, but that agents require resoluteness — a capacity to act on a plan against the wishes of their future selves.

*4. The objection.* Resoluteness presupposes that the agent can rank her own future preference profiles. To be resolute is precisely to prefer that she continue to act on the plan's preferences rather than on the preferences she will later have; she is treating one preference profile as better than another. But if her preference profiles are objects she can rank, then they are objects she can trade, and an exploiter can sell her a change in them. Offer her, for a fee, a change in what she values about her own preferences — say, a change that makes her stop caring whether she keeps faith with the plan. If by her current lights she prefers that change, she pays the fee and takes it. Her resoluteness is now gone, and the pump of section 2 resumes, one level down. And the move repeats: any preference she holds about her own preferences can be attacked by an offer that alters the level above it, and she will pay whenever the alteration is one her current preferences endorse. There is no highest level at which she is safe, because safety at a level would itself be a preference, and every preference can be priced.

I picture the exploiter as a ratchet rather than a demon: she never needs a cyclic preference at any single level. She needs only, at every level, the same two ingredients — a predictable change in the agent's preferences, and an agent who will pay, by her current lights, for a change she currently wants. The agent is, at every instant, getting exactly what she wants, and is nonetheless being drained.

*5. The conclusion.* If this is right, the money-pump argument does not terminate in a requirement of resoluteness. It generates an infinite regress of levels, and diachronic rationality has no fixed point. Either the exploiter is defeated at some level by a preference that cannot be priced, or the argument shows that the very idea of ranking one's own preferences is incoherent, or something else that I have not seen. I cannot tell which, and this is why I want help.

I would like your comments to address the following, as specifically as you can, and to give me reasons rather than verdicts.

First, on soundness: does money actually change hands, net, in the regress construction? Is the agent genuinely worse off at the end, or is the phrase worse off simply undefined for an agent who gets what she wants at every instant — and if the latter, does the whole argument collapse into a pun on two senses of worse off?

Second, on the pivotal premise: is it legitimate to treat an agent's preferences over her own preference profiles as themselves objects she can rank and trade? Or does the construction equivocate, using preference in one place for a ranking over outcomes and in another for a ranking over rankings, so that the levels do not really stack?

Third, on originality: be blunt. Is this a relabeling of known material about adaptive preferences, higher-order preferences, or resolute choice, dressed up in my own notation? If it is, say which known idea it is closest to and where I have departed from it.

Fourth, on the conclusion: does it overreach? What is the strongest objection to it, and does the argument survive that objection in a weakened, conditional form — for instance, only for agents whose preferences are fully mutable and fully priceable?

Fifth, on the deepest point of method: I suspect I have slid between a claim about rationality — what a rational agent's preferences may be — and a claim about exploitability — what an exploiter can extract from an actual agent. Tell me whether the essay depends on that slide, and if so, whether it can be repaired or only abandoned.

Finally, if you think the argument can be stated more forcefully than I have managed, please restate it in its strongest form and then try to defeat it. I would rather be refuted cleanly than praised vaguely.
