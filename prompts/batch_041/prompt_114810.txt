I am a novelist, and for some years I have kept a private ledger — half superstition, half research — of the sad works of art that give me pleasure and the sad works that only cost me something, and I have decided I want to know what rule, if any, the ledger obeys. The entries, roughly as I have them. I seek out the moment in the Oedipus story when the king learns what he has done, and the pity and the dread of it I would not trade away; the grief is part of the pleasure of the play. A film engineered to make its audience cry, the strings swelling over the dying child, does make me cry, and I resent it, and I have never watched it twice. A great documentary about a famine that is happening in the world as I write this I watched once, as a duty, and did not enjoy a minute of it; the very idea of enjoying it seems to me obscene. Yet a great memoir of the author's own bereavement, written many years after the fact, I have read twice, and the grief in it is a pleasure I return to. When a friend sits across from me and tells me, unfinished, of a grief still happening to them, I am moved and I am not enjoying it, and it would be monstrous if I were. A song about heartbreak I will play on repeat for weeks; my own heartbreak I do not enjoy at all. A novel that ends in unredeemed devastation, written with great art, I count among the best reading of my life; a novel of unrelieved misery that is poorly made and seems to exist for its own sake I put down with distaste. And then the entry that embarrasses me most: I have wept while writing the death of a character, and those afternoons — the wound at its freshest, the thing not yet shaped — were among the happiest of my working life.

My working guess has been that I enjoy a sadness only once it has been finished — when the suffering is over and has been given a shape I can hold, so that what reaches me is the scar and not the wound — and that I recoil when the suffering is still open and makes a live claim on me, or when the shape is a counterfeit, a trick to extract my tears. But the ledger will not sit still under that rule. The memoirist's grief was real and I enjoyed it, while the famine's was real and I did not, and the only difference I can name is that one grief was survived and authored and the other is ongoing and undefended; yet I have also seen a fictional film of that same famine — a masterpiece, by any measure — and I enjoyed it, which makes me suspect that fictionality is doing work after all, and that my memoir verdict is the odd one out. And the maker's entry sits sideways to the whole scheme: if the pleasure of tragedy were the pleasure of the finished and the held, I should have been miserable at the desk and happy only at the rereading, and it was the reverse — the making was the joy, and it was made out of the open wound.

What I want from you is the principle, or the small set of principles, that best captures these verdicts, stated precisely, with each clause tied to the entry that forces it, and an honest account of which of my verdicts the principle cannot save, so that I know what to give up. I am especially interested in whether the maker's pleasure and the audience's belong in the same ledger at all, or whether I have been conflating two different things under the word enjoyment. And please tell me what your principle predicts for a case I have not listed: a video game in which the player is a parent burying a child — the same sadness, but now with my hands on it.
