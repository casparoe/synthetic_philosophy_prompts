I'm a TA for an upper-level metaphysics seminar (mostly grad students, a couple of scary-good undergrads) and the prof has dumped the final on me because she's at a conference, so I'm drafting it over the weekend and I need help. It's a 3-hour closed-book in-class essay, which means I can't have them cite anyone — honestly fine, because half the class would just name-drop "counterfactual dependence" and hope for the best anyway. I want ONE essay question on causation by omission that a good student cannot answer by reciting the standard back-and-forth between the Lewis-style counterfactual account and the physical/conserved-quantity view that says absences can't cause anything at all. The thing that keeps bugging me is that all the textbook examples — the gardener who doesn't water the plant, the lifeguard who doesn't jump in — are rigged to make the counterfactual story look clean, because in each one the omission is the sole difference-maker between the outcome and its alternative. So I want to hand them a case where that assumption breaks.

Here's the case I want them to work with. A small town's only pharmacy is supposed to restock a certain drug; the pharmacist lets the order lapse over a paperwork screw-up. Separately, a hospital physician, who knows the patient needs that drug, is supposed to phone the pharmacy to confirm it's in stock and fails to do so. Either the pharmacist restocking OR the physician checking would on its own have saved the patient; the patient dies. So the death is overdetermined by two omissions, and neither omission is even counterfactually necessary for it — flip either one and the patient still dies. I want the question to force them to say whether both omissions caused the death, neither, or only one, and then — the part I actually care about — what that does to the counterfactual analysis, given that neither lapse is a difference-maker in the naive sense. And I want them to feel the pull of the other side too, because the person who says absences just aren't causes (so neither lapse caused anything, the death is simply something that befell the patient) seems to be denying something nearly everyone wants to say. So the real crux is whether the counterfactual machinery is doing honest work here or whether it's quietly smuggling in "the omission made a difference" in a case where, if you look at the physics, nothing is there at all. Have them also say what a criterion would have to look like to count the pharmacist's lapse as a cause while NOT counting, say, the fact that the patient's neighbour happened not to be a doctor as a cause — i.e., once you've let one absence through the gate, how do you keep the background non-events out?

So please give me three things. First, the essay question itself, phrased the way it'd appear on the paper (they get 3 hours, closed book, and it should be answerable in that window). Second, a model answer of roughly 700 words that a strong student could plausibly produce in that time — one that actually takes a side and defends it rather than surveying positions. Third, a marking scheme out of 25 that separates an A from a B by naming the specific moves a top answer has to make and the specific moves that should cap someone lower. For the scheme I want the must-hit points listed with the marks each is worth, and one line per point on how students typically blow it — e.g. treating the overdetermination as if it were just the familiar preemption puzzle, or assuming that because neither omission is necessary the counterfactual account is simply refuted, when it isn't, it just needs the chain/quasi-dependence move to get off the ground. Don't make any marks hang on who said what or on dates or titles; naming a view is fine, but the question should be gradeable by someone who's never read the literature.
