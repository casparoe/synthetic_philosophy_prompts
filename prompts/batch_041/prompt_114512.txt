I am a fourth-year PhD student in philosophy, writing on causation and causal inference, and I sit as the outside member of the Serious Incident Review Panel at the teaching hospital affiliated with my university. The panel meets monthly, I hold one vote, and by rotation I draft the written decision for whichever case we close. Ours closes on Thursday and I would like you to draft that memo.

The case, in the terms the panel has it: a 71-year-old inpatient with progressive renal impairment was prescribed a drug that is contraindicated below a certain renal threshold; her most recent value was below it. She deteriorated overnight and could not be resuscitated. Four features of the episode are undisputed. First, nineteen months earlier the pharmacy informatics lead retired an entire class of renal-dosing and interaction alerts, with the sign-off of this panel's predecessor, on the documented ground that the class was being overridden in roughly 96 per cent of firings. Second, the prescribing resident did receive a lower-priority warning that morning and overrode it; her record shows forty-one overrides of that warning class in the preceding month, every one of which a later audit judged clinically appropriate. Third, the pharmacist's independent verification did not happen: the order was flagged stat, the pharmacy queue stood at thirty-five minutes, and it is local custom on that ward for stat orders to be released without the second check. Fourth, the automated dose-adjustment rule in the record system has a hard threshold that the patient's value missed by one point.

The panel is split. The chair, a nephrologist, holds that the informatics decision is the root cause and wants the retired alert class reinstated wholesale. The director of nursing holds that the missing pharmacist check is the root cause and wants a rule that stat orders never bypass verification. Our junior medical member proposes a third test, which she calls the widest-range test: designate as the cause whichever single factor, had it alone been different, would have prevented the death across the largest range of plausible assumptions about the other factors. The medical director wants the memo to say that the charter's demand for a single root cause is itself the problem and should be struck.

My own difficulty is this. When I try to state what the panel has actually been doing, I find two different questions in play. One asks whether the death would have occurred had some factor been otherwise, taking the rest of the episode as it in fact unfolded; on that question the missing verification is a cause and the retired alert class is not, because the override record makes it likely the resident would have overridden a reinstated alert as well. The other asks what would have happened had we reached in and changed just that one factor while holding everything else fixed; on that question the retired alert class fares even worse, and yet I cannot bring myself to say that a decision which removed the last automated safeguard across a whole hospital caused nothing at all.

I would like a memo of no more than two pages that recommends a single designation and a concrete course of action, with an owner and a date, that the panel can vote on; that explains, in language the clinicians will accept and without turning into a seminar, why that designation rather than the other two; that takes the medical director's objection seriously and says honestly what is lost by keeping the single-root-cause clause and what is lost by striking it; and that assesses the widest-range test on its merits, saying whether it is a better instrument for this panel than the two questions we have been confusing or merely relocates the disagreement. Please do not cite literature or supply a reading list: the memo will be read by people who will not follow it up. A redacted version goes to the family, so the closing paragraph should state plainly what we are telling them and what we are changing.
