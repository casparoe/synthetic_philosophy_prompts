An interlocutor of mine regularly closes arguments with the maxim 'the ends do not justify the means', most recently to reject a proposal under which one person would be badly wronged in order to spare five others the same wrong. He cannot tell me which claim he takes the maxim to be making, and I suspect that several quite different claims are being run together under it. Set out the distinct theses that sentence could be asserting, ordered from the trivial to the very strong, and give a verdict on each: which are near-truisms, which are substantive and defensible, and which are untenable as they stand. Then say which of those readings would actually support his verdict in the case at hand, and which ones generate what is sometimes called the paradox of deontology — the charge that if each such wrong is a serious evil, a view which regards it as one should prefer a single wrong to five, yet the maxim forbids the one wrong that would prevent the five. Where a reading generates that paradox, say whether it can be escaped without abandoning the maxim. Lay the readings out as a menu with verdicts rather than defending a single interpretation at length.
