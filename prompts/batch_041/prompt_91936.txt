I belong to a small reading group that has been working through a popular introduction to metaphysics, and last week we reached the chapter on possible worlds and modality, where the author sets out to explain David Lewis's counterpart theory by contrasting it with the transworld-identity picture he associates with Kripke. His exposition, as best I can reconstruct it, runs roughly as follows. On the transworld-identity picture, one and the same individual — you, for instance — exists in many possible worlds and simply has different properties in each of them; on Lewis's picture, by contrast, no individual inhabits more than one world, and everything is confined to a single world. The work that identity was doing is then taken over by the counterpart relation: for each way you might have been, there is an individual in some other world who is your counterpart, and a claim such as "you could have been a banker" is true just in case a counterpart of you is a banker in its world. So far I think I follow, and I can see why the author is at pains to insist that a counterpart is not identical with you. But two of his further claims lost me. First, he says that the counterpart relation is simply the relation of overall similarity between individuals, and that — being a relation of that kind — it is reflexive, symmetric, and transitive, so that if someone is your counterpart you are theirs, and chains of counterparts remain chains of counterparts; he adds that this is what lets the apparatus "do everything identity does" in the modal setting. Second, he draws the moral that Lewis's manoeuvre does not so much solve the problem of transworld identity as relocate it, and that a well-known objection — the one usually credited to Kripke, to the effect that Humphrey cannot coherently care whether his counterpart wins the election, since the winner would not be him — shows the whole approach to be a mistake rather than a live option.

I have the uneasy feeling that the author is being unfair to Lewis somewhere, but I cannot tell whether my discomfort is substantive or merely the friction one feels with unfamiliar machinery, and I would rather not settle into an opinion until someone has walked me through it. Would you audit the passage for me? Specifically: which of the author's claims a defender of counterpart theory would accept as fair, which are the author's own glosses that Lewis would push back on and why, and whether the concluding verdict — that the view merely relocates the problem and is refuted by the objection about Humphrey — actually follows from the parts that are correct. Where the summary is accurate but easy to misread, I would also value a note on how the view is stated precisely, so that I can see what the simplifications buy and what they cost.
