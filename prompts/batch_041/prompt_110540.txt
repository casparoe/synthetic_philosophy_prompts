I just spent the evening arguing with a friend about the Pasadena game, and we ended up stuck on a question neither of us could answer. Here is the game as we set it up: a fair coin is flipped until it lands heads; if it lands heads on the nth flip, the payoff is 2^n/n dollars when n is odd and −2^n/n dollars when n is even. The expected value is the sum of the payoffs weighted by their probabilities, which works out to the alternating harmonic series. That series converges, but only conditionally: by rearranging the order in which the outcomes are summed, you can make it converge to any real number you like, or diverge. So the game's expected value is not well-defined. This is different from the St. Petersburg game, where the expected value diverges to positive infinity—there the expectation is at least a definite extended real number, even if it is not finite. In the Pasadena case, the standard machinery of expected value simply fails to assign a value.

My friend's view is that this shows rational choice theory has nothing to say about the Pasadena game: if there is no expected value, there is no fact of the matter about what it is rational to do, so any choice—playing, refusing, paying to avoid it—is rationally permissible. I suspect that is too quick, but I do not want to just assert that. I want to know what a theory of rational choice would have to deliver in order to count as an adequate treatment of cases like this. So please write the desiderata: what must a theory of rational choice under undefined expected value be able to do?

I want the full specification. Which cases must it get right—not just the Pasadena game itself, but variants like the St. Petersburg game, games where the expectation is undefined but dominance reasoning still seems to apply, games where every outcome of one prospect is better than every outcome of another but the expectations are undefined, and cases where you must choose between two undefined-expectation prospects? Which distinctions must it respect—for example, the distinction between an infinite expectation and an undefined one, between a prospect whose value depends on the order of summation and one whose value is simply divergent, between risk attitudes that are permissible and those that are required? Which results from probability theory, measure theory, or decision theory must it be consistent with—for instance, that expected value should be order-independent when the sum is absolutely convergent, or that if one prospect dominates another then it should be preferred? Which puzzles must it dissolve or explain—like the intuition that you should be willing to pay a small amount to play the Pasadena game, or the intuition that you should not pay anything, or the intuition that the game is a swindle?

Also, flag any pair of desiderata you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied. That is the part I most want to see: if the list itself forces a choice between, say, order-independence and the ability to rank certain undefined-expectation prospects, I want to know where the tension lies and why. And if some desideratum is really just a prejudice of one camp—say, the demand that every prospect have a determinate rational ranking, or the demand that rationality be silent whenever expectation is undefined—say so and explain why it should or should not be on the list.

Then, briefly, how do the main existing approaches do against the list? I am thinking of views that say you should maximize expected value wherever it is defined and otherwise use some other rule, views that extend the real numbers with infinities and infinitesimals, views that replace expected value with something like a risk-weighted or rank-dependent utility function, views that appeal to dominance or to a lexicographic ordering, and views that say rationality is permissive when expectation is undefined. I do not need a full scorecard, but I would like to know which requirement each approach most obviously fails, and whether any approach satisfies all the non-negotiable requirements.

The purpose here is to evaluate whether my friend's permissivism is defensible, so the desiderata should be sensitive to that question: if the list can be satisfied by a theory that gives determinate verdicts in the Pasadena case, then permissivism is not forced; if the list itself contains an unsatisfiable pair, then maybe my friend is right that something has to give. But keep the weight on the criteria and the cases behind them, not on the scoring.
