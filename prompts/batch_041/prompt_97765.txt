Two friends of mine, Dana and Marcus, got into it at a dinner I hosted last week and it's still bugging me, so I want an actual verdict instead of a vibe.

The setup: Dana asked a colleague to read a draft she'd written and tell her what he thought. He replied with one line — that it was "certainly very thorough" — and nothing else. Dana is convinced he insulted the draft, that what he really meant was that it was bloated and padded and bad. Marcus says that's not in there at all: all the man said was that it was thorough, which is true and even sounds like praise, and Dana is inventing an insult.

The back and forth went about like this.

(1) Dana: Meaning isn't just the words. If he intended me to walk away thinking it was bloated, then that's what he meant, and I did walk away thinking it.

(2) Marcus: Then meaning is whatever's in his head, and you don't have access to his head. You have the sentence. And the sentence is neutral at worst.

(3) Dana: No, the point is he knew I wanted a real opinion, and he picked that one safe sentence and nothing else. Saying only the safe thing, when a real comment is what was asked for, is itself the comment.

(4) Marcus: But you'd call it paranoid if a stranger did that. Any sentence can be read as a dig if you squint. If he'd written "it's long," you'd say he was calling it bloated. If he'd written nothing, you'd say the silence was damning. Where does it stop?

(5) Dana: It stops where the reading is calculable — where you can see why he'd write that and only that, given that he's a competent speaker being cooperative and I asked him for a real opinion. He broke a rule on purpose and the break is the message. It's the faint-praise recommendation letter.

(6) Marcus: Fine, but you can't check any of it. He can just say "I only meant it was thorough" and he's off the hook, and now you're insisting you know his intentions better than he does.

Neither would budge and I stayed out of it. Can you go through it move by move and say who's right at each step — where Dana's reading is legitimate and where it's overreach, where Marcus is right and where he's missing something? And then, stepping back, tell me which of their two pictures of "what was meant" the philosophy of language actually backs, if either. I have a hunch this is definately the implicature thing, but I don't want a "Dana's basically right, that's implicature" answer — I want the part where the standard picture itself strains on a case like this: the part where you have to nail down what was literally said before you can call anything an implicature, and the part where you're inferring intentions you can't observe.
