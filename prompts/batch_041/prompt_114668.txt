For the past year I have been trying to settle, for myself, whether my own small contributions to large harms — a flight, a portion of meat, a vote, a few purchases — matter morally, and I keep running into the same wall. Every argument I can reconstruct either proves too much, so that I am implicated in almost everything, or too little, so that nothing I do makes a difference and I am off the hook. Before I choose a side I want the specification first. Write out the requirements that any adequate theory of an individual's contribution to a collectively caused harm must meet — the spec I would want in hand before entertaining anyone's theory.

Give me, first, the cases the theory has to get right, each with a sentence on why it is a real case rather than a trick: the ordinary threshold case, where many small contributions together cross a line and mine is neither necessary nor sufficient; a case where I cannot know how many others are contributing, or whether I am pivotal; a case where the harm travels through a norm or a market rather than through physics, so that my restraint changes who consumes but not how much; a case where everyone in my position reasons identically, so the theory has to tell each of us what to do when we all think the same way; and a case where one much larger actor causes most of the harm and I cause a sliver, so that the theory neither dissolves my sliver into their wrongdoing nor inflates it to their size.

Then the distinctions it cannot collapse: making a difference to a harm versus being part of one; causal contribution versus moral responsibility; doing versus allowing; the blameworthiness of a group versus that of its members; what is true of my act after the fact versus what I could have known beforehand; and the size of the harm versus the strength of the complaint against me.

Then the results it cannot contradict, so far as I can tell: that at the margin a single act often changes nothing; that small contributions nonetheless aggregate; that thresholds and non-linearities are real; that I cannot know other people's choices; and that whatever the theory demands must be livable by an ordinary person who cannot coordinate with strangers.

For each requirement, give the consideration behind it. Then tell me which are genuine requirements and which are the prejudices of one camp — I would rather lose a requirement than keep a counterfeit one. Flag any subset that looks jointly unsatisfiable, and say which single requirement does the most to exclude candidate theories; if the most plausible answers survive the list only by quietly dropping something, name what they drop. A short scorecard would help — act-by-act consequentialist, what-if-everyone-did-it, participation in a wrongful practice, group-agency, and expressive or virtue-based answers — but keep it brief, since the requirements are what I actually need.

Finally, tell me where your reasoning is most likely to fail and what argument or consideration would change your mind about which requirements belong on the list.
