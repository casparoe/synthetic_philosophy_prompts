Draft essay for comment: Advance Directives as Trusts, Not Commands

1. The standard opposition.
Advance directives are usually defended in one of two registers. In the autonomy register, the directive is an exercise of self-determination: the earlier self authored the later one, and authorship carries authority over what happens to the thing authored. In the welfare register, the directive is at most evidence about what would now serve the person's interests, defeasible by what the person now seems to want. Once the debate is posed this way, it turns entirely on whether the earlier self's authority survives the change in the later self's values, and both sides are stuck.

2. A third framing: fiduciary authorship.
I want to propose that we stop modelling the directive as a command issued by a sovereign and start modelling it as a trust instrument. The earlier self is not a ruler but a settlor; the later self is not a subject but a beneficiary; the clinician or institution is a trustee. This changes three things at once. Instructions in a trust are read in light of their purpose rather than their letter. A trustee owes a duty of loyalty to the beneficiary that can require departing from the settlor's wishes when circumstances have changed materially in ways the settlor could not have anticipated. And the settlor's wishes carry weight without a veto over the beneficiary's interests. The question I am unsure about is whether this is a genuine third position or the welfare view in costume. My inclination is that it is genuinely third, because it preserves a distinctive kind of authority — the author's authority over the meaning of her own project — while denying her authority over the lived experience of the beneficiary.

3. Two registers.
Concretely, every clause in a directive should be assignable to one of two registers. The presumptive register contains instructions that bind unless the interpreter can give specific, articulable reasons for departing. The axiomatic register contains instructions that bind even against the later self's apparent wishes. The twist is this: the earlier self should not be permitted to assign her own clauses to the axiomatic register. Whatever we think about the metaphysics, declaring one's own preferences non-negotiable is the move that stands most in need of justification, and it is made under the least information the author will ever have. Axiomatic status should instead be earned by a stress test: a clause counts as axiomatic only if it was affirmed repeatedly over a long period and survived an explicit attempt to argue oneself out of it — an adversarial audit the earlier self runs on herself. Everything else defaults to presumptive.

4. Proportionality of authorship.
A second constraint: the authority of a directive should be proportionate to the deliberative footprint that produced it. An afternoon's reflection should not govern a decade of life. So the binding force of a clause should scale with how long it was held, how many occasions it was reconsidered, and how much the author knew about the conditions it would govern. This departs from the usual binary treatment, in which a directive is either valid or invalid.

5. Division of epistemic labour.
The earlier self has authority over the evaluative question — whether a life of this shape is one she would count as hers — but almost no standing on the experiential question of how bad it actually is to be that person. She has never been demented. Directives should therefore be written to be silent on the experiential question: they should specify thresholds in terms of capacities and projects, and explicitly delegate to the interpreter any judgment about how a given level of impairment feels from the inside. The corollary is that a directive reading, in effect, if I cannot recognize my children, let me go is authoritative, whereas one reading, in effect, if I am ever in a state I would find unbearable, is not a directive at all, since its trigger is inaccessible to its author.

6. Who interprets.
If the interpreter is a loving spouse, we should expect capture. The spouse has a stake in the earlier self's version of events and will read the later self's contentment either as genuine or as evidence of decline, depending on which reading keeps faith with the person she married. I would therefore split the interpretive function. One role goes to someone intimate with the earlier self, whose job is to reconstruct the author's purposes. A second role goes to someone who never knew the earlier self, whose job is to speak for the later self's present interests and who must have standing to contest. Neither can override the other; disagreement goes to a third party.

7. Stress test: fluctuating capacity.
The binary earlier/later model breaks down when capacity fluctuates — the person who is lucid in the morning and disoriented in the evening, or whose depression remits and returns. Here the interesting question is not which self is authoritative but how to sequence the selves. My proposal is a recalibration interval: on each return to lucidity, the person has standing to amend the directive, and amendments made during a lucid interval are presumed to be her most considered view, since they were made by someone with recent experience of both states — a perspective no one else, including the earlier self, can occupy. Lucidity rather than the document becomes the seat of authority. I recognize that this also opens a route for manipulation, and I have not worked out how to close it.

I would like your comments on this draft. Please do the following, in this order. First, tell me whether fiduciary authorship is a genuine third position or whether it collapses into the welfare view with extra machinery; if it collapses, say exactly which step does the collapsing. Second, say which of the mechanisms in sections 3 through 6 are doing real work and which are decorative, and be willing to say that some of them should be cut. Third, give me the strongest objection you can construct against the whole apparatus, in particular from a critic who thinks it is paternalism laundered through legal form. Fourth, tell me whether the proportionality principle in section 4 generates absurd results — consider a lifelong conviction that was formed in a single moment, and a trivial preference held for forty years. Fifth, say whether the split-interpreter proposal in section 6 is administrable at all, or whether it merely relocates the disagreement. Sixth, identify any place where the essay simply assumes what it needs to argue. Finally, suggest a title and tell me which single paragraph is the weakest. Do not suggest readings, sources, or citations; work from the argument as it stands.
