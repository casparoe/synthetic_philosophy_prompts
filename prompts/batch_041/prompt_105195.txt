I am designing a graduate seminar unit on causation by omission, and I am trying to stress-test five candidate criteria for when an omission counts as a cause. A colleague insists that any acceptable criterion must be purely counterfactual; I am not so sure, because the cases that interest me involve roles, practices, and omissions of omissions. The criteria are meant to be applied to a small battery of cases: in Case 1, a hospital pharmacist is distracted by a personal call and fails to restock a drug, and a patient dies; in Case 2, the hospital board never adopts a restocking policy, and a patient dies; in Case 3, a passerby who is a compatible blood donor does not donate, and a patient dies; and in Case 4, a supervisor fails to notice that a subordinate failed to restock the drug, and a patient dies. The five criteria are: (A) the counterfactual test, on which an omission causes an outcome if the outcome would not have occurred had the agent performed the omitted action; (B) the sufficient-set test, on which an omission causes if it is a necessary element of a set of actual conditions sufficient for the outcome; (C) the normality test, on which an omission causes if it departs from what is normal or expected in the circumstances; (D) the interventionist test, on which an omission causes if an intervention that prevents the omission would change the outcome; and (E) the role-based test, on which an omission causes only if the agent occupied a role whose constitutive rules assigned her the omitted task.

Please rank these five criteria from most to least defensible as accounts of causation by omission, judging them by how well they count the pharmacist and the supervisor as causes while stopping short of counting the passerby as a cause, and by how well they handle Case 4, where the omission is an omission to notice another omission. Give me exactly five numbered items, one per criterion, ordered from strongest to weakest, each a sentence or two explaining why it lands where it does. Do not include an introduction or a conclusion.
