My flatmate is three years into a PhD on Moore's paradox and she has extremely strong, annoyingly specific views about which sentences are "absurd" — that's her word, she means you can't get them out as a straight assertion, they somehow cancel themselves. Her rulings so far, all of them meant as flat sincere claims about the world and not as hedges, jokes, reported speech, or bits of fiction:

- "The keys are in my coat pocket, but I don't believe they are" — absurd.
- "I believe the keys are in my coat pocket, but they aren't" — absurd as well.
- "I believe the keys are in my coat pocket, and I also believe they aren't" — fine, she says. Thats just someone admitting to a contradictory state, sad but not paradoxical.
- "The keys are in my coat pocket, but my sister doesn't believe they are" — fine.
- "The keys were in my coat pocket, but I didn't believe they were" — fine.
- "Suppose the keys are in my coat pocket but I don't believe they are" — fine, and she thinks this one is obviously fine in a way the first one isn't, which I don't really follow.

Now we're argueing about this one: "The keys are in my coat pocket, but I don't know that they are." What will she say?

I want the rule she's actually running, not just the verdict. Is it about what the speaker can coherently believe? About what a flat assertion commits you to — believing the thing, knowing it, something else? Which of those fit her six rulings equally well, what does each one predict about the new sentence and on what grounds? Then your best guess at which rule is really hers and why, and tell me which further sentence I should put to her — the one where the surviving rules come apart, so her answer would actually settle it.
