I run a small reading group and we've spent the last few weeks on Kelly betting and it's turned into a mess of people talking past each other. Half of them think Kelly is just expected utility maximization with log utility and there's nothing more to say about it, the other half think it's some deep fact about time averages and ergodicity, and one guy keeps saying the whole question is ill-posed because you never actually know the probabilities. A couple of them keep saying geometric rationality, which I think just means optimizing the growth rate instead of the expected value, but I'm honestly not sure they all mean the same thing by it. I want to end the session with a quiz — which bet-sizing philosophy do you actually hold? — so please write me one.

Format: 8 to 10 questions, each one a concrete case with 2 or 3 answer options, plus a scoring key at the end that maps answer patterns onto positions. Nobody should be able to guess which answer belongs to which position from the wording, so no question can just ask whether you think Kelly is normatively binding. Every question has to be about what you'd do or what you think is true in a specific situation, and the answer options have to be things a sane person might actually say.

Here are the cases I want in there (add one or two of your own if they help pull positions apart, but these need to be covered):

- A single even-money bet on a 60/40 coin, you can stake any amount, and then you're done betting forever.
- The same 60/40 coin but a thousand rounds in a row, and you can re-size your stake every round.
- The same thousand rounds, except you can only bet in $100 increments and your whole bankroll is $1000, so there's a floor under your bet size.
- You have $9000 and you need $10000 by Friday or something genuinely bad happens to you. There's a 50/50 double-or-nothing bet available for exactly the $1000 you're short.
- A coin whose bias you don't know, and you'll be flipping it a lot so you can learn the bias as you go.
- You're 85, no kids, no cause you care about, and this is the last bet you will ever get to make. It's positive EV.
- You're betting other people's money and your comp is 20% of the profits with no downside if you lose. Same 60/40 coin, thousand rounds.
- A thousand copies of you each get offered the same one-shot 60/40 bet, independently, and you care about all of them equally.
- You're a piece of software with a bankroll and an API key, and you can be snapshotted and rolled back to before any losing bet, so losing isn't really absorbing for you.
- And an insurance-ish one: a 1% chance of losing $100k, and you can pay someone to take that risk off you.

The scoring key should land people on one of these: Kelly is just expected utility maximization with log utility and there's nothing special going on; growth-rate maximization as a normative prescription no matter what your utility function is; the regime view where it depends on whether you can rebet, so one-shot means maximize EV and repeated means maximize expected log; the time-average or ergodicity view where the objective is the growth rate of your actual wealth over time and expected value is the wrong sort of average; survival-first, where the point is never to risk an absorbing state; fractional Kelly as the honest answer because your probabilities are wrong; the ambiguity skeptic who thinks the probabilities aren't knowable and the optimization is fiction; and the deflationary view that bet sizing isn't a decision-theory question at all, it's about incentives, contracts, and how much variance you can actually stomach. If you think one of those is really two positions, split it.

Then three extras. First, a list of answer combinations that don't correspond to any of the positions, with a sentence on each saying whether nobody lives there because the view is incoherent, just unattractive, or merely unexplored — e.g. is there anyone who says maximize EV even over a thousand multiplicative rounds and just accepts going broke with probability approaching one? Second, flag which pairs of positions the quiz cannot actually seperate, and say which case would be needed to pull them apart. Third, a short closing section for whoever takes it, saying what their result doesn't tell them. Keep the whole thing in plain language — half the group doesn't know the jargon and I don't want to have to define ergodicity at the door.
