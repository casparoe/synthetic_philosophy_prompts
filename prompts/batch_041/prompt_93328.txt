I'm an undergraduate in my first metaphysics course, and we've just started on material constitution — the statue and the lump of clay, and what we ought to say about them. A friend of mine, a philosophy graduate student who is usually a careful reader, gave me the following summary of the debate and assured me it was the standard picture. I'd like you to help me work out how much of it I should actually trust.

His account runs roughly like this. The puzzle starts from the observation that a statue and the lump of clay it is made from occupy the same region of space at the same time, are made of exactly the same matter, and came into existence in the same way; yet they seem to have different properties, since the lump would survive being squashed into a ball while the statue would not. Leibniz's law — the principle that no two things can be in the same place at the same time — is a law of logic, so anyone who says the statue and the lump are one and the same object is simply denying logic; we are forced to admit two objects where we thought there was one. He says this is really the same puzzle as the problem of the many, which asks how many objects there are when a cloud's water droplets are arranged cloudwise. Those who accept the two objects say that constitution is not identity: the statue and the lump are two distinct things that nevertheless have all the same parts and all the same properties, which is why critics have always found the position incoherent. The other side says the two are identical and tries to explain the apparent difference in properties away. And since both sides agree that the terms 'statue' and 'lump' pick out the same material stuff, he concludes that the dispute is really about how we use words, that the consensus nowadays is that it is a pseudo-problem which dissolves once we stop asking confused questions, and that the intellectually honest position is therefore to admit that statues, tables and lumps of clay do not really exist at all.

Would you go through this claim by claim and tell me what a philosopher working on material constitution would accept, what they would find misleading but defensible, and what is simply mistaken? I'm especially interested in whether the concluding claims follow from whatever parts of the summary are accurate, and in what the two-object view still owes us that the summary never mentions. I'm not after a reading list — I just want to know where the summary misleads me, and how someone who knows the literature would restate the points it gets wrong. My own suspicion is that the last step, about the whole thing being a merely verbal dispute, is too quick, but I can't say why.
