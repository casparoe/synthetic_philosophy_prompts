I've been lurking on LessWrong and the MIRI-adjacent blogs for years, and the thing I keep coming back to is the tiling / self-trust problem for agents that reason about their own reasoning. Here's a thought experiment I want to actually work through with you. Take Kurt Gödel — not the incompleteness-theorem caricature everyone memorized in a logic class, but the real guy, who spent his later years arguing that the human mind can't be a machine and that we have some kind of mathematical intuition no formal system captures. Now imagine he'd stuck around long enough to read the modern literature on self-trusting agents: the observation that an agent who wants to trust its own future conclusions — who adopts something like the schema that whenever it proves X, X is true, or that its successor's proofs are sound — runs straight into Löb's theorem, so that any consistent system that proves its own soundness turns out to prove everything and becomes useless as an agent. The agent can't just trust itself naively, and the whole field is a pile of partial workarounds: bounded proof search, reflection principles, dropping the full soundness schema, probabilistic versions, that kind of thing. My friend who works on proof assistants and I were arguing about this over beers and couldn't agree on what Gödel would've made of it, so I want your take.

Please think carefully first and start with a short brainstorming pass about which of his known commitments are actually load-bearing here — his Platonism, his anti-mechanism, his attitude toward the limits of formalization, his conservatism about new axioms, whatever you think is doing the work — and then give me the answer.
