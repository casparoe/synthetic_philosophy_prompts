Two people on the review panel I sit on got into a fight over whether the precautionary principle is an actual decision rule or just a way of laundering your fear of anything new. The case in front of us: releasing a gene drive to crash the invasive rodent population on an island, where the rodents are driving a few endemic birds extinct, and where the drive could plausibly cross to the mainland or jump species.

Sam said the principle is basically empty. It tells you to avoid the new thing but never the old one, so it's status-quo bias with a lab coat on. It's self-defeating too, since being precautionary about the drive means being precautionary about the near-certain extinctions we'd be waving through. And he said any version you actually write down can't be falsified, because whatever goes wrong just gets called 'unforeseen.' He'd rather we do plain expected-value reasoning with some discount for irreversibility.

Riya said that's a caricature. The principle was never 'avoid new things,' it's: when harms are irreversible, the burden of proof shifts onto whoever wants to act, and the bar goes up as the evidence gets thinner. She says that isn't status-quo bias, because the existing harms are known and already being managed while the drive's harms are unknown -- the asymmetry tracks known vs unknown, not old vs new. She granted it can't pick a threshold by itself, but said neither can 'reasonable doubt,' and nobody calls that empty.

Sam came back that burden-shifting quietly smuggles in a second premise -- that the status quo is acceptable -- which is exactly what's false here, since doing nothing is also a choice, with victims. Riya said fine, then just treat it as maximin: pick the option whose worst case is least bad. Sam said maximin is worse than useless, it would have blocked basically every technology that ever helped anyone, and it double-counts the worst case just for being vivid. Neither would budge and I had to call the meeting.

I'm just the bystander here and honestly can't referee this myself. Can you go through their exchange and tell me who's right at each step, not just who wins overall -- and what both of them are missing? Take a definite position wherever one exists; don't just tell me it's complicated and leave me to pick.
