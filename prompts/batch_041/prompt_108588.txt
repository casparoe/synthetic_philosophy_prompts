I'm a site reliability engineer at a mid-sized payments company, nine years on call, and I think in my own dialect the way most people think in their first language: error budgets, toil, runbooks, graceful degradation, blast radius, bus factor, single points of failure, mean time to recovery. I run our incident reviews with a small homemade taxonomy I've never seen anyone else use — silent failure, cascading failure, brittleness under load, and what I call absorbed toil, meaning work that keeps the system up but shows up on no dashboard because nothing would have paged if it had stopped. We also keep a document per service called the care plan, which is a pun I did not intend and can no longer unsee. My philosophy reading is shallow and recent: Noddings, Tronto and Kittay, nothing older, and I keep circling a suspicion I'd like you to test rather than flatter. My suspicion is that the ethics of care, once you strip the rhetoric, is a claim about the value of redundancy and responsiveness, and that everything in it could be said in my vocabulary if I got the translation right.

So: restate the ethics of care in my terms, as faithfully as you can manage, using my words rather than theirs and without quietly importing my assumptions and calling the result a translation. Take in the relational view of persons, on which selves are constituted by their relations rather than merely connected by them; the insistence that moral attention is owed to this particular other and not to a case falling under a rule; the sequence of phases that runs from noticing a need, through taking responsibility for it, through doing the work competently, to its being received by the one cared for; and the dependency-work strand, in which giving and receiving care are structurally asymmetric and the one giving is somebody's charge's charge, so to speak.

Then the part I actually want. Open with your verdict on my suspicion and then work through the residue. Which claims came across intact — I'd guess that the run from attentiveness to responsiveness maps almost exactly onto monitoring, feedback and remediation, and that competence is just the difference between a runbook and a wish — which came across only after being bent out of shape, and which have no counterpart at all in what I know. I'm particularly interested in two things. First, whether a vocabulary that treats components as interchangeable and redundancy as substitutability can express the care ethicist's claim that the one caring is not a stand-in for anyone else, and what a service-level obligation loses that a caring relation has, and the reverse. Second, whether a picture in which nodes are prior to edges can express the claim that the relation is prior to the relata, or whether that claim simply cannot be stated in my terms at all. Finally, adjudicate the residue honestly: where my vocabulary cannot say what they are saying, is that because it is missing something, or because the original claim was doing less work than it sounded like it was doing — because a fair amount of care ethics, read charitably, sounds as though it might be an argument about the limits of measurement wearing an ontology as a disguise. Don't give me a literature survey and don't cite anything, I have the books and I'm not writing a paper. Write it as one continuous piece of prose, no headings, no bullet points, no numbered lists, no bold, no tables — the way you'd put it in a long email to a colleague — and in British English.
