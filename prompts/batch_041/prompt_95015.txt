Between 1945 and the 1990s German courts had to decide what to do with defendants whose conduct had been authorised by statutes that were, on any ordinary test, validly enacted: the wartime laws under which officials had denounced and dispossessed their neighbours, and the East German border regulations under which guards had shot people fleeing westward. The courts' characteristic move was not to hold those statutes valid but iniquitous, and not to convict on some doctrine of justified disobedience, but to reason that a statute offending against elementary requirements of justice to an intolerable degree lacks the character of law altogether; and that since it had never been law, treating it as a standard of guilt did not offend the prohibition on retroactive punishment. I want a philosophical reconstruction of that practice. What conception of law does it presuppose — does it commit the courts to natural law, or is it equally available to a positivist who holds that the rule of recognition of this particular system contains a moral criterion, or who argues that the GDR's own constitution and international obligations already made the practice unlawful, so that no conflict between law and morality ever arose? Which rival views does the practice rule out, and which parts of it are merely fitted by these theories rather than required by it? Is the practice coherent with the same legal order's ordinary insistence that validity is one question and moral merit another, and with its ordinary refusal to apply criminal law retrospectively? And what does the threshold of intolerable injustice tacitly assume — can it be specified without a substantive account of the core of justice, or does it smuggle one in? Finally, what would a legal system have to give up, and what would it have to accept about unpunished historic wrongs, if it abandoned the practice and reverted to a strict separation of law and morality?
