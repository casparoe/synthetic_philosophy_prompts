I have just agreed to co-translate a small collection of poems built on severe formal constraints — a lipogram, an acrostic that spells a name, a text whose form depends on a grammatical feature English lacks — and my co-translator and I have been arguing for months without either of us shifting. She holds that a rendering which drops the constraint is not a translation of that poem at all; I hold that the constraint is a means, and that a version which keeps the constraint while wrecking the sense has failed worse. Write the joint statement that an adversarial collaboration between us should have produced by now: the claims we can both sign about what a translation is and about what our quarrel is not; the genuine disagreements, each phrased so that whoever holds it would accept the wording; the cruxes — I suspect one is whether the constraint belongs to the identity of the work or only to the source language, and another is whether a different constraint of comparable difficulty in the target can discharge the obligation — together with any crux we have missed, and, for each, a concrete rendering problem we would both agree bears on it, with each of our predictions about how it should come out recorded in advance; and, for each of us, what we would treat as a reason to abandon our own position. Please use the distinction between a constraint that is constitutive of the text and one that is merely ornamental as shared vocabulary throughout, and note which of our disputes turn on where that line falls. If part of what we have been calling a disagreement is only about words, say which part.
