I sit on the board of a small Catholic foundation that hands out one big grant a year, and this year we've walked into something I did not sign up for. A coalition in a country I won't name is trying to get rid of a president who won a clean election eight years ago and then spent the next seven rewriting the constitution, jailing the opposition press, and letting his family take over the customs service. The coalition has two wings. One is doing it the boring way — court challenges, an impeachment push, a general strike. The other wing has people in it who have said, more than once and not always privately, that if the courts fail then "whatever it takes" is on the table. They've asked us for money, and our grant cycle closes before our next board meeting.

Half the board wants to fund the whole coalition, on the grounds that the guy is plainly a tyrant and any tool that removes him is a tool for the common good. The other half — led by a canon lawyer who is normally my ally — says flatly that the tradition forbids killing tyrants and that funding a group with a wing that talks like that makes us complicit. He keeps saying "Aquinas is clear on this." I think he's wrong, and I think he's wrong in a way that matters, but I also don't think Aquinas gives us a clean green light, and I need to be able to say why in front of people who will actually listen.

What I want from you is the memo I'll circulate before the meeting. Work through the Thomistic machinery honestly rather than just importing modern human-rights language, because this board thinks in that vocabulary and I need them to see that the tradition is more interesting than either camp is pretending. A few things I specifically want you to sort out, because they're where I think the real argument is:

- The tyrant-by-title versus tyrant-in-practice distinction. Our guy acquired power legitimately and then governed tyrannically. Does that change the analysis compared to a usurper, and if so, how, given that the passages people usually cite weren't written with our guy in mind.
- Who counts as the public authority whose action the tradition requires. Our canon lawyer will say: no public authority here, so no. But there's an impeachment process, there are courts, there's an international body that has already issued findings, and there's a bishops' conference that has been conspicuously silent. I want a principled answer to what makes an authority "public" for this purpose, not a list of candidates.
- The caution about the remedy being worse than the disease. This is the one I find genuinely hard, and I don't want it waved away. If the strongman falls and the country fractures, or his brother takes over and is worse, what does that do to our judgment about funding a coalition that includes people who might not stop at the constitutional track? Be specific about how that consideration should cash out in an actual funding decision, not just as a disclaimer.
- The sedition point, which I think is the crux and which both camps are getting wrong. My understanding is that disturbing a tyrannical regime isn't sedition because the regime isn't ordered to the common good — but then there's a caveat that if the disturbance damages the people more than the tyranny did, it becomes sedition after all. If that's roughly right, then the whole decision turns on a prediction about magnitudes, which is exactly the kind of thing a grant committee is bad at. I want you to face that rather than hide it.

The bottom line I need from you is a concrete recommendation: fund the whole coalition, fund only the constitutional wing, fund neither but issue a statement, or something else — with a condition or a time frame if that's what the answer requires. Give the canon lawyer's position its strongest form somewhere in there, because if I can't answer his best version I shouldn't be voting at all.

Format: lead with the recommendation in the first sentence, then the reasoning, no preamble, no restating what I've told you. It's going to be read aloud at a table, so keep it tight.
