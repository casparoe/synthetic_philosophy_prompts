I've talked myself into a view about inheritance and I want you to try and knock it down. The view: what's wrong with dynastic wealth isn't mainly that heirs start ahead in some race -- the equal-opportunity framing has always felt a bit tired to me -- it's that a big enough inheritance buys a private exit from the web of mutual obligations everyone else is stuck inside. You still work, still pay, still show up, still depend on public things; the heir doesn't have to. Call it the opt-out objection. On this view the case for taxing inheritances hard isnt revenue and isnt punishing the dead, it's keeping the exit door from being privately purchasable.

So what would count against it? Give me the defeaters ranked, fatal first. I want the empirical ones (do heirs actually opt out, or do they mostly keep working? does the exit really need *inherited* wealth, or just wealth?), the conceptual ones (does the objection prove too much, since any pile of money buys some exit? does it quietly collapse back into the equal-opportunity story, or into a republican worry about domination rather than anything specific to inheritance?), and the design ones (if the exit is the problem, why is the estate the right base rather than the recipient's lifetime receipts, or a wealth tax, or nothing at all?). Tell me which of these my own side would concede rather than wave away, and finish honestly: does the view risk anything, or can it absorb every counterexample I throw at it? If it can, say wether that's fatal or just the price of a normative claim.

Structure it as: "The view, restated" (~100 words), "Fatal if true" (~250), "Awkward but survivable" (~250), "What my own side gives up" (~150), "Insulation check" (~150), "Bottom line" (~80).
