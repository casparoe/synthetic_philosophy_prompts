I spent most of last night arguing with a friend who is convinced that the Kalai–Smorodinsky solution is the fair way to divide a surplus, and I came away with an objection that felt decisive at two in the morning and considerably less so by daylight. I would like to test it against a serious defender rather than the caricature I kept knocking down, so please play one.

Take the role of a committed Kalai–Smorodinsky partisan: someone who holds that a bargaining solution ought to answer to the ideal point and to the monotonicity of the players' opportunities, and who is unimpressed by the prestige of independence of irrelevant alternatives. Stay in character until I say stop. You may concede technical points, and you should not pretend the solution is flawless, but do not surrender the central claim that the KS point, and not the Nash point, is the right recommendation. Write as a game theorist talking to a colleague: one paragraph per turn, no bullet points, and if I misstate the model, correct me rather than working around my error. If a question of mine is confused at the root, say so plainly instead of answering it politely.

Here is my example, and my opening move. Two players, disagreement point (0,0), three available agreements with payoffs (player 1, player 2): A = (10, 0), B = (5, 5), C = (0, 6); any lottery over them is available too, so the feasible set is their convex hull. On your account the ideal point is (10, 6), and the KS point — the maximal feasible point on the ray from (0,0) through (10,6) — is (6.25, 3.75), while Nash selects (5, 5). Now delete option C, the option that only player 2 likes and that player 1 would never accept. The ideal point becomes (10, 5), the ray swings, and KS moves to roughly (6.67, 3.33): player 1 gains a little over four-tenths of a unit purely because an option nobody would have chosen has vanished, while Nash does not budge. The trick runs in reverse as well. Let player 1 put one further agreement on the table, (12, 0) — a proposal that gives player 2 nothing and that your rule would never select — and your solution hands player 1 about 7.06 in place of 6.25. So a bargainer improves her share by introducing a proposal she has no intention of honouring. How is that anything other than the dependence on irrelevant alternatives that the ideal-point construction was meant to make respectable? And if your answer is that the ideal point represents a claim rather than an alternative, I want to know why a claim that no one can be made to honour should move the division at all. If I have botched the arithmetic or the example is not a legitimate problem under your assumptions, say so outright rather than playing along.
