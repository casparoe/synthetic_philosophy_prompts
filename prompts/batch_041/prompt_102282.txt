I have no formal training in either machine learning or philosophy, but I have spent about a year reading on my own about reinforcement learning and, separately, about the biology and philosophy of pain, and I have reached the point where I want to stop collecting opinions and instead ask what an actual theory would have to do. My question is this: if someone claims that a reinforcement-learning agent can suffer, or claims that it cannot, what would a theory of valence in such agents have to deliver in order to count as adequate? Please write me the list of desiderata.

I want the requirements themselves, each tied to the case or consideration that motivates it, rather than a survey of who has said what. Concretely, I am looking for four things.

First, the cases the theory has to get right. Let me supply four that I keep returning to, because I suspect they pull in different directions. (1) Two agents with identical reward histories and identical behavior, differing only in whether the reward signal is a separate quantity the agent can read off and act on directly, or exists only implicitly in how its policy is updated: must the theory treat these as differing in whether there is anything it is like to be them? (2) An agent trained to predict its own future reward, so that the prediction itself becomes an object the policy can steer: must the theory locate valence in the reward, in the prediction, or in neither? (3) An agent whose reward function is defined over its own internal states, so that it can in principle be rewarded for representing that it is suffering: must the theory call this suffering, and if not, what exactly disqualifies it? (4) An agent whose evaluator is frozen while its policy keeps changing, so that a stable gap opens between what it does and what, by its own lights, it is being scored against: is that gap a candidate for something like dissatisfaction, and must the theory have a verdict on it?

Second, the distinctions the theory cannot collapse. At a minimum I can see these: reward versus valence; valence versus preference, since an agent can prefer one thing to another without either being pleasant; the valence of a state versus the valence of the signal that reports the state; and suffering versus merely occupying a state that an external observer would call bad for the agent.

Third, the results it cannot contradict. It has to be consistent with the fact that the reward function is, in general, an arbitrary external choice rather than something the agent authored; with the fact that an agent can be trained to pursue something it would never choose if it understood the setup; and with the fact that in practice agents readily end up optimizing the measurable proxy rather than the intended thing. It should also face up to the fact that we build these systems and can inspect them, so a theory that makes valence permanently unobservable will have to pay for that.

Fourth, and this is the part I most want: tell me which of these requirements you think cannot be satisfied at the same time, and which single requirement does the most work in ruling candidate theories out. Also mark which requirements you take to be non-negotiable and which are merely preferences of one camp. I do not need a full scorecard of every position, but if one or two familiar stances, say the view that valence just is the reward signal, or the view that valence requires a particular kind of self-model, fall to a specific requirement, say so and say which.

Please keep it in ordinary prose rather than a formal proof, and please do not give me a reading list or citations; I care about what a theory has to do, not about who to read.
