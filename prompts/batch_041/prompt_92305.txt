Ok, long-ish question from someone with no econ or philosophy background, so bear with me.

I'm a backend engineer. I have a brokerage account I mostly ignore, and over the last few months I've noticed that the same argument keeps showing up whenever somebody offers me anything. Some real examples from my life:

- A recruiter cold-emailed me about a startup. My friend's take: if it were a good place to work they wouldn't have to cold-email people; the good ones get applicants.
- I listed an old GPU at a price I thought was fair and someone offered me *more* than I was asking. My brother: nobody pays over asking unless they know something you don't, so don't sell it to him.
- I found what looked like a free-money promo buried in a broker's API docs. Coworker: if that were real, thousands of people would already be farming it, you're not the first person to read that page.
- My landlord offered to let me break my lease early if I found a replacement tenant. My partner: why would he offer that unless something's about to go wrong with the building?

These all feel like one move, but when I try to actually write the move down, what's underneath it is different every time, and some of the versions contradict each other:

(a) GPU case: the other side knows more than me, so run.
(b) API case: the other side knows nothing more than me and neither does anybody else, so there's nothing there to find, so... also don't bother.
(c) Recruiter case: it's kind of its own thing — the fact that they have to ask at all is itself the bad news, regardless of who knows what.

So (a) and (b) are opposite stories about who's informed, and both get used to tell me to walk away, and (c) is a third thing that's about the asking rather than about the thing being offered. And the whole move obviously proves too much: why would a baker sell me bread unless he knows something bad about the bread? So there has to be some condition that switches the alarm on, and I can't find it.

There's also a version of this that eats itself, which is the part that really got me going. If the argument works, then everyone knows it works, so everyone stays away from anything that smells like a deal, which makes the deal better for whoever does show up, which means... somebody should show up? I can't tell if that's a real observation or me just tying myself in knots.

What I want to know: is there a name for the thing I'm circling? I'm not looking for "efficient markets" or "information asymmetry" or "caveat emptor" — I know those words, and each one covers maybe a third of this and none of them covers the whole. I'm after the specific move where the bare existence of an offer gets treated as evidence against taking it.

And I won't be satisfied with just a label. I want:

- the nearest real concepts, how they differ from each other and from my mash-up — I have a strong suspicion I'm squishing two or three separate ideas together and calling it one pattern
- whether (a) and (b) are the same argument in different clothes or two genuinely different arguments that just happen to land in the same place
- what the missing condition is that stops this from condemning every ordinary purchase, and whether that's one condition or several
- the self-eating version: is that a known thing or a mistake on my part, and if it's known, is it treated as a real problem or a cute footnote

My gut says the "why is this available to me" alarm shouldn't fire there, but I can't articulate why not, and I'd like you to tell me which feature of that case is doing the work.
