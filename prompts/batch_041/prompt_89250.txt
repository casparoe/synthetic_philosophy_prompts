I came up through the rationalist blogs rather than a philosophy department, and in two weeks I am presenting a paper at a small workshop on skill, knowledge, and action. My thesis is that the long standoff between intellectualists in the Stanley and Williamson line and their dispositionalist critics is a verbal dispute about what knows how ascribes, whereas the question everyone actually cares about — whether skilled performance is guided by representations at all — is orthogonal to it: the literature has conflated the ascriptive question with the etiological one, and once you separate them the intellectualist's practical mode of presentation does no explanatory work, because whatever it adds to the proposition is individuated by the very ability it was introduced to explain.

The room will be philosophers of mind and action who work on know-how, two of them loyal to Stanley and Williamson and fluent in the linguistics, who will go after my reading of practical modes of presentation; a couple of phenomenologists in the Dreyfus line who think I have conceded too much merely by granting the linguistic question any interest; one cognitive scientist who studies motor learning and automatization and will want to know what my claim predicts about transfer and about the familiar degradation of skill when performers attend to the mechanics of what they are doing; and a chair allergic to deflationary readings of disputes.

Draft my Q&A prep sheet: the objections I will actually get, phrased in each objector's own idiom and tagged by who raises them, ordered by how likely they are times how much damage they do, each with a reply I can give in two minutes and a note on where that reply is softest, what I should concede up front, and the one question I am hoping nobody asks. I would rather have the strongest version of each objection than the version that is easiest to answer.
