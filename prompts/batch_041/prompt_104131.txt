You are a historian of the French Revolution who also works on the philosophy of the social sciences. At a recent family dinner an argument about present-day unrest turned on a comparison a relative of mine kept pressing: a society under enough strain is a pressure cooker, grievances accumulate like steam, reform is the valve, and if you seal the valve the whole vessel eventually blows. He used it to conclude that the Revolution was inevitable, that the Terror was just what happens when pressure has nowhere to go, and that anyone who talks about ideology or accident is missing the physics. I have not been able to stop chewing on it, because it seems to settle the whole class-struggle-versus-ideology-versus-contingency dispute with a single kitchen image, and I suspect it is doing more work than it can bear.

Please audit the analogy rather than adjudicate the Revolution. Think carefully first, then give me a table of correspondences — what plays the role of the steam, the valve, the vessel, the cook, the thermostat — and then the places the mapping breaks. Separate the disanalogies that actually matter for his conclusion from the ones that are merely decorative, and tell me whether the inevitability claim survives once the breaks are on the table. If there is a better analogy for what actually happened between 1789 and 1794, say what it is and why it does better; if the honest answer is that no single image can carry this, say that instead.

Be explicit about which parts of this are settled among people who know the period and which are genuinely contested, and do not present a contested reading as the consensus. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of my question.
