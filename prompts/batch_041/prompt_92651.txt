Suppose Thomas Hobbes could be brought up to date on the formal study of the prisoner's dilemma — one-shot and indefinitely repeated — and on the game-theoretic and evolutionary work on how cooperation among self-interested agents can arise and stabilize without any central enforcer: tournament results in which nice, retaliatory, forgiving strategies do well; folk-theorem results about what repeated play can sustain; models in which reciprocity, reputation, and assortment keep cooperation going in populations of self-interested replicators. He knew none of this, but he did hold that covenants without a coercive power are not enough to secure anyone, and he let the Fool press him on whether it is ever rational to keep faith when breaking it pays. Reconstruct his likely response. Which results would he take as showing his problem is less severe than he supposed, which would he treat as already contained in his account of the laws of nature and in the distinction between obligation in foro interno and conduct in foro externo, and which would he reject as presupposing the very thing at issue — that the parties already expect future dealings to be honored? Then say whether he could regard iterated-dilemma cooperation as genuine obligation or only as prudence, and whether an evolved disposition to reciprocate would count for him as a moral motive or merely as a natural cause of peace. Where his doctrines genuinely underdetermine the answer, say so instead of settling it, and identify what in his text would have to decide the matter.
