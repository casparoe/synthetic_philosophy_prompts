I chair a standing incident review panel at a mid-sized firm that operates autonomous systems in three settings: warehouse logistics, clinical decision support, and municipal traffic management. When one of these systems contributes to a harm—a worker injury, a contraindicated clinical recommendation, a collision pattern at a signalized intersection—the panel has forty-eight hours to produce a written responsibility determination for the board, our insurer, and, where required, the regulator. Our current practice is ad hoc: legal says the operator is responsible, engineering says the system did it, operations says the vendor is responsible, and the lay ethics representative writes a dissent. I need a reusable decision procedure, not a treatise.

The conceptual anchor is the problem usually called the responsibility gap for autonomous systems. A gap arises when a harm is caused by an autonomous system and no human agent satisfies the ordinary conditions for responsibility—causal contribution, knowledge, control, and the capacity to have acted otherwise. Our panel must decide, case by case, whether this is a genuine gap or whether responsibility can be traced to a person, a role, or the organization as an entity. I do not need citations, reading lists, or names of philosophers. I need the idea turned into something four non-specialists can run in two days.

Please write that procedure. It should be a sequence of questions to ask in order, with instructions for what counts as a sufficient answer, what counts as a non-answer, and whether a non-answer stops the procedure with a negative determination or moves it to a different track. It should tell us when to assign responsibility to an individual, when to assign it to a role or to the organization, and when to declare that no assignment is warranted. It should mark clearly the points where the remaining question is one of values rather than facts—acceptable risk, acceptable opacity, acceptable reliance—and therefore must be handed to a named human signatory rather than settled by the procedure.

We have been using three informal labels internally and have never turned them into tests. A gap of ignorance is a case where no one in the organization could reasonably have known the relevant fact in time. A gap of fragmentation is a case where the knowledge existed somewhere in the organization but no role was charged with assembling it. A gap of abdication is a case where someone could have known and chose not to. The procedure should classify each incident into one of these or into a case where responsibility attaches cleanly, and it should say what follows from the classification. You may add other distinctions if they earn their place, but do not bury the procedure in theory.

The procedure must handle three hard cases. First, a warehouse robot injures a worker after a routine software update changed its path-planning in a way no engineer foresaw. Second, a clinical decision support tool recommends a contraindicated drug because its training data encoded a rare interaction, and the physician follows the recommendation. Third, an autonomous traffic signal interacts with human drivers to produce a collision pattern that no single driver intended and no single engineer designed. Apply the procedure to all three. Include at least one application in which the procedure assigns primary responsibility to a party our engineers will find uncomfortable—for example, the operator or the end user rather than the developer—and explain why the procedure requires that result.

The panel has legal, engineering, operations, and lay ethics representation, but no one with formal philosophical training. The output must be a written determination with a named sign-off, ordinarily the chief operating officer and general counsel. The procedure should say what it cannot do, where it will predictably misfire, and what kinds of cases should be escalated rather than decided by the panel at all.
