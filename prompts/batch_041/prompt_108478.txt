Ok, longtime lurker here (mostly LW, some of the other rat-adjacent blogs, the comment sections I should probably stop reading). Epistemic status: vibes plus having watched this one fight for three years. I want help with a writeup.

There's a dispute in phil of value that I keep seeing relaunched, and I'm fairly convinced that at least a chunk of it is people talking past each other. What I want is the advesarial-collaboration document the two camps should have produced instead of the forty blog posts and the three reply papers.

Camp 1 — the fitting-attitude people. Their line: value gets analysed in terms of fitting attitudes. For something to be good/valuable is, roughly, for it to be the sort of thing there are reasons to favour, where favouring covers desire, admiration, approval, preference, and friends. They think this is a genuine analysis — it tells you what value is, in terms of stuff (reasons, attitudes) that's supposedly more basic, and that's meant to be real progress over taking value as primitive.

Camp 2 — the wrong-kind-of-reasons people. Their flagship case is the demon: a demon threatens to destroy the world unless you admire him. Plausibly you now have a reason to admire him, since your getting into the admiring state is what saves the world. But this plainly doesn't make him admirable, or valuable, or good. If the fitting-attitude analysis were right, the existence of a reason to favour him should make him valuable, and it doesn't — overgeneration. And the standard patch, restricting the analysis to reasons of the right kind (reasons that bear on the object rather than on your having the attitude), either (a) is circular, because the way you're told to sort the reasons is by asking which ones make the attitude fitting or speak in favour of the object, which is precisely what the analysis was supposed to explain; or (b) smuggles value/fittingness in as a primitive, in which case it isn't an analysis at all, just a biconditional that happens to hold. So: at best a necessary truth about value, not a reduction of it.

I think both camps are right about something and I want it in one document. My guess at the cruxes, though don't just take my list:

- can "object-given" / "the right kind" be spelled out without using normative vocabulary — and if not, does that kill the analysis or just mean it's a seperate kind of claim (a necessary truth, a mark of the concept, whatever)?
- does the problem generalise? If you can run the same demon trick against fitting-attitude analyses of rightness, blameworthiness, epistemic justification, beauty, etc., is that evidence the framework is broken or evidence the phenomenon was never really about value?
- are wrong-kind reasons reasons at all? Or is "reason" doing two jobs — a consideration bearing on whether the object is F, versus a consideration bearing on whether to have the attitude — in which case part of the fight is verbal and somebody should just say so?
- how much weight should the demon intuition carry, given that it's a verdict about the very phenomenon under dispute?

Deliverable: the joint statement, written so a card-carrying member of each camp could sign it without wincing. Don't give me a bibliography or a bunch of "see so-and-so (2011)" — I know the literature, I want the substance, not the map. Structure it like this:

1. "Shared ground" — six to eight numbered claims both camps accept, including about the demon case itself and about what the disagreement is not about. ~250 words.
2. "The disagreement, in each side's own words" — one paragraph in Camp 1's voice, one in Camp 2's, each phrased so that side would actually put its name to it. ~150 words each.
3. "Cruxes" — three or four, each a neutral statement of the sub-question plus why settling it would move somebody. ~80 words each.
4. "Tests" — a table with columns: case or argument | Camp 1's prediction | Camp 2's prediction | what would count as a win for each. Include the demon, at least one case from outside the value case (something about reasons for belief or for intention — the toxin thing, a belief pill, whatever actually works), and at least one case built to test whether the restriction can be stated without circularity. ~400 words.
5. "Verbal or genuine?" — which bits of the fight, if any, are about words, and what you'd have to say to dissolve them. ~150 words.
6. "What neither side will sign" — the residual, stated plainly. ~100 words.

If the honest answer at some point is that the camps only look like they disagree because they're answering different questions, say that in section 5 rather than fudging section 2.
