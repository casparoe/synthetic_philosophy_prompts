I direct grantmaking at a small family foundation whose trustees have committed it to doing the most good it can, and who expect our portfolio to be ranked by expected value rather than by the appeal of the causes. Our staff is four people, we review proposals monthly, and we fund in global health, animal welfare, and technology policy. Every cycle we hit the same wall: for any grant, the consequences run on indefinitely — a deworming programme changes school attendance, later earnings, marriage and fertility patterns, and past that, nothing we can trace — while the standard we are supposed to apply presupposes that consequences are the sort of thing that can be totalled and compared. Write the procedure we should actually run on a proposal: a checklist we can work through in about an hour before the committee meets, with the questions in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where it terminates in a no, and where it must hand the decision to the trustees because the question is one of value or of sheer guesswork rather than of fact. It should say how far down the causal chain we look and defend that cut-off rather than assume it; what to do when we know a long-run effect could be large but not whether its sign is positive or negative; what to do with a proposal whose payoff rests on a tiny probability of an enormous benefit; and whether we should be comparing across our three areas at all. Then run it on three proposals: a deworming programme with good short-run evidence and a disputed long-run literature; a pilot for a labour-saving technology in a low-income economy whose employment effects nobody can forecast; and a research grant whose value rests on a one-in-ten-thousand chance of a very large benefit. Where the procedure returns an answer the trustees will not like, say so rather than smoothing it over. Finally, mark the steps that are judgment calls dressed up as procedure, and distinguish clearly what is settled among people who work on this from what is genuinely contested — including whether cluelessness is a fatal objection to the trustees' project or a manageable one — instead of presenting one side as the consensus.
