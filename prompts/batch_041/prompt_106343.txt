I belong to a reading group that has spent the last several weeks on Strawson's account of the reactive attitudes, and a friend and I have reached an impasse that I would like an outside number on. The proposition I want your credence in is this: that the reactive attitudes — resentment, gratitude, indignation, guilt, and the rest of the family of responses we have to one another as participants in a shared life — can be vindicated from within the practice alone, so that no external, metaphysical question about whether their targets are ultimately responsible is left unanswered by anything more than a restatement of the practice itself. I read Strawson as making two claims, one psychological and one normative: that we cannot help having these attitudes, and that there is no coherent standpoint from which to demand a further justification of them. My friend thinks the second claim is a bluff — that the unavoidability of the attitudes is a fact about our psychology with no normative force whatsoever, and that a determinist who keeps resenting his enemies is simply being inconsistent rather than refuting anyone. My own credence in the proposition is around 0.65; hers is around 0.2. I want your number, and I want it decomposed rather than asserted.

The sub-questions I can see the number resting on — add, cut, or split them as you think right — are these. (a) Whether the attitudes really are psychologically non-optional in the way the argument needs, or whether the objective stance is a genuine, stable alternative we can actually inhabit rather than a posture we can only adopt in patches. (b) Whether the distinction between the participant and the objective stance survives the case of the temporarily impaired agent — the friend who snaps at you after three nights without sleep, whose diminished self-control you fully acknowledge, and whom you resent anyway — or whether that case shows the stances to differ only in degree and to be mixed in every real response. (c) Whether justification from within a practice is a coherent form of justification at all, or an elaborate way of declining to answer the question. (d) Whether the skeptic's own position can be held without inconsistency — whether his indignation at being wronged, or his hurt at being treated as a mere object of management, quietly reinstates the very attitudes he claims to have suspended. For each sub-question give me a number and one line of justification, say how the numbers combine into your final credence, and tell me which single term that final number is most sensitive to. Then tell me whether the disagreement between my friend and me is most likely located in (a) through (d) or somewhere I have not named — and whether any of these sub-questions is really a disguised dispute about how a word is to be used rather than about anything in the world, since I suspect at least one of them is and I would like to know which.

Finally, be explicit about your own confidence in the decomposition rather than only in the final figure: which term you are least sure of, what consideration or argument would move your number most, and what would have to be shown for you to come down near my friend's 0.2 or, alternatively, to go above 0.9. I would rather have a number you are prepared to defend and then revise than a survey of the positions on offer.
