Question 3 (20 points).

A textbook presents the following principle, which it calls the Chance-Deference Principle:

(CDP) A rational agent's credence in a proposition A, conditional on the objective chance of A being x, is x — no matter what else she knows.

(i) State the Principal Principle (PP) as precisely as you can, and say how PP relates to CDP: is CDP a correct statement of PP, a strengthening of it, or a distinct principle?

(ii) What work does PP's admissibility clause do, and what would go wrong if PP were stated without it? Illustrate with a case.

(iii) Is PP best understood as a synchronic constraint on an agent's credences at a time, or as a diachronic rule telling her how to update? Defend your answer briefly.

Student's answer:

The Principal Principle (PP), introduced by David Lewis, connects an agent's subjective credences to objective chance. Where ch_t(A) is the objective chance of A at time t and E is the agent's evidence, PP says that for admissible E,

C(A | ch_t(A) = x ∧ E) = x.

So if the objective chance of heads is 0.5 and I know this, my credence that it lands heads should be 0.5; the chance is what I should defer to.

(i) CDP states the same content without the restriction to admissible evidence. This is not a substantive difference. The conditional credence in PP is already taken relative to the agent's evidence, so the phrase 'no matter what else she knows' adds nothing; and the admissibility clause is a technical device whose only job is to exclude pathological bodies of evidence, such as self-referential or contradictory ones. It does not change what PP requires in any ordinary case. CDP is therefore best read as a rough but essentially correct statement of PP.

(ii) The clause is there to stop an agent from feeding PP evidence that would make the conditional credence incoherent — for instance evidence that contradicts the chance facts themselves. Once such cases are set aside, the agent's evidence is admissible, PP applies, and her credence should equal the chance. If an agent somehow had evidence that settled A without going through the chances, that evidence would be inadmissible and PP would say nothing about it; but since no such evidence is available in ordinary cases, the clause does no work there.

(iii) PP is most naturally read as a diachronic rule, i.e., as a rule telling an agent how to update. What it tells her is: when you learn that the objective chance of A is x, set your credence in A to x. In this respect PP is a deference principle, and it is structurally the same as the Reflection Principle: both require a rational agent's credence to match the credence of an authoritative standpoint, the only difference being whether that standpoint is her own future self or the objective chances.
