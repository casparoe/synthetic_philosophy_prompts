ok so I'm trying to get straight on causation by omission before I write anything about it, and the thing thats bugging me is that everyone seems to be working from a different shopping list of what a theory of it even has to do. so before I read more, give me the desiderata -- what any adequate account of causation by omission has to deliver.

Stuff I think belongs on the list, but you tell me if it does and what else is missing:

- the basic cases it has to get right: the gardener who doesn't water the plant and it dies; failing to warn someone; not throwing the life ring; a doctor who doesn't prescribe; a parent who doesn't feed the kid. and the flip side, the omissions where the counterfactual holds but we still don't want to say it caused anything.
- whether there's a real distinction between omitting to do something and merely not doing it, and whether an omission is a thing in the world at all or just a way of describing the world (the "we don't perceive omissions, we infer them" line of thought)
- whether omissions can be causes in the first place, or whether omission-talk is really responsibility-talk wearing a causal costume
- the profligacy problem: if absences and not-doings count as causes, then for any event there are infinitely many omissions that "caused" it -- I didn't water your plant either, and I didn't warn you about the traffic, and I didn't do the thousand other things that would have prevented it. what in the theory stops everyone from being a cause of everything?
- the selection problem: given all the things the gardener didn't do, what makes *not watering* the cause rather than any of the others? I keep seeing answers that quietly appeal to what was expected, what was salient, what was abnormal, what he had a duty to do -- and I want to know whether that's cheating or whether it's actually a requirement that the theory track the normative/pragmatic facts.
- double prevention and cases where we want a non-event sitting in the causal chain
- whether causation by omission is the *same* relation as ordinary causation or a different one, and whether the account owes us one unified story or is allowed to be two
- extensionality: does the relation have to be indifferent to how the events are described? a lot of the accounts I've seen seem to need the description to matter, and I can't tell if that's a feature or a bug
- how it should relate to but-for causation and to legal doctrine -- should the theory vindicate the law's way of talking or is it allowed to revise it?

then the parts I actually care about: which of these are genuine requirements and which are just commitments of one camp. my suspicion is that "must be purely metaphysical, no normative or pragmatic ingredients" and "must handle profligacy with a non-selective criterion" are doing a lot of unearned work, but tell me if I've got that backwards. flag any pair you think can't be jointly satisfied -- that's the bit I most want, if the list itself turns out to be a result. and say which single requirement does the most to knock out the accounts people actually hold.

context for the purpose: I don't care about this for its own sake, I want to know when we're entitled to say someone's inaction caused a harm, so a theory that only works for toy cases and can't say anything about policy-ish cases is not good enough for me.

also, where you can, separate what's settled from what's genuinely contested among people who work on this. don't hand me one camp's picture as the consensus. I'd much rather get "nobody agrees about X" than a tidy answer. a list is fine, table if you feel like it.
