I am a moral philosopher preparing to present a paper at the weekly reading group of a department whose senior faculty are, with one exception, Kant scholars of a fairly orthodox stripe; the exception is a virtue ethicist known for work on the unity of the virtues, and there is a recent hire in moral psychology who runs experiments on the stability of moral motivation and regards armchair claims about character as empirically naive. I have thirty minutes, of which I expect the last fifteen to be question period.

The thesis I am defending is this. Moral worth is located neither in the token motive on which an agent acts nor in the action's conformity to duty, but in the counterfactual robustness of the agent's reason-responsive dispositions; and it is comparative and gradable rather than all-or-nothing. I hold four claims in particular. (1) The question whether someone acted from duty is ill-formed; the well-formed question is how her dispositional profile compares with the profiles she might have had and with those of others similarly placed. (2) Robustness is assessed along four dimensions of variation: the agent's antecedent mood, the presence or absence of an audience, the magnitude of the personal cost, and whether the beneficiary is kin. (3) Responsiveness to the reasons that there in fact are does not divide cleanly into moral and non-moral components, so an agent's susceptibility to prudential and aesthetic reasons can partially constitute her moral worth. (4) Because agents are poor reporters of their own motives -- the experimental work on confabulation makes this vivid -- moral worth is to be assessed from the outside, by the counterfactual profile, and not by the agent's own account of why she acted.

I want a question-period preparation sheet, not a defense of the view. Give me the six to nine objections I will actually get from this room, ordered by how dangerous they are (likelihood weighted by damage), and phrased the way each objector would phrase it -- the Kantian's objection in Kantian vocabulary, the virtue ethicist's in hers, the psychologist's in his. Assume I know my field: no literature surveys, no reading lists, no citations, and no explaining to me what anyone is known for. Distinguish the objections that have real force from the ones I can dispatch in a sentence.

Present the main deliverable as a single table with exactly these columns, in this order:

- Rank (1 = most dangerous)
- Objection (the objection in the objector's own voice, one to three sentences)
- Objector (which camp raises it)
- Why they will raise it (one line tying the objection to that camp's commitments)
- Likelihood (high / medium / low)
- Damage if I fumble it (high / medium / low)
- Reply (what I say, delivered in under two minutes of speech)
- Soft spot (where my reply is weakest, stated plainly)
- Concede or hold (what, if anything, I grant before replying)

Below the table, add three short items: (a) the question I am hoping nobody asks, with a response that does not concede the thesis; (b) the two sentences of concession I should open the question period with, if any; and (c) the one objection that will sound fatal to the room but is not, together with the single sentence that dispatches it.

Write in American English.
