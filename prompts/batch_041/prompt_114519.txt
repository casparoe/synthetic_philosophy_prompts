I hold a position on the Sleeping Beauty puzzle and I want it stress-tested rather than defended. Here it is, stated as precisely as I can.

On Sunday, Beauty is told the protocol: a fair coin will be tossed; if it lands heads she will be woken exactly once, on Monday; if it lands tails she will be woken twice, on Monday and again on Tuesday; between awakenings she is drugged so that she cannot tell the awakenings apart and retains no memory of the earlier one. She wakes. She cannot tell whether she is in a heads-world on Monday, a tails-world on Monday, or a tails-world on Tuesday. My claim: on waking, her credence that the coin landed heads should be 1/2, not 1/3.

My reason is not merely that the coin is fair. It is that waking tells her only that she is now occupying one of the awakenings that the protocol assigns to one outcome or the other, and how many awakenings an outcome generates is a fact about the experimenter's bookkeeping, not evidence for or against the outcome. Generalized: a hypothesis does not become more likely by virtue of the fact that, if it were true, there would be more of me, or more occasions of me, around to notice it. I take that as a general principle about self-locating belief, not a special plea for this puzzle.

I know there is a large body of argument aimed at people who say what I just said. What I want from you is a map of how exposed my position actually is.

- Which arguments and cases are genuinely load-bearing against me, and which only look decisive because they assume a self-locating principle at the outset that I am entitled to reject?
- Take two variants seriously. First, the coin is tossed on Monday night, after the first awakening, and decides whether there is a second awakening on Tuesday. Second, the lopsided variant: heads gives one awakening, tails gives a hundred. What is my position forced to say in each, are those answers tolerable, and is the difference between the variants principled or ad hoc?
- What does my position cost me formally? I suspect I owe an account of how probabilities decompose across the days, or of why some self-locating propositions cannot be conditionalized on in the ordinary way. Tell me exactly what I lose and whether any of it is a real loss.
- Where do my view and the thirder's actually come apart in a way that could be checked, not just in the number they assign here? Include what my view implies about how I should bet if I am offered wagers on each awakening.
- What would a committed defender of my view concede counts against it, as opposed to dismissing? Where would that defender be right to dismiss?
- My general principle has consequences outside the puzzle. Spell out the cosmological one: a theory on which there are vastly more observers than its rival should get no boost in credence from that fact. If that consequence is unacceptable, say so and say why; if it is acceptable, say what makes it acceptable when it feels so wrong.

I cannot look anything up, so give me no citations, reading lists, or names to chase. Give me the arguments themselves, and describe any case you use in enough detail that it stands on its own.
