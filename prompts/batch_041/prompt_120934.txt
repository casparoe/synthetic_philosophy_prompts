A friend of mine who teaches aesthetics is the most serious Tolstoyan about art I have ever met. Following What Is Art?, she holds that something is a work of art only if it infects an audience with a feeling its maker actually had; that the strength of the infection depends on the sincerity, individuality, and clarity of the feeling transmitted; and that the feelings worth transmitting are the ones that draw people together rather than set them apart. For several years now I have been sending her things and she sends back a verdict. The record so far:

- She thinks the best thing she has heard in years is a phone recording, made by a granddaughter, of an old woman singing a lullaby in a language neither of them speaks.
- She thinks a student documentary about a man caring for his severely disabled adult son, shot on a borrowed camera, is close to a masterpiece, though it is grim and hardly anyone has seen it.
- She says a shaky phone video of a village funeral, sung badly and off key, is real art.
- She calls the highest-grossing superhero film of the past decade a machine for producing sensations rather than a work of art, and says the same of a stadium charity single with forty credited writers.
- She dismisses a horror film that genuinely terrifies audiences on the ground that a scream on cue is a reflex, not an infection.
- She has no patience for a much-praised literary novel built out of irony and puzzle-box tricks, which she calls a performance for other writers, or for a sold-out recital by a young virtuoso violinist, where she says the audience is applauding difficulty rather than receiving anything.
- She agrees with Tolstoy's low opinion of Shakespeare and of Wagner, and sees no reason to be embarrassed about it.

Next month I am sending her a ninety-second video with forty million views: a migrant father reunited with his daughter at an airport. It was generated end to end by an AI video model from a prompt written by an anonymous college student, who says she wrote it while thinking about her own father. There is no director, no actor, no composer, no camera. Viewers report sobbing, and the comment sections are full of people saying that whoever made it understands loss.

I care less about her one-line answer than about the machinery behind it. Which of her past verdicts are actually load-bearing, and which rules does her record fix as opposed to leave open? A mutual friend insists she is really running something simpler and more sociological, that real art is what ordinary people make and can receive, and that professional elite production is counterfeit by definition. Tell me what each of the surviving rules predicts about the AI video, which prediction you would bet on and why, and whether the student's own feeling counts for anything under the rule you favor. Then name the single case I should send her next if I want to force the rules apart, one on which the rival rules must give different answers, and say what each would predict for it. If her record turns out to be less Tolstoyan than she believes, say exactly where the divergence lies.
