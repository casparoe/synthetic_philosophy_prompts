I have been trying, on my own, to work out which animals are conscious, and I would like comments on my attempt: whether the reasoning holds up, where it is weakest, and whether the conclusion is better or worse defended than the usual ones. I am not after reading suggestions; I want to know whether the argument itself works. What follows is the gist of an essay I would write.

The arguments I keep running into come in two families. One looks for behaviour — mirror self-recognition, guarding a wound, playing, deceiving — and says the behaviour would be hard to explain without consciousness. The other looks at the brain and says that an animal wired like us in the relevant respects probably feels like us. Both leave me uneasy for the same reason: they tell me when a creature acts like us or is built like us, not when there is anything it is like to be it. A thermostat is built to hold a temperature, and that does not make it care.

So I want to try a third route. What I take to mark a conscious creature is not what it can do but whether it can be wrong about itself. A fish that jerks away from a hook may simply be in a state that drives it to escape harm, with nothing anywhere that registers the state as its own condition. A creature that can mistake its own hunger for something else, or feel a pain in a limb that is no longer there, or take its own movement for another's, has something that represents its own state as a state — and that, I want to say, is where there is something it is like to be it.

Applied to cases, this makes an insect a likely failure, since nothing suggests it can be mistaken about itself rather than about the world; a fish doubtful; a bird that recognises itself in a mirror a probable pass; and an octopus the hard case, because its arms seem to act half on their own, which might mean no single thing represents the whole creature.

My questions are these. First, is the criterion circular — does "can be wrong about itself" already smuggle in the very consciousness it is meant to detect? Second, is it genuinely a third route, or a restatement of one of the familiar two in different words? Third, does it wrongly deny consciousness to creatures we have good reason to think have it, and if so, does that refute the criterion or only show that I have applied it too strictly? And fourth, what evidence or argument should change my mind, in either direction?
