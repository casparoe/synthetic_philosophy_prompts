I chair the safety committee of a large hospital group, and at Thursday's meeting we must settle our policy on near-miss reporting. Two of our sites have been running opposite schemes. At the largest, every near miss must be logged before the shift ends, with the reporting clinician named, and failure to log is a disciplinary matter; logged reports tripled, but our own review found that entries grew formulaic and self-protective, and after two staff were disciplined for late logging the numbers fell back to where they had started. At the smallest site reporting is voluntary and anonymous: it is thin, and the same three errors recurred for two years without anyone joining them up. Our staff want opposite things — at the large site most favour enforcing the rule to the letter, while at the small site most say a strict rule would stop them reporting at all — and one board member has argued that a rule everyone knows is broken in practice teaches staff that our rules are optional, and will corrode the ones that matter.

Draft the written opinion I will circulate. Recommend one of three options — enforce the strict scheme across the group; keep it strict but create a formal route for declared exceptions; or withdraw the rule and rely on professional judgement supported by anonymised review — and commit to a time frame and to the evidence that would make us revisit the decision. Set out the strongest case for the option you reject, in terms its supporters would recognise, and say plainly what we are asking of staff when we require them to follow a rule they may privately think is a waste of a shift.
