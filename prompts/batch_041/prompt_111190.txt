Exam question:
Compare Simpson's paradox with the sure-thing principle. Does the possibility of Simpson-style reversals pose a genuine counterexample to the sure-thing principle, or does it instead show that the principle must be applied to an appropriate partition of states? In your answer, explain how the two ideas differ in what they take as fixed, and discuss whether the correct response to a Simpson reversal is always to prefer the aggregate comparison.

Student's answer:
Simpson's paradox occurs when an association present in every subgroup reverses or disappears when the subgroups are combined. For example, a treatment may be better than a control in both mild and severe cases, yet worse overall, because the treatment is given mostly to severe cases. The sure-thing principle says that if you prefer an action to another action in every state of the world, then you should prefer it overall. At first glance, Simpson's paradox looks like a counterexample: if we treat each subgroup as a state, then the treatment is better in every state but worse overall. This would show that the sure-thing principle is not universally valid. However, the standard reply is that Simpson's paradox does not really refute the sure-thing principle, because the sure-thing principle concerns states of nature, whereas Simpson's subgroups are often defined by variables that are affected by the action or correlated with it. So the two ideas are compatible once we recognize that the subgroups are not the same as the states. The practical lesson is that when a Simpson reversal occurs, we should always rely on the aggregated comparison, since the sure-thing principle tells us to evaluate actions by their overall outcomes rather than by their outcomes within arbitrary subgroups. The only exception is when the subgroups are fixed in advance and are causally relevant, but in that case the sure-thing principle does not apply anyway.
