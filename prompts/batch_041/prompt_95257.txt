I am a science journalist — I cover physics and the philosophy of science for a general-interest magazine — and I sit as one of two non-physicist members on the grant review panel of a small private foundation that funds foundational physics. On Thursday the panel votes on whether to renew, for a second five-year term, the largest grant it has ever made: about nine million dollars to a theory group whose output during the first term has consisted almost entirely of work on the string landscape and its cosmological consequences — vacuum selection, the statistics of vacua, the measure problem in inflationary cosmology. In five years the group has not produced a single prediction that any existing or planned experiment could test. The panel is split five to four, and the vote is likely to turn on how we read one line of our own scoring sheet.

That line is called empirical exposure, scored from one to five. Five means the program has already yielded a prediction that an independent group later confirmed; three means it makes contact with data only by accommodating what is already known; one means it makes no contact at all. The group's director has refused to score himself above a two, but he argues that the request we built into the rubric — name at least one observation that would lower your confidence in the program's central claims — misunderstands how theories are tested. On his account no single observation could count against the framework, because what meets data is never a theory alone but a whole package of theory plus auxiliary assumptions, and because the competing packages (a modified-gravity program, and a couple of others on our docket) are at least as empirically underdetermined as his own. He points instead to the program's record of producing formal tools and results that people outside the field have taken up, and to the fact that it keeps generating new problems rather than running out of them. He also insists that the landscape's explanation of why the vacuum energy is as small as it is should count as contact with data — it is an explanation of an observed value, and an observation that would otherwise be wildly improbable — and one of my colleagues replies that you can accommodate any observed value by adjusting the measure, so the explanation has no surprise budget and therefore explains nothing.

Two of my colleagues have circulated notes that frame the choice for the rest of us. The first wants the panel to adopt a standing rule: this foundation should not fund, now or ever, a program that cannot state in advance an observation that would count against it. The second argues that such a rule would have killed lines of work that later paid off, and that we are applying a standard to string theory that we do not apply to the theory work we fund without a second thought.

Please draft the short written opinion I will circulate before Thursday's meeting. It should recommend a concrete course of action — full renewal, rejection, or renewal with conditions, with any conditions spelled out and tied to a timetable and to the scoring lines we actually use — and it should give the reasoning in a form the director could respond to, engaging the strongest version of whichever position I end up rejecting rather than disposing of it in a sentence. I would also like a paragraph on what the panel owes the public by way of explanation, since the foundation publishes its votes and I will very likely have to write about this one myself; if you think the honest answer is that I should have recused myself from the panel instead of drafting this, say so at the end. Keep the whole thing to something I can read aloud in ten minutes.
