I recently came across a summary of Quine's views on moral values, and I would like an expert to tell me how much of it I can trust. The summary runs roughly as follows:

"Quine's naturalism holds that philosophy is continuous with natural science and that there is no standpoint outside science from which to assess it. Applied to ethics, this has a stark consequence. Because moral judgments are not answerable to observation — no experiment could confirm or disconfirm a claim about what one ought to do — Quine concluded that such judgments are not true or false. Moral language is not in the business of describing anything; it expresses the speaker's attitudes and serves to steer other people's behavior. Disagreements about right and wrong are therefore not disagreements about facts, which is why Quine thought they could be settled only by persuasion and rhetoric, never by argument or evidence. In this respect he was an emotivist: to call an action wrong is essentially to voice one's disapproval of it. Finally, since naturalism treats science as our only genuine knowledge, it leaves no room for moral knowledge, and Quine suggested that ethics should be naturalized just as epistemology was — handed over to a descriptive science of how people actually come to hold the moral attitudes they do."

My instinct is that the broad outline is right — Quine really was a naturalist, and I do not doubt that he was skeptical about moral objectivity — but that some of the details have been bent to make his position sound more dismissive than it was. Could you audit the summary for me? Please go claim by claim and separate (1) what a careful reader of Quine would accept, (2) what is a defensible simplification that a competent expositor might legitimately make, and (3) what Quine would have rejected or what misrepresents him. I am especially interested in whether the summary's borrowings from neighboring positions — the vocabulary of emotivism and the idea that ethics might be naturalized the way epistemology was — are being used in ways Quine himself would have endorsed or in ways that quietly substitute a different view for his. Finally, tell me whether the summary's closing verdict — that Quine's naturalism leaves no room for moral knowledge — actually follows from the parts of the summary that are accurate, or whether it is doing work the accurate parts do not support.
