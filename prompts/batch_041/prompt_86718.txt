I'm writing the final for my upper-level rule-following seminar. By the end of term they all know the Kripkenstein set-up backwards -- quus, 68+57, the whole bit -- so a question they can pass by reciting that is useless to me. I want one 90-minute essay question that makes them argue about whether the sceptical solution actually solves the paradox or just changes the subject, and I want two specific things in the mix. First, the circularity worry: when Kripke says the solution rests on agreement in the community, that looks like it already presupposes the members are following a rule about what counts as agreeing with each other, and if their responses are just dispositions then the original sceptical argument should re-run at the level of the group. Second, a seperate case I'm going to make up and put inside the question itself, so they cant look it up: a small community where the uniformity is not spontaneous but produced by coercion, or by everyone being drilled the same way by the same authority from childhood. Does the account have anything to say about the difference between agreement and enforced uniformity, or is agreement doing the work only because it's meant to be a brute fact we don't interrogate? I also want them to commit on whether the solution is non-factualist about meaning ascriptions, and whether that's compatible with still treating arithmetic as objective. Write the question, a model answer of about 600 words, and a marking scheme of exactly five numbered points, one or two sentences each, adding up to 20 marks -- no intro or closing remarks, just the five points. And keep the question itself short enough that they spend the time thinking rather than decoding it.
