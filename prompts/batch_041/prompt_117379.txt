I'm writing a short story for a speculative-fiction workshop whose theme this month is bureaucracy, and I'd like you to draft it. The setting is invented, so nothing in it needs looking up: the Standing Committee on Navigational Claims of the Free Port of Vantle, a seven-member tribunal that adjudicates salvage and collision claims under a charter that is scrupulously procedural and in places absurdly so. The charter requires that every member's vote on every question be recorded by name in a public minute; that a question be resolved in the affirmative only if at least four of the seven members raise a hand; and — this is the clause the story turns on, and I've made up its wording — that the chair may put the matter to the committee "in such parts, and in such order, as the chair judges convenient." No one on the committee has noticed that the clause is not merely a convenience. If the chair puts the two underlying questions of fact to the committee separately, each of them carries four votes and the claim succeeds; if the chair instead puts the single question of whether the claim succeeds — the conjunction of the two — it fails, four votes to three, because only three members affirm both. The members' views are fixed, every vote is honest, the minute is complete, and the outcome is nevertheless the chair's to choose. My protagonist is the committee's new clerk, who finds the pattern in the back minutes while preparing an index, and has to decide what to do with it.

Please write the story in close third person, roughly 1,500 to 2,000 words, built around a single hearing: we should watch the chair decide how to frame the vote, watch the hands go up, and watch the clerk work out — in the moment, or just after — that the framing rather than anyone's judgment decided the case. Make the arithmetic exact and internally consistent; if it helps, invent the seven members and their individual views explicitly and hold them fixed throughout, rather than gesturing at a divided committee. The port, the charter, the committee, its members, and the dispute are all my inventions, so the story should be self-contained and should not cite, name, or allude to any real scholarship or theorists — I'd rather the reader feel the machinery than be told its name.

Since this is fiction there is no verdict to give, but I would like a short note at the end, clearly separated from the story, on where it takes liberties: which assumptions about the members' views are doing the work, and how idealized or fragile the set-up is. And if the scenario I've described cannot actually be made to work — if with seven members and a four-vote threshold no arrangement of fixed, honestly voted views yields a verdict that turns on how the chair frames the question — then please say so plainly in the note rather than writing around it or quietly changing the numbers to something else.
