I am preparing a seminar on the physics of information and would like help with a passage from Maxwell's Theory of Heat that I have never been able to read with confidence. Here it is:

"... if we conceive of a being whose faculties are so sharpened that he can follow every molecule in its course, such a being, whose attributes are as essentially finite as our own, would be able to do what is impossible to us. For we have seen that molecules in a vessel full of air at uniform temperature are moving with velocities by no means uniform, though the mean velocity of any great number of them, arbitrarily selected, is almost exactly uniform. Now let us suppose that such a vessel is divided into two portions, A and B, by a division in which there is a small hole, and that a being, who can see the individual molecules, opens and closes this hole, so as to allow only the swifter molecules to pass from A to B, and only the slower molecules to pass from B to A. He will thus, without expenditure of work, raise the temperature of B and lower that of A, in contradiction to the second law of thermodynamics."

The clause I cannot get past is "whose attributes are as essentially finite as our own." Maxwell insists on the being's finiteness, yet he also has it do what is "impossible to us" and presents the result as a "contradiction" to the second law. If the being is finite, it would seem to be subject to the same physics we are, and the contradiction should be merely apparent; if it is not so subject, then its finiteness is doing no work. I would like an interpretation that takes the finiteness clause seriously rather than treating it as a throwaway, and that says precisely what kind of necessity Maxwell is attributing to the second law here — whether he means the law is false as a universal statement, true only as a statistical regularity for beings with our cognitive limitations, or something else again. I am less interested in the later information-theoretic resolutions than in what Maxwell himself is claiming, though I would like to know whether the finiteness clause anticipates or complicates those later arguments. Please stay close to the text, and be explicit about which parts of your answer are interpretation and which are reconstruction.
