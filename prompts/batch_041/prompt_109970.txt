I'm a fourth-year in a philosophy of biology program, midway through a chapter on the units of selection debate, and I've stalled on the case I wanted to build the thing around. The case is the 2010-2011 eusociality fight: the Nature paper where Nowak, Tarnita and Wilson argued that kin selection had been oversold as the explanation for eusociality -- haplodiploidy doesn't line up the way the textbook story says, the relatedness asymmetries in the hymenoptera aren't carrying the explanatory load, and you get further by treating the colony itself as the thing selection is sorting -- followed the next year by the 137-author rebuttal Nature ran against it, Wilson's 2012 book, and Dawkins' extremely irritated review of that book in Prospect. I don't need any of this summarised. I've read the paper, I've read the rebuttal, I've read the blog aftermath, I know who was annoyed at whom and I know the backstory with Wilson drifting away from Hamilton over thirty years. What I can't do is turn the episode into an analysis instead of a chronology, and that's the thing I'm asking for.

So: run the episode through the machinery we actually use for this debate. Gene's-eye and inclusive fitness on one side, multilevel and group selection on the other, the replicator / interactor / vehicle vocabulary, and the Price-style covariance decomposition that both sides keep appealing to as if it settles something. Concretely, on each side, what is supposed to be the unit of selection, and what is the thing whose heritable variation in fitness is being tracked -- the gene, the individual, the colony, the queen's reproductive monopoly? What counts as evidence for or against the claim, and what observation could have settled it? As I read it, the rebuttal's central move is that inclusive fitness isn't a rival theory of eusociality at all but a fully general bookkeeping scheme: every multilevel model can be rewritten in inclusive-fitness terms and every inclusive-fitness model rewritten as multilevel, so the claim that kin selection can't explain eusociality is a category error rather than a finding. But if that's right, the obvious comeback is that a scheme which can't be wrong isn't an empirical theory either, which is more or less what Nowak and co. were complaining about in the first place. I'd like you to take that exchange seriously and say where the two sides are disagreeing about the world and where they're just disagreeing about how to carve up the same algebra.

Then the part I actually care about: what does the episode show about the units-of-selection framework itself? A confirmation, an anomaly, or evidence that the question of what the unit of selection is is a translation question wearing empirical clothes? Be honest about where the framework goes silent rather than making it speak -- I'd rather get "this question is under-specified, here's why" than a tidy verdict. And since I have to say something about it in the chapter: is this round just the 1970s sociobiology fight with different names on the letters, or did the technical content actually change the shape of the dispute? Don't bother with citations or a reading list, I know the literature -- I want the arguement.
