I run the historical claims unit at a national human rights commission — three researchers, me, and a legal officer we borrow from the justice ministry two days a week — and most of our work is turning the petitions that arrive about historical wrongs into a recommendation the commissioners can actually vote on: acknowledge and apologize, acknowledge without apologizing, decline, or hand the matter to another body. We get thirty to fifty a year. The documentation is wildly uneven, the petitioners are usually elderly, and a badly drafted statement can be read by them as an apology and by everyone else as a refusal; our chair has said more than once that we are not going to be the office that produces another paragraph in the passive voice in which a wrong is done by nobody in particular. What I don't have is a framework. My training is in law and public administration, and what I know about apologies I know from having read a great many of them.

Please write me a decision procedure my unit can run on a petition in about ninety minutes, grounded in whatever the philosophy of language and moral philosophy have actually worked out about what an apology is and when it succeeds — the idea that an apology is a kind of act rather than a report, with conditions that have to be met for it to come off at all; the difference between apologizing and expressing regret, sympathy, or an excuse; the problem of standing, that is, whether we can apologize for something done by people who are dead and by an administration that no longer exists, and whether the relevant we is the state as a continuing institution or the present citizenry; what collective blame and collective apology even amount to when no living person did the deed; whether an apology with nothing material attached is hollow, and conversely whether attaching money converts an apology into a settlement; the awkward case where victims and perpetrators are all dead, so that the real addressees are descendants and the general public rather than the person harmed; and the worry that an office like ours can inflate the currency by apologizing too often. Turn that into questions to be asked in order, with what counts as a sufficient answer to each, the points where the procedure stops with a no, and the points where it has to hand the decision to a person because what remains is a question of values or of law. Then run it on three items from the docket. First, a petition from an association of descendants of people sterilized under a eugenics statute that stood on the books from the 1930s into the 1970s and that our own state administered; the last identified survivor died three years ago; the association wants the state to say plainly that the program was a wrong and explicitly does not want compensation, on the grounds that money would let us call the matter settled, while our legal officer warns that any acceptance of blame creates exposure in litigation that is already pending. Second, a petition about a massacre in the 1890s carried out by a chartered company our state licensed and later nationalized, on territory that was at the time under another empire; the descendants' group is split, half wanting an apology from us and half saying we are the wrong addressee and that our apologizing would let the imperial successor off the hook. Third, a wrong for which the state already issued a formal apology in the 1990s, which the petitioners now say was inadequate and want superseded. For at least one of these I expect the procedure to give an answer I won't like; I would rather find out now than in a press conference.

Don't cite anything — I won't chase sources and our legal officer won't read them. Don't build the whole thing on one contested theory of apology either: where the expressive view and the reparative view would diverge, show me both branches and tell me which one our commission is implicitly committing itself to when it takes one. Say plainly where the procedure will misfire and which kinds of petition it simply cannot handle, and note which steps are judgments dressed up as procedure so that I can stop pretending they are mechanical. Close by telling me how confident you are in the claims about apology that the procedure rests on and which single step you are least sure about.
