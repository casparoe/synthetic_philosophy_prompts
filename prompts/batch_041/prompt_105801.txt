I'm writing the take-home paper for a final-year course on constitutional monarchy, and I've decided the essay will be set in a fictional jurisdiction so that no student can look up the answer. I want one question — ninety minutes, twenty marks — whose difficulty comes from a crux rather than from coverage. The students will have the standard material at their fingertips: the dignified/efficient distinction, the claim that conventions are not enforced by courts, the classic conditions under which a head of state may refuse a dissolution, and the asymmetry between refusing assent, refusing a dissolution, and dismissing a government. The question has to be one that none of that answers by itself.

The jurisdiction I've invented is the Kingdom of Serrenia, a unitary parliamentary monarchy with a short entrenched constitution and a thick layer of convention. Two articles matter.

Article 12: "Every act of the Sovereign in matters of state shall be countersigned by the Prime Minister or by another Minister, who shall thereby assume responsibility for it."

Article 13: "The Sovereign shall appoint as Prime Minister the member of the Assembly who is able to command the confidence of a majority of its members."

Everything else is convention. Two conventions are uncontested: the Sovereign does not refuse assent to a bill that has passed both chambers, and a Prime Minister who has lost the confidence of the Assembly either resigns or obtains a dissolution.

The fact pattern is this. After a general election the Prime Minister's party holds 250 of 500 seats. The Assembly passes a motion of no confidence in her, 300–250. She declines to resign and advises the Sovereign to dissolve, on the ground that the opposition's alternative arrangement — 251 seats, twelve of them from a party that has promised support on confidence and budget votes only — is too fragile to constitute a government. The leader of the opposition has written to the Sovereign asserting that she commands the confidence of a majority within the meaning of Article 13 and asking to be appointed. The Sovereign's private secretary has advised that Article 12 leaves no discretion: whatever the Sovereign does must be countersigned, and the only minister available to countersign is the incumbent Prime Minister, who will countersign a dissolution and nothing else. This holds for the appointment of a new Prime Minister as much as for a dismissal, since that too is an act in matters of state — so the one person who can authorise any resolution of the crisis is the person the crisis is about. Nobody in Serrenia has ever resolved this.

Please write three things. First, the essay question itself, framed so that students must take a position rather than survey the options. Second, a model answer of about 600–700 words, self-contained: I don't want it resting on real-world precedents the students haven't been given, so it should argue from the provisions and conventions as stated. Third, a marking scheme worth 20 marks, built from five must-hit points, with the marks for each and a one-line note on the mistake that typically costs a student that point. The question and the scheme should reward reasoning about the relation between legal rules and conventions — in particular about what codifying one convention (Article 13) does to the uncodified conventions around it, and about whether a countersignature requirement can survive the one act it was never designed for. Mark moves, not names.

In the model answer I want a committed line, not a menu: resolve the countersignature puzzle one way and defend it against the obvious alternative, say what the Sovereign should do and what follows if she does it, and be explicit about what would have to change in the drafting to make the opposite answer correct. If you think some residual indeterminacy cannot be drafted away, name precisely where it sits and why.
