Read Ursula K. Le Guin's short story The Ones Who Walk Away from Omelas through the lens of Harsanyi's aggregation theorem, and tell me what the story shows, dramatizes, and complicates about the theorem, and where the theorem fails to capture what the story knows. I want this for a graduate seminar on distributive justice, so it needs to be faithful in both directions: neither the story reduced to a fable that illustrates the arithmetic, nor the theorem bent to fit the story's mood.

Treat the following background as given, and do not ask for citations or sources.

The story, published in 1973 and subtitled as variations on a theme by William James, answers a passage in James's 1891 essay The Moral Philosopher and the Moral Life. There James invites us to imagine a world in which countless millions are kept permanently happy on the single condition that one lost soul on the far edge of things leads a life of lonely torture, and he reports that most of us would find such a world hideous at that price. Le Guin makes the bargain concrete: a city of joy whose entire good rests on one child kept in filth, darkness, and misery, and whose citizens are told the terms and mostly accept them, while a few quietly walk away.

Harsanyi's aggregation theorem, in the form I want you to use, runs roughly as follows. If a society's preferences satisfy the standard expected-utility axioms and respect the Pareto principle, then the society's welfare must be a weighted sum of the individuals' utilities. Harsanyi motivates the weights with a veil-of-ignorance argument: a chooser who does not know which member of the society they will be, and who assigns an equal probability to being any one of them, should maximize expected utility. On this reasoning the social ranking comes out as a form of average utilitarianism, and Harsanyi used it to reject Rawlsian maximin as an irrational kind of extreme risk-aversion.

With that in hand, I want four things.

First, settle whether the theorem, correctly applied, licenses Omelas or condemns it. Note the arithmetic by which a large population makes the chance of being the child vanishingly small, so that an expected-utility maximizer behind the veil may well choose the bargain; then say whether that is the theorem's real verdict or a misapplication of it, and whether the equiprobability assumption is doing illegitimate work by converting a person into a lottery ticket whose bad outcome can be diluted by numbers.

Second, say where the story genuinely illuminates the theorem: which features of Omelas make the veil, the weighting, and the aggregation vivid in a way that a formal statement does not, and what the story makes you notice about the difference between choosing an arrangement and merely living inside one.

Third, say where the story resists the reading, and be honest that it does. Focus on the ones who walk away. Are they simply irrational, violating the expected-utility axiom, or do they register something the weighted-sum framework cannot represent: a refusal to be complicit, a deontic constraint that blocks aggregation, or a claim that the child's suffering is not commensurable with the citizens' pleasures and so cannot be summed at all? Consider also whether it matters that the good is constitutively dependent on the suffering rather than merely statistically correlated with it, and whether the story trades on the reader's inability to hold a large number of small pleasures and one enormous pain on a single scale.

Fourth, address the Jamesian frame. James treats the repugnance as a datum, a fact about our moral psychology that any theory must accommodate; Harsanyi's theorem seems to treat it as an intuition to be overridden by rational aggregation. Does Le Guin side with James, does she leave the question open, or does she show that the two are not as far apart as they look?

End by saying what the story adds to the debate over aggregation that the theorem and its critics do not, and name the single scene or image you would put in front of the seminar to make that contribution land.
