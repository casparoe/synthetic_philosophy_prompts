I've been running a small reading group on the nineteenth-century quarrel between W. K. Clifford and William James over the ethics of belief — the one in which Clifford maintains that holding a belief on insufficient evidence is a wrong committed always and everywhere, and James replies that for certain options, namely those that are forced, live and momentous, our emotional and volitional nature may legitimately settle the matter where the evidence cannot. We have already been through the standard illustrations (Clifford's shipowner, who talks himself into thinking his vessel seaworthy and collects the insurance when it goes down) and I should like something lighter with which to close the final session.

Would you write me a joke on this material? I should prefer a two- or three-hander to a one-liner, and it ought to be genuinely funny to someone who knows the texts — that is, the humour should turn on the actual machinery of the two positions rather than on the bare fact that philosophers disagree. So that it does not become the same Clifford-versus-James gag everyone reaches for, please set it in a scenario of your own invention; I have had the notion of a municipal Bureau of Evidential Affairs, a petty bureaucracy that issues licences for belief and audits the credences of citizens, staffed by a Jamesian clerk and a Cliffordian inspector who fall out over a single applicant. The punchline should hang on a real feature of the theories — the criteria an option must satisfy before it qualifies for James's dispensation, or the way Clifford's rule appears to indict the very belief that Clifford's rule is true — and not merely on the words evidence and faith. A short note afterwards, explaining what makes the joke work for a reader who knows the debate, would be welcome, though the joke itself should stand without it.
