I am a novelist at work on a book about two researchers who share a lab and disagree — bitterly, politely, and for years — about what they are looking at. The object in question is a small transformer, a few million parameters, trained to do nothing but predict the next move in a corpus of chess games recorded in algebraic notation. It has never seen a board, a diagram, or a sentence of natural language. One of my characters holds that its internal states represent the board; the other holds that they merely encode the move history in whatever form makes prediction easy, and that calling that representation is a compliment the evidence does not earn.

I need a number I can defend on the page, and I need to see the argument that produces it. Let me fix the proposition so that it can be true or false. Say that an internal direction is load-bearing for the board state just in case all four of the following hold: (i) a linear probe fit to the network's activations decodes board state well above chance; (ii) intervening on the activations along that probe direction changes the predicted next move in the way a genuine representation of the board would predict, rather than in the way any perturbation of comparable magnitude would; (iii) the same direction, up to the linear symmetries of the architecture, is recovered in independent retrainings from different random seeds on the same corpus; and (iv) a probe of matched capacity, fit to a randomly initialized network of identical architecture, yields a direction that, transplanted into the trained network, is not causally efficacious. The proposition is: this network contains at least one such direction.

Give me your credence in that proposition, decomposed. I want the sub-questions it rests on — whether the probe decodes anything at all; whether the decoding survives the causal test or turns out to read a correlate the network computes by some other route; whether the direction is a fact about the learned solution or a seed-specific accident; whether the control in (iv) is well posed or nearly vacuous; and the bridge premise, namely the probability that passing all four is sufficient for representation in the philosopher's sense rather than merely for something weaker and easier. Give each a number and a line of justification, say how the terms combine, and identify the one the final figure is most sensitive to, along with the experiment or argument that would move it furthest.

One last thing. One character puts the proposition at 0.85; the other puts it at 0.15. Tell me where their disagreement most plausibly lives — in which term, and whether it is an empirical quarrel or a philosophical one.
