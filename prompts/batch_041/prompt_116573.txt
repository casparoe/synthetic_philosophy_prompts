Below is a draft essay of mine. I would like detailed comments on it, in the form of a referee report: an assessment of whether the argument works, where it goes wrong, and what would have to change for it to succeed. Please do not spend space summarizing the essay back to me or praising it; I am interested in objections and repairs.

---

The question I want to settle is whether the commensurability of pleasure and pain is compatible with the lexical priority of severe pain. I think it is, and that the appearance of incompatibility comes from running two different comparisons together.

Start with two intuitions that most people share. First, mild experiences of the two kinds do trade off: a headache is worth enduring for an afternoon of genuine enjoyment, and we can say roughly how much enjoyment it takes to make the trade worth making. Second, severe experiences do not trade off: no quantity of mild enjoyment justifies a day of torture, and this is not because the enjoyment is small but because it is the wrong sort of thing to be weighed against torture at all.

A simple additive axiology captures the first intuition and violates the second. Enough headaches weighed against enough enjoyments will eventually, at some finite exchange rate, outweigh any fixed quantity of torture. So the additive view is out. The usual repair is to make negative value lexically prior: any outcome containing more pain is worse, whatever the pleasure. That captures the second intuition and, as usually stated, discards the first, since on a strictly lexical view a single mild headache is worse than any amount of mild enjoyment. That is absurd.

I propose a hybrid that keeps both. Divide the negative scale at a threshold. Pain below the threshold belongs to the moderate band; pain at or above it belongs to the severe band. The value of an outcome is then computed in two stages. First, moderate-band pains and all pleasures are summed on a single commensurable scale, so that headaches and enjoyments trade off at a fixed rate. Second, severe-band pain is compared lexicographically: an outcome containing more severe-band pain is worse than one containing less, no matter what the moderate band says. Where the severe-band components are equal, the moderate-band sum decides. I will call this threshold-limited negative priority.

Note what this does and does not deny. It denies that pleasure can compensate severe pain. It does not deny that pleasure and severe pain are commensurable in the sense of both being magnitudes of the same general kind, intensities on a common experiential scale. My claim is that the commensurability of experiences is one thing and the comparability of the outcomes containing them is another. Two quantities can be measured on the same scale, so that it makes sense to ask how many mild pleasures equal one severe pain in intensity, while the outcomes containing them are still ranked by a rule that never consults that ratio. The ratio exists; the ranking ignores it. If that is right, then the dispute between those who say the two are commensurable and those who say severe pain is lexically prior is a dispute about which question is being asked, not about the structure of experience.

Three objections.

First, the threshold is arbitrary. Any particular placement will seem stipulative, and if the threshold is vague then the ranking is vague, and vague lexicographic rankings threaten transitivity: if A sits just below the threshold and B just above, A and B are ranked as if on the same scale, while B and a slightly milder C are ranked lexicographically, and transitivity can fail at the edges. My reply is that vagueness in a threshold is not the same as arbitrariness in the principle, and that a vague ranking is still a ranking: the relation is determinate where the cases are clear and indeterminate where they are not, and any transitivity failure occurs only among the indeterminate pairs, which is a general feature of vague predicates rather than a special defect of this view.

Second, the view is non-Archimedean, and some will say that this alone disqualifies it. The Archimedean axiom says that any positive quantity, however small, can eventually outweigh any other quantity if repeated enough times. My view denies this across the threshold. But the Archimedean axiom is precisely what is in dispute: it is a substantive claim about how values combine, not a precondition for talking about them, and the second intuition above is a denial of it in a particular case. To invoke the axiom here would be to assume the additive view in order to refute the non-additive one.

Third, and most troubling to me: the view makes severe pain non-compensable, and non-compensable goods and bads are notoriously able to license monstrous conclusions. If severe pain is lexically prior, then avoiding one severe pain can justify imposing any number of moderate pains, and a policy producing a few severe pains is to be rejected even if it prevents an enormous number of moderate ones. I accept this consequence. It is the price of the second intuition, and any view that respects that intuition will pay it. What I want to know is whether the price is higher than I think.

---

I would like comments of the following kinds, in roughly this order of importance.

1. The central move is the paragraph distinguishing commensurability of experiences from comparability of outcomes. Does it actually hold, or have I equivocated on commensurable by sliding between a claim about measurement and a claim about ranking? If the move fails, say exactly where.

2. Is threshold-limited negative priority genuinely a distinct position, or does it collapse into ordinary lexical negative priority once the moderate band is allowed to be summed? Consider cases where the moderate band contains both pains and pleasures and the severe bands are equal.

3. Does my reply to the vagueness objection work? I claim transitivity failures are confined to indeterminate pairs, which I treat as benign. Is that treatment defensible, or does it simply relocate the problem?

4. Is the third objection fatal rather than merely costly? If you think it is fatal, tell me what the best retreat is that preserves the second intuition.

5. Finally, identify the single weakest step in the essay and say what would have to be added or revised to repair it. If you think the essay needs a distinction it does not draw, name the distinction and show where it does its work.
