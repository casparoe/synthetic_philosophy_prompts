I'll give you the setup and then what I actually want. I retired two years ago after thirty-odd years as a hydrologist with a state water agency, and I've spent a good chunk of that retirement reading political philosophy — mostly the stuff about who gets to decide things, which I wish somebody had handed me at forty instead of sixty-eight. Through a chain of events I won't bore you with, I now chair a volunteer committee in the little town where we retired; we're called the Water Futures Committee, which sounds grander than it is. Here's our mess. Our valley sits over a shallow aquifer that a big farming operation has been pumping hard, and the state has told us that before it will impose a cap — something like a forty percent cut in permitted withdrawals — the town has to hold a referendum. Under state law the clerk runs it and only registered voters of the town may vote, full stop. But the people whose wells actually go dry are mostly not in our town: there's a city about fourteen miles downstream that draws from the same formation, plus a couple of unincorporated hamlets, and they'd outnumber us three to one. Meanwhile a fair number of our own residents live up on the ridge and pull from a different, deeper source entirely, so a cap costs them nothing. And the farm itself is a corporation that owns a few thousand acres in the township, pays property tax, employs about sixty seasonal workers who live in trailers for part of the year, and has no residents at all. In my reading I keep running into two principles for who belongs in a decision like this, and they come apart right here in front of me: one says everyone whose interests are affected should have a say, the other says it's everyone subject to the authority making the decision — subject to the rule, bound by it, made to obey it — regardless of whether they come out worse. I've also noticed, and this is the part that keeps me up, that in our case the choice between them basically decides the referendum: include downstream and the cap passes, exclude them and it fails. So the rule for drawing the boundary is doing the work the vote is supposed to do.

What I want from you is to turn whichever of those principles (or some defensible combination) into a procedure my committee can actually run — not an essay about the problem, a procedure. We meet ninety minutes a week, nobody on the committee has read any of this except me and I'm an amateur, and we have to hand the town council a recommendation in eight weeks that the clerk can implement without a law degree. So: the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, which steps are mechanical (something a clerk can check against a record) and which are irreducibly judgment calls, where the procedure should stop with a flat no, and where it has to hand the question to an elected body because it's a question of values and not of fact. Then run it on three of our cases and show your work: (a) the downstream city, whose residents are affected but not governed by us; (b) our own ridge residents, who are governed by us but plainly unaffected; (c) the farming corporation, which is governed, taxed, and employs people here but has no human residents to register. At least one of the three should come out in a way my committee won't enjoy hearing, and I'd rather you tell me which one that is than be diplomatic. Finally, be straight with me about the circularity I described — the fact that the eligibility rule flips the result — because if there's no way out of it I need to know that before I stand up in front of the council and pretend otherwise.
