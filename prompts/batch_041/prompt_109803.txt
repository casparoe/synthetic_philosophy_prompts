I'm prepping a long-form interview for a small history-of-philosophy podcast, and the guest is a scholar who works on Schrödinger's later philosophy — the stretch where he keeps insisting that consciousness is numerically one and that the plurality of minds is the illusion, the Upanishad-flavored monism he pushed in his later lectures and essays. She knows the material cold, so I don't want questions that let her just do the "great physicist discovers Eastern wisdom, how lovely" routine; I want the ones that actually put the position under pressure. Give me exactly five questions, numbered, one or two sentences each, no intro and no wrap-up at the end, and wherever she has an obvious answer ready, fold in the follow-up that makes that answer harder to use.

1. The way I read him, the real puzzle is arithmetical rather than mystical: physics can only describe the world as an object, so the knower can never appear inside the description, and yet there seem to be billions of knowers. Ask her whether the oneness claim is supposed to solve that or dissolve it — and if she says dissolve, ask her what then distinguishes your experience from mine other than a change of subject.

2. He also held that the wave function is a catalogue of our expectations rather than a physical thing in its own right. Ask whether that epistemic reading of the formalism is independent of the monism, or whether the monism is quietly doing work in it — and if she says independent, ask what would actually change in his physics if he'd dropped the Vedanta entirely.

3. He came to the Upanishads through Schopenhauer and through German romanticism more broadly, which makes me suspicious that what he calls Vedanta is a nineteenth-century reconstruction. Ask how much of his Advaita is really Schopenhauer's, and whether he handles the machinery of maya and the two levels of truth or just flattens the whole thing into the world being an illusion — then, if she concedes he flattens it, ask an illusion for whom, since the appearance has to appear to something.

4. He says consciousness can't be accounted for in physical terms and is absolutely fundamental, and he also says it's one. Ask whether those are one thesis or two, and where he actually sits between panpsychism, idealism and some kind of neutral monism, since the fundamentality claim is much weaker than the oneness claim and the physics doesn't obviously need the stronger one.

5. Science is a social practice — lots of inquirers checking each other, disagreeing, replicating — and if the plurality of minds is mere appearance I can't see what grounds that practice. Ask her that, then push on the ethical version of it: does the oneness underwrite compassion for particular creatures, or does it license a quietism in which their suffering is finally unreal, and does he have any principled way of taking the first without sliding into the second?
