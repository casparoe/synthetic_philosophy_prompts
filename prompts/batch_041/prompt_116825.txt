I have been trying to settle what I actually think about divine hiddenness by working through cases and trusting my reactions rather than any theory, and I would like help turning the reactions into a principle. Take the God in question to be the classical one: all-powerful, all-knowing, perfectly loving, and desirous of a relationship with every person. I am agnostic, and what I want to know is whether the pattern of unbelief in the world is evidence against that God, and if so, exactly what it is evidence against. Here is what I find myself thinking, case by case.

1. A former roommate of mine was raised by cheerful secular parents, has never once seriously considered whether God exists, and lives decently and contentedly. Her unbelief does not strike me as evidence against theism at all. She was never in the running.

2. A close friend prayed, read, and sought for a decade, changed her life in the ways her tradition asked, and never had anything she could honestly call an answer. She now says she cannot believe and is grieved by it. This strikes me as a serious problem for theism, the strongest item on my list.

3. A colleague lost his faith when his daughter died. He is angry, says he wants nothing to do with God even if there is one, and has stopped looking. My instinct is to say his unbelief is not evidence, because it is resistant rather than honest inquiry. But his resistance is itself a response to God's silence, and it seems circular to use the silence to explain why the silence is not a problem.

4. A devout Muslim acquaintance has what I can only describe as a rich and lifelong sense of the divine. His case does not trouble me at all as an argument against theism. But if that experience counts as contact with God, then God appears content with contact that leaves a person with false beliefs about him; if it does not count, I have to give up my verdict on case 4.

5. Someone I know spent a year seeking in earnest, felt nothing, and has since settled into a calm, entirely untroubled unbelief. Oddly, this troubles me more than case 2, and I cannot defend the asymmetry: the anguish in case 2 at least looks like something still reaching for contact, whereas here the absence seems complete.

6. A person raised in a community where disbelief is socially punished professes belief, secretly doubts, and never investigates. I count this as a mild problem rather than a serious one, but I would like to know why it is weaker than case 2.

7. A person who reports a vivid sense of God's presence, and believes on that basis, raises no problem for me. But if God can do that for her, the question of why he does not do it for my friend in case 2 becomes sharper rather than softer.

What I am after is the principle, or the smallest set of competing principles, that captures this pattern, stated precisely enough to generate verdicts on cases I have not considered, with the case that motivates each clause. My own hunch is that the thing that matters is not belief but contact, whether a person has been addressed by God. What I cannot settle is whether being addressed is something God does or something a person receives: if the first, then presumably everyone is addressed already, through conscience and the world, and hiddenness is never a problem; if the second, the whole issue turns on facts about a person's receptivity that look like luck. Please state the principle in whichever of these forms the cases actually support. Then tell me what it implies for a person who seeks and has an experience she attributes to nature; for someone who seeks only for consolation; for someone who never seeks but would have found God had she looked; and for someone who has an experience of God and later concludes it was a delusion. Finally, I want an honest statement of which of my seven verdicts the principle cannot accommodate, and whether the version that saves all of them is worth its complexity, or whether the simpler version that costs me a verdict leaves theism with a real evidential problem or dissolves it.
