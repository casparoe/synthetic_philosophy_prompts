I am at work on an essay about the family of puzzles that now travels under the name of rational irrationality: cases in which a person fares best, by the lights of their own aims, by being the sort of person who will later act against those aims. Because I write for readers with little patience for anachronism, I want to put one of these puzzles to a philosopher who died before the vocabulary for it existed, and I want a careful prediction rather than your own verdict. The philosopher is Henry Sidgwick.

Here is the case, which Derek Parfit called the hitchhiker. You are stranded in a desert, and you know that a single car will pass. The driver will stop and carry you to town only if he believes you will pay him a sum on arrival; if he thinks you will not pay, he drives on and leaves you where you are. You will never see him again, and no one else will ever learn what you did. Once you are standing in the town with your wallet intact, handing over the money is pure loss, since there is nothing further to gain by it. But this is precisely what a sharp driver will anticipate, so whether you get a lift turns on whether you are the kind of person who would pay a debt that self-interest, at the moment of payment, tells you to disown. I have a second case in reserve: a stranger offers you a large sum for undergoing a day of discomfort tomorrow, on the condition that you form the intention tonight, knowing all the while that when tomorrow comes you can keep the money and skip the discomfort.

Sidgwick built his ethics on what he called a dualism of practical reason: the principle of rational self-love and the principle of rational benevolence, the latter the utilitarian standard he himself held, each of which he took to be independently defensible, with neither derivable from the other. He found no argument that reconciled them, and he hinted that any reconciliation might have to be bought with a postulate about the world, since duty and interest coincide only if something like a benevolent ordering of things makes them coincide.

So how would Sidgwick have received these cases? Would he see the hitchhiker as a problem for rational self-love in particular, or for the dualism as a whole? Would he say the self-interest theory survives by splitting into two tiers, a criterion at the level of the dispositions one ought to have and a decision rule at the level of the acts one ought to perform, and would he regard that split as a repair of egoism or as its surrender? Would he accept the modern label of rational irrationality, or insist that the disposition to pay is not irrational at all and that the puzzle trades on a confusion between reasons for acting and reasons for being a certain kind of agent? And does the convergence of self-love and benevolence on the very same disposition, in cases of this shape, look to him like the secular reconciliation he could not find, or like a local coincidence that leaves the dualism exactly where he left it?

Where his settled commitments underdetermine the answer, say so plainly, and tell me which commitment would have to give way on each candidate reading. I want the reasoning laid out step by step; I do not need a reading list, and I would rather you not name works or page numbers you cannot vouch for.
