I've spent a while reading the empirical literature on why some countries are rich and others poor, and there's a feature of its method that I can't stop turning over. The most ambitious work proceeds by finding an accident of history or geography — the disease environment that determined where European colonizers could settle and survive, the reach of a parasite that made animal traction unworkable across much of a continent, the suitability of land for a particular plantation crop — and using that accident as an instrument for the quality of a country's institutions, where institutional quality is itself proxied by something like a commercial rating of the risk that contracts will be expropriated. Out of this comes a causal claim about institutions, pitched at the scale of the world's entire income distribution, resting on a few dozen countries observed once each.

I'd like you to reconstruct the philosophy this practice commits you to, the way you'd reconstruct the presuppositions of a legal doctrine. What theory of causation does it need? Presumably one on which causes are manipulations of well-defined variables, counterfactuals for a country are coherent objects, and history is a sequence of interventions that could in principle have been rerandomized — but tell me whether it needs anything that strong, or whether a thinner regularity view would do. What theory of institutions does it need, given that the thing being instrumented for is a single scalar index? What does it assume about the unit of analysis, given that it treats the country as a causally coherent entity rather than a bundle of path-dependent accidents? I'm especially interested in culture, the third candidate explanation: it's the one that fits the machinery least well, and I want to know whether that's a fact about culture or a fact about the machinery.

Then separate the commitments the practice strictly needs from the ones that are merely convenient, and say where it is underdetermined — where two rival metaphysics would license the same regressions and the same coefficients. Finally, suppose we replace the presupposed picture with one on which historical causation is conjunctural, equifinal, and not decomposable into separable variables. What does the honest version of this research program look like then? If the answer is that the question cannot be answered causally at all, I would rather hear that stated plainly, along with what the field should be doing instead, than receive a list of the usual caveats.
