Ok so I'm writing a piece off the back of the Schiavo case — you know the one, Florida, 1990, she collapses in the hallway of her apartment in her twenties, cardiac arrest, oxygen starvation, ends up in what they were calling a persistent vegetative state, no written directive anywhere, and then seven years of courts and politicans fighting over a feeding tube because the only real evidence of what she would have wanted was a few things her husband said she'd told him years before. Tube came out for good in 2005, she died about two weeks later, her parents never accepted it.

What I want is to use that mess to stress-test the doctrine sitting under every living will in the country — the one that says your earlier, competent self has authority over you later, incapacitated self. Precedent autonomy: the earlier self governing the later one from beyond capacity. I know the textbook version, skip it. What I want is the actual mapping. Who is the earlier self here, and who is the later self? If the diagnosis is right, is there even a later self with interests to be ruled over, or is the doctrine quietly pretending a person is still in the room because otherwise it has no subject matter? And if there's no now-self, what was the court actually doing — adjudicating her wishes, or deciding who owns the body and the relationship, with deference to her as the costume?

Run it both directions. First, what precedent autonomy says each actor should have done — the husband, the parents, the judge, the governor, the congressmen who came back for the signing. Then which parts of the episode the doctrine explains cleanly, which it misdescribes, and where it has nothing to say. Then the return trip: what does Schiavo show about the doctrine itself? Confirmation, anomaly, or a case it never anticipated — one where the real action isn't autonomy at all but the evidentiary bar the courts were actually applying, given that a straight best-interests standard would have left the tube in? Don't force the theory to speak where it goes quiet; say so.

Bottom line up front, under 700 words.
