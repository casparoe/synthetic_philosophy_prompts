For thirty-five years I earned my living as a taxonomist of weevils — most of it in a museum basement with a drawer of pinned specimens, a hand lens, and a card index — and I have spent the four years since my retirement reading philosophy of biology in earnest. I am asking you to settle something I never could, and I would rather have your settled judgment than an even-handed survey that leaves the choice to me.

Here is the case I have in mind, drawn from a genus I worked on for most of my career. It comprises nine named forms on a chain of five islands and one adjacent mainland peninsula. Two of the named forms differ only in the shape of the aedeagus and in average body length, and both characters vary clinally along the chain, so that on the middle island the two intergrade completely. Molecular work — mitochondrial sequences and a handful of nuclear loci — recovers seven deeply divergent lineages; four of these are morphologically indistinguishable from one another, and two of those four occur together on the same island without interbreeding. Two of the named forms turn out not to be each other's closest relatives. One island population is an all-female parthenogenetic offshoot nested genetically inside a mainland sexual lineage, morphologically distinct, and confined to a different host plant. And where the ranges of the two northern forms meet, they hybridize across a narrow zone that has looked stable for as long as anyone has collected there.

Please do three things, in this order.

First, say briefly how each of the following would delimit species in this complex: the biological species concept (Mayr's reproductive criterion); the ecological species concept (Van Valen); the diagnosable phylogenetic species concept (Cracraft); the cohesion species concept (Templeton); the evolutionary species concept (Simpson and Wiley); the general lineage concept (de Queiroz); and the plain morphological species concept I was trained on.

Second, sort all of these into tiers by a single criterion, which I have come to call the fifty-year test: when a graduate student in 2075 with vastly better genomic data reopens my drawers, which of these delimitations will she find defensible, and which will she conclude I had merely drawn a line where my instruments gave out? Give me tiers rather than a spectrum, and place each concept where you think it belongs.

Third, commit. Tell me which single concept — or which explicitly stated policy for combining them — I should adopt as my working notion of species for this group, and if that requires conceding that several of my named forms are not species, say so plainly. If you think the question is malformed in the way that pluralists and eliminativists about species claim, tell me so, but only after you have done the exercise as asked. Where you are genuinely torn, say where and how much, rather than falling back on "it depends."
