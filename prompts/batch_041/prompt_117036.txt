I'm a third-year PhD student in computational social science, and my thesis has drifted into cliodynamics — the Turchin-style structural-demographic program, the secular-cycle stuff. I went in as a skeptic, honestly, half hoping to find the whole thing was just curve-fitting dressed up in differential equations, and I've come out the other side as a believer, which is making my comittee a little nervous. The version of the view I hold, roughly: long-run political history in agrarian and early-modern states really does come in rough cycles on the order of a couple centuries, and the engine of those cycles is elite dynamics. The elite population grows faster than the surplus that can support it, so you get elite overproduction — too many aspirants chasing a fixed number of elite positions — which produces intense competition, factional infighting, and eventually a pool of frustrated counter-elites who can mobilize an immiserated commoner population; add a state whose fiscal obligations outrun its revenue, and you get the whole package: unrest, civil war, sometimes collapse and reset. In the thesis I've been formalizing this as basicly a predator-prey system, elites as the predator and the peasant surplus as the prey, and the fits are honestly beautiful on the handful of cases I've tried.

What I want from you is the opposite of a pep talk. I need a map of what would count against this, and specifically which pieces are load-bearing, sorted by how much each defeater should actually move me — from "this sinks the whole thing" down to "annoying but survivable." A few things I'd especially like you to get into: (a) what would have to show up in the historical record for the elite-overproduction mechanism specifically to be wrong, as opposed to the weaker claim that history just comes in rough cycles, which I care about much less; (b) what my predator-prey formalization is actually adding, and what would reveal it as a flexible curve-fitting exercise — whether the same series would fit some boring null model or a regime-switching process just as well, whether the parameters are even identifiable from the data I have, whether my detrending is manufacturing the cycles it then "finds"; (c) what the people working inside this tradition would themselves concede counts against them, as distinct from what historians who dislike the whole project would say — I don't want a list of criticisms from people who wouldn't have accepted the framework on any evidence; and (d) whether the theory has quietly been qualified into unfalsifiability, since every case that fails to cycle seems to get an external shock or an "immature cycle" to explain it away, and if it has, whether that's fatal or just the normal way a social-science framework matures.

For what it's worth, the thing that would personally move me most is a case where elite overproduction was measured independently of the outcome — so no circularity, no reading instability backwards into the elite data — and the society still sailed through without serious trouble. I'm also privately worried that "elite" is doing too much work, that it gets defined partly by the theory itself and so the mechanism can't really fail, and I'd like you to tell me whether that worry is well founded or just a philosopher's tic. Don't just catalogue objections, though — tell me which ones a defender should genuinely lose sleep over and which ones I can shrug off. End with a one-line verdict on how exposed the position really is, and finish with a last line exactly in this form: Verdict: <robust | exposed | badly exposed | unfalsifiable-as-stated> — <one short sentence>.
