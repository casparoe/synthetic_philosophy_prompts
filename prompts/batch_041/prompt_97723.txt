I'm a third-year undergraduate in a seminar on cultural psychology and the philosophy of social science, and I want help with a debate that our class — and, as far as I can tell, the literature — keeps running without ever resolving: whether national character is a coherent notion, and whether it is ever legitimate to generalize about peoples.

The impasse has a recognizable shape. One camp treats the question as empirical and largely settled: cross-cultural research reports stable between-country differences on dimensions such as individualism versus collectivism, or tightness versus looseness, so of course populations differ in their typical dispositions. A second camp treats national character as a discredited essentialist construct — a residue of the mid-century national-character tradition — and argues that the observed differences are either too coarse, too unstable, or too confounded by institutions and ecology to deserve the name. A third camp concedes that differences exist but insists the live question is normative: when, if ever, may one generalize about a people, given the harm that stereotyping does. Nobody's arguments seem to touch anybody else's, and after a term of this I suspect the participants are not actually disagreeing about the same thing.

My hypothesis, which I would like you to test rather than assume, is that at least three distinct questions are fused under the single label national character: a conceptual question about what the construct could even mean, an empirical question about whether the posited regularities hold, and a normative question about the permissibility of generalizing. If that is right, the debate is stalled because each camp answers a different one of these and treats its answer as settling the others. A classmate has added a distinction I find promising: between character in the robust sense of an internalized disposition that causes behavior and character as a mere aggregate regularity in a distribution — suggesting that much of the disagreement evaporates once the two are separated.

Please give me a diagnosis, not a survey. Commit to a verdict: is this a substantive disagreement, a verbal one about the word character, a set of questions conflated under one heading, or something else — and which of those, if any, is doing the real work? Ground the diagnosis in what each side actually maintains, tell me whether the classmate's distinction helps or merely relocates the problem, and say what would count as progress: a conceptual clarification, a decisive empirical result, a norm for permissible generalization, or an argument that no progress should be expected. Take a definite position and defend it; do not lay out the options and leave the choice to me.
