In a reading group on combinatorial games, someone closed off a question about a strategy-stealing proof — the kind of argument that establishes the first player must win a symmetric placement game such as Hex without ever producing the winning strategy — by invoking the principle that you can never be worse off for having an extra option. I have since heard the same one-liner pressed into service well outside game theory: that enlarging a menu is always good for the chooser, that burning your bridges is irrational, that a negotiator who can walk away is strictly better off, that a chess player handed a free pass to skip a move would only gain.

Please lay out the distinct precise claims that sentence could be making, ordered from the nearly tautological to the substantive, and return a verdict on each: which are true by construction, which hold only under special structural conditions (and say which conditions), and which are straightforwardly false. I want a menu with verdicts rather than a single preferred reading, and I especially want to know which reading the strategy-stealing argument actually relies on, and whether that same reading is the one the menu, negotiation, and free-pass uses require — or whether those uses are quietly swapping in a different and weaker or stronger claim. I am unsure whether an extra option means one the chooser may simply ignore, one the opponent can observe and respond to, or one the chooser is obliged to take.

For each verdict, state what would change your mind, and flag the reading on which your reasoning is most likely to fail.
