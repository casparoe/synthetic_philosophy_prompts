I am not a professional historian or philosopher; I have only been reading and thinking on my own about what happens when a community's memory of its past and the documentary record disagree. Suppose a town has always told itself that it was founded by a band of refugees who fled persecution and were taken in by a generous local family, and that this story is the reason the town today treats hospitality as its civic identity and funds a shelter for newcomers. Then a researcher working through old land registers and court files concludes that the refugees were ordinary economic migrants, that no persecution is recorded, and that the generous family was simply selling land at market rates. The town council now has to decide what to teach in its schools and what to put on the monument. My question is this: when a community's memory and the historical record conflict in this way, which one ought to give way?

I want to say the record should win, because memory is notoriously selective and flattering, while history is the disciplined pursuit of what actually happened. But something bothers me about that. The record in this case is not a photograph of the past; it is a pile of documents that someone had to interpret, written by people with their own interests, and they leave out almost everything about how the migrants understood their own lives. Meanwhile the town's memory is doing work the record cannot do: it is what makes the town the kind of place that shelters strangers. So I have begun to wonder whether the real answer is a rule I made up for myself, namely that memory should give way whenever it is merely decorative, and the record should give way whenever the memory is load-bearing for some present obligation the community has taken on. Is that the right way to think about it, or am I missing something about what memory and history actually are? I would rather be told that my whole way of setting up the question is confused than be handed a tidy method that quietly assumes the confusion.
