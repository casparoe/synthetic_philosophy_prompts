Last week a friend of mine who works on technology standards brought an argument we had been having to a close by announcing that history is locked in. The argument concerned a case of the familiar kind: a convention that beat out rivals which were, on some measures, better, and which we are all now stuck with. His claim was meant to dispose of my question, which was whether the outcome we actually got was inevitable or whether some early accident explains why we are living with it. What bothered me afterwards is that I could not tell which of several quite different things he had asserted. He may have meant that an arbitrary early event is why we ended up here; or that no available move now leads anywhere else; or that a better arrangement was available and we cannot reach it; or, stranger still, that we would have arrived at this same arrangement no matter how the early events had gone. The phrase seemed to be doing several jobs at once, and the more I pressed, the less clear it became which one was load-bearing. The same conversation also featured the phrase it is just path dependence, used in much the same conversation-stopping way.

What I would like from you is a menu rather than a verdict. Set out the distinct precise claims that history is locked in — together with the looser talk of path dependence and lock-in that travels with it — could be making, ordered from the nearly empty to the very strong. For each reading, say what it commits its holder to: whether it is a claim about the past (a fluke sent us here), about the present (no available move leads out), or about a counterfactual (had the fluke gone the other way, we would have reached this outcome regardless); whether the relevant obstacle is cost, coordination, the absence of any better alternative, or something else; and whether the claim is about the world or only about the limits of our explanations. Then give me your verdicts: which of these readings are platitudes that no one could sensibly deny, which are substantive and defensible, which are probably false, and which of them would actually license my friend's move of treating the question as closed.

Two things I would especially like you to sort out. First, I suspect the slogan is sometimes used to assert contingency and sometimes to assert necessity — that the early accident is the whole story, and that nothing could now dislodge the result — and I want to know whether these are really compatible or whether one of them is doing the work while the other is only rhetorical. Second, I want to know which readings make the slogan a denial of the thought that things could have gone otherwise, and which make it a version of that thought, since I have a suspicion that my friend and I were using the same words for incompatible ideas. Please lay the readings out as a numbered list I can take back to him, with a sentence or two of verdict attached to each, and mark clearly any place where the slogan has to be given a technical sense that ordinary speakers would not recognize.
