Until recently, the ethics of choosing one's children was an abstract topic for me. It is no longer: my partner and I are in the middle of an IVF cycle, and our clinic has offered us preimplantation genetic testing with a polygenic panel that reports, alongside a few serious childhood conditions, scores for traits such as height, educational attainment, and susceptibility to several common adult diseases. We will have to decide which embryo to transfer, and I would like your help in the form of a ranking rather than a survey.

Please rank the following considerations by how much weight each should carry in that decision, giving each a numerical weight out of 100 so that the weights sum to 100:

1. The future child's expected lifetime welfare, in the spirit of the principle often called procreative beneficence — that among the children one could have, one should choose the one expected to fare best.
2. The future child's interest in an open future, that is, in having as many life paths as possible left available.
3. The positional or competitive character of a trait: some traits benefit their holder only because others lack them, so selecting for them cannot make everyone better off and may merely intensify a competition.
4. The expressivist concern: choosing against a trait can express a judgment about the worth of people who have that trait.
5. My partner's and my reproductive liberty — the claim that this decision is ours to make and not one that others may make for us.
6. The risk of compounding existing social injustice, for instance if the traits that can be scored most reliably are precisely those that already advantage the socially advantaged.

Three things I want beyond the ranking itself. First, rank the list twice — once as weights within our personal decision, and once as considerations that should constrain what a clinic or a state may offer, require, or forbid — and tell me where the two rankings come apart and why. Second, do not treat welfare as a single scalar: distinguish traits that would be harmful to a child under almost any social arrangement from traits whose disvalue is contingent on how our society happens to treat the people who have them, and say how that distinction should move an item up or down. Third, test each item against a constraint I will state in my own words, since I am not borrowing it from anyone: a selection is defensible only if I could explain it to the resulting child, once grown, without their reasonably concluding that they were chosen as a product rather than welcomed as a person. Tell me which items survive that test, which are weakened by it, and which of the six you would drop altogether as not a genuine consideration. If you think the whole framing is mistaken — for instance, if the decision should not be made by weighing these at all — say so, and tell me what should replace it.
