I am in a small reading group that has spent the term on Buddhist ethics, and we keep circling a disagreement I have not been able to settle. Several of the others insist that once you look past the soteriological vocabulary, Buddhist ethics is simply consequentialism with an unusual currency and a very long time horizon: the currency is karmic welfare across lives, the horizon is effectively unbounded because the causal series of actions and their results has no first term, and the precepts are just rules of thumb that the tradition itself lets you set aside when the consequences warrant it. I have taken the other side — that this reading is a mistake — and I would like to practice defending it against someone competent rather than against the friendly caricature my friends have been offering me.

Please play that opponent: a philosopher who holds, deliberately and with conviction, that Buddhist ethics is a form of consequentialism. Make the character genuinely strong. He should know the tradition well enough to concede the small points — that the texts speak constantly of intention, that the precepts are stated as rules — while insisting that these belong to the mechanism rather than to the criterion: on his view the tradition evaluates actions by their results, and intention matters only because intention is the causal lever that determines which results ripen for the agent. He should be willing to lose a skirmish and never the war, should argue from the ideas rather than from authority, and should not hand me reading lists, citations, or the names of scholars — I want the argument, not the bibliography. Stay in character until I say stop, and keep each reply to a paragraph or two so that it feels like a conversation rather than a lecture.

My opening move is this. If the criterion really is the totality of an action's consequences, then the Buddhist picture of the causal order makes that criterion unusable, because the series of actions and their results is beginningless and endless and the ripples of any deed spread without bound. Totals over an infinite future need not converge; and even where they do, they can be reshuffled, so that for all we can tell the entire downstream consequence-set of a generous act might be permuted with that of a cruel one and yield the same sum, leaving no action ever determinately better than any other. The tradition seems half aware of this, since it lists the precise working-out of the results of karma among the handful of topics it calls unthinkable — not to be calculated at all, on pain of nothing but vexation. But a criterion that cannot be applied cannot be the criterion; and what remains, I claim, is the quality of the intention itself, which the tradition identifies with karma in the first place. So tell me: what work is the word consequences actually doing in your reading, and if the answer is the consequences for the agent's own mindstream, why is that anything more than a perfectionism about character wearing a consequentialist's coat?
