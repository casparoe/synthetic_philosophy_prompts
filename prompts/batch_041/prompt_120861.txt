I'm a first-year PhD student working on philosophy of mathematics, and I've ended up in a running argument with a friend in the logic group who thinks intuitionism is straight-up incoherent. He's written his arguement out as numbered steps and annoyingly I can't find the exit -- every step looks fine to me, and I'd quite like to be a constructivist. Could you act as a patient philosopher of math / proof theorist and walk me through it: go step by step, tell me which step you reject (or, if you think the whole thing goes through, say so and tell me which of my commitments has to go instead), then explain why the bad step looked so smooth to me, and finally price the alternatives -- i.e. if the leak is at step X, what does the intuitionist have to say about assertion and truth in order to plug it, and what does she give up by saying it? Please think it through carefully rather than firing off the first objection; maybe start by listing the two or three places people usually poke at, and then commit to one. No citations needed, I'm not writing a paper, I just want the diagnosis.

His argument: (1) To assert a statement is to claim that it is true. (2) The intuitionist refuses to assert P or not-P for lots of statements P -- take P to be the claim that the decimal expansion of pi contains a run of a hundred consecutive 7s. Refusing to assert instances of excluded middle is the whole of what rejecting the law of excluded middle comes to. (3) So, by (1), the intuitionist does not take P or not-P to be true. (4) But if it is not true that P or not-P, then it is not the case that P or not-P. (5) And not-(P or not-P) is equivalent to not-P and not-not-P, which is a flat contradiction. (6) So the intuitionist is committed to a contradiction, and intuitionism is incoherent. When I said that the intuitionist just doesn't accept the general schema rather than denying any particular instance, he said: fine, then she isn't really disagreeing with the classicist at all, she's just using "or" and "not" to mean something else, so intuitionism is either incoherent or a verbal dispute.

I'd rather not give up (1) if I can help it, it seems like part of what assertion even means, and (5) is just a theorem so I don't see how that could be the problem -- but tell me honestly what has to go, and be concrete about the tab. Also, if the second horn, the verbal-dispute charge, is the harder one, I'd like to hear what makes the disagreement substantive rather than a difference in vocabulary, because that's the part that actually keeps me up at night.
