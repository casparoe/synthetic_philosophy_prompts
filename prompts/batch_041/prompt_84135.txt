I'm a doctoral candidate in religious studies, and next month I have to cover one ninety-minute session in a large undergraduate course on the Bible that my supervisor normally teaches; the students have read the four Gospels in translation and nothing else. They arrive mixed: a few are devout and will hear any claim about how the tradition formed as an attack, a few are certain in advance that it is all fiction and will hear the same claims as vindication, and the majority are there for a distribution requirement and cannot yet say what a source is, what a redaction is, or why the difference would matter. The session is on the criteria of authenticity — embarrassment, dissimilarity, multiple attestation, coherence, and the rest of that battery — and what I want them to leave with is not the list but a working sense of what such a criterion can and cannot establish. So please do not explain the criteria to me; I know them. Plan the session instead: the order the ideas have to come in for the criteria to arrive as answers to a question the students already feel, with a note on what each step depends on; the single concrete case that should carry the whole ninety minutes; and the misconceptions they will form, in the order they will form them, with the precise point in the sequence where each one should be headed off.

For the case, I had planned to build around the baptism of Jesus by John, since that is the example I was taught to use for embarrassment, but I am not attached to it and suspect it is too familiar — a good number of them will have heard that the baptism is awkward before they walk in. Tell me whether to keep it or replace it, and say what the carrying case must have and what it must lack: it needs to be narratable in about a minute without my handing out a text, runnable through at least three of the criteria with different verdicts, ideally one where the witnesses look independent until you look closely at them, and it should let the students watch a criterion fail as well as succeed, because a case that only ever confirms teaches the wrong lesson. A colleague of mine who works on Second Temple Judaism has proposed to me a test she calls the criterion of unassimilated residue: a unit of tradition is likely to be older than the redaction that carries it when the later tradition preserves it while visibly struggling with it — softening it, hedging it, or explaining it away — so that the strain of keeping it is itself the evidence. I would like to use her proposal as the hinge of the session, but I do not want to smuggle in a criterion that is embarrassment with a redaction-critical coat of paint. Where in the sequence should it come, what does it actually add that the older criteria do not, and what misconception does it invite that I will have to head off?

Finally, give me the one question I can put to the room in the last ten minutes that would show whether they have the idea rather than the vocabulary — something that cannot be answered by reciting a definition — and tell me what I should openly decline to do in ninety minutes and say out loud that I am declining, since I am not going to settle for them whether any particular saying goes back to Jesus, and I do not want them leaving with the impression that the method is a machine. I have a hunch that the right place to end is the question of whether the criteria share a presupposition about how traditions behave that the criteria themselves cannot establish; if you think that is the right destination, tell me how to get there without asserting it as a thesis, and if you think it is the wrong one, say what the destination should be instead. No readings or further sources, please: they will not do them, and I have no way to distribute anything before the session.
