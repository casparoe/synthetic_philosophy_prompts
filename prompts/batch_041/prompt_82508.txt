I have been asked to lead a ninety-minute continuing-education session on why promises bind, for a mixed group of adults — several of them lawyers, none of them philosophers — and what I want from you is not the argument but a plan for teaching it. The line I want the session to earn rather than assume is this: the hard question is not why we should keep promises but how saying something can create an obligation in the first place, and any answer worth having has to do two things that a straightforward appeal to the harms of broken trust cannot. It has to explain why a promise is not merely a very heavy reason among the reasons one already has, but a reason that rules out acting on some of them — why something better coming up is not, by itself, something the promisor is free to weigh. And it has to explain why the obligation is owed to the person who was promised to, in the sense that she, and no one else, can release the promisor from it.

Given that target, plan the explanation: what has to be in place before the question even registers as a question, and in what order the pieces have to come so that each one is motivated by the piece before it rather than simply asserted. I want the session built around one case that does the real work, and I want you to tell me what that case must have and what it must lack — in particular, it has to be a case in which the consequentialist fallback is unavailable, so that breaking the promise costs no one anything measurable and the promisor has an obviously better use for his time. I had thought of a promise made to someone who has since died, and separately of a promise to do something the promisor would certainly have done anyway; tell me whether either can bear that weight, or what should replace them.

Then the misconceptions. I am expecting at least these, and I want them predicted in the order they will actually surface and pinned to the precise point in the sequence where each should be headed off: that a promise is just a prediction of one's own behavior; that it binds only because someone is relying on it; that the promisor can release himself; that a promise no one knows about is not binding; and that an insincere promise was never a promise at all. Several of the lawyers will try to convert the whole thing into a question about contract law, and I would rather that be disposed of in a sentence than in a lecture. Tell me what I am missing, especially the misconception that the sequence itself will generate.

Finish by giving me a single question to put to the room at the end — something that would expose whether they have grasped the structure or merely collected three positions — and tell me what I should refuse to explain at this level, and say so out loud. Please write this in continuous prose: no headings, no numbered steps, no bullets, no bold, no tables, the way you would write it out in a long email to a colleague. I don't need a reading list; nobody in the room would read it.
