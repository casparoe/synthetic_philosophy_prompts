I teach an undergraduate course on strategy and international security, and I am marking the midterm. Below you will find the exam question and one student's answer. I would like you to grade the answer and supply the comments that justify the grade. I need the assessment to be specific enough that I can see exactly which claims the grade rests on, so please name particular sentences and assess them one by one rather than giving me an overall impression of the response. You do not need citations or page references; what matters is whether the reasoning holds up.

The exam question was as follows.

"In a crisis between two nuclear-armed states, each of which possesses a survivable second-strike force, a leader may deliberately take steps that raise the probability of events slipping beyond anyone's control: mobilising forces visibly, loosening the constraints on subordinate commanders, telling the world that matters are getting out of hand. Explain why such a move can improve the leader's bargaining position rather than merely endanger it. In your answer, (a) identify what does the bargaining work in this manoeuvre, if not a threat of deliberate attack; (b) explain why the adversary's belief about the leader's intentions matters, and in which direction; (c) distinguish the manoeuvre from a straightforward threat to attack deliberately if demands are not met; and (d) state the conditions under which it fails."

The student wrote the following.

"Brinkmanship is the manipulation of shared risk. Its distinctive move is not to threaten a deliberate attack — a threat the adversary can dismiss, since executing it would be suicidal — but to arrange affairs so that an outcome nobody wants becomes possible and is no longer entirely under one's own control. Mobilising visibly, loosening the constraints on lower commanders, or announcing that events are slipping away all do the same thing: they replace a decision the leader could take back with a probability he cannot switch off. That is where the manoeuvre's leverage comes from. The adversary now has to reason about a risk rather than about a bluff, and the leader's inability to control events is precisely what makes the risk real.

The adversary's belief matters, and it matters in a particular direction. The tactic works only if the other side is convinced that the leader is resolved enough to go over the brink — that if his demands are not met, he will choose the catastrophe rather than climb down. Absent that conviction, the move buys nothing and merely raises everyone's exposure. This is why brinkmanship is often described as the rationality of irrationality: it can be rational to be believed willing to do what no rational agent would do. Schelling's madman theory is the clearest statement of the logic, and Nixon's conduct of the Vietnam War is the standard illustration of it in practice.

The tactic is also genuinely dangerous rather than merely risky: the exposure is real and two-sided, so a state that practises brinkmanship gambles with its own cities and not only with the adversary's. But because the danger is common to both sides, each has a strong interest in pulling back, and the manoeuvre therefore tends in practice to bring the parties to the table rather than to carry them over the edge; the shared risk is self-limiting. It fails when the adversary is willing to run the risk longer, or when the loss of control is so complete that the catastrophe arrives before any concession does."
