Right, I'm a psych PhD student who's gone too far down the methods rabbit hole, and I want a sanity check on my comittments before I say any of this out loud in a seminar. Here's where I've landed.

1. Evidence is a Bayesian business. What data tell you is how much they should move your credences, and a p-value isn't a measure of evidence at all. I also take the likelihood principle seriously: if two experiments hand you the same likelihood function, they hand you the same evidence, so it shouldn't matter that you'd planned to run twenty more participants or that you peeked at the data halfway through.

2. The replication crisis is mostly a selection problem. You can fiddle with your covariates, your exclusion criteria, your outcome measure, and only the version that landed under p < .05 makes it into the paper. So the fix is pre-registration and registered reports — lock the analysis before you see the data.

3. I don't really believe in a single true effect size for most of what we study. Effects are context-bound, and the picture of one number sitting out there waiting to be estimated is a fiction. So when someone fails to replicate a study in a slightly different setup with a different population, they haven't refuted anything — they've tested a different claim.

4. That said, when a big batch of our published findings got re-run and only a minority came back, I read that as evidence that a lot of what got published was simply false. That's the whole reason I care about any of this.

5. I'm suspicious of priors in psychology. I don't think anyone can honestly put a number on how likely it is that ego depletion is real, so when I see a Bayesian paper with a prior over the effect size I basically read the prior as made up. A well-powered pre-registered frequentist study with the analysis plan filed in advance strikes me as more trustworthy than a Bayesian analysis whose prior was picked because it looks neutral.

And one more thing I've been chewing on, which I don't think is a standard view so much as something I've invented in my notes — I've started writing as if every study has two readings. There's the evidential reading: what does this data do to my credences. And there's the audit reading: if a whole field works like this, what fraction of what it publishes is true. I can't tell whether that distinction is doing real work or whether I'm just dressing up the thing everyone already knows.

So: which of 1–5 are actually pulling against each other, which pairs only look like they clash, and what's the cheapest way back to a package that hangs together? Rank the tensions by how bad they are — I'd rather be told that one of them is fatal than get a list where everything is equally worrying. For each genuine one, say what has to give and what I lose by giving it. And at the end, tell me which single change cleans up the most mess, even if it isn't the one I'd enjoy making. I don't need a lecture on the replication crisis, I need the audit.

Write it in British English please — my supervisor is pedantic about -ise and I'll never hear the end of it.
