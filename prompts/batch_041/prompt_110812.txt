I spent thirty-odd years as a field geophysicist and since retiring I've been sitting in on a philosophy of science reading group at the university, which has been a hoot, though it's made me realize I've been carrying around a Popper-shaped picture of how science works since about 1975 and never questioned it. Now they've asked me to lead one of our lunchtime sessions, and I've landed on Lakatos's research programmes, because I think it describes the thing I actually did for a living better than either Popper or Kuhn does. The audience is eight or nine working scientists, mostly from my old institute, plus a couple of the group's regulars — they know what a hypothesis test is, they've never read any philosophy of science, and they'd riot if I handed them reading. So plan how I should teach it rather than explaining it to me. What has to be in place before research programmes even make sense as a move? I'm assuming falsificationism first, then the problem that a single test never seems to land on a single hypothesis, but tell me if I've got the dependency backwards or if something else has to come before that. Then the order everything else goes in, so that the hard core, the protective belt and the two heuristics arrive as things the audience wants rather than as vocabulary to be memorized. And I don't want to use the textbook history cases — Prout, Bohr, that crowd — because half the room won't know them and I'd end up teaching nineteenth-century chemistry instead of philosophy. So propose the one carrying example, ideally something I can build out of a generic lab or fieldwork situation the room will recognize, and say what it has to contain and what it mustn't; my hunch is that it mustn't be a case where the programme was simply wrong, or the whole point about degenerating programmes gets lost.

The misconceptions are where I'll come unstuck, so be specific about them and about where each one gets headed off. The one I can already hear coming is 'so the hard core is just the stuff you refuse to give up' — the dogmatism reading, which I think misses that the hard core is a positive commitment that generates work rather than a stubborness. Another is that a research programme is a paradigm with a new name, and I want to know exactly where in the sequence to kill that one before it sets, and whether I should mention Kuhn at all. There's also the one that bit me when I first read about it: the two senses of 'progressive', theoretical versus empirical, and whether a programme can be one without the other, which I'd like to get right since it's exactly the kind of thing this crowd will ask. And the point that the heuristics are a rational reconstruction of what a programme looks like from the outside rather than a rulebook the scientists were consciously following — I only half understand that, it never occured to me before, and I'd like it somewhere in the sequence, though I can't tell whether it's a finishing move or something that has to come early or the rest won't hold.

Finish with the one question I should put to the room at the end that would show whether they've actually got the appraisal-by-problem-shifts idea or have just learnt four new words, and tell me what I should openly refuse to go into at a lunch hour, and to say so up front.
