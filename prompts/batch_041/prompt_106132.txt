I am a software engineer with no formal background in philosophy, and I have become interested in the question of what, if anything, separates science from pseudoscience. The answer I encounter most often is that a scientific claim must be falsifiable — that there must be some possible observation that would count against it — whereas pseudoscientific claims are framed so that no observation could ever refute them. This has real intuitive appeal, since it resembles the discipline of writing tests that a program can fail, but I am not convinced it can carry the weight placed on it. Please write an essay that weighs the case for and against falsifiability as the mark of science and that ends by defending a verdict rather than splitting the difference.

Two difficulties should be central. First, the conjunction problem: if a claim earns its scientific status merely by being refutable in principle, it appears that any claim can be granted, or retained in, that status by conjoining it with further assumptions, or by adding ad hoc repairs whenever a prediction fails; a criterion satisfiable so cheaply seems to separate nothing. Second, consider a graded alternative I have been toying with: instead of asking whether a claim is falsifiable at all, ask how sharply it shifts the expected probability of particular observations relative to its rivals, so that a claim counts as more scientific the more its predictions diverge from those of the alternatives and the harder it is to accommodate the evidence after the fact. Assess whether this comparative, graded version does better than the binary one, or merely relocates the difficulty.

Reach a clear conclusion about where the demarcation line should be drawn, if anywhere, and state how confident you are in that conclusion and which single claim in the essay you are least sure of.
