I'm a graduate student in political philosophy, and I'm drafting a short position paper whose whole point is to lay out my own commitments rather than to defend someone else's line. Before I commit to it, I'd like a candid audit of whether the package actually hangs together — I have a vague sense that one or two joints are loose, but I genuinely cannot see which, and I'd rather find out now than in the seminar. Here is where I've landed.

First, legitimacy. I take a coercive law to be legitimate only if every person bound by it could reasonably accept it as expressing equal concern for their interests. I don't think it's sufficient that the law happens to produce good results, and I don't think a majority gets to decide whose interests count in the first place. Second, the epistemic point: when a polity must settle a question that has a fact of the matter — whether a drug is safe, whether a policy will actually reduce unemployment — there is a correct answer, and its being correct doesn't depend on who is doing the deciding; the truth of "this policy will work" isn't constituted by the procedure that selects it. Third, aggregation: I'm persuaded by the Condorcet-style argument that a large, diverse electorate judging independently will, under the right conditions, track the truth more reliably than any small body of experts, and that this is a real reason to prefer broad participation over rule by the wise. Fourth, the empirical hangover: I also think those conditions mostly fail in practice — most voters are uninformed about the substance of policy, vote on identity and affect rather than evidence, and, crucially, their errors are correlated, since they consume the same media and respond to the same partisan cues, so the independence the aggregation argument needs is largely absent. Fifth, a prescription: because of the fourth point I've been drifting toward some form of epistocracy, and the specific design I've sketched is this — a randomly selected panel of citizens, who deliberate in a structured way and must clear a modest knowledge threshold before serving, issues a binding verdict on a given policy question, with the general electorate able to override it only by a supermajority in a referendum. Sixth, a verdict on a case that I hold with some confidence: I have no trouble with ordinary lay juries deciding guilt beyond reasonable doubt in criminal trials, even though they are plainly less informed than the judges and lawyers in the room, and I don't experience this as a regrettable concession to practicality — I think that is simply what justice requires.

So I'd like you to go through these and tell me which pairs are genuinely in tension and which only appear to be, ranking the genuine ones by severity rather than treating them all as equally bad. For each genuine tension, say what the least costly revision would be and what that revision costs me; for the apparent ones, say briefly why they dissolve. And please be explicit throughout about which parts of this are settled and which are genuinely contested among people who work on these questions — I don't want a contested position presented to me as if it were the consensus.
