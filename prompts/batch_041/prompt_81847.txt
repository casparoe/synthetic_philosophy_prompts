I'm a software engineer with no philosophical training, and I keep running into something in planning meetings that I have no good word for. Two estimates can carry the same number and still be completely different things to act on. One colleague says there's a seventy percent chance the migration lands this quarter, and I will build the schedule around it without much worry, because we've done twenty of these and roughly fourteen came in on time. Another gives the same seventy percent for a rewrite on a stack nobody here has used, and I want to double-check everything and keep a fallback ready. The number on the slide is identical; what differs is how much is standing behind it.

There's a second pattern I suspect is connected. For some decisions there is no series of similar cases to count at all — we are choosing one architecture, once, for a product that doesn't exist yet — and yet people still write a number on the slide. The number then seems to summarise the quality of the argument rather than the frequency of anything, and two people can agree on the argument and still disagree about the number without either of them having made a mistake.

Is there an established name for what I'm circling, and is it one phenomenon or two? I know roughly the distinction people draw between risk and uncertainty, and I've heard of error bars and confidence levels, but none of those seems to be about the part I care about: that the strength of the case behind a probability is a separate thing from the probability itself, and that we act on the two separately. Please give me a definite verdict on which existing concept, if any, is the best home for this, and which parts of what I've described it fails to cover. If nothing fits, coin a term and define it, with edge cases — does it still apply when nothing is being repeated, and does it apply to an everyday judgement like thinking some claim is probably true? I don't need a reading list or citations; I want the concepts sorted out and the boundaries drawn, and I'd rather you take a position than lay out the options.
