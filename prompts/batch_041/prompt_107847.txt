I am a high school student in a philosophy of biology seminar, and I am trying to get better at arguing rather than just absorbing readings, so I would like to spar with someone who holds a position I am inclined to reject. Please play a committed defender of the etiological, or selected-effects, account of biological function — the view that to say what a trait is for is to say what it was selected for, rather than what it happens to contribute right now. Play him as a real philosopher rather than a straw man: he should concede minor points, admit where the account gets awkward, and give my examples a fair hearing, but he must never give up his central thesis that function claims are grounded in a history of selection. He should speak in plain, patient prose without dumping jargon, keep each reply to a tight paragraph or two, and stay in character until I say "stop." If I ask him a factual question about some organism, he may answer it as a philosopher of biology would, but he should not break character to hand me a reading list or a set of citations; I want an argument, not a bibliography.

Here is my opening move, and I would like his reply to it. My teacher tells me that a trait's function is just whatever it currently contributes to an organism's survival and reproduction, and that all the talk about ancestors and selection is a distraction. I think I can beat that, but the case that makes me nervous about my own side is this: imagine a single bacterium that picks up a gene by horizontal transfer, a gene that happens to code for a protein that pumps a toxin out of the cell. Nothing in this lineage was ever selected for that pumping — the gene simply arrived — but the first time the toxin shows up, the pump works, the cell survives, and its descendants inherit the gene and thrive. If you ask what that protein is for, the answer seems to be that it is for getting rid of the toxin, and that answer seems true on the very first day, before any selection history exists. So either your account has to say the protein has no function yet, which sounds plainly false, or it has to say the function appears retroactively once the lineage persists, in which case the history seems to be doing no real work. Tell me which it is. And if the account can survive that case, tell me what it does with the mirror image: a structure that was unmistakably selected for something long ago but no longer contributes anything at all, where your account seems to insist it still has a function while the causal-role people say it has lost one. I will admit up front that I do not know how to stop the causal-role account from saying that the heart's function is also to make a thumping noise, so I expect you to press me hard on that.
