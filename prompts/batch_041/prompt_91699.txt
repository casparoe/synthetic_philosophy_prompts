I'm a backend engineer on a team split across Amsterdam, London and Tokyo — a Dutch manager, several Japanese colleagues and me, British — so I spend a fair amount of my week deciding how blunt to be. I have no background in philosophy and nothing to look things up with, so please don't give me names, references or reading lists; I want the principle, if there is one, that fits the verdicts below.

- My manager asks, in a review with the whole team present, whether I think her proposed re-architecture will hold up. I think it won't. I say so flatly, with the reason, and I am confident that is right.
- A colleague in Tokyo asks me the same kind of question about his design document, in a meeting that includes his team and his manager. I say only that there are a couple of areas I would like to talk through with him afterwards. I am confident that is right too.
- The same colleague asks me the same question on a call with just the two of us. Here I think I should say plainly that the document is not ready, and that the hedging which was right in the previous case would be wrong.
- A colleague asks whether I thought his talk landed. It didn't, and he is giving it again to a customer next week. I say it went well. I think that was wrong and that I owed him the truth.
- My aunt asks whether I enjoyed the meal she cooked. I didn't much. I say I did, and I feel entirely fine about it.
- A friend sends me a short story she is about to send to an agent. I think it isn't good enough. I tell her it is strong and suggest two small cuts. I keep going back and forth on whether that was right.
- A recruiter at another company asks whether I am happy where I am. I am not, and I am interviewing. I say I am happy. I think that is fine.
- A colleague asks whether anyone else has raised concerns about his document. No one else has, but only because I haven't asked anyone. I say no one else has. I think that is wrong, though every word of it is true.

The rule I have been running, roughly: how much accuracy I owe someone scales with what they are about to do with what I tell them. If they will act on it, they get the whole truth; if the only thing they can do with it is feel something, they get very little. Call it a truth budget. That seems to get the talk, the document and my aunt's cooking, but I cannot see how it gets the short story, it seems to license the recruiter lie for the wrong reason, and it says nothing about why my Tokyo colleague deserves a hedge in front of his team and plain speech on a private call.

So: is there one principle that gets all eight, or two — a truth budget and something like a face budget — and what work is each clause doing? Apply the best version you can state to two cases I haven't given: a stranger on a forum asking whether his plan will work, and a close friend asking whether I think she was right to turn down a job offer six months ago, which she cannot undo. Tell me which of my verdicts the principle cannot accommodate, and which of them I should give up if it comes to that. Please also say what would change your mind — which case or argument would move you to a different principle, and where your reasoning is most likely to fail. Write the answer in British English.
