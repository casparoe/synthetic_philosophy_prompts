I have no academic training to speak of -- I did bookkeeping for a mid-sized freight company for eleven years and read popular science on the side -- but a question has been lodged in my head since a long drive last spring with two friends, one of whom consults for an environmental firm. We got onto the proposed geological epoch defined by human impact, the Anthropocene, and I noticed that the part of it I find genuinely strange is not the science but the bookkeeping. Somebody, somewhere, has to draw a line in a layer of mud or ice and declare that everything above it belongs to a new chapter of Earth's history and everything below it to the old one. My friend said this is simply settled by evidence: you find the layer where the fallout or the plastics or the soot first appear, and that is that. But it seemed to me the evidence can only tell you where a mark is, never which mark counts as the beginning, nor how large a mark has to be to count -- and those look like decisions rather than discoveries, made by a small group of people on behalf of everyone else, most of whom will never meet them.

I would like you to write a short story that puts this problem into a situation rather than a discussion. Here is the setup I have been turning over, and you should feel free to build on it or bend it: in the story, the body that names eras works under a rule -- call it the survivor rule -- that a span of time may be named only after the last person who lived through its beginning has died, so that the name is always chosen by people who never experienced the events themselves and can never be thanked or blamed for the choice. The story takes place over a single day in a small lakeside town that happens to sit on top of the sediment layer under consideration as the official marker. That day, the last surviving witness is expected to die. The committee has narrowed the question to two rival candidates: one places the boundary at the moment of the greatest physical change, the other at the moment when people first understood what they were doing -- the harm against the awareness of the harm -- and it cannot agree, so by its own rules the tie will be broken by lot rather than by argument. I would like the townspeople to have their own stake in which marker is chosen, one that has nothing to do with stratigraphy and everything to do with how they want their parents and grandparents remembered.

Please make it roughly fifteen hundred words. I am not looking for a story that ends with someone explaining the lesson; I would rather the philosophical question be carried by what the characters want from each other and from the dead. Both sides of the disagreement should feel live, and I would like at least one scene in which the naming actually happens -- the moment the line is fixed -- so that the reader feels the arbitrariness of the act even as it becomes official. It is important to me that no one in the story is a fool and no one is simply correct.
