Ok, I want you to try to break a view I've talked myself into about Goodman's grue, and I want it done properly -- not the textbook version where someone says entrenchment, everyone nods, and we move on.

The view: the new riddle of induction isn't really a problem about confirmation at all. It's a problem about which predicates pick out genuine properties, and once you say that projectibility is just a brute fact about the world -- green is the kind of feature that can show up in a law, grue isn't, end of story -- the paradox loses its teeth. On this account grue isn't projectible for the same reason witch isn't a natural kind: not because the word is new, or ugly, or unentrenched, but because there's no property there for a law to be about. So the gap between green and grue isn't epistemic at all, it's ontological, and no possible evidence could have favored grue in the first place.

What I want from you: what would count against that. Not a literature survey -- I can't look anything up and neither can you, so give me arguments and cases, generated from scratch if you have to. Rank them, fatal down to merely awkward, and tell me which ones a defender of this line would themselves concede are real problems versus which ones they'd just wave away. And be blunt about whether I've actually solved anything or just moved the bump under the carpet: if genuine property ends up meaning property that shows up in the inductions we already accept, then I've said nothing, and I'd rather you tell me that than be polite about it.

Two things I want you to spend real time on, because they're the ones that keep me up at night.

First, the two-communities case, pushed harder than the usual telling. Imagine a community whose basic color words are grue and bleen, learned from birth, used in every ordinary context, so both predicates are exactly as entrenched and exactly as simple *for them* as green and blue are for us. Every induction they make works. Does my view definately say they're wrong about the future, or does it say they're talking about different properties and both are right? If they're wrong, I want to know what makes us right other than the fact that we're the ones doing the talking. If they're both right, I want to know wether there being a fact about which properties are genuine survives that, or whether I've just admitted that genuineness is relative to a language -- which is the thing I was trying to avoid.

Second, and this one's less standard: what does any of this look like for something like a language model? Entrenchment is supposed to be about the history of a word's use in projections, but for a model, what counts as one symbol versus two is decided by a tokenizer, and simplicity is plausibly something like description length in whatever the model's internal code turns out to be. So is my view committed to saying that whether grue is projectible could depend on how the input happens to get chunked -- which smells like a reductio -- or is that just what you should expect once you take the ontology seriously? I honestly don't know which way this cuts and I want your read, plus a note on whether the answer changes if the model was trained on a corpus that happens to contain alot of grue-ish predicates.
