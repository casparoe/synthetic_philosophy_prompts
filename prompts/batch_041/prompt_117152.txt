I'm putting together the final for my upper-level philosophy of science course, and I want one essay question on Popper's critique of Darwinism that can't be answered by reciting the usual line that Popper was just wrong about natural selection being unfalsifiable. A student asked me last week whether Popper's charge was aimed at the whole theory or just at the slogan about survival of the fittest, and I realized I couldn't give a crisp answer without working through the details myself. So I definately want the question to force students to seperate the core principle of natural selection from the loose adaptationist reasoning that Popper may have been reacting to. It should ask them to explain the tautology charge and say whether it can be defused by defining fitness independently of survival, to give at least one concrete example of a risky prediction that natural selection makes and explain how it could be falsified, and to assess whether Popper's later statement that he had changed his mind about the testability and logical status of natural selection was a full concession or only a qualification. The question should also make them take a definite stand on whether Popper was ultimately right, wrong, or partly right, and defend that stand rather than just surveying the options.

Please write the essay question itself, a model answer of about 700 words, and a marking scheme that separates an A from a B by naming the specific moves each must contain. In the model answer, I want a committed verdict, not a fence-sitting summary. The marking scheme should say what an A answer must do that a B answer might miss — for instance, distinguishing between the principle of natural selection and specific models that make testable predictions, or explaining the role of auxiliary hypotheses in making selection testable. Also, please make sure the model answer doesn't just say Popper was wrong; it should engage with why his critique seemed plausible and what grain of truth remains in it.
