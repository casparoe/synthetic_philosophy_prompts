In a graduate seminar I've been sitting in on, the instructor opened the unit on promissory obligation with the following thumbnail sketch, and several colleagues have since been repeating it as though it settled the question:

A promise binds because breaking it would disappoint expectations one has deliberately raised. The obligation is therefore a special case of the general duty not to cause harm: by promising, you lead others to rely on you, and frustrating that reliance is simply one way of harming them. There is nothing distinctively moral about the institution of promising itself; it is a useful coordination device whose binding force is exhausted by the harm of disappointed reliance. That this cannot be the whole story is shown by the promise made to someone who dies before the breach — such a promisee suffers no disappointed expectation, so on this account the promise would not bind at all, which is absurd; the expectation account must therefore be false.

I have my own suspicions — the harm-based reading strikes me as too thin, and I think the closing move does something illicit — but I can't yet say precisely where the sketch goes wrong or which of its parts are worth keeping. What I want is an accuracy audit, not a general essay on promising.

Specifically: first, which claims here would a careful defender of the expectation account accept as fair, which are loose but defensible simplifications, and which are outright misrepresentations? Second, the sketch treats "expectation" and "reliance" as interchangeable, and I suspect that is where the trouble starts — can you say what turns on keeping them apart, and whether collapsing them changes what the account is committed to? Third, is the case of the promisee who dies before the breach a refutation of the account, a genuine puzzle it must confront, or an objection that only bites against a version the sketch has already distorted? Fourth, does the conclusion that promising "reduces to" the general duty not to harm follow from anything accurate in the sketch, or is it an overreach? And fifth, the sketch equates a promise's being binding with its being wrong to break; I've encountered the idea that promissory obligations are directed — owed to a particular person and waivable by that person — and I want to know whether the sketch's framing can even register that distinction, and what it would take to accommodate it.

Please don't hand me a bibliography or a reading list; I want the analysis itself, in your own words, so that I can see what survives the audit and what doesn't.
