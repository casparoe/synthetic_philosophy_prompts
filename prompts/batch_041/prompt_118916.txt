I'm a science journalist and I've been sitting on a half-written feature about contradiction for close to a year now — it started as a piece about the old semantic paradoxes and mutated into something about the people who think some contradictions are simply true, and I keep getting stuck in the same place. Every time I try to lay the options out on a page they collapse into "classical logic versus dialetheism," which I'm pretty sure is wrong, because half the logicians I talk to tell me they're paraconsistent but *not* dialetheist and then wave their hands, and the other half start arguing about what the word "not" means. So what I'd like from you is a decision tree — an actual tree, not a vibe — that takes a reader who's willing to say "fine, some of our best theories come out inconsistent" and routes them, node by node, to a named position. Every internal node should be a question a reader could answer without already knowing which leaf it leads to, and ideally a concrete case or a concrete inference rather than an ism: is the liar sentence both true and not true, or neither? if a theory says P and also says not-P, does that make "P and not-P" assertible, or do the two just sit there unjoined? when P and not-P are both in play, may you go on and infer Q? does the negation you're using actively exclude P, or does it merely fail to assert it? and what happens to your whole setup once you bolt on a conditional that actually detaches and then feed it the Curry sentence? I want the tree to reach at least these leaves and keep them seperate from each other: plain classical logic, no true contradictions, explosion fine; the "true contradictions are real, the liar and the set-theoretic paradoxes are our best evidence, explosion has to go" view; a paraconsistency that's about inconsistent bodies of information or inconsistent theories rather than the world, so nobody needs a true contradiction; the non-adjunctive or discussive option where each source is consistent on its own and only the merged discussion isn't, so P and not-P are both in play while their conjunction never gets asserted; the da Costa-ish move of holding on to classical reasoning for the "well-behaved" sentences via a consistency operator; a relevant-logic route where explosion fails for reasons of relevance rather than any glut; an adaptive-logic route where you treat a contradiction as an abnormality and claw back classicality wherever you can; and the vagueness-driven glut option where a borderline case makes a predication and its negation both come out true (I think that one's called subvaluationism and it's the dual of the supervaluationist gap view — if I've got the label or the direction backwards, say so). Include whatever leaf you think belongs in the gap between the gaps people and the gluts people, and please tell me whether your tree can even separate the dialetheist who thinks classical negation is simply the wrong negation from the classical logician who thinks the dialetheist must mean something different by "not" — that one smells like a verbal dispute to me, and if it is, flag it rather than papering over it.

Two things to keep in mind. Build it from the logical space first and locate the positions inside it afterward, rather than listing the schools and drawing arrows between them — I want to see which combinations of answers are actually available, which are empty, and for each empty one a sentence on why it's empty (incoherent, unattractive, or just nobody's bothered to go there). And the audience is smart magazine readers, not logicians, so keep it in plain English, no citations, no reading list, nothing I'd have to look up — I can't use those anyway, and the model writing this doesn't get to search. If a leaf has no well-known name, call it "unnamed" and describe it in a clause. Eight to twelve nodes and seven to nine leaves feels about right, but I'd much rather the tree be honest about its limits than hit a number.
