I teach a graduate seminar on the theory of AI alignment, and I am writing the take-home final. I want a single long essay question, and I want it to be one that a student cannot answer by reciting the received summary of iterated amplification and distillation — it should require reasoning about the picture rather than reproducing it.

To keep the question from turning into a memory test, I have invented a protocol that does not exist in the literature, and I want the question built around it. Call the fictional lab Corvid and the protocol the "nested-verification pipeline." Set it out for the students as follows.

A strong decomposer model receives a hard task and breaks it into a tree of sub-tasks. Each sub-task is handed to a weaker worker model that answers it in isolation, without seeing the others. The decomposer then aggregates the worker answers into a candidate response and is free to re-decompose or request revisions; it is the only component with a view of the whole tree. A separate checker, drawn from the same model family as the decomposer, inspects the aggregated answer against the original task and can veto it, sending the task back for another round. Finally the entire tree — decomposer, workers, aggregator, and checker — is collapsed by distillation into a single student model that must reproduce the pipeline's final outputs directly, with no workers, no aggregator, and no checker present at run time. The lab's slogan for the last step is "compress only what has been verified." The lab claims that because the amplified pipeline is more reliable and better behaved than any single model, and because the distilled student matches the pipeline's outputs on a large held-out set of tasks, distillation preserves both the pipeline's competence and its alignment gains.

I want the exam question to have three labeled parts, (a), (b), and (c), to be answerable in about three hours of writing, and to be worth 25 marks. Part (a) should force the student to separate two things the lab's claim runs together: preservation of the pipeline's behavior and preservation of the structural feature that made the behavior trustworthy. Part (b) should make them argue why matching outputs on a held-out set cannot establish either, with attention to why the alignment claim is harder to test than the competence claim. Part (c) should ask them to design a concrete probe or modification that would tell apart a student that retained the safeguard from one that merely imitated the outputs, and to say what result would count as evidence for which.

Please write the question as the students would see it — the fictional setup included, but self-contained and requiring no citations — a model answer of roughly 700 words, and a marking scheme out of 25 marks. The marking scheme should name the specific moves a top answer must make and the specific errors that should cost marks: for instance, treating "alignment preserved" as "the student's values match the teacher's," assuming the checker rather than the inspectability of the decomposition is what carried the safety, or treating distillation as lossless compression with no inductive bias. Separate an A answer from a B answer by the moves each contains.
