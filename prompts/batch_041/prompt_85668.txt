I'm a science-and-ideas reporter and I'm three weeks into a piece on a materials lab that keeps telling me their new stuff is "disposed to" do things, and I've stopped being able to tell when that's a claim and when it's a vibe. Before I file I want the spec, not the vibes: what does any account of "this thing is disposed to do X when Y" have to deliver before I can interrogate these people properly? Give me the requirements list -- the cases it has to get right, the distinctions it can't collapse, the results it can't contradict -- with the consideration behind each one, not just the label.

Here's whats actually on my desk, so you can tell me which of my demands are doing real work and which are just my own hang-ups:

- the fuse thing. A nanomaterial the lab calls disposed to conduct, except that hooking up the probes is what wrecks the conductive structure. So nobody will ever watch it conduct. Don't test it and you learn nothing; test it and it's dead. One of the grad students told me it conducts "when nobody's looking," which I'm fairly sure isn't a thing you can put in a paper.
- the water that eats sugar. A sample of water with nanobots in it. Drop sugar in, sugar's gone, every time, in every condition I can think of. But it's the bots doing it, and a rival lab says the water isn't disposed to dissolve sugar at all, it's just arranged so that dissolving always happens. They're fighting about this and I can't tell if there's a fact of the matter or just a word.
- the vase in the glove. My fact-checker's favorite. The vase would break if dropped, except the exhibit has a sensor and a net that catches it. It has never broken and never will. Still disposed to break?
- the glowing rock. Half-life stuff. The physicist says the nucleus is disposed to decay, but nothing has to happen to it first, and when it goes it's a coin flip. So the if-Y-then-X shape doesn't seem to fit at all, which he seems to think is my problem rather than his.
- the solvent nobody's made. A chemist tells me a compound is disposed to dissolve in a solvent that doesn't exist and maybe never will. Is that a claim about the compound or a claim about a possible world?
- and the line I keep getting from everyone: that the disposition is "nothing over and above" the structure that produces it. I want to know what requirement that's meant to satisfy, and whether it satisfies it or just re-describes it.

Then the honest call: which of these requirements are non-negotiable and which are just what one camp happens to care about. And the part I actually need for the piece -- flag any two things on my list that I probaly can't have at once. If a plain if-Y-then-X story can't survive the fuse, the water, and the vase together, say so flat out; that's the paragraph I'm running. Also tell me which single requirement does the most work, i.e. kills off the most accounts people actually hold.

Don't bother scoring the contenders against the list, I'll do that in the piece. And make the very last line start with Verdict: followed by just the one requirement that eliminates the most otherwise-decent accounts, short phrase, nothing after it.
