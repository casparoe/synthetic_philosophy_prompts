I have been reading rationalist blogs and forums for about fifteen years, and the argument I keep returning to — in comment threads, in reading groups, in my own head on long walks — is the one about whether mental illness is real or a myth. What frustrates me is that the participants usually agree on the empirical material. They agree that psychiatric categories are not validated by any single biomarker, that treatment response is heterogeneous, that the same label can cover a person in agony and a person who is merely inconvenient to the people around them, and that the history of the manual includes categories later judged to have been mistakes. The disagreement is about what follows from all that, and it is almost never stated cleanly, because people reach for labels (Szaszian, biomedical, social-constructionist) instead of saying which claim they are actually committed to. I want a classification instrument rather than a verdict.

Please build me a taxonomy of the positions on the question, presented as a decision tree. Each branch node must be a question that separates its siblings, phrased so that a reader can answer it without already knowing which position the answer leads to — a concrete case or a first-order claim, never "do you think mental illness is real?". Each leaf should carry the name of a position (or "unnamed", where nobody seems to defend it) and one sentence on its most awkward commitment.

The distinctions I care about, because they are the ones that actually split my reading group, are these: whether "disease" or "illness" is an evaluative term or a descriptive one; whether a mind can be ill in the same sense that a body can, or only in a metaphorical or borrowed sense; whether a diagnosis functions primarily to explain something or primarily to license social control; whether involuntary intervention is ever justified by a diagnosis alone; and whether the right response to a category that generates false positives is to reform the category or to abolish it.

Build the nodes on these cases, at minimum: a man whose apathy traces to a visible brain lesion; a woman whose grief has outlasted every culturally sanctioned timetable; a person whose only "symptom" is a preference their society punishes; someone with hallucinations who is untroubled by them and functions well; a person in severe distress who functions impeccably; addiction; and a child whose behaviour is unmanageable in a classroom but unremarkable at home.

Please make sure the tree can reach at least these leaves: the strict view that the whole category is a myth sustained by institutional interests; biomedical realism; the view that the categories are value-laden but can still be genuine dysfunctions; social constructionism; the position that abolishes the category while keeping the clinics, on frankly instrumentalist grounds; and a pluralist view that some categories are myths and others are not. Where a combination of answers is coherent but unoccupied, note it and say briefly whether it is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. Where two leaves cannot be separated by any question you can honestly ask, flag them as such rather than inventing a distinction. I would also like the instrument to expose the hidden evaluative premise at each leaf, since that is the part people never say out loud.

Eight to twelve branch nodes and six to nine leaves, please. Write in British English, and note that no node may be a question about treatment efficacy or aetiology, since people at opposite leaves agree on those and I do not want the tree sorting them by accident. Tell me how confident you are in the taxonomy as a whole, and single out the one branch you are least sure is a genuine distinction rather than a verbal one or an artefact of the cases I chose.
