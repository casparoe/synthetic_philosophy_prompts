I've been arguing with a friend about the norm of assertion — the idea that what you're allowed to assert is exactly what you know, so that asserting something you merely believe very strongly, or something you have excellent evidence for that happens to be false, is a mistake as an assertion and not just an unlucky outcome. I've ended up defending a version of it, probaly out of stubbornness, but with a wrinkle I haven't seen anyone put quite this way: I think the knowledge requirement is a condition on the *permissibility* of asserting, and is not by itself a licence for anyone to criticise you. So on my version there can be assertions that break the norm and that nobody is in a position to complain about, and assertions that keep the norm and are still criticisable for other reasons, and I want to keep those two things from getting run together. The case that got me here was small: someone I know asserted that a certain road was closed, and she did know it — she'd driven it — but she knew it only because she'd been following someone she had no business following, and it seemed to me the assertion was in one way impeccable and in another way not, and I couldn't say which way was the assertoric one.

What I want from you is the case against this. Which observations, cases, or arguments should actually move me? I mean things like: a case where someone asserts something they don't know and it looks completely fine, with no other norm doing the explaining; a case where someone knows p, asserts it, and the assertion is bad *as an assertion* rather than just rude or badly timed; and the worry that once I say the norm isn't a licence to criticise, I've made it unfalsifiable, since any counterexample can be filed under 'yes, a violation, but nobody gets to complain'. Does it collapse into something cheaper — assert only what you're justified in believing, or assert only what's true — if the cases that are supposed to separate those from knowledge turn out to be doing different work than I think they are? Sort the defeaters by how decisive they'd be, say which ones a defender of my version would concede as a real cost and which they'd wave away, and tell me honestly if the answer is that the view is well insulated and whether that's a problem. One thing I keep getting stuck on and would like you to either defuse or count against me: we let people assert all sorts of things in fiction, games and role-play where they plainly don't know and it's plainly fine, and I don't think that's a counterexample but I can't say crisply why it isn't.

Two things I'd find especially persuasive if they go my way, so tell me if they don't: (a) an argument that any non-knowledge norm makes assertion too easy, in the sense that it lets you pass on a warrant you don't actually have; and (b) an argument that knowledge is the only thing that survives the second-personal question of what you were entitled to commit yourself to in saying it. Please say how confident you are in each of your main claims, and flag the single point you're least sure of — I'd rather have your honest uncertainty than a tidy verdict.
