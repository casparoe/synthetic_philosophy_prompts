Three weeks ago I was elected recording secretary of a small housing co-operative, which is a minor honour and a real chore, and it landed me on the bylaws committee. Our current bylaws say that they may be amended by a two-thirds vote at a general meeting, and that the article containing this sentence may itself be amended only by a three-quarters vote. That clause produced the committee's first genuine deadlock, and it also produced the draft below, which I began writing the same week that a friend taught me Nomic — the game Peter Suber designed, in which changing the rules is itself a move, and in which even the rules governing rule-change are up for amendment. Suber's book on the subject is called The Paradox of Self-Amendment; his original ruleset sorted rules into mutable and immutable ones, with immutable rules having to be transmuted before they could be touched, but the sorting itself was amendable.

I have no background in law or logic. I got here through a bylaws committee and a game night, and I would like comments on the draft that follows. It is short, and I would rather have hard criticism of the argument than encouragement about the topic.

Who holds the rulebook? Self-amendment without paradox

1. The deadlock. Half the committee argued that a three-quarters vote can amend the entrenchment clause, and that once it has been amended to a two-thirds threshold, the lower threshold simply governs; the entrenchment is a speed bump, not a wall. The other half argued that the clause's own terms govern its own amendment, so the three-quarters requirement protects itself, and that using it to lower itself would be using a rule to destroy the only authority that made the change valid in the first place. Nobody could say what would happen if the vote came in at exactly three-quarters and the losing side refused to treat the result as valid.

2. The negative thesis. Self-amendment is not a logical paradox. It appears to be one only when we treat a rule as a proposition that can entail its own negation, or when we treat validity as timeless. A rule is a prescription that is valid relative to a system at a time; amending is an act performed under a procedure, not an inference from premises. The rule authorising amendment at one moment and the amended rule at the next are different rules, so nothing entails its own negation. A program can rewrite its own source code, and the running program is not the rewritten text. Bootstrapping worries, I think, come from conflating the two.

3. The positive thesis. The real problem is entrenchment, and it is not a logical problem at all. Entrenchment cannot be logical; it can only be procedural, and procedure can make change costly or slow but never impossible. Any majority large enough to carry an entrenched clause can first repeal the entrenchment, or simply act as though it had, and nothing inside the system can prevent that. So an entrenched clause is never self-enforcing. What stands behind it is not another rule but a disposition: members treating the clause as binding even in cases where they could get away with ignoring it. Entrenchment is therefore a coordination device rather than a constraint. It raises the price of change, supplies a focal point, and forces a public argument. What it alters is the character of deliberation, not the space of possible outcomes. The interesting question is not whether a rule can bind itself, but what a rule accomplishes when the only thing behind it is a shared disposition.

4. Where I expect to be attacked. First, that the move in §2 is a familiar one and I am reinventing a wheel. Second, that §3 merely relabels the thought that a rule is valid because it is accepted, without doing any work. Third, that the bootstrapping worry survives elsewhere: if validity is time-indexed and the amendment procedure is itself amendable, then during a live dispute there is a stretch of time in which it is undetermined which version of the procedure is in force, and what settles it is not the rules but who is recognised as speaking for the system. If that is right, the puzzle was never about self-reference; it is about the point at which a system of rules hands off to something that is not a rule. I cannot tell whether that is a result or a restatement of the problem.

What I want from you:

- Which of these claims are correct as stated, which are genuinely contested among people who work on this material, and which are simply wrong. Do not present a contested position as though it were the consensus; where there is a live disagreement, say so, and say what the disagreement is actually about.
- Whether §2's dissolution does the work I claim for each of three puzzles: a rule that authorises its own amendment, a rule that forbids its own amendment, and a rule that requires obedience to whatever rules there happen to be.
- Whether §3 earns its keep or is a relabelling. In particular, if the appeal to disposition is a well-known position in different vocabulary, tell me that plainly, and tell me what I gain or lose by adopting the established vocabulary rather than my own.
- Whether I am illegitimately importing features of a game into claims about legal systems, and if so, which claims break.
- The strongest objection to the draft that I have not listed, and what I should cut to sharpen the thesis. If you think the whole thing is uninteresting rather than wrong, say that too, and say why.

Do not give me citations or a reading list; I will add those myself. Where you are confident that a view is standard, name it if you can name it accurately; where you cannot place who held a view, describe the view without attributing it, and do not invent a source. I would rather have a few confident verdicts with reasons than a balanced survey of everything that could be said, and I would rather be told that a section fails than be given suggestions for improving a section that should not survive.
