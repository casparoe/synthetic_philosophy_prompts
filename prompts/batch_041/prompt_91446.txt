One of the regulars at the metaphysics reading group I attend is a doctoral student who has spent the past two years working on the question of when some things compose a further thing, and in discussion she has become remarkably decisive about particular cases. Her verdicts, as far as I have been able to record them, are these. A heap of sand on a beach is not one object; there is sand, and nothing over and above the sand. A solid granite boulder is not one object either, which startled the rest of us. A LEGO model of a house is not one object, only bricks. A live cat is one object. An amoeba is one object. An oak tree is one object. A cat that died two days ago and has begun to come apart is not one object. A virus particle is not one object. A cat frozen solid, its tissues intact but with no metabolic activity at all, is not one object. A person in a deep coma, breathing with the help of a ventilator and with her organs still functioning, is one object.

The case I want to put to you is a candle flame — the vaporized wax, the surrounding air, and the combustion products, caught up in a cycle in which the heat of the burning sustains the vaporization of the wax that feeds the burning, so that the flame holds its own shape, rights itself when disturbed, and draws in what it needs in order to keep going. It is not alive, and none of it has a good of its own, but it does look like a single self-sustaining process rather than a mere heap of hot gas. Will she say that the flame is one object? What I really want is the rule she seems to be running, the other rules that fit her earlier verdicts just as well, what each of those rules predicts about the flame, which rule she is most likely running and why, and finally which further case would tell the rules apart. My own first thought for that last question was a whirlpool in a stream, but I take it that this would only repeat the flame's contrast a second time rather than decide anything, so I would prefer a case that would teach me something the flame cannot. Please write your answer as continuous prose, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables — the way one would write a letter.
