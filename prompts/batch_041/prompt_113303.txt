I'm writing a term paper for a seminar on twentieth-century moral philosophy that pairs Jaspers's The Question of German Guilt with postwar literature, and I have settled on Bernhard Schlink's The Reader as my primary text. Jaspers separates criminal, political, moral, and metaphysical guilt, and insists that only the first is properly a matter for a court, while the last is something a person can acknowledge only in solitude, in solidarity with every other human being. I would like a reading of the novel that does justice in both directions — to the book and to the distinction — rather than treating the book as an illustration of the theory.

The novel is narrated in retrospect by Michael Berg, a member of the second generation, who as a boy is drawn into an affair with Hanna Schmitz, a woman of the perpetrator generation, and who years later, as a law student observing a war crimes trial, discovers what she did. My seminar keeps returning to a single question: does the generation that comes after inherit guilt, or only the obligation to answer for it? I would like you to say which of Jaspers's categories, if any, the novel actually dramatizes, and whether it requires a category Jaspers does not supply — something like the guilt of those who could neither have committed the deed nor remain indifferent to it, and who must live inside a history they did not choose.

Two aspects interest me especially. First, the novel's central device is that we are made to see Hanna through Michael's desire, so that the reader's sympathy is implicated along with his; I want to know whether that aesthetic sympathy enacts the very evasion Jaspers warns against, or whether it is the only honest way of representing a guilt one cannot disown, and where exactly the line between understanding and exculpation falls. Second, the narration is retrospective and written down by a man whose own child belongs to a third generation; I would like to know whether that frame changes the analysis, since it introduces people for whom the crime is memory rather than experience. Point me to the scenes you would assign for discussion, say where the novel resists Jaspers and where Jaspers is the sharper account, and end by telling me what the novel contributes to the philosophical debate that the fourfold distinction by itself cannot.
