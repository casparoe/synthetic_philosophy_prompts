Our foundation has been making small, one-time cash grants to individuals in financial distress for three years now, always case by case and without written criteria, and a disagreement on the board has made me suspect that we have quietly been running two different philosophies at once. I would like an outside read on whether our record is coherent and on what it requires of us in the matter now before us.

Our decisions to date:

- We granted funds to an applicant born with a progressive joint disease, to install a stairlift in her home; she was employed and otherwise self-sufficient.
- We denied funds to an applicant who had drained his savings on a collection of vintage wristwatches and then missed a rent payment; he was healthy and employable.
- We granted funds to an applicant who was laid off when his plant closed and needed bridge money to relocate for a new job.
- We denied funds to an applicant who had left a secure job to pursue an unpaid creative project and then ran short.
- We granted funds to an applicant whose chronic illness prevented her from working, to cover ongoing treatment costs.
- We denied funds to an applicant with a comfortable income and no unmet need who asked for money on the ground that he would enjoy having more of it.
- We granted funds to an applicant who described herself as content with her frugal life but whose housing had failed a safety inspection and needed repairs she could not afford.

Two trustees keep pressing rival slogans. One holds that we exist to correct bad luck, not bad choices. The other holds that we fund what a person needs in order to function, not what a person happens to want, and he has already waved off the applicant before us as an instance of the expensive-tastes problem.

The case before us: an applicant was raised from infancy in a household where only a rare and very costly cuisine was served, and she now finds ordinary food nauseating. She is healthy when she forces herself to eat ordinary food, she holds a steady job, and she has close friends; her only complaint is that her life feels gray and joyless without the cuisine, which her income cannot cover. She is asking for a recurring grant.

Tell me, first, which of the two slogans our past decisions actually fit, and whether some third principle fits them better. Then tell me how we should decide the present case if we want to remain consistent with ourselves, which of our earlier decisions is the closest analog, and, if the precedents genuinely underdetermine the matter, which principle we would be adopting by granting and which by denying. I care less about a survey of the literature on distributive justice than about what our own record commits us to. Please answer in American English.
