I'm a science journalist, and next month I am running a ninety-minute evening masterclass on intuitionistic logic and mathematical constructivism for adults who are comfortable with school algebra and have read a little popular mathematics but have never seen a formal proof system. I do not want the explanation itself; I want the plan for giving it. Set out the order in which the ideas must arrive, and why the dependencies run as they do — in particular, what must already be accepted about what a proof is before the meaning of the connectives can be given, and what must be in place about the connectives before the failure of excluded middle can look like a consequence rather than a decree. Then the one example that should carry the session. I would like it to be the theorem that there are irrational numbers a and b with a raised to the power b rational, whose familiar proof turns on whether the square root of two raised to the square root of two is rational — a question that proof itself never settles. My understanding is that the same statement has an elementary constructive proof by an entirely different route; if that is right, the example does double duty, showing that constructivists do not so much deny classical theorems as reject particular proofs of them. Tell me whether it will bear that weight, where in the sequence it belongs, and what to use instead if it will not. Finally, map the misconceptions in the order this audience will form them, with the point in the sequence at which each should be headed off — I expect they will hear intuitionism as a psychological doctrine about mathematical intuition, and will assume that intuitionistic logic is classical logic with a single axiom struck out — together with the one question I should ask at the end to tell whether the point has landed, and what I should openly refuse to cover at this level.
