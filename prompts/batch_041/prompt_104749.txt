I'm presenting to our hospital's ethics committee next month a proposal about advance directives, and I want to be ready for the fight. My line is that the authority of an advance directive doesnt actually come from the earlier self having "jurisdiction" over the later one -- that whole framing is a mistake -- it comes from honoring it being a promise-like practice the patient is the beneficiary of. And I want to argue a directive should bind only when the earlier self did what I'm calling anticipatory identification: actually pictured becoming the demented person, with those specific impairments, and still wanting the directive followed. Most real directives fail that test, so I'm proposing they get weaker default weight than everyone assumes. The room: the chair is an old-school principlist who'll frame everything as autonomy vs. beneficence; a geriatric psychiatrist who thinks the "then-self" is a fiction and the person in front of her is the only patient; a disability rights advocate who's allergic to the assumption that life with dementia is a fate worse than death; and a palliative care doc who's seen how messy these cases really get. Give me the objections I'll actually get from these four, in their vocabulary, ordered by how much damage they do to the proposal, each with a two-minute reply and a note on where the reply is soft. And tell me the one question I'm praying nobody asks.
