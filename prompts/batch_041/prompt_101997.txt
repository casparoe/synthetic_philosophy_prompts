The claim is that when a person will face a long series of bets with known odds, the only rational amount to stake is the fraction of current wealth that maximizes the expected logarithm of final wealth — the fraction that maximizes the geometric growth rate — and that any other fraction is not a different taste but a mistake. I have also read that the argument turns on a distinction between averaging across many parallel copies of yourself and averaging across time for a single person, and that these two averages can point in different directions. I want your credence that the claim is true, as a number between 0 and 1. I do not want a bare number. Please decompose it: how much of your credence depends on reading rational as maximizes expected utility of final wealth rather than as maximizes the almost-sure long-run growth rate; how much depends on whether logarithmic utility of wealth is a substantive fact about rationality or merely one risk preference among many; how much depends on whether the time-average argument establishes a genuine dominance over other fixed fractions rather than a mere tendency to pull ahead; how much depends on whether the argument survives when the number of bets is finite and the person cannot literally bet forever; and how much depends on whether irrational is the right word for someone who understands the argument and still prefers a different risk profile. Give each term a rough probability and a one-line justification, then combine them into an overall number. Finally, tell me which term you are least confident in and what argument or piece of evidence would move the overall number most. My own credence is about 0.65, and I would like to know whether I am more likely wrong about the mathematics or about the word rational.
