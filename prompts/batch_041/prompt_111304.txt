BEGIN STUDENT ANSWER

On the traditional analysis, knowledge is justified true belief. For a subject S to know a proposition p, three conditions must hold: p is true, S believes p, and S is justified in believing p. A Gettier-style case shows that these three conditions can all be met even though S does not know. Consider Mira. Mira sees her friend Ravi drive up in a car and wave from the driver's seat. She forms the belief that Ravi owns a car. In fact, Ravi borrowed that car from a rental agency, but he also owns a car that is currently in the shop. So Mira's belief that Ravi owns a car is true, and she believes it. She is also justified, because seeing someone driving a car is normally good evidence that they own it. Nevertheless, she does not know that Ravi owns a car; her belief is true only by coincidence. So the case shows that justified true belief is not sufficient for knowledge. The problem is that Mira's justification depends on a false lemma, namely that the car Ravi is driving is his. Once we notice this, we should say that Mira was not really justified after all. The real lesson is that the justification condition is too weak: it allows a belief to count as justified even when the evidence for it is disconnected from the truth. The no-false-lemmas condition tries to fix this by requiring that the justification not depend on any falsehood. But that condition is too strong, because almost all of our justified beliefs depend on some false background assumption. A better fix is reliabilism, which says that knowledge is true belief produced by a reliable process. Mira's process—seeing someone in a car and inferring ownership—is not reliable, so reliabilism correctly denies that she knows. Reliabilism also avoids the false-lemma problem entirely, since it does not require the subject to have any particular beliefs about her evidence.
