I'm assembling the question list for a long-form interview on our department's podcast, and I'd like you to draft it. The guest is a philosopher whose forthcoming book defends a broadly Moorean theory of value: she accepts the method of absolute isolation as the test of intrinsic value, she accepts the principle of organic unities, and she agrees with Moore that the greatest goods are the enjoyment of beautiful objects and the affections of persons. Her own contribution is an argument that these two goods have different structures — the value of aesthetic enjoyment is conditional on the beauty of its object, whereas the value of personal affection attaches to the relation itself rather than to any state of the lover — and that Moore's claim that the greatest intrinsic goods are states of consciousness therefore has to be abandoned. I don't want questions that let her simply restate the position: where the framework generates a puzzle, put the puzzle to her directly, and where a standard reply is available, fold the follow-up into the same question so that the reply is already foreclosed. Give me exactly five questions, numbered, each one or two sentences, with no introduction and no closing remarks.

1. Moore's test asks whether a world containing only the item in question would be good; when the item is a beautiful object that no one perceives, does the test return a verdict of intrinsic goodness, and if it does not, is that a discovery about beauty or evidence that the test is tracking something narrower than intrinsic value? If she replies that the test merely reports our considered judgments, ask what prevents it from reporting our prejudices about what is natural.

2. If the value of aesthetic enjoyment is conditional on the beauty of its object, the bearer of value looks like the whole rather than the enjoyment taken as a mental state — does she accept that this falsifies Moore's claim that the greatest goods are states of consciousness? If she answers that the whole simply is a complex state of consciousness, press her on what the conditionality adds that a bare appeal to organic unity would not already supply.

3. On her account, does affection value the friend for the friend's own sake, or does it value the relation, with the friend figuring only as a constituent of a valuable whole? If she says the relation is the bearer of value, ask what the isolation test delivers for a world containing a single person who loves another who does not exist.

4. If the value of a whole is not the sum of the values of its parts, what does the friend contribute to the whole, and what constrains which wholes count as genuine unities — otherwise any whole can be assigned any value by positing one? Ask her to specify what would show that a proposed organic unity is spurious.

5. Since Moore treats goodness as simple and indefinable, what does she say to an interlocutor whose isolation intuitions run the other way, who reports that a world of unperceived beauty is no worse than one of appreciated beauty? Press her on whether her view can call that interlocutor mistaken rather than merely differently constituted, and if it can, on where exactly the mistake lies.
