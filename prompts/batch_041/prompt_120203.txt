I am not a philosopher or an economist, but I have been turning over a puzzle about a driver that I came across, and I have built an argument around it that I cannot accept. The puzzle is this. A driver is on a road with two exits. If he takes the first exit, he gets 0. If he continues past the first, he reaches the second exit; if he takes that one, he gets 4. If he continues past the second, he gets 1. The catch is that he cannot tell the two exits apart. His memory of having passed the first exit is gone, and there is no signpost or landmark that distinguishes them; at each exit, the information available to him is exactly the same. He must choose whether to exit or continue at whichever exit he is at.

I have written out the reasoning that seems to show he cannot rationally choose at all. (1) At any decision point, a rational agent should choose the option with the highest expected value given the information available at that point. (2) If the driver is at the second exit, the information available includes that exiting gives 4 and continuing gives 1, so he should exit. (3) If he is at the first exit, the information available includes that exiting gives 0 and continuing takes him to the second exit, where the rational choice is to exit for 4, so he should continue. (4) Therefore, at the first exit he should continue, and at the second he should exit. (5) But the information available to him is the same at both exits; he cannot tell them apart. (6) So the same information supports both continuing and exiting, which is impossible. (7) Therefore there is no rational choice in this situation, or rationality itself is impossible here. (8) But this is just a man with a bad memory on a road; there must be something rational for him to do. So one of the premises must be false.

I would most like to keep (1), because it seems to me to be little more than a definition of what it is to be rational. But I cannot see which of the other steps to give up. Is the trouble in (1) itself, in the way (2) and (3) describe the information available, or in the move from (2) and (3) to (4)? If I give up (1), what should replace it, and what does the replacement cost me in ordinary cases where I do know which situation I am in? If I keep (1), how do I avoid (6) without simply denying that the driver's situation could ever arise? A friend suggested that the driver should leave a note for himself at the first exit, or drop a pebble, so that at the second exit he would know he had already passed the first. Another friend says he should simply commit to a rule—continue at the first exit and exit at the second—and that the rule will somehow guide him even though he cannot tell which exit he is at. That sounds like magic to me, but I may be missing something. Does either suggestion dissolve the puzzle or just change it into a different one? I would like a step-by-step diagnosis, with a clear statement of which claim is doing the illegitimate work and what each possible repair would cost.
