OK so my friend Maya and I got into a two-hour fight last night about pain asymbolia, which she thinks is a neurological curiosity and I think is basically the whole ballgame for the question of why pain is bad. In case it's not on the top of your head: after a stroke that hits the insula (and in some other cases too), people will report that the stimulus hurts — they can localize it, they can rate how intense it is — but they say it just doesn't bother them, and they often won't even pull their hand away. Pain without the unpleasantness. Fun wrinkle that kept us shouting: the standard clinical definition of pain bakes the unpleasantness right in (roughly, an unpleasant sensory and emotional experience tied to actual or potential tissue damage), so on a literal reading these people aren't in pain at all, which is exactly the move Maya kept making.

Here's what I want. A credence, with the reasoning laid out. The proposition: the badness of an episode of pain — what makes it bad for the one having it — is not carried by the pain sensation itself but by a seperate affective/motivational component, so that in principle there could be a creature in genuine sensory pain for whom that pain is not bad at all. I'm at 0.7 on that. Maya's at 0.15, and her line is that either the patients aren't really in pain (see the definition) or the badness is still there and they're just not acting on it.

Give me your number, then decompose it. My guess at the joints, though rearrange them if you think the real joints are elsewhere: (1) asymbolics are genuinely in pain, i.e. they've got the sensation and not just some non-painful something they're mislabeling; (2) they genuinely lack the aversive/unpleasant bit, rather than having it and failing to act on it; (3) the strong 'not bad at all' reading is right, rather than 'less bad' or 'bad but not motivating'; (4) lesion cases reveal the ordinary structure of pain rather than being a weird re-wiring that tells us nothing about the rest of us; (5) even granting the dissociation, pain's badness is really carried by something else that survives it — it's a signal of tissue damage, it's the kind of state you're supposed to escape — so the dissociation is real but doesn't show what I think it shows. Number for each, one line of why, how they combine, and which one your final number is most sensitive to.

Then: Maya and I are 0.55 apart. Which single sub-question do you think we actually differ on? I have a suspicion it's not the one I'm most unsure about, and I want to know if you agree, because if it is the one I think it is then our arguement isn't really about the patients at all.

And one last thing, a case I made up to test myself — tell me if it moves your numbers. Suppose there was a drug that kept the hurt exactly as intense and exactly as localized, but deleted the minding: no urge to pull away, no oh-god-make-it-stop, just the pure sensation of damage, like a readout. If you had a root canal coming up, would you take it? And does your answer there tell us anything about whether pain is bad in itself, or is that a different question wearing the same clothes?
