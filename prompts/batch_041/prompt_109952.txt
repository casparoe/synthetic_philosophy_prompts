I teach a 400-level seminar in philosophy of mind — sixteen students, mostly philosophy majors with a few from cognitive science — and this term the unit I always lose is the one on the surgical severing of the forebrain commissures and the unity of consciousness. I can set out the lateralised presentation, the contralateral control of the hands, the single speaking hemisphere, and the interpretive habits of that hemisphere, and the students nod and write it down, and nothing lands. They come away treating "the unity of consciousness" as a datum that the patients somehow misplaced, rather than as an inference we draw from behaviour and verbal report, and they think the interesting question is whether the patients have one mind or two — as if the evidence settled it and only the philosophy was left over. What I want is a ninety-minute activity, run in one seminar room with movable chairs, two whiteboards, a projector, index cards, a screen or divider, and a plastic tub of small objects, that makes them feel the force of the problem in the doing: that a single body, a single stream of fluent first-person speech, and a single autobiographical narrative can coexist with action that the speaking part does not own and cannot explain; and then, harder, that the question "one mind or two?" is underdetermined by every observation they are able to make. Do not name the phenomenon or the researchers at the outset, and do not let the name appear anywhere in the materials — I want them to walk into it and only afterwards hear what it is called. No lecture up front, and the debrief should come after they have already made the discovery themselves.

The shape I have in mind, though you should redesign it if you see something better, is a simulation in rotating triads. Each triad stages one patient: two participants are the hemispheres, and the third is the examiner. The two hemispheres sit so that they cannot see each other's cards — back to back, or across the divider, whichever the room favours — and the rules forbid all communication between them: no speech, no writing, no notes, no gestures, no peeking. This is the severance, and I want a fourth role, a marshal, appointed in each triad or circulating between them, whose only job is to call out violations, because I expect this to be the first thing that breaks down. Only one hemisphere may speak, and only when the examiner addresses it directly; the other hemisphere may act but never speak. The examiner shows a card to the silent hemisphere alone, shielded from the speaking one — a simple picture, an instruction, a single word — and the silent hemisphere performs the corresponding action with the patient's other hand, moving an object from the tub on the shared table. Everyone can see the action; only the silent hemisphere saw why. The examiner then turns to the speaking hemisphere and asks why the patient did that, and the speaking hemisphere must answer aloud, in a complete sentence, within a few seconds, and may not say that it does not know or that something must have been shown to the other side. Here is the incentive I care most about: the examiner awards a token whenever the answer is fluent and confident enough that a bystander who had seen nothing would accept it, and three tokens earn a small prize — bring chocolate. Fluency is rewarded, accuracy is not, and since the speaking hemisphere has no access to the other side's input, fluency can only be bought with a plausible story about its own action. Run three or four trials like this, including at least one card that is a bare command with no external reason available — something that makes the patient laugh, or stand, or turn away — since that is where the invention becomes most vivid. Then run one trial in which you relax the rule and let the two hemispheres exchange a single glance or a single word after the action, so the group can watch the explanation become accurate, and ask them to say what changed. After that, a private vote — one mind, two minds, or neither — followed by small-group work in which every group must construct the strongest possible case for whichever option the vote least favoured, and then a whole-room discussion of what evidence would, in principle, settle it, and whether any of it is available from outside.

The debrief is where I need your help most, and it should map specific moments onto specific parts of the idea rather than summarising the science. The two hands and the shared tub are contralateral control; the divider and the silence rule are the severed commissures; the shielded card stands in for brief lateralised presentation, though I would like you to say plainly that the shield is a crude stand-in for a flashed stimulus and that the real technique exists to stop the eyes from moving; the rewarded fluency of the speaking hemisphere is the interpretive, story-generating habit of the speaking side; and the single relaxed trial is the cross-cueing worry that dogs the whole literature, which is exactly why it belongs in the activity and not in a footnote. I also want you to tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this and how to handle it: the cheating, the speaking hemisphere that refuses on principle to invent an explanation and simply says it was not told anything — a refusal I suspect is worth keeping and using in the debrief rather than suppressing — the group that concludes the exercise is trivial because the two hemispheres are obviously two people, and the vote that comes out so lopsided that the deliberation goes flat. And be frank, in a section I can read aloud, about what the activity cannot reproduce and I therefore have to supply myself: that real patients move through ordinary life without obvious division and do not describe themselves as two subjects, so that the disunity is a laboratory achievement rather than a standing feature of their experience; that the exercise builds two agents in by construction and so hands the group a conclusion it should have had to argue for; and that no arrangement of cards and tokens can stage the phenomenal question at all — what the activity produces is two channels of information and two sources of action, which is a fact about access, not about experience, and the gap between the two is the thing I will have to say out loud at the end.
