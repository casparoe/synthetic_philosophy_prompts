I am grading the following exam question from an intermediate course on induction and confirmation, and I would like a point-by-point assessment of the student's answer rather than a general impression. Please assign a grade out of 10 and then give the specific comments that justify it: what the answer gets right, what it gets wrong, and how serious each error is. Where the answer reaches a defensible conclusion by an unsound route, or commits a small but consequential slip, say so explicitly.

Exam question. Goodman introduced the predicate 'grue' to raise a problem for induction. (a) State the grue hypothesis and explain why, before the relevant time, the evidence so far confirms 'all emeralds are green' and 'all emeralds are grue' equally well. (b) Explain the resolution Goodman himself proposes, and say what kind of fact that resolution appeals to. (c) A chemist has long been characterizing a newly isolated element, call it kryon, which has so far always been found to be a semiconductor. A colleague proposes the predicate 'semiconductive*': a sample is semiconductive* just in case it is semiconductive when tested before the year 2100 and insulating when tested from 2100 onward. The colleague claims that Goodman's lesson entitles us to treat 'all kryon is semiconductive*' as no worse supported than 'all kryon is semiconductive'. Evaluate the colleague's claim, and assess whether Goodman's own resolution settles it.

Student's answer. Goodman's grue hypothesis is that every emerald is grue, where an object is grue if and only if it is examined before some fixed time t and is green, or examined at or after t and is blue. Before t, every emerald we have examined has been green, and because all of those examinations happened before t, every one of them was also grue. So the evidence that all examined emeralds are green confirms 'all emeralds are green' and 'all emeralds are grue' to exactly the same degree, even though the two hypotheses disagree about emeralds we have not yet examined: the first predicts they will be green, the second that they will be blue. This is what makes the riddle new. It is not the old Humean worry about whether the future will resemble the past; it is that even if we grant that the future will resemble the past, the evidence does not tell us which of two incompatible ways of resembling it to project.

Goodman's own solution is that the two predicates are not on a par because 'green' is entrenched and 'grue' is not. Entrenchment is a matter of how often a predicate has actually figured in projections that have stood up; 'green' has a long record of such use and 'grue' has none, so we are justified in projecting green rather than grue. This shows that grue fails to pick out a natural kind: it is a disjunctive, gerrymandered predicate, whereas green carves nature at its joints.

In the chemist's case, the colleague is right that the two hypotheses are equally confirmed by the tests done so far, since every tested sample was tested before 2100 and so was both semiconductive and semiconductive*. But the conclusion that they are no worse supported does not follow. Goodman's criterion directs us to compare entrenchment: 'semiconductive' is an established predicate of physics with a track record, while 'semiconductive*' is a fresh coinage, so we should project the former. This is a fact about our linguistic habits rather than about kryon itself, which is why the matter is pragmatic: if physicists took up 'semiconductive*' and it kept delivering results, it would become entrenched in its turn, and we could then project it.
