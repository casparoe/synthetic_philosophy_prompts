I teach a 75-minute session of an upper-level undergraduate seminar on free will and moral responsibility; there are about sixteen students, and I want a runnable classroom activity that makes them feel the regress at the heart of Galen Strawson's Basic Argument before I ever name the argument or its author. The activity should do the work: by the time we debrief, the students should have run into the impossibility themselves, in the middle of a game, and should be annoyed about it.

Please design the activity in full: materials, roles, rules, a scoring scheme that needs no money, a minute-by-minute timeline for the 75 minutes, and a debrief.

My sketch, which you should feel free to improve or discard: run a staged responsibility tribunal over a fictional case of my own invention, so that nothing rides on a famous example the students may half-remember. A defendant has done something plainly wrong, and the question before the room is not whether the act was bad but whether the defendant is the ultimate source of the act in the strong sense that blame would require. One side must build a ledger from the act backward, explaining what made the agent act as they did. Every entry they enter must itself be explained by something already on the ledger, and the ledger can only be closed if someone plays a founding move that is not itself explained by anything earlier. I want a deck of influence cards (temperament, upbringing, an unlucky encounter, a prior choice, and so on) and a rule that makes the founding move expensive and immediately challengeable: if a team plays it, the other team may ask what made the agent capable of that founding move, and the founding move comes off the ledger. I expect the ledger never to close, and I want the rules arranged so that this is a discovery the students make, not a fact I announce.

The debrief should map each stage of the game onto a step of the argument, say plainly which premise the game makes feel irresistible and which premise it quietly assumes on the players' behalf, and distinguish what the game shows from what it merely stages. It should also include a second, shorter round with a changed win condition, so that the students see that the everyday practice of holding people answerable survives the collapse of the stronger notion, and can say exactly what the difference between the two is.

Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this and how to handle it, and be frank about which feature of the argument the activity cannot reproduce and that I will have to supply myself.

In the written materials and the debrief notes, separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who work on this, and do not present a contested reading as the consensus.
