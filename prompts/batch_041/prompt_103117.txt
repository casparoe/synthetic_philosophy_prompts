I run a small reading group on the philosophy of economics, and we have spent the past month on Friedman's F-twist — the doctrine, from his 1953 essay on the methodology of positive economics, that a theory's assumptions need not be realistic in any descriptive sense, that a hypothesis is to be judged by the accuracy and scope of its predictions rather than by the truth of its premises, and even that the more significant a theory is, the more wildly inaccurate its assumptions are likely to be (Samuelson coined the name F-twist for the view). Half the group finds this liberating — the standard illustration being the firm that behaves as if it equated marginal revenue and marginal cost even though no one in the firm has ever performed the calculation — and half thinks it is a licence to say anything at all provided the regression fits. I would like to tease the second half, and I have decided the most efficient way is a parody.

Please write, in roughly 650 words, the Aims and Scope and Instructions to Authors of a fictional journal — call it the Journal of As-If Economics — in which the F-twist has been fully institutionalized as editorial policy. Format it as numbered clauses in the deadpan register of a real style guide, and give it the ordinary furniture of such a document: a statement of scope, submission requirements, review criteria, a specimen abstract or reviewer checklist if it helps, and a short section on conflicts of interest. The humour should come entirely from the content rather than from winking asides. Let the policy follow its own logic off a cliff: authors might be required to declare an Assumption Unrealism Index and to justify any assumption that turns out to be true; a model whose assumptions are all descriptively accurate might be desk-rejected as degenerate, on the grounds that it then merely restates the data; reviewers might be instructed to disregard evidence about what agents actually do or think, since such evidence speaks only to descriptive accuracy and not to predictive performance; a special issue on maximally false assumptions might be announced with a submission deadline; and interviewing one's own subjects might be treated as a disclosure-worthy conflict of interest. It should be legible to a reader who has never heard of Friedman, and funny to someone who has: each clause should be a straight-faced exaggeration of something a genuine defender of the F-twist could actually be brought to say, rather than a strawman, and a methodologist reading it should feel that the author knows exactly what is being satirized. The target is the practice and its rhetoric, not any named individual, living or dead, and the piece should never step outside its institutional voice to explain the joke.
