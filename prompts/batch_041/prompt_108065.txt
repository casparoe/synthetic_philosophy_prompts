I sit on the health and wellbeing scrutiny panel of our county council. I came to it through the patient forum rather than through politics — I am a retired district nurse — so I have had to learn this sort of argument on my feet, and I would rather get this one right than win it. Next Tuesday the panel votes on whether to renew the falls-prevention programme that sends a physiotherapist into the homes of residents over eighty: three years, roughly four hundred thousand pounds, out of the same pot that pays for the school nursing contract, so the vote is genuinely contested and the numbers are not comfortable either way. One of my fellow members, a co-opted academic who is otherwise very sensible, has raised the same objection at the last two meetings, and it has unsettled two of the newer members. His argument is that the programme is pointless whichever way things go. Either it is already true, now, that a given resident will fall next winter — in which case nothing our physiotherapist does can alter it — or it is already true that she will not fall, in which case there is nothing to prevent. On his view we cannot make a difference to an outcome that is already settled one way or the other, so the honest course is to admit that we are funding this for appearances rather than because it works. Someone told me afterwards that this is an old puzzle, something to do with a sea battle and whether a prediction of it can be true before it happens, which did not help me much, because our own risk model hands us statements of exactly that kind: the spreadsheet we were given says this resident has a thirty per cent chance of a fall, and that resident a five per cent chance, and I cannot see how we are supposed to use those numbers if we are not allowed to say that any statement about next winter is true or false yet.

I have to write the panel's written opinion — the document that goes into the papers and that the cabinet member will actually read — and I want it to answer him properly rather than simply outvote him. Please draft it: one page, recommendation first, with a figure and a time frame. I am inclined to fund the programme, but I would rather you told me what the argument actually supports; if the honest answer is 'fund it on a shorter cycle' or 'fund it with an independent evaluation attached', say so and say why, and if it is 'do not fund it', say that too. Then the reasoning, in a form my colleagues can follow without any background in this: what is right in his argument, where exactly it goes wrong, and what turns on the difference between 'it is already true that she will fall' and 'nothing she or we can do will make any difference' — I have a suspicion that the word 'settled' is doing work it has not earned, but I cannot put my finger on where. I would also like you to sort out the business with the risk scores, because either the future is open, in which case I do not understand how a thirty per cent chance can be true, or it is not, in which case I do not understand why the physiotherapist is worth paying. Set out the strongest version of his case before you answer it, since two members are close to voting with him on the strength of it, and finish with what the panel should say to the physiotherapy team, who have heard the argument second-hand and are worried we are about to cut them. Please write it in British English.
