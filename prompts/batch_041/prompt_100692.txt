Ok, longtime lurker on the rationalist blogs here, and I've finally written down an argument I keep half-remembering from old evidence threads, and now I can't find my way out of it. Here it is, as clean as I can make it.

(1) Bayesian confirmation says evidence E confirms hypothesis H exactly when P(H|E) > P(H).
(2) Bayes' theorem gives P(H|E) = P(E|H)P(H)/P(E). So if P(E) = 1, then P(H|E) = P(H), and E confirms nothing.
(3) Anything I actually know -- the reading on the dial, the perihelion of Mercury, whatever -- I know it, so for me P(E) = 1.
(4) So nothing I already know can confirm anything.
(5) And this isn't a one-off: every bit of evidence starts unknown and then becomes known, so as my stock of evidence grows, its power to confirm anything decays toward zero. The more I know, the less any of it supports.
(6) So the standard story that general relativity was confirmed by Mercury's perihelion has to be wrong -- that anomaly was in the tables before Einstein, so on this picture it was inert.
(7) A theory of confirmation on which one of the best-confirmed theories in physics was never confirmed by its most famous piece of evidence is a bad theory.

So something has to go. I'd like to keep (1) and (2); those are just Bayes, and I don't see how you run confirmation theory without them. I've heard people mumble "well just never be 100% sure of anything empirical" as the fix, but that feels like a dodge -- alot of the time I really am sure, and I want to know what the theory says about *that* case, not just get told to be less confident.

Which step do I reject, and what does rejecting it cost? If it's (3), then tell me what the actual probability of something I'm certain of is supposed to be and why it isn't 1. If it's somewhere else, tell me where the slide happens and what else I have to give up to plug it -- I suspect the real move is that E is doing two different jobs in (1) and (6), but I honestly can't say which. Price out the options for me.
