I'm putting together a handout for a grad reading group on expressivism, and I need you to build me one case, not explain the literature to me. Take the role of someone who knows the quasi-realist tradition cold, and think carefully before you write — brainstorm four or five candidate embeddings first, then commit to the best one. Don't give me a bibliography, and don't tell me what other people have said about this; the case has to stand on its own.

Set-up, so we're on the same page: on the expressivist story, a moral sentence — say one saying that lying is wrong — doesn't describe anything, it expresses a conative attitude (disapproval, a plan, acceptance of a norm, pick your poison). The Frege-Geach squeeze is that the same sentence shows up in unasserted positions, in an antecedent or under negation or inside an attitude report, where nobody's expressing anything, and arguments built out of it are still obviously valid.

Build me a case that squeezes exactly there. Specs:

- It's got to be something a real person could actually say — a short exchange between two people, ordinary speech, not a toy formalism. And not the lying / little-brother conditional that's in every intro textbook; renaming it doesn't count, and neither does swapping in a different sin.
- The inference has to be valid in a way no competent speaker would deny, and valid in a way the naive expressivist reading breaks: read the two premises as attitudes and you get two different attitudes rather than one claim occurring twice, so the argument looks like an equivocation.
- The moral term has to occur somewhere it can't be expressing anything, and the validity has to depend on that occurrence — if I delete it, the argument sails through and the case is dead.
- And if you can make the negation side bite in the same case, so that whatever fix handles the embedded occurance doesn't automatically tell you what the negated form expresses, even better.
- Whole scenario under 250 words.

The point of the exercise is whether the expressivist's compositional story can actually be told, so the case has to generate the squeeze rather than announce it. Then do the check, and be honest.

Format it like this:

1. The case — 200-250 words, just the exchange or scenario, no commentary.
2. The check — the argument in explicit form, then for each premise what the expressivist says it expresses, then exactly where the validity is supposed to be lost. ~300 words.
3. The repair, and whether it works — the best expressivist fix you can come up with (higher-order attitudes, minimalism about truth, whatever), then your verdict on whether it saves the case or just moves the bump somewhere else. ~250 words.
4. What's doing the work — name the single feature that makes the case bite, and say what happens if I remove it. ~150 words.
5. The expressivist hits back — in their voice, the strongest objection to the case itself, then whether you think it lands. ~200 words.
