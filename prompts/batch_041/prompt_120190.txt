I have no training in philosophy, but for about two years a friend and I have been arguing about what makes a life meaningful, and the argument has hardened into two positions neither of us can talk the other out of. I have since learned that something like my side was argued by Moritz Schlick in a 1927 essay on the meaning of life: roughly, that the activities which make a life meaningful are the ones we do for their own sake, the way we play, rather than for what they produce, and that the insistence on a purpose beyond the activity is itself a main source of the emptiness people complain about. My friend holds the opposite: that an activity is meaningful only if it aims at something beyond itself — a result, a contribution, another person's good — and that doing something for its own sake is either a description of intense absorption, which is not the same as meaning, or a way of avoiding the question of what the activity is for.

I would like you to write the document we should have produced by now: the joint statement of an adversarial collaboration between these two camps, as if we had spent a year working in good faith and were now writing up what we could both sign. Please organize it as follows.

First, a numbered list of agreed claims: what the word meaning is being asked to do here; what both sides can accept about the experience of absorption; whether both sides must agree that many activities are done for their own sake and aimed at results at the same time; what neither side needs to deny about boredom, craft, care, or routine. Keep these claims substantive rather than safe — an agreement that costs neither side anything is not worth listing.

Second, a statement of the remaining disagreement, with each side's position phrased in wording that side would accept as its own.

Third, the cruxes: the sub-questions such that settling one would move at least one side. I suspect some of them are whether doing something for its own sake and doing it for the sake of an outcome are exclusive descriptions at all; whether a person's own felt sense of meaninglessness is decisive evidence about meaning; whether the distinction belongs to the activity or to the attitude brought to it; and whether a life can be play all the way through or only in intervals. For each crux, give a concrete case from ordinary life that both sides would agree bears on it — a craftsperson making objects that get used, someone practicing an instrument with no audience, a person tending a garden they will not live to see, care for someone who will not recover, a game played for hours — and record what each side predicts about that case, in advance, before saying who seems right.

Fourth, wherever the dispute is merely verbal, say which part and why. If the two sides turn out to be answering different questions under one heading — one about what makes an activity worth doing, the other about what makes a life add up to something — say so.

Two requests about how to handle what you know. Separate throughout what is settled from what is genuinely contested, both among careful readers of Schlick and among people who think about this topic generally, and do not present a contested reading as the consensus; if you think one of the two positions misreads him, give that as your judgment and mark it as such. And do not give me a reading list or citations, since I have no way to check them.
