I spent thirty years as a field biologist before retiring, and only lately have I turned to philosophy; the reference class problem caught my attention because it formalizes a difficulty I met constantly in assigning probabilities to singular events. I would like you to construct a thought experiment to specification.

The case: a single, clearly individuated event belonging to two reference classes, one nested within the other, with the available statistics pointing in opposite directions. Arrange it so that the narrower, more specific class is the more relevant one but is supported only by a small, unreliable sample, while the broader class contains it, is statistically robust, and is correspondingly less informative. The setting should be realistic — medical, actuarial, or ecological, not science-fictional — and the scenario itself must fit within a single paragraph. It must be a genuine feature of the case that no larger sample for the narrow class exists, rather than one merely left unconsulted.

Then, in no more than a further four hundred words: (i) state the verdict and the reasoning for the principle that one should always reason from the narrowest available reference class; (ii) do the same for a reliability- or shrinkage-based approach that lets the robust broader estimate pull the answer toward itself; and (iii) do the same for the subjectivist view that direct inference is not objective and that the "problem" is really a choice of prior. Identify the one feature of the case that makes these verdicts diverge, say what becomes of the divergence when that feature is removed, and explain how a defender of position (i) would argue that the case shows nothing. The point of the exercise is to determine whether the tension between relevance and reliability is a genuine dilemma for direct inference or merely an artifact of rules that leave too much unspecified, so the case must generate its verdicts from the positions rather than stipulate them.
