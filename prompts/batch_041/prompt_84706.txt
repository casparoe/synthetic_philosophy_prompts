I am part of an interdisciplinary reading group — philosophers, statisticians, and a couple of machine-learning researchers — that has spent the term on Keynes's Treatise on Probability and the chapters of the General Theory concerned with long-term expectation. We keep running into a disagreement that we cannot settle from the texts alone, and I would like you to write a sustained commentary that adjudicates it. The disagreement concerns a contemporary research programme we have been calling layered uncertainty forecasting. It distinguishes four layers: first, ordinary risk, where a well-defined chance mechanism generates outcomes; second, parametric or model uncertainty, where the form of the data-generating process is assumed but its parameters must be estimated from finite samples; third, structural uncertainty, where the relevant variables, mechanisms, or even the space of possible outcomes are themselves contested; and fourth, evidential weight, understood as the completeness and relevance of the evidence supporting a probability judgement, as distinct from the sharpness or calibration of that judgement. The programme builds forecasting systems — some of them using large language models, prediction markets, or ensembles of neural networks — that aim to output a probability, an interval, and a separate weight score for each forecast. Its proponents claim that this layered approach is a natural formalisation of Keynes's own distinction between probability and weight, and of his insistence that uncertainty need not be numerical or even comparable across cases. Its critics claim that the approach is a category mistake: for Keynes, probability is a logical relation between evidence and hypothesis, not a psychological or algorithmic output, and the attempt to automate it in this way betrays the very point of his view.

I would like you to write a commentary, in the persona of a careful intellectual historian and philosopher of probability, that predicts what Keynes would have thought of this programme and of the wider set of posthumous developments it represents. Please address, in whatever order seems most illuminating: (1) whether layered uncertainty forecasting is a faithful extension or a distortion of his position; (2) what he would say about calibration, sharpness, and scoring rules as norms for probabilistic judgement; (3) whether an AI system could possess weight of argument in his sense, or whether that notion is essentially tied to a human enquirer's total evidence; (4) how he would advise a central bank, public health agency, or climate assessment body that had to act on such forecasts; and (5) whether his views on probability and uncertainty commit him to any particular stance on the politics of expertise — for example, on who should be trusted to interpret model output. I am not asking for a survey of the secondary literature, and please do not provide citations, reading lists, or bibliographies; reason from the concepts and arguments themselves. Where you attribute a view to Keynes, make clear whether it follows directly from his stated positions, is a plausible extrapolation, or is a more speculative reconstruction, and say what feature of his work would settle the matter. Please write in British English, using British spelling and punctuation, and aim for about 1,200 to 1,500 words.
