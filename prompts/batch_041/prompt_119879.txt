I'm a retired actuary and I chair the three-person mediation panel that has to recommend a split under our city's new shared-savings agreement with the regional water utility. The deal is that whatever efficiency gains the retrofit program produces get divided between the utility and the ratepayers, and if the two sides can't agree we're supposed to hand them a number. I've been reading up on bargaining theory to prepare and I've basically fallen for the Kalai-Smorodinsky solution: you find each side's best possible payoff (the utility's max retained savings, the ratepayers' max relief), take the no-deal point as the origin, draw the straight line out to that ideal corner, and recommend wherever the line cuts the efficiency frontier. The story is clean — each side gets a share proportional to how much it could plausibly get on its own best day, and nobody gets punished for having a weaker fallback than the other. It also happens to be indifferent to the units, which matters here because one axis is dollars of ratepayer relief and the other is dollars of avoided capital spending, and the two sides keep insisting those aren't the same kind of dollar.

The trouble is my two co-panellists. One keeps saying we should just use the Nash solution because it's the standard one and because it satisfies independence of irrelevant alternatives — his arguement being that our recommendation shouldn't flip if some new option turns up elsewhere in the set that has nothing to do with the two of them; and honestly I can see the force of that, because we've just had a late-breaking third option appear (a low-flow rebate tier) that only raises the utility's ceiling, and under my preferred approach that alone moves the number we'd recommend, which the utility's counsel is already calling a windfall. The other panellist just wants 50/50 and says all the axioms are rationalization. On top of that the frontier isn't a clean curve: there's a fixed treatment-plant upgrade that has to be paid no matter what, so the feasible set has a dent in the middle and the straight line out to the ideal point seems to land somewhere that's actually dominated by a point slightly off to the side of it, which I suspect means the whole construction needs a patch. And the utility keeps hinting it can make the status quo worse for the ratepayers, which would move the origin and change our answer, and I can't tell whether that's a legitimate feature of the approach or a bug in it.

So: I have to circulate a written opinion before Thursday's hearing. Draft it for me — a clear recommended course (a number, or a rule for getting one, plus a time frame for the panel to issue it), the reasoning behind it, and an honest engagement with the independence-of-irrelevant-alternatives objection rather than just waving it off, since that's the strongest thing on the other side and the utility will quote it back at us. Also tell me what to do about the dent and about whether the disagreement point should be allowed to move once the sides start maneuvering — I'd rather the opinion say plainly where the approach breaks and how we patch it than pretend it doesn't. Keep it something I can actually read aloud to two hostile parties.
