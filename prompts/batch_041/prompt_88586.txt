I am a first-year PhD student in logic who came to this after two years working on the kernel of an interactive proof assistant, and I have just finished a seminar on intuitionistic logic and constructive mathematics that has left me with a case I cannot get straight. The seminar presented constructivism as a doctrine about what a proof is and what a proof of an existence claim must deliver: on the Brouwer–Heyting–Kolmogorov reading, the meaning of a statement is given by what counts as a proof of it, a proof of an existential claim or a disjunction must exhibit the witness or the disjunct, and the law of excluded middle is not available where it would be applied to a predicate over an infinite domain that is not decidable in principle. What I keep hearing, in the same breath, is that the controversy over the 1976 Appel and Haken computer proof of the four colour theorem vindicates this outlook, since the proof is an enormous case analysis that no human being can survey and therefore, on the constructivist view, is not a proof at all. A classmate calls the theorem a reductio of classical mathematics; another calls it a triumph of constructivism. I suspect both are conflating things, and I want you to work the case out properly rather than give me a survey of the literature.

The mapping I want runs in one direction first. Take the constructivist account of proof seriously as a theory of what the Appel and Haken proof is and does: the argument is a finite check over a finite list of reducible configurations, the case distinctions are decidable, and nothing turns on excluded middle over an infinite domain. On the face of it, then, the theory should have no complaint about the proof; if anything it should regard it as more tractable than the usual informal argument, since every step is finitistic and checkable in principle. Say whether that is right, and whether the constructive credentials of the proof survive the 1997 simplification by Robertson, Sanders, Seymour and Thomas and the 2005 formalisation by Gonthier in a general-purpose proof assistant. Then identify what the actual objection was, in its own terms: it was about surveyability, about the impossibility of a human being grasping the argument, about whether the result is known a priori or trusted on the authority of a machine, and about mathematical understanding rather than mathematical content. Tell me whether constructivism has the resources to state that objection at all, or whether the objection belongs to a different family of views that has been rhetorically attached to constructivism.

Then run the case back against the theory. If constructivism is silent about the surveyability objection, does that count against it as a philosophy of mathematics, or only against the rhetorical use of it? Distinguish, as sharply as you can, three things the word constructive is used to mean: the meaning-theoretic thesis that proofs are constructions, the existence thesis that a proof of an existence claim must yield a witness, and the epistemic thesis that a proof must be humanly surveyable or intelligible; then say which of the three, if any, the four colour theorem actually threatens. Test the resulting view against two contrasts I take to be relevant: the classification of finite simple groups, whose proof is not computer-assisted but which no single person has read end to end, and the Kepler conjecture, whose 1998 proof by Hales was accepted on the strength of a very long computation and was only formally verified in 2014. Which verdicts does constructivism give in these cases, and does it give them for the right reasons? Finally, tell me what it would take for the constructivist position to accommodate the surveyability worry without simply collapsing into strict finitism, and what case would show that the accommodation fails. I am not looking for citations or a reading list; I want the argument, and where the theory runs out, an honest statement that it does.
