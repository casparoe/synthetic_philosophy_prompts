I run a philosophy workshop in my department — mostly grad students, there's a few faculty, around 16 people, and I've got 75 minutes with them. I want to spend it on the utility monster, but I don't want to just describe the thought experiment and then take a poll on whether people would feed everyone to the monster. I want them to build the monster themselves out of a game that looks boring and fair at the start, and I only want to say the words "utility monster" once we're well into the debrief. No lecture up front.

Here's a rough shape I have in mind, but improve it if you see something better. Everyone gets a card with a secret number on it — that's how many "points" they get per token they end up holding. The group has a fixed pool of tokens to divide (say 60 of them), and the stated goal is to arrange the split so the total points across the whole group come out as high as possible. Most people's numbers are small and boring, like 1 through 5. One card has a huge number on it. The wrinkle I want to sneak in: for that one person the points are superlinear — the more tokens they're holding, the more each extra token is worth to them — so the total-maximizing split is to hand them basically the whole pool. Then in the debrief I want to flip that person's utility to diminishing returns and show them the monster shrinks or vanishes depending on a shape detail of the utility function that nobody on the outside could ever see.

What I need from you:

- the rules, written so a facilitator could run it cold without me in the room
- the payoff scheme, no real money — points on a whiteboard are fine
- how to keep the numbers hidden during play and then reveal them so the reveal actually lands instead of feeling like a gotcha
- a 15–20 minute debrief that maps what just happened onto the real idea, including where they'll want to say "well obviously we wouldn't do that" and why that reflex is the interesting move and not a dodge
- what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle it
- be honest about what the game can't reproduce about the monster, and that I'll just have to tell them that part

One more thing: I want the debrief to also get at the fact that this same setup gives completely different answers depending on what you're maximizing — total points, the worst-off person, the number of people above some line — and that the monster is really a symptom of one specific rule and not of "aggregation" as such. Don't let them walk out thinking the monster is just a silly trick.
