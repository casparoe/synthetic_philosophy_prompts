I teach a graduate seminar on rule-following, and I have grown tired of the way the Kripke–Wittgenstein paradox gets domesticated: students become fluent about plus and quus and never have to say what any of it amounts to outside arithmetic. For the final session I want to run the whole apparatus against a case where the rule was not mathematical, the stakes were enormous, and the community that is supposed to do the work of the skeptical solution was conspicuously failing to agree. I have settled on the Florida recount of 2000.

Write the analysis I will present. Take the following as given, since I do not want the session to turn on my students' recall: Florida law directed the county canvassing boards to determine the voter's intent; a punch-card ballot may be counted or not depending on whether the board treats a dimpled or a partially detached chad as evidence of that intent, and the counties did not all treat it alike; the contest ran for five weeks; it ended when the Supreme Court, dividing five to four, held that the use of different standards across counties denied equal protection, and stopped the recount on that ground; the opinion said its holding was limited to the present circumstances; and the certified margin was in the hundreds of votes.

Then do four things.

First, state the paradox as it applies here. Not the slogan about indeterminacy, but the specific claim: that no fact about a counter's training, dispositions, or past usage settles whether a dimpled chad is a vote, in the way that no fact about my past usage settles whether I meant addition or quus — the function that agrees with addition on every pair of numbers that has figured in my practice and diverges beyond it. Say what work the invented function does in the argument, and why the structure transfers to a rule about paper.

Second, keep the skeptical paradox apart from the skeptical solution, and say what the solution licenses: not the recovery of a determinate content the rule had all along, but a move to the conditions under which an attribution of a rule is assertible, and an appeal to agreement in a shared practice. Then judge the episode. Did the recount supply a community whose agreement could ground the attribution, or a community with no shared practice to appeal to — and if the latter, does the case suggest that agreement in the community yields conformity rather than correctness, which is the objection usually pressed against the solution?

Third, take the objection that this is a case of epistemic rather than semantic indeterminacy: that the rule was perfectly determinate and the trouble was that no one could see, from the ballot, whether it had been satisfied. Say what turns on that distinction, and whether it survives contact with a rule whose application consists in inspecting a physical mark.

Fourth, say where the apparatus runs out. A demand for one uniform standard is either a demand that there be a rule or a demand that there be a fact of the matter, and the holding does not tell us which; an opinion confined to the present circumstances is an odd object for a theory that locates the rule in a community's continuing to go on in the same way. If the skeptical solution cannot handle the case, say what account of the rule's determinacy would, and what it would have to claim about the ballots.

Keep the mapping disciplined: fix once and for all who counts as the rule-follower — the individual counter, the county board, or the legal community — and do not let the answer drift into generalities about interpretation. I have no use for citations; I want the case worked. Close by telling me how confident you are in each of the main claims, and which one you are least sure of.
