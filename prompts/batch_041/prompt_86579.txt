I've been reading rationalist blogs and their comment threads for years, and I keep running into a pattern I don't have a name for. Two instances, both of which I've watched happen to people I know.

A friend joined a religious community whose members insist the practice only works for those who believe in it. He joined because he wanted it to work. Five years on he still doesn't believe, and the members tell him this is exactly what they'd expect: you can't believe on purpose, and wanting the payoff is the surest evidence that you don't have the belief.

A colleague spent years trying to become the sort of person who gives without keeping score. Every step she took toward it was taken for a reason, which made it, she said, just more scorekeeping; when she stopped trying, she stopped improving.

The obvious neighbour is the old puzzle about the billionaire who pays you if, at midnight tonight, you intend to drink a poison tomorrow afternoon, and puts the money in your account before you have to drink it; I know the standard replies — form the intention and then change your mind, or arrange in advance to be the kind of agent who can't reconsider. Neither reply touches my two cases, because in neither is there a decision to make and later reverse: one of those states can't be entered by trying at all, and the other can only be entered by someone who isn't trying.

What I'm after isn't a label so much as a carving. Is there an established concept for this family — payoffs that attach only to states you can't occupy for the sake of the payoff? If there is, tell me which of my cases it covers and which it doesn't, and how each stands to the poison puzzle. If there isn't, coin one and mark its edges: does it take in the poison puzzle, does it take in someone who wants to be spontaneous, and is the impossibility in the belief case the same kind as the impossibility in the character case?
