Write a pantoum on the Andromeda case often used to make the Rietdijk–Putnam argument. The scene is fixed: two people pass each other on a street, walking in opposite directions, and because their planes of simultaneity tilt opposite ways they disagree by days about what is happening now in the Andromeda galaxy — to one walker the fleet has already sailed, to the other the decision has not yet been taken — though neither can see anything of it, since its light will not arrive for another two and a half million years. Let the pantoum's machinery do the philosophical work. Because the second and fourth lines of each stanza return as the first and third of the next, a line that is present tense in one stanza should read as past or future in the next, the same words meaning something different in the new frame; and because the poem's opening line comes back as its final line, the poem should end exactly where it began. I want that return to be the argument's conclusion — the block, no becoming, the end folded into the start — while the poem also exposes the premise that the relativity of simultaneity alone does not supply: that being real for this observer can be treated as being real, so that presentness transmits from frame to frame, which is just what critics deny when they insist the present is only local. Do not assert or resolve that premise; let the form carry it and let the last stanza turn on it rather than settle it. Accuracy before beauty: someone who knows the argument should be able to point to the line where the disputed premise enters, and someone who does not should still be able to tell the two walkers apart.
