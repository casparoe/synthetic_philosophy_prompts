I'm a philosophy PhD student who's been sitting in on a CS department's AI alignment reading group, and we keep coming back to debate-based oversight: two models argue about a question, a weaker judge (a human with limited time, or a weaker model) decides who won, and the winner's answers get used as the training signal. What I can't get anyone to pin down is what the protocol is assuming philosophically, because our group's version has accreted a pile of design choices that nobody defends anymore. Here's the setup as we actually run it: (a) the judge only reads the two closing statements plus whatever excerpts the debaters choose to hand it, never the full transcript; (b) both debaters get an identical token budget, and going over is scored as a loss; (c) a debater may call a "checker" — a tool that returns a yes or no on a claim — except the checker is a model trained on roughly the same data as the debaters, so it isn't an oracle; (d) the judge's verdict is then treated as the target for fine-tuning the debaters, even though everyone in the room agrees the judge is less capable than they are; and (e) there's one move we've banned outright: you can't win by showing that the question is ambiguous, that the concepts involved are unsettled, or that there's no fact of the matter to be had. The thing I find strange is that (a) and (b) look like they're engineered to make the honest debater's job harder, while (d) and (e) look like they're engineered to guarantee the honest debater wins regardless — those seem to pull in opposite directions and I want to know if that's a real tension or just my confusion.

So: what does this package presuppose? I'm after the implicit theory, not a survey. What view of truth does (e) commit us to — and does the protocol need that view, or would a deflationary reading work, where "winning" is simply whatever the judge decides and truth never enters the picture at all? What view of adversarial argumentation does the whole thing rest on: is it the Mill-style marketplace-of-ideas picture, a Popperian conjecture-and-refutation picture, or something about adversarial legal process, and do those come apart here? And the obfuscated-arguments worry specifically — when someone says a dishonest debater can bury a flaw somewhere the judge will never dig it out, what are they actually claiming? That truth has a shorter description than a lie, that justification is cheap to fake, that the judge is bounded in a particular way, or that the subject matter is computationally hard? Those seem like very different diagnoses that get run together. Does (d) make the setup viciously circular, or is that just ordinary bootstrapping that any self-training scheme has to accept? And I'd like to know which rival views the practice quietly rules out — e.g. does (c) presuppose that verification is in principle available from within the same epistemic position as the claim being verified, and does that assumption do any real work, or is it decorative?

Please don't hand me a menu of readings and leave the choice to me, and no reading list — I don't have library access this month and anyway I want your reasoning, not a bibliography. Take a position: tell me which commitments the protocol actually requires versus which ones our group inherited for no good reason, and make a call on whether the honest debater can win in principle given (a) through (d), including the case where you think the answer is that the question is underdetermined until you fix some detail I haven't specified — if so, say which detail and what turns on it. Flag the places where you're genuinely unsure rather than papering over them, but still commit.
