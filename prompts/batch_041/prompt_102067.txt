I am a philosopher of medicine preparing to present a proposal to my hospital's clinical ethics committee, which has been asked to advise on whether the institution should offer a set of interventions -- growth-hormone regimens for short but healthy children, cognitive enhancers for healthy adults, and a forthcoming muscle-preserving drug for age-related frailty -- under its existing benefit plan. The committee's advice will turn on whether we can say which of these are treatments and which are enhancements, and I intend to argue that the received distinction is a mess but can be rebuilt. My proposal, which I will call the reaction-norm account, is this: for any individual, the reaction norm is the range of phenotypes their underlying constitution could have realized across the environments realistically available to them during development; an intervention is therapeutic when it restores a function that has fallen below the lower edge of that range through insult or deprivation, or moves a person toward the favorable end of the range, and it is an enhancement when it pushes the person past the upper edge of the range. The account is offered as a coverage criterion, not a moral one: it says what a just benefit plan may be asked to fund, and deliberately says nothing about whether enhancement is admirable or base. I will also argue that a coverage criterion is unavoidable, since any plan must draw some line, and that the reaction-norm account draws it with fewer arbitrary results than the alternatives (species-typical functioning, opportunity, and the bare therapy/enhancement contrast).

The audience matters more than usual here, and I know it well. The committee has about ten members. Three are clinicians who will be asked to apply whatever we adopt: an endocrinologist who runs the growth clinic and is protective of her patients' access to treatment, a psychiatrist who prescribes stimulants and is wary of being cast as a gatekeeper for student enhancement, and a rehabilitation physician who thinks the interesting cases are all ones where the insult and the environment are indistinguishable. One member is a disability studies scholar who regards the therapy/enhancement distinction as an instrument of normalization and will attack any criterion that presupposes a normal function. One is a philosopher of biology who knows the plasticity literature and will press on whether an individual can be said to have a reaction norm at all, as opposed to a population-level statistic. One is the hospital's counsel, who wants a criterion that survives a coverage-denial appeal and is not embarrassed by the fact that the same drug will be therapy in one patient and enhancement in another. One is a patient advocate who will speak for people whose condition is stable and who will hear in my account a suggestion that their current functioning is a deficit. One is a health economist who is not interested in the metaphysics and will ask only what the criterion costs and whether it is gameable. I have forty minutes to present and the rest of the hour for questions.

Draft my Q&A prep sheet. I want the objections this committee will actually raise -- not the ones I would get at a philosophy conference, and not the ones a bioethicist would raise in print -- ordered by how much damage they would do if I fumble them, and phrased in the vocabulary of the member who will raise them. For each, give me a reply I can deliver in about two minutes, a note on where that reply is soft and what a sharp follow-up would be, and a flag on whether I should concede the point up front rather than fight it. Include the question I am hoping nobody asks, and tell me which single reply has to land for the proposal to leave the room alive. Assume I cannot revise the proposal during the meeting, and do not tell me to go read anything.
