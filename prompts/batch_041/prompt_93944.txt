I've recently taken over the grading for an upper-level course on aesthetics, and I could use a second pair of eyes on one submission. The subject has become unexpectedly personal for me: after a late-night argument following a film screening — whether a work can be genuinely great and genuinely cruel at once — I went back to Tolstoy's What Is Art? and found that the distinctions I'd been reaching for in that argument were ones the book actually makes. Which is why I'm uneasy now: I suspect my students have absorbed the headline claims without the distinctions that make the argument interesting, and I don't entirely trust myself to catch the subtler errors at speed.

Here is the question they were set:

Tolstoy denies that art is to be defined by beauty or by the pleasure it gives. Set out the definition he offers in its place, and explain the work that feeling does in it. Then explain how he uses that definition to separate genuine art from what he calls counterfeit art, and how he distinguishes good art from bad. Finally: does his account leave room for a work that is both real art and morally repellent? Answer with reference to the text.

And here is the answer I'm grading:

Tolstoy's whole argument rests on the claim that art is not about beauty. Beauty is a term invented by critics and philosophers, and it explains nothing: people call a work beautiful when it pleases them, and different classes and ages are pleased by different things. What actually happens in art, Tolstoy says, is that one person has felt something and then, by means of an external form — words, colours, sounds, movements — communicates that feeling to others, who are infected by it and come to feel what the artist felt. Art is therefore a means of union among people: it is not the mere expression of the artist's emotion but its transmission, which is why it requires an audience.

Two things follow. First, sincerity. If the artist does not really feel what he transmits, the work is not art but counterfeit: it imitates the external signs of feeling — the effects of art — while being empty inside. Tolstoy thinks most of the European canon, and especially the fashionable and the obscure, is counterfeit in exactly this way, produced for and by an idle educated class and admired only because people have been told to admire it. Second, intelligibility. Because art communicates feeling, it must be accessible to ordinary people; a work that requires special training to be appreciated has failed as art, whatever its refinements.

His standard of value then follows from his definition. Since art is infection, a work is better art the more completely and directly it infects its audience; the counterfeit is unmasked precisely by its failure to move us. This is why he can dismiss so much of what is called great art: judged by transmission rather than by reputation, it transmits nothing. And because genuine feeling is by its nature unifying, he can also deny that a work which transmits cruelty, contempt, or sensual excitement is really art at all — such a thing is counterfeit, since true art always draws people together. The future of art, for Tolstoy, therefore lies with works that can reach everyone.

Please give it a grade on the usual A–F scale, with a plus or minus if you think one is warranted, and then the comments I'd need to write in order to justify that grade. I care far less about a general impression than about a point-by-point assessment: go through the substantive claims in order and say, for each, whether it is accurate, partly accurate, or mistaken, and where it goes wrong. Some of the slips may be in the details rather than in the headlines, so please don't stop at the first paragraph, and don't treat a sentence as correct merely because the surrounding sentence is. If a claim is right but for a slightly wrong reason, say so; if a claim is defensible in general but not Tolstoy's, say that too. The assignment was marked out of twenty, with five points for each of the four parts of the question, but you needn't be bound by that breakdown — I'd rather have precise comments than a tidy number. The student is bright and will read these closely, so err on the side of over-explaining exactly what the problem is.
