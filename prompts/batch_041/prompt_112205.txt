You are a philosopher whose specialty is Kant's practical philosophy, with a particular focus on his theory of right and his writings on international order. I would like you to reason slowly and carefully, and to begin by laying out, for yourself, the two or three most defensible ways of reconstructing the argument below before you decide where it fails.

A reading group I sit in has built the following argument out of Kant's essay on perpetual peace, and we have grown suspicious of it because it seems to prove more than Kant would permit. This is our reconstruction:

(1) For Kant, a condition of right among persons is possible only under a common coercive authority that settles disputes by law rather than by each party's own judgment; a state of nature is lawless precisely because there is no common judge.

(2) The duty to leave the state of nature and enter a civil condition is, for Kant, not merely prudential but unconditional: one is not only permitted but required to compel others to enter such a condition, since rights remain insecure so long as anyone may be judge in their own cause.

(3) Kant also holds that states, in their mutual external relations, stand in a state of nature, understood as a condition of war in the sense of standing threat if not actual hostilities.

(4) States unavoidably interact: they share borders, trade, send travelers abroad, and can wage war against one another.

(5) Therefore the very reasoning that generates an unconditional duty for individuals to exit their state of nature generates an unconditional duty for states to exit theirs, into a civil condition with a common coercive authority standing over them, that is, into a world state.

(6) Kant nevertheless rejects a world state, on the ground that a single universal monarchy or world republic would be prone to tyranny and would endanger the very freedom it was meant to secure, and he proposes instead a voluntary federation of free states.

(7) A voluntary federation, however, is only a revocable treaty among sovereigns: it has no common judge and no coercive enforcement, so it leaves its members exactly where step (3) says they were, namely in a condition of war. Kant himself treats a federation as something other than a state and warns that such agreements can be broken.

(8) So either the analogy in step (5) is defective, or Kant's rejection in step (6) is inconsistent with his own premises.

The question I care about is not which conclusion is more congenial but where the reconstruction goes wrong, if it does. I would prefer to keep both (2) and (6): each seems to me to be doing genuine work in Kant's system, and abandoning either looks expensive. So please do the following. First, identify the step or steps you reject, and make the strongest case for that step before you reject it. Second, say whether the defect is an equivocation, a scope or quantifier slide, a failure of analogy, or simply a false premise, and defend that diagnosis. Third, price the alternatives: for each fork, tell me what the Kantian who takes it must concede and what pressure it puts on the rest of his position. Fourth, if you think the argument is valid as it stands, say so and tell me which of Kant's own commitments has to give way instead. Please do not stop at the observation that Kant preferred a federation to a world state; that is step (6), and I already know it. I want to know what the preference costs him and whether it can be held consistently with (2). Structure your answer around the numbered steps.
