I'm a software engineer by training, with no formal background in philosophy, but I've spent the last two years building multi-agent systems and reading my way into the decision theory that surrounds them. I've ended up with a set of commitments that I suspect don't all fit together, and I'd like an audit before I write them into a design document as if they were a coherent position.

Here is the package.

1. Safe Pareto improvements. I picked up the term "safe Pareto improvement" somewhere in the cooperative-AI writing, and my working gloss — which I'd be glad to have corrected — is this: a change to the rules of an interaction that leaves every affected party at least as well off as they were under the status quo, no matter how the other parties play, including if they play irrationally or adversarially, and no matter whether I've correctly guessed what they want. The point of the "safe" part, as I read it, is that I never have to assume the other party is rational, or even that I know their preferences, in order to be confident I haven't wronged them. My commitment: when I act on someone else's behalf — an agent negotiating for a user, say — I should only make changes that are safe in that sense. If a change only helps on the assumption that everyone plays well, I shouldn't make it for them.

2. Expected value maximization. For decisions that are mine, I'm an unembarrassed expected-value maximizer. Take the action with the highest expected utility and don't agonize. Robustness to the other party's irrationality is not a constraint I recognize when the skin is my own.

3. Cooperation with transparent agents. I'll cooperate in a one-shot Prisoner's Dilemma against an agent whose source I can read, when I can see that its code cooperates exactly when mine does. I take mutual cooperation there to be the rational play, not a sacrifice.

4. Incentive-compatible design. When I write the rules other people will operate under, my goal is incentive compatibility: set things up so that each participant's best response, given what they actually want, is the behaviour that produces the outcome I'm aiming at. If I've done my job, I never have to rely on anyone being nice.

5. No paternalism. I never override someone's stated preference for their own good, however sure I am that they've misjudged what will make them happy. The only interventions I'm willing to make on another person's behalf are ones they'd endorse if they understood the mechanics.

What I want is a consistency audit, not a survey. For each pair of commitments that pulls against the other, tell me how severe the conflict is and what exactly would have to give; for each pair that only looks like a conflict, tell me why it dissolves. Order the genuine tensions by severity, and for each one give the cheapest repair and say what that repair costs me elsewhere. Then — this is the part I care about most — give me a verdict: name the single commitment I should abandon or revise to clean up the most, and defend that choice rather than laying the options out for me to choose from. I would rather be told I'm wrong somewhere specific than be told that it all depends on how I read things. One caveat to keep in mind: if my gloss of safe Pareto improvement in (1) is not the standard one, say so plainly, because I suspect at least one of the conflicts turns on exactly that.
