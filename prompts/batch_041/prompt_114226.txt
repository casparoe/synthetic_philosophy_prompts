For the last few months I have been arguing about corruption with a friend who works in development finance, and I want to settle what I actually think. Her position is that "corruption" is a thick concept in the philosopher's sense — it describes and evaluates in one breath — and that its evaluative half is local, so an anti-corruption index is mostly a measure of how much a country resembles Denmark; she also likes to point out that the countries that score worst on those indices tend to be the ones whose last election went the wrong way for the people compiling them. I half-agree with her, and I cannot locate the other half. What I have is a set of verdicts on particular cases, and they do not visibly descend from one principle. I am not asking what the law says, or what an index ought to measure; I am asking what I am tracking when I call a thing corrupt, if I am tracking anything at all. Please argue from the cases rather than from the literature: I do not want a reading list or citations, which I cannot check from where I am sitting. The cases, with my honest verdicts:

1. Speed money at a public counter. A clerk in a licensing office where salaries are low and lately unpaid takes the equivalent of two days' wages to move a man's permit application to the top of the pile. The permit would be granted on the merits either way; the man simply skips a three-week wait. Corrupt — not close.
2. The thank-you box of chocolates. A family gives a nurse a box of chocolates after a difficult delivery. Nothing was asked for, nothing was bought, and she would have done exactly the same for anyone. Not corrupt.
3. The tip for the good table. A diner slips the maître d' a banknote and gets the table by the window, ahead of a couple who arrived twenty minutes earlier and are still waiting. Not corrupt. Grubby, perhaps, but not corruption.
4. The well-qualified nephew. The chair of a hiring panel does not recuse himself; his nephew applies; on any independent reading of the file the nephew is the strongest candidate, and the panel hires him. I want to say this is not corrupt. I wish the chair had recused, and I would call the process tainted, but "corrupt" strikes me as too strong.
5. The lawful donation. A businessman gives the maximum permitted donation to a mayor's campaign. After the election he gets a half-hour meeting with the mayor and a hearing on his zoning application that his competitors, who gave nothing, cannot get. Nothing is promised, nothing is decided at the meeting, and the application is judged on its merits. Corrupt — and this one makes me angrier than the speed money, even though nothing illegal happens anywhere in it.
6. The cousin at customs. A trader brings his cousin, a customs officer, a bottle of good whisky when he visits for a family dinner. Two weeks later the cousin clears the trader's container on the day it lands rather than a week later — which is what he does for anyone who asks him nicely, except that the trader never had to ask. I want to say not corrupt: it is a gift, it is family, and he got nothing he was not already entitled to. Then I notice that it is case 1 with a family tree attached, and I cannot tell whether anyone is worse off.
7. The consultant who used to be the deputy. A ministry buys badly overpriced equipment from a firm that has retained the minister's former deputy as a "consultant." Every step complies with procurement law. Corrupt.
8. The farmer who gives in. In a village where the irrigation clerk decides the order in which fields are watered, nearly everyone gives the clerk small gifts, and those who do not are watered last. A farmer whose crop fails once starts giving gifts too. The clerk is corrupt; the farmer is not — he is being squeezed, and I do not blame him at all.

My best attempt at a principle so far is this: corruption is what happens when someone occupying a role is moved, in the exercise of that role, by a consideration the role is meant to exclude — money, kinship, friendship, faction — rather than by the role's own rationale. That seems to get 1, 2, 3, 5, 7 and 8 right, but it convicts the nephew (4) and the cousin (6), which I resist. A rival principle — corruption is the use of a role to secure a private advantage the role does not authorize, at some cost to those the role exists to serve — acquits 2, 4 and 6, but lets the mayor off the hook in 5 (he personally gains nothing but a war chest) and cannot explain why the maître d' escapes in 3. A purely harm-based reading handles 1, 4, 5, 7 and 8 but convicts the maître d' in 3, which I will not accept. What I want is the principle, or the small set of principles, that best captures these verdicts — stated precisely enough that I could apply it to a new case without writing to you again. If no single principle saves all eight, tell me which verdict it forces me to abandon and defend that abandonment; I would rather lose a verdict than accept a principle with a clause invented solely to preserve it, and if a clause is doing that kind of patching work I want it flagged as such. Then apply your principle to two cases I have not decided: (a) a journalist pays a police officer for a copy of an accident report that the officer is legally obliged to give her for free but will not; and (b) a regulator leaves a public post for a lucrative job at the very firm she used to regulate, twelve months later, with no finding of a quid pro quo. Finally, I would like you to be candid about your own confidence and about the state of the question: say plainly which parts of this are settled among people who study it and which are genuinely contested, rather than letting a contested view pass as the consensus.

Please structure the response as follows: a section titled "Verdicts Restated," in which you compress my eight cases and their verdicts to their essentials (about 100 words); a section titled "The Principle," in which you state your preferred principle or principles and show how each clause earns its place by reference to a specific case (about 500 words); a section titled "What It Costs," in which you name the verdicts, if any, that your principle forces me to give up, say whether a more complicated principle would have saved them, and state why you think the trade is worth it (about 250 words); a section titled "Two Undecided Cases," giving your verdicts on (a) and (b) with your reasoning (about 300 words); and a closing section titled "Settled and Contested," on what is common ground in this area and what is not (about 200 words).
