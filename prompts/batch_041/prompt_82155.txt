I have sat for eleven years on the memorial commission of the mid-sized city where I grew up — an unpaid body of nine that meets four times a year and rules on petitions to put up plaques and monuments, to name and rename streets and schools, to open or reseal archival collections, and to fund or discontinue public commemorations — and I have just spent an evening arguing about it with a friend who teaches the region's history at the university. His line is that a community owes a debt of memory to those it has wronged, that the debt is owed to the dead and not to the living's convenience, and that a city which stops telling a hard story has not healed but only buried it; my own view, which I defended badly and am no longer sure I hold, is that remembering is a practice with costs as well as benefits and that there are cases where the decent thing is to let something lapse. The argument ended without either of us conceding, and it left me with a question I actually care about, because of the petition we vote on in November: not what memory demands in the abstract, but what this body, given what it has already done, will do next, and what its doing it would reveal about the rule it has been running all along.

The record, as I would summarize it for a stranger: in 1987 we approved, unanimously, a memorial in the old market square to the city's Jewish families deported in 1943 by the city's own police under the occupation administration, together with an annual public reading of the names, which we have paid for every year since. In 1994 we approved a plaque and an archive collection for the Roma families expelled from the city in the 1880s under a municipal resettlement order, but declined the memorial and the compulsory school unit the petitioners had asked for, minuting that the dead should be named and the record kept, but that the city should not be organized around its own shame. In 2001 we refused a petition to commemorate the city workhouse of the 1920s, where inmates died of neglect and mothers were separated from their children, on the ground that the harshness of the period and the absence of any intent to humiliate did not amount to a wrong of the kind that generates a duty to remember. In 2009 we refused to open the police files naming the local people who denounced their neighbours to the occupation authorities in 1944, sealing them for a further twenty-five years over the objection of the denounced families' descendants, on the ground that the dead are past being protected and the living are not, and that in a city this size the descendants would have to go on living with one another. And in 2016 we approved, over the protests of the merchants' descendants, a school unit on the slave-trading families whose fortunes built our library. The petition now before us asks us to undo the first thing we ever did: the family association of the 1943 deportees — or rather a majority of it, since a minority has written to us insisting that the memorial stay — has asked us to remove the memorial and end the reading, arguing that the dead do not need it, that the ceremony has been taken over by a far-right group which turns up with banners and treats our commemoration as a grievance of its own, and that the stone has been defaced three times in five years. The council has offered to move it into the museum courtyard, and the police say they can protect the square if we want them to.

What I want from you is not a survey of what the literature on memory and forgetting says; I can read that myself, and it has not helped. I want a prediction of what my colleagues will actually decide in November, including the form the decision takes, since removing the stone, moving it, keeping it while ending the reading, and keeping everything as it is are all live options and are not equivalent. I want the candidate rules the record above is consistent with, including any that fit it just as well as the ones I have named, and a note on which of our decisions each rule has to explain away. I want an account of which rules this petition actually turns on and what each of them predicts about it; your best guess at the outcome, with the reasons for preferring it; and, most usefully, a description of some other petition we might plausibly receive in the next few years that would separate the rules the November vote leaves entangled — a case that would tell us whether what has been driving us is the gravity of what was done, our own part in it, the wishes of the people who carry the memory, or the harm that remembering does now.
