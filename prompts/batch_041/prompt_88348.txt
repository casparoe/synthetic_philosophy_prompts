A friend and I spent most of yesterday evening arguing about the phrase "the will of the people," and we reached the point where I think we were disagreeing about what the phrase is supposed to mean rather than about any particular policy. I would rather settle it with a constructed case than with another evening of shouting, so I would like you to build me a thought experiment to order. The case should be a single realistic decision — a referendum, a town vote, a ballot on something mundane and administrative, such as the formula by which a public utility's charges are set — engineered so that three different readings of the phrase return three different verdicts about whether the outcome really expresses the people's will. Treat the specifications as binding, since they are the whole point. Under the aggregative reading, on which the people's will is the sum of the actual wills of the whole electorate, the verdict must be that the outcome does not express the people's will even though it carried by a large majority. Under the Rousseauian reading — the general will as against the will of all, with the further requirement that a law be general in its object rather than aimed at particular persons or groups — the verdict must be that the outcome does express the general will even though the process that produced it was defective. Under a third, deliberative reading, on which the people's will is what they would have willed under better information and more reflection, the verdict must be that the question is indeterminate, and the indeterminacy has to arise from the structure of the case rather than from vagueness in the reading. The specifications pull against one another, so some ingenuity is needed: the majority must be large and uncoerced; the defect must not be fraud, suppression, foreign interference, a misleading question, a bundled package, or a law that singles out one group; and nobody in the story may be a villain. Keep the scenario itself to a single paragraph, and if you cannot satisfy every specification at once, say which one you dropped and why.

After the scenario, walk me through it. For each of the three readings, give the verdict, the reasoning that produces it, and the feature of the case the verdict turns on. Then tell me which single feature is doing the load-bearing work — the thing whose removal collapses the case back into the uninteresting version, where the aggregative reading says yes and the Rousseauian says no — and describe what the case looks like once that feature is gone. Finish with the strongest attack on the case from the quarter that comes off worst: what a defender of that reading would say I have smuggled into the setup, and whether the objection is decisive. I do not need citations, reading lists, or scholarship; I want the case and the argument, and the verdicts have to fall out of the theories rather than being stipulated in advance.
