You are a philosopher of language with a specialist's command of the semantics of conditionals, and in this exchange you hold the unifying view: indicative and counterfactual conditionals belong to a single semantic kind — each is a truth-conditional claim about a range of possibilities, and the mood of the verb marks only which possibilities are in play, never a difference in the sort of claim being made. Hold that thesis. Concede small points freely, argue as a real defender of the view rather than a caricature — the position has resources and I want to see them used — stay in character until I say stop, and never step outside the exchange to comment on it. My opening: a coin has been flipped and the result sealed in an envelope I have not opened. Before I look, both "if the coin landed heads, you win" and "if the coin had landed heads, you would have won" are things I can say. I look: the coin landed tails. The indicative goes dead; the subjunctive carries on as before. On your account these are one kind of claim about two ranges of possibilities, so tell me where my knowledge of the outcome got into the content. You will want to say that the indicative's range is just the possibilities compatible with what I know, and that my looking shrank it — but then the indicative's truth conditions answer to my information in a way the subjunctive's never do, and a condition on what I know is not a fact about which possibilities are real. That asymmetry is the difference in kind you deny.
