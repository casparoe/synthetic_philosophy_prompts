I teach a 90-minute session in an upper-level undergraduate seminar on rational choice and its discontents — sixteen students, most of whom can state the definition of a Nash equilibrium but none of whom has worked through the centipede game. I want to run an activity in which they feel the backward-induction argument from the inside: they should reach, through their own play and their own arguing, the conclusion that stopping on the very first move is the only thing a rational player can do, and then watch that conclusion come apart when a single feature of the setup is changed. The mechanic I want to build on — and which I'd like you to work into something runnable — is a two-part parlour game. In the first part, play a finite version with a known last move, so the induction bites cleanly and the group can derive it themselves. In the second part, play a version in which the endpoint is uncertain: after every pass, whether the game continues is decided by a draw whose contents the players cannot inspect, so nobody knows in advance how long the shadow of the future really is. My hunch is that the first version will produce the smug immediate defection the theory predicts, and the second will produce cooperation that the group cannot easily explain away, and I want the activity to make that contrast land as an experience rather than as a lecture. Please don't let them hear the name of the game or the phrase "backward induction" until the debrief.

Please give me the full package: the rules and a payoff table (paid in tokens or wrapped sweets — nothing that needs real money, and scaled so the numbers are easy to read off a card); the materials and how to set up pairs or small groups, including whether one member should be given a special role such as keeper of the pot; a minute-by-minute timeline for the 90 minutes that leaves room for the group to argue with itself before I say anything; and, crucially, a debrief that maps what happened at particular moments onto the pieces of the backward-induction argument — the last player's incentive, the penultimate player's reasoning, the unravelling back to the first move, and the role played by the assumption that everyone knows everyone is rational. Tell me honestly what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it — my guess is that someone will "irrationally" cooperate in the first version and the group will burn ten minutes litigating whether that was a mistake, and that the uncertain-end version will feel to them like a different game rather than the same one relaxed — and how I should handle each of those. Finally, be frank about which part of the idea the activity cannot reproduce and that I will have to supply myself; I already suspect that a game with sweets cannot generate the full regress of reasoning about reasoning, and I would rather you name the gap precisely than paper over it.
