**Hinges you can doubt**

Wittgenstein's late notes on certainty (the ones he wrote in the last year or so of his life, which came out long after he died) are mostly about Moore holding up a hand and saying he knows it's a hand. Wittgenstein's reaction, as I read him, isn't that Moore is wrong, but that Moore has misdescribed what he's doing. The interesting part is the thought that some of our certainties don't function as claims at all: they're the things that have to stand fast for doubt and inquiry to get off the ground in the first place. He calls them hinges. You can't doubt that you've got hands in the middle of a conversation about whether you've got hands, because the doubt needs something to stand on. And there's that image of a riverbed: propositions that are rock solid for one person or generation can end up in the flow of debate, and the other way round.

Loads of people now use this to talk about trust — trust in testimony, in science, in institutions. I think that's a mistake unless we seperate hinges into two kinds, and I want to defend the split.

First kind: constitutive hinges. Doubting them would break the practice of doubting itself. That my memory works at all. That other people have minds. That the world didn't begin five minutes ago with my memories already in place. If you genuinely doubted one of these you wouldn't be doing a worse job of inquiring — you wouldn't be inquiring at all. Wittgenstein's examples are almost all like this.

Second kind: sponsored hinges. These are also held without evidence and also sit in the background, but what holds them up is other people and institutions. That the tap water is safe. That a document with a certain letterhead has actually been reviewed by anyone. That the person answering the phone at the hospital is a nurse. That a photo of a protest was taken where it says it was taken. Doubt here is perfectly possible; it's just expensive. If I doubt the water I can boil it, buy a test kit, or ask a chemist, and none of that breaks the practice of doubting — it *is* the practice of doubting.

So the classic move — doubt presupposes certainty, therefore these things can't be doubted — only works for the first kind. Applied to the second kind it's a bait and switch, because the "can't" has quietly changed from a logical can't into a practical can't, and practical can'ts are exactly the sort of thing we should be suspicious of. It's like telling someone they can't question the referee because the game needs referees. Fine, the game needs someone to make the calls; it doesn't need *this* referee.

This is where I want to introduce what I've been calling a hinge hostage. Sometimes a community ties a sponsored hinge to its identity, so that doubting the hinge gets read as betraying the group. Then the cost of doubt isn't epistemic work, it's exile. I think a lot of what looks like stubbornness in arguments about medical or historical claims is this: the person isn't weighing evidence against evidence, they're being asked to pay a price that isn't measured in evidence at all. Hinge hostages also explain why the "you can't doubt it" move gets made so often and so sincerely: for them it really is true that they can't, just for social reasons rather than logical ones.

Related, and I'm much less sure about this bit: hinges seem to drift without anyone deciding. Not long ago, knowing what year a treaty was signed meant a trip to a library; now it means asking a machine. Nothing about the proposition changed, but it's position in the riverbed did. If hinges can drift silently like that, the picture of them as the stable ground looks wrong — they're more like sediment, laid down by whatever we happen to be doing.

The objection I can't shake: have I just reinvented the difference between basic and non-basic beliefs, or between what's held without evidence and what's held on evidence? I want to say no, because my test isn't how basic or how certain something is, it's what happens to the practice of doubting when you take it away — does the practice collapse, or does it just get harder? But maybe that's a difference of degree wearing a difference of kind's coat. And I'll admit a problem at the end: everything I've called a sponsored hinge was learned from other people, and arguably so was everything else, so a critic could say all hinges are social and my distinction only tracks how much institutional scaffolding a hinge happens to have at the moment.
