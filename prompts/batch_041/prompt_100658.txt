I am assembling a handout for a graduate reading group on free will and artificial agency, and I need a decision tree that sorts a reader into a position on a single question: where the line falls between mechanical rigidity and genuine flexibility, and what follows from that for free will. The familiar furniture — the digger wasp whose burrow-checking routine can be looped indefinitely by moving the prey, Dennett's use of it as the image of life under determinism, the compatibilist/libertarian/hard-determinist standoff — is too well rehearsed to sort anyone by itself. So build the tree from distinctions a reader is unlikely to have been handed in a survey, and use at least these three, adding others only if they do diagnostic work the three cannot:

1. Thin versus thick rigidity. Thin rigidity: no alternative behaviour is available to the system at all. Thick rigidity: alternatives are available and never taken, or the repertoire of responses is finite and fixed even though it is large. Ask the reader, by way of a case rather than a definition, whether the wasp is thin-rigid (it could not have done otherwise) or thick-rigid (it could have, but the rule never fires); and whether a fluent generative system that emits a different sentence every time, but nothing outside its repertoire, is flexible in either sense.

2. The escape-rule regress. Consider a system that detects its own rigidity and rewrites the offending rule, where the rewriting rule is itself fixed; then a system that rewrites the rewriting rule; and so on. Ask whether each level buys genuine flexibility, or whether the regress shows that flexibility is always a fixed point at some level — and whether the demand for an unregimented level is coherent, or is the demand that a free act be uncaused in a more respectable costume.

3. Variability versus repertoire extension. Ask whether flexibility consists in varying output within a fixed space or in adding an item that was not in the repertoire before, and whether the second can be accomplished by a system that cannot represent its own rules.

Requirements. Eight to twelve leaves. Every branch node must be phrased as a question the reader can answer without knowing which leaf it leads to, and no node may simply ask which label the reader prefers. At minimum the leaves must include: a gradualist compatibilism on which freedom comes in degrees; a hierarchical or identification-based compatibilism; a reasons-responsiveness compatibilism; a libertarianism that requires the agent to originate rather than merely transmit the action (split it into agent-causal and event-causal leaves only if your tree genuinely separates them); hard determinism; a hard incompatibilism that pairs the denial of free will with the claim that the belief is worth keeping; a revisionism that treats the question of whether we have free will as the wrong question; a Strawsonian view on which the rigidity/agency distinction tracks the stance we take rather than the mechanism we inventory; one leaf for the position that the wasp's rigidity is an artifact of the experimental set-up rather than a fact about the wasp; and one leaf for flexibility sufficient for agency without any inner self — competence without comprehension. Build the tree from the logical space first, as combinations of answers, and only then locate the named positions in it; where a combination is empty, say so and diagnose it briefly as incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. Mark any leaf nobody actually defends as unnamed. Flag explicitly any pair of leaves your tree cannot separate. Each leaf carries one sentence stating its most awkward commitment. No citations, no reading lists, no dates.
