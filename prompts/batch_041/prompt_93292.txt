I run a puzzle night in the back room of my local library — about a dozen adults, forty-five minutes, a whiteboard, nobody with any background in philosophy or psychology, and a crowd that mostly comes for crosswords and logic grids. Next month I want to spend the hour putting across one idea instead of running a competition: that a concept is not a fixed label but something that stretches and slips depending on how you use it, and that much of what feels like solving a puzzle is really choosing which stretch to make. I came to this on my own from something I once read about a computer program that solves letter-string puzzles and whose authors kept insisting the interesting part was watching the program's concepts shift from one problem to the next. I couldn't tell you where I read it, and the program itself doesn't interest me; what I want to know is whether the idea can be put across with paper puzzles in forty-five minutes. So I have built a small set of pairs to hang it on. The first is abcd becoming abce, followed by mnop. The second is the same abcd-to-abce, followed by zyxw. The third is abcd-to-abce again, followed by the single string abbccc. The first has a flat, obvious answer. The second, if you carry over the rule the way you applied it the first time, ends in a doubled letter and looks wrong, and there is a different reading of the change that doesn't. The third has an obvious answer that alters only the final letter, and a reading one level up, at the level of the repeated blocks, that alters the whole last block instead. Don't explain the idea to me; I already believe it. What I want is the plan. What has to be in place before the third pair can land the way it should — I assume the group has to reach the first answer with real confidence, so that the second can take it away, but tell me if that is wrong or if something else has to come earlier. Which single pair should carry the whole session, and what does it have to have that the other two lack; if none of mine can carry it, say so and give me the one that would. The misconceptions in the order they will arrive — I am expecting someone to conclude that any answer is as good as any other, someone else to decide the puzzle is simply badly made, and one regular to insist that this is only wordplay and would never work with numbers — and the exact point in the sequence where each should be headed off rather than saved for the end. Then a question for the last five minutes whose answers would let me tell apart the people who have actually got the idea from the people who have merely learned to say that readings differ. Tell me what to leave out at this level and say openly that I am leaving it out. Be committed: choose the pair, fix the order, and defend the choices; don't lay out three ways I could run the evening and leave the decision to me. If you think the framing itself is a mistake, say so — but then give me the session you would run instead.
