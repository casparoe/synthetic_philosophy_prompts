I'm doing a long interview for a small podcast with a historian who argues there's no such thing as a natural famine: the natural-versus-political split is, on his view, a responsibility-hiding device, and every famine on record involved a distribution choice that somebody had the power to make differently. He's great at the big picture and slippery once you get specific, so I want questions that make him do real work — no "what drew you to this" filler. Draft eight, each with a one-sentence follow-up keyed to the answer he's most likely to give. I need at least one on Bengal in 1943: does the entitlement story — people starving while food moved elsewhere — amount to an argument about politics, or only about markets, and how does he stop the war and the cyclone from letting "natural" back in through the side door? At least one on Ireland: it was part of the UK, with Irish MPs at Westminster, and grain was still being shipped out of the country — so is that a democracy that starved, and if so what survives of the claim that a free press and free politics prevent famine? And at least one on the Great Leap Forward, where the food wasn't so much absent as requisitioned, hoarded and misreported up a chain of command: I want him to say what the crime was and who did it.

Then the bit I actually want to trap him with. Ive been chewing on the idea of the unowned famine. If these are political crimes, but the causation runs through thousands of small decisions — quotas set by local cadres, harvests exaggerated to look good, grain moved to the cities where the politically important people lived — then you've got a crime with no criminal, and "politics" starts doing the job "nature" used to do: hiding responsibility behind an abstraction instead of a storm. Give me one question that forces him either to name perpetrators or to concede that his framing diffuses responsibility just as efficiently as the one he's attacking, plus a follow-up for whichever way he goes. Then one more on whether "crime" needs intent at all, or only a foreseeable alternative that somebody chose not to take.

Under 750 words, British English, and keep the questions short enough to actually say out loud.
