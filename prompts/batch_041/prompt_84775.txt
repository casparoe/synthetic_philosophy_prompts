I have been sitting in on a small reading group that two friends run out of the philosophy department, and last week two of the regulars — Dario, who works on mathematical modeling in the sciences, and Fen, who is finishing a dissertation on scientific realism — fell into an argument that ran well past the end of the session and that I have been unable to resolve in my own head since. The question was whether it can ever be a genuine, objective fact that one false theory is closer to the truth than another, or whether talk of truthlikeness is merely a façon de parler we could drop without loss.

Dario opened by saying the notion has to be real: nobody serious denies that Newton's mechanics is closer to the truth than Aristotle's physics, or that a theory which gets the inverse-square law right is closer than one which gets it wrong, and if we cannot say that, we cannot say that science makes progress at all. Fen said she was happy to utter such sentences as loose praise but denied that there was any fact underneath them. Her first argument was that "closer" would have to mean "more true consequences and fewer false ones," and that no false theory can beat another false theory on that score: any theory with a single false consequence has infinitely many, and for any two false theories one can always exhibit a false consequence of the one that the other avoids, so neither ever dominates the other — and since all our theories are, on any realist view, at least partly false, the relation would be empty in precisely the cases we care about. Dario replied that we need only count true consequences: the theory with more true consequences is the closer one. Fen answered that this makes any inconsistent theory maximally close, since from a contradiction every true sentence follows, so "P and not-P" would outrank quantum mechanics; and that even among consistent theories one can inflate the count by conjoining true trivialities, so the measure rewards padding. Dario then suggested restricting to consequences below some complexity threshold, or weighting them by their informativeness. Fen said this only relocates the problem: a threshold or a weighting is a free parameter, and any such parameter can be tuned to rank whichever theory one already prefers first, so the supposedly objective relation turns out to encode our preferences. Dario's last move was to say that counting consequences was the wrong picture anyway — that closeness is a matter of how near a theory's claims lie to the truth in a space of possibilities, measured by something like distance, and that where such a space is available the verdict is as objective as a distance in meters. He offered an example: two rival estimates of some constant, one placing it between 3 and 5, another between 4.9 and 4.95 — if the true value is 4.92 the second is closer, if it is 6 the first is closer, and that seems to be a fact about the world and not about us. Fen countered that the distance depends on how one sets up the space — whether one measures in the value of the constant, in its logarithm, or in some quantity with physical significance — and that different but equally legitimate setups yield different verdicts; and that in any case one needs the true value in hand to compute the distance, which is the very thing in dispute in real science.

I have no stake in this and I am not asking you simply to declare a winner. What I want is a careful referee's report on the exchange as it actually went. Please go through it move by move — Dario's initial claim, Fen's infinite-false-consequences objection, Dario's true-consequences-only revision, Fen's inconsistency and padding objections, Dario's complexity-or-weighting fix, Fen's free-parameter reply, and finally Dario's distance-in-a-space-of-possibilities proposal together with Fen's parameterization and known-truth rejoinders — and for each move say whether it is sound, whether it overreaches, and what the person making it is entitled to claim. Where one of them is right about a narrower point but draws too broad a conclusion, or where the two are talking past each other, say so explicitly. Finally, tell me what both of them appear to be missing, and which of the two, if either, has the stronger overall position.
