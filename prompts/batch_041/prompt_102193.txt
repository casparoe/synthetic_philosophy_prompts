Last week a friend and I argued for the better part of an evening about Fitch's paradox of knowability, and we ended further apart than we began: she holds that the argument simply refutes the knowability principle, while I hold that it refutes only one particular combination of assumptions and leaves a defensible version of the principle intact. We could not agree on what the derivation actually turns on, so I would rather settle the question with a decision tree than with another evening of the same argument. Please build one for a reading group of graduate students who already grant that the derivation goes through in classical logic and who want to locate themselves within the space of responses.

Construct the tree from the assumptions first and the positions second. Every internal node should be a single question about one ingredient of the argument, phrased so that a reader can answer it without already knowing which position that answer commits them to; the branches should record the answers that ingredient permits. The ingredients I take to be genuinely separable, and that I want represented as nodes, are these: whether the knowability principle is a single universally quantified claim or only a schema with an instance for each proposition; whether knowledge is factive; whether knowing a conjunction entails knowing each conjunct; whether the underlying logic is classical or intuitionistic; whether the principle ranges over all propositions or only over a restricted class; and whether knowable means knowable by some possible knower at all, or knowable by an ideal community at the limit of inquiry.

Then place the positions at the leaves, identifying each by its content rather than by the name of anyone who held it. Include at least the following: the realist response that abandons the principle as false; the restriction of the principle to propositions whose truth does not depend on what is known (so that the offending instances drop out); the restriction to a privileged class of basic or decidable propositions; the move to intuitionistic logic, which blocks the step that delivers the collapse; the actualist move, which keeps the principle but confines it to what is actually known or knowable by non-ideal knowers; and the dialetheist option, which accepts that some truths are both knowable and unknowable. In addition, include two moves that are usually left off the standard list: the restriction of the principle to propositions that are not themselves about what is or is not known, and the denial that knowledge distributes over conjunction. For each leaf, give the position's most awkward commitment in a single sentence. Where two leaves cannot in fact be separated by your tree, say so plainly rather than manufacturing a distinction to keep them apart.

The tree should also mark which combinations of answers no one occupies, and for each empty combination diagnose briefly whether it is empty because the view is incoherent, because it is coherent but unattractive, or because it is merely unexplored. Aim for seven to nine leaves.

Finally, close with a short note that separates what is settled here from what is genuinely contested among people who know the material, and refrain from presenting any single diagnosis of the paradox as the consensus; I want to see, in particular, where the disagreement about which premise is the culprit is a real disagreement rather than a verbal one.
