Okay so I've been lurking on rat-adjacent blogs for like a decade, and I've got two friends who are both the type to argue about this stuff at 1am, and last week they got into it about sphexishness and I genuinely could not referee it.

Quick setup, in case this isn't already loaded in your head: the story goes that there's a wasp (a sphex wasp) that provisions her burrow with a paralyzed cricket. She drags the cricket to the burrow mouth, leaves it sitting there, and goes inside to check that everything's fine. If you nudge the cricket a couple of inches away while she's in there, she comes out, drags it back to the mouth, and goes in to check again — and if you keep doing it, she just keeps looping. No amount of repetition makes her go "huh, this is the 14th time, maybe I should look at this differently." People use "sphexish" for behavior like that: mechanically rigid, no capacity to notice that the routine has stopped serving the point.

So, the argument. My friends are Ada (very much an everything-is-degrees, nothing-is-a-natural-kind person, does a lot of RL stuff) and Marcus (reads a lot of philosophy of mind, thinks the interesting stuff is always on the inside).

Marcus's first move: sphexishness is a fact about the mechanism, not the behavior. You can't tell from outside. A thermostat and a person both keep the room warm, and they're not both sphexish; what makes the wasp sphexish is that nothing inside her represents the goal as a thing separate from the routine. Behavior is just the shadow.

Ada: then it's unfalsifiable and doing no work. If the only way to be sphexish is to have the wrong kind of insides, and you can never inspect the insides, "sphexish" collapses into "agent whose guts I can't see." She wants it behavioral. She defined something on the spot she calls sphex depth: take the agent, take the goal, find the smallest change to the situation that makes the agent's behavior stop serving that goal. The wasp's depth is about two inches. A chess engine's depth is enormous. A human's is bigger still. Nobody's is infinite. Degrees all the way down.

Marcus: the metric is broken twice over. First, it measures how good the environment is at fooling the agent, not anything about the agent — a chess engine that can't see the refutation of the move it's about to play is just a wasp with a deeper burrow; it plays the losing move happily, and there's no event inside the engine corresponding to "huh, something's off." Second, and worse, sphex depth is defined relative to a goal description, and picking the description is where all the cheating happens. If he describes the wasp's goal as "re-check the burrow whenever the cricket is at the mouth," she's a fantastically flexible agent flawlessly tracking a weird goal. Sphexishness only appears relative to a goal you've already decided is the real one, and he says Ada has no principled way to pick it.

Ada: fine, that's a real problem, but it cuts her way, not his. If sphexishness is always relative to a goal attribution, then it isn't a property of agents at all, it's a relation between an agent and a describer. Which means you can't settle "are we sphexish?" by introspection; the answer is that we're sphexish with respect to the goals we can't currently represent, and we have no way to know in advance which those are. And she says his insides criterion has the exact same disease — he's just reading the real goal off the insides.

Marcus: there's still a difference between an agent that *could* notice the loop and one that can't. The wasp has no counterfactual where it pauses and thinks "wait, twelve times?" A stuck human has that capacity. That's why "sphexish" is an insult when applied to us — it means failing to use a capacity you have — and a mere description when applied to the wasp.

Ada: "could in principle" is doing all the work and it's unfalsifiable. Every finite agent has some perturbation it can't survive. The difference between her and the wasp is depth, not kind, and "has the capacity in principle" is just depth said in a way that sounds qualitative.

There was also a concrete thing they fought about, which might matter more than the abstractions: our flatmate Ben spent two hours on a puzzle game repeating the same failed approach. Marcus said that was straight-up sphexish. Ada said no — Ben *believed* it would work this time, he had a reason, and a reason-guided agent is the opposite of a sphexish one. Marcus said the belief is the loop. That's where the conversation died, and they've been cold to each other since, which is definately too much.
