I teach a graduate seminar on medieval Jewish philosophy and contemporary animal ethics, and I want to set an exercise that forces students to reason from a thinker's principles rather than from a stock summary of his conclusions. Suppose Maimonides could observe the present-day debate over animal agriculture. He would encounter phenomena with no medieval analogue: industrial confinement; the widespread use of stunning before slaughter, which some halakhic authorities treat as disqualifying and others defend; cultivated meat that could be produced without killing; and a thought experiment that has begun to circulate in some Jewish environmental circles, in which a rebuilt Temple would use cultivated meat rather than slaughtered animals, on the theory that the sacrificial commandments were a temporary pedagogical accommodation to a violent ancient cult. Taking his discussion of the sacrificial commandments as your central point of reference, reconstruct the position he would most likely take on three questions: first, whether the sacrificial system was, in his view, a permanent divine institution or a pedagogically motivated accommodation; second, whether the industrialization of animal killing makes the original sacrificial rationale more or less defensible; and third, whether he would regard cultivated meat as a halakhic and moral advance, a category confusion, or something else. Be explicit about which of his principles you are extrapolating from and where the extrapolation is underdetermined; I am less interested in a verdict about what Maimonides would say than in the quality of the inference.

Please write in American English. I do not need a bibliography, reading list, or citations; reason from the ideas, and do not guess at specific chapter numbers or quotations. For each major claim, state how confident you are in it, and conclude by identifying the single point in your reconstruction that you are least sure about and what evidence or argument would most change your assessment.
