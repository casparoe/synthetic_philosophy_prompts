I have been following, for several years now, the dispute in philosophy of science between those who think the history of science tells against scientific realism and those who defend realism against that history, and I have come to think the argument has stalled in a way that is more interesting than either side lets on. The anti-realist case is a plain induction: a long list of theories were once counted empirically successful and are now counted false — phlogiston chemistry, the caloric theory of heat, the luminiferous ether, and others — so the success of our present theories gives us little reason to think them true. The realist reply has been equally stable for decades: the induction is too coarse, because it treats theories as indivisible wholes, whereas the parts of past theories that did the predictive work were frequently retained and it was only the idle ontological gloss that was discarded; realism, on this view, is a claim about the working posits, not about every entity a theory mentions. A third party, the structural realists, splits the difference by holding that what survives theory change is structure rather than the nature of the entities. Each of these positions, as far as I can tell, is defended with arguments whose strength depends on unstated premises about how theories are to be individuated and about which reference class of past theories the induction is supposed to range over — all theories ever held, or only mature theories with novel predictive success, or only their working parts. That is my hypothesis, and I would like it tested rather than assumed.

I am also struck by a charge that circulates but never quite gets settled: that the realist's selective-retention move is circular or ad hoc, because the criterion for what counts as a working posit can only be applied after the fact, once we already know which parts survived the transition. The realist, naturally, denies that this is a defect; the anti-realist says the realist has merely redescribed the historical record without explaining it. Meanwhile the realist's own argument — that the predictive success of science would be a miracle if its theories were not at least approximately true — and the anti-realist's meta-induction both look like probabilistic inferences, but they draw on different reference classes, and neither side seems able to justify its choice of reference class without already assuming what it is trying to prove. So I cannot tell whether the two camps are making incompatible claims about the same subject, or whether they are answering two different questions under a single heading: one about what we are entitled to infer, the other about what truth and reference can be said to consist in.

What I want from you is a diagnosis rather than a verdict. Is this impasse a substantive disagreement, a verbal one about how to count theories and what counts as retention, or two groups answering different questions under one name? Is there a hidden formal or empirical question doing the work — for instance, whether empirically successful is a projectible predicate at all, or whether the relevant reference class can be fixed by any non-question-begging procedure? If my hypothesis about individuation and reference classes is right, what follows for how the debate should be conducted; if it is wrong, where exactly does it fail? And given your diagnosis, tell me what would count as progress here: a distinction, a formal result, a decision procedure for theory identity, a reframing of the question, or nothing at all — and, if nothing, why the expectation of resolution is itself the mistake. I am not asking for a survey of the positions or a reading list; I want the structure of the disagreement exposed and an honest account of what could move it.
