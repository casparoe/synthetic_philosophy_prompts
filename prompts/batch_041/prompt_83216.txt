I run a monthly lunchtime session at work — 45 minutes, about twenty people, mostly engineers plus a couple of PMs, no philosophy background, and a healthy suspicion that "tech and society" talks are just vibes. Next month is Winner's "Do Artifacts Have Politics?" and I need the plan for how to explain it, not the explanation.

Here's what I keep botching, and it's why I'm asking: there are three different claims bundled into that question and they turn to mush when I try to run them in sequence. (1) A design can settle a public question by itself — lock in who gets through the door, who's welcome, who gets moved along — with nobody having to intend that, so "did they mean it?" is beside the point. (2) Some technologies you can't run at all without a particular social order coming along with them: you can't have the thing and also have it flat and decentralized. (3) The weaker one: the thing doesn't require that order, it's just extremely convinient for it, and the political fact is that we built this version when the alternative was sitting right there. When I run the three together I end up sounding like I'm saying everything is political, and that's where I lose the room.

So: what has to be in place before the question even lands, in what order, so that (1), (2) and (3) come out distinct rather than as one mush? What one case should carry the whole thing — I'd been planning the Robert Moses parkway bridges to Jones Beach, the ones supposedly built too low for buses so the beach stayed out of reach for people who couldn't drive, but I have a nagging memory that story has been challenged and I'd rather not open with folkore; my fallback is the benches with the armrests you can't lie down on, which they've all seen. Which should carry it, why not the other, and what does the carrying case have to have and have to lack? The misconceptions they'll arrive with or form, in the order they form them, and exactly where in the sequence I head each one off — I'm guessing "that's just technological determinism" is one and "if it had bad effects then it's political" is another, but I don't know which lands first. One question to ask at the end that shows they got the distinction instead of just agreeing with me. And what I should openly refuse to go into at this level and say so.

Keep the settled and the contested apart for me — if the bridges story, or one of those three claims, is genuinely disputed by people who work on this, tell me, rather than handing me the tidy version because it teaches better.
