A member of a decision-theory reading group I sit in on brought in a puzzle, and two of the others have now argued over it twice without getting anywhere. The puzzle: a driver is on a road with two turnoffs before his destination. Turning off at the first yields 0, at the second yields 4, and passing both yields 1. The turnoffs are indistinguishable, and the driver is absent-minded in the strong sense that on arriving at one he cannot tell whether it is the first or the second and retains no memory of what he decided or did earlier in the drive. A plan for him is therefore just a probability p of turning off at whichever turnoff he finds himself at.

Their exchange runs roughly as follows. (1) A: the rational thing is to stay on at the first and turn off at the second, which yields the best outcome, 4; so the puzzle cannot show that ordinary expected-utility reasoning fails. (2) B: that plan is unavailable — if he cannot distinguish the turnoffs, then to turn off at the second just is to turn off at the first, and always turning off yields 0 while never turning off yields 1. The best he can do is turn off with probability one third, worth 4/3, which beats either. (3) A: granted, but 4/3 is still worse than the 4 he can describe, so the puzzle shows only that such a driver is badly off, not that his reasoning is flawed; and total absent-mindedness is an artificial stipulation, since a driver who remembers even that he decided to keep going could infer he is at the second turnoff and collect the 4. (4) B: the stipulation is the puzzle; the question is what is rational for the agent as described. The real lesson concerns how he must reason at a turnoff: he cannot credit continuing with the value it has when he is at the first turnoff, since he does not know he is there, so the value of continuing is a mixture over both possibilities weighted by his own plan — his credence about where he is and his plan have to be settled together. (5) A: that fixed-point story resembles the self-locating reasoning in the Sleeping Beauty problem, and importing one unresolved paradox to settle another seems no help; Beauty's payoffs do not depend on her own plan the way the driver's do.

Please adjudicate the exchange move by move, saying who is right about which specific claim and briefly why, what each side gets right even where it overreaches, and what both are missing. I would also like to know whether any defensible reading makes move (1) correct after all, or whether it is simply mistaken as stated. I have no stake in the outcome.
