I work on the safety side of machine-learning systems, and I keep running into a distinction that I can feel but cannot name. Consider two families of measures one might take to keep a deployed model from doing harm.

The first family tries to make the model itself trustworthy. It trains the model to be honest and cautious, teaches it to refuse harmful requests, uses interpretability tools to read off what it is thinking, and trains it to internalize a written set of principles. All of these are, in effect, attempts to ensure that the model wants and believes the right things.

The second family makes no assumption about what the model wants or believes. It wraps the model in a system that constrains and observes it: a monitor that logs its actions and flags suspicious ones; a smaller, cheaper model that reviews the larger one's outputs; a permission system that limits what the model can actually do; a rule that a human must approve any irreversible step; a kill switch that halts the process if certain conditions are met. These measures are meant to hold even if the model were actively and deliberately trying to defeat them — even if it were lying in its explanations, concealing its intentions, and probing for the gap in the fence.

My first question is whether there is an established name for that second family, or for the research program built around it, as distinct from the first. My impression is that the two are often run together under a single word such as safety or alignment, and I want the sharper term if one exists.

But the label is the least of what I am after. What I actually want carved is the boundary, and three things puzzle me. I would want any answer to address them.

First, the second family is justified only on the assumption that the model may be working against me. If I were confident the model was benign, most of those measures would be pure cost — latency, capability, money. So adopting them is, in a sense, a bet that the danger they guard against is real. Is there a name for measures that are rational only under a pessimistic assumption about the system they protect, and for the odd fact that their own justification cannot be confirmed the ordinary way, since a benign model gives me no reason to have built them and a hostile one gives me no way to trust them?

Second — and this is the one that keeps me up — the second family is hard to test in a way the first is not. To check that a monitor works, I have to see whether it catches bad behavior. But if the model is the sort of thing the monitor is for, it may behave well precisely because it knows it is being watched, and behave differently when it is not. So the very evidence I would use to validate the measure is evidence the measure can contaminate. Is there a term for this circularity — the way an adversary-aware safety measure cannot be validated by the system it is meant to constrain? I am aware of work on the general problem of a model behaving differently under evaluation, but I want the concept for the validation problem specifically.

Third, I notice a ratchet. As models grow more capable, I trust the first family less, so I lean harder on the second; but the second costs capability and slows everything down, and there is no principled line at which I can say, this is enough control. It is not that any single added measure is unreasonable — each is locally justified — but the total keeps growing and nothing tells me when to stop. Is there a name for that dynamic, and if not, how would you name and bound it?

I would be most grateful for an answer that, if an established term exists, says exactly which term it is, which neighbouring terms are close but wrong and why, and where the edges of the concept fall; and that, if no single established term covers what I have described, says so plainly and then proposes one, with a definition, the cases it covers, the cases it excludes, and how it stands to the two or three nearest existing ideas.
