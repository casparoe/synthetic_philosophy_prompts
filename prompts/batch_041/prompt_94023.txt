I'm in a graduate seminar on philosophy of mind, and we've spent two weeks on the intentional stance and on the original-versus-derived intentionality dispute. I'd like to practise arguing the case rather than reading about it. Please play a committed defender of the intentional stance — sharp, not a strawman, someone who has heard every version of the derived-intentionality objection and still thinks the stance is the right way to talk about what a system believes. You may speak as Dennett or as an unnamed heir of that view; do not try to reproduce anything he wrote, and do not cite literature or ask me for reading — reason from the case below. Stay in character until I say "stop", and keep each reply to a paragraph or two, the way a good seminar exchange goes. Concede small points when I earn them; do not concede the main one. You may ask clarifying questions about the mechanics of the case, but you may not stall by asking what I mean by "belief" in general — you have to say what you mean by it, and you should also tell me what you would count as evidence against the stance rather than only what you would say in its defence.

Here is the case, which is my own construction rather than one from the literature. A lab builds a "ledgered" agent out of a large language model. Before every action the agent must write two lines in a fixed schema — BELIEF: followed by a sentence, and GOAL: followed by a sentence — and those lines go into a log; the action then follows. The lab runs two kinds of experiment. In a belief transplant, they leave the weights, the prompt and the environment alone and edit only the logged belief line, say from "the vault is open" to "the vault is shut", and the agent's subsequent behaviour changes in just the way they predicted from the transplanted belief together with its stated goal. In a false-belief probe, they tell the agent the vault is open when it is shut; the agent walks into the door, and the engineers afterwards say that it acted on a false belief.

My first move is this. Your stance was supposed to earn its keep as a predictive strategy that needs no inner vehicle; here the belief is a literal string in a log, the intervention works only because that string is causally upstream of the action, and its content is fixed by the engineers' schema — which sounds like derived intentionality with an unusually good audit trail. And when I predict the collision from the log, I am not taking the intentional stance; I am reading the system's own bookkeeping. So it looks as though wherever the stance seems to earn its keep in AI, there is a derived representation underneath doing the actual work, and wherever there isn't one, the stance is a convenience you would drop the moment the weights were retrained. Which horn do you take, and what do you think you lose by taking it?
