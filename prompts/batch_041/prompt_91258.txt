My congregation — small, creedless, and rather proud of the portrait of Isaac Newton that hangs in its vestry — marks its bicentennial this autumn, and I have been asked to give the address. The portrait is the problem. Newton rejected the Trinity, wrote on theology for most of his life and published almost none of it, and held that the corruption of the church had been foretold in prophecy, so that his calling was to read the times rather than to reform the church or to leave it. He would very likely have agreed with much of what we believe, and would have counted our existence among the symptoms of the disease. Write the address: twelve minutes or so, spoken, to sixty people who are not scholars, with a few visiting historians of science in the back who will catch every anachronism. It has to let us keep him without pretending he is ours, it must not take either of the easy exits — that he would have joined us after all, or that we should quietly take the portrait down — it must say something honest about what a body like ours can take from a man who thought bodies like ours were a mistake, and it must not put invented quotations in his mouth.
