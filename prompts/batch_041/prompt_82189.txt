You are a philosopher of mind with expertise in the metaphysics of causation, the epistemology of self-knowledge, and the debate over phenomenal consciousness. I want you to work slowly and carefully on the following. Before you answer, brainstorm the candidate diagnoses in a scratch section, then commit to one.

Background. Epiphenomenalism, as I will use the term, is the view that phenomenal properties — the felt qualities of experiences — are caused by physical properties of the brain but themselves cause nothing; the physical world is causally closed. A colleague reconstructs an argument she says shows that epiphenomenalism is self-undermining. Here it is.

(1) Epiphenomenalism is as just defined: experiences are caused by physical states and cause nothing; the physical is causally closed.

(2) When I judge that I am now undergoing a particular experience — say, that I am seeing a certain shade of red — that judgment is itself a physical event in me, and it reliably matches the experience it reports. I do not merely guess what I am feeling.

(3) Where a state S and a judgment J about S are reliably correlated, either the correlation is a coincidence, or S causes J, or some third thing causes both.

(4) The correlation between my experiences and my reports of them is not a coincidence. It is exceptionlessly reliable and extends to experiences I could not have predicted.

(5) By (1), experiences cause nothing. So the correlation is explained by a common cause: the physical state C that produces both the experience and the report.

(6) Now consider my zombie twin: physically identical to me at every moment, hence in the same state C, but undergoing no experiences at all. By (1) and (5), C is sufficient for the report; whether an experience accompanies it makes no difference to what gets said. The twin sincerely asserts, in exactly my words, that it is conscious.

(7) So the causal chain leading to my assertion that I am conscious would run exactly the same course even if I had no experiences. My assertion is not sensitive to the presence of the experience it purports to report.

(8) A judgment that would have occurred whether or not its subject matter existed is no evidence for the existence of that subject matter.

(9) Therefore my apparent evidence for the existence of my own experiences — that I seem to be conscious — is no evidence at all. Any argument for epiphenomenalism that begins from the reality of experience is gutted; the view cannot be supported by experience. Epiphenomenalism is self-stultifying.

Two of my friends think the argument proves too much. One says that if it works, it refutes not only epiphenomenalism but every view on which the felt quality of an experience is not itself the physical property that fixes the report — so property dualism and non-reductive physicalism fall with it, leaving only type-identity. The other says (8) is the real engine, and that a sensitivity requirement of that form would also strip us of evidence for anything that does not cause our beliefs — mathematical objects, the normative — which would be a reductio of (8).

I am not asking you to tell me whether epiphenomenalism is true. I want a diagnosis. Which step should be rejected? Be precise about whether the flaw is a genuine error — an equivocation, a slide from causation to justification, a false dichotomy in (3), a scope or modality mistake, a step valid only under a reading the premises do not support — or whether the argument is valid and the only question is which plausible premise is least costly to give up. For the step you name, explain why it looked innocent, then price the alternatives: what each rival rejection would cost, and whether the cost of your preferred rejection is paid only by the epiphenomenalist or by everyone. In particular, say whether the argument, properly repaired, leaves epiphenomenalism standing, and whether your repair preserves (8) as a usable principle for the past, other minds, and abstracta.
