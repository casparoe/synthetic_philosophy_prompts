I want to work through a case against the Reflection principle in the form of a sustained exchange, and I want you to take the defending side. Play a committed defender of the principle: someone who holds that there is a genuine coherence requirement linking an agent's present credences to the credences she expects to have, that violating it is a defect in her doxastic state rather than a departure from a merely optional ideal, and that the requirement is not empty. This is not a request for a survey of what others have said. Argue from the position, in the first person, in continuous prose rather than numbered lists, at roughly a paragraph or two per turn, and stay in character until I say 'stop'. Do not refer to authors, works, or the literature by name, and do not attribute views to anyone; if you need a distinction, name and define it yourself. Concede particular points when the strongest version of your position concedes them — you may distinguish coherence from evidential support, or narrow the principle's scope — but you may not abandon the requirement, and if you narrow it you must say what work the narrowed version still does. If you think my formulation of the principle is wrong, give yours and say plainly which version you are defending, since my objection is aimed at a particular one. Announcements that a case falls outside the principle's intended range will be treated as substantive claims to be defended, not as repairs.

My opening. Tonight I shall take a pill. I have excellent, inspectable evidence that the pill reliably induces, by tomorrow morning, a settled conviction that a certain factual claim H about the distant past is true — a credence of about 0.9 — and that it induces this conviction regardless of whether H is true: the conviction is produced by the drug, not by any information about H. As it happens I have no other evidence bearing on H; my credence in it is 0.5, and I am 0.99 confident that my credence tomorrow will be 0.9. Now, the principle as I understand it requires my present credence in H conditional on my future credence in H being 0.9 to be 0.9 — that is, P(H | P_tomorrow(H) = 0.9) = 0.9 — and since I am nearly certain that my future credence will be 0.9, it requires my present credence in H to be about 0.9. But my present credences are, by hypothesis, precisely what the evidence supports: the probability of H conditional on my later holding a drug-induced 0.9 is just 0.5, because the drug's operation is independent of H, and my present credences already satisfy every ordinary requirement of probabilistic coherence. So the principle demands that I adopt an opinion I know to be unsupported, on the strength of a fact about my future psychology that I know to be unconnected with the truth of that opinion. That looks like a reductio. The natural repair — that the principle governs only future credences that are themselves rational, or only credences I now endorse — seems to me either to exclude the case by fiat, since whether my future self will be rational is precisely what is in question, or to leave a principle too weak to do the work you want from it in the argument for conditionalizing or in the Dutch-book argument. So: which version are you defending, why does it survive the pill, and what does it still rule out? If you survive this, my next move is a case in which I expect to lose evidence I now have.
