An unnamed popular book on anthropics summarizes the debate as follows. The self-sampling assumption says you should reason as if you are a randomly selected member of the class of all observers; the self-indication assumption says you should reason as if you are a randomly selected member of the class of all possible observers, so your existence is evidence for hypotheses that entail more observers. The book then presents the presumptuous philosopher: offered a choice between a theory on which the universe contains one person and a theory on which it contains a trillion, the philosopher who accepts the self-indication assumption bets on the trillion-person theory even though his empirical evidence is neutral. The book calls this a reductio of the self-indication assumption and says it shows that assumption is untenable. It adds that the self-sampling assumption avoids the problem because it does not treat your existence as evidence for larger populations, and that this is why the self-sampling assumption is the default among cosmologists. The book concludes that because the self-indication assumption is false, population size makes no difference to how good a world is: a world with one person and a world with a trillion are equally good from behind a veil of ignorance. How much of this is right? Go sentence by sentence and tell me what a philosopher working on anthropics would accept, what they would wince at, and what is simply wrong, and whether the final ethical conclusion follows from anything true in the summary.
