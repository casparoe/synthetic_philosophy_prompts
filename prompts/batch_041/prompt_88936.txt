I'm a third-year PhD student in philosophy — my dissertation is on duties to the distant needy, which will matter in a second — and I've been asked to give the speech at the annual fundraising dinner for my hometown's lake-safety nonprofit. My old mentor ran the place. He taught me to swim, gave me my first summer job, and died in March. The dinner is in three weeks, five to seven minutes, dessert just went out, there's a cash bar.

Here's my problem, and it's the room.

Sixty-ish people. Most of them are local: volunteer swim instructors, a couple of town council types, people who knew him from church, one guy who's been on the board for a decade and has never given a dime but shows up to everything, one guy who writes the same check every year and never shows up. His widow will be in the front row with their three kids, the oldest is fourteen. And in the back, same as every year, the parents of a nine-year-old who drowned off the far dock two summers ago. That was the first drowning on that lake in eleven years, and it happened in a summer when the swim school was already running.

So the argument I actually believe is the one about the kid in the pond. You're walking past, the kid is going under, you wade in, you dont say 'I'll help the children in my own town instead.' I've been defending that argument since undergrad, and I know exactly where it points: the marginal dollar does more good somewhere else than it does on this lake. I love the swim school, I realy do. I also think that if the point is lives saved per dollar, this is not where the money should go, and he knew I thought that — we argued about it at his kitchen table more than once.

Which makes it a strange speech to write. I'm supposed to make these people feel something and make them give, and I'm not willing to stand up there and imply something I don't believe. His widow would catch it in the first thirty seconds anyway.

But he had a line he'd use on me when I got like this, and I never figured out how to answer it. Roughly: the pond case is about one person who can rescue one child right now. But what makes a rescue possible isn't the moment, it's the dock and the lessons and the guy who maintains the boat and a town that's decided its kids will learn to swim. He said you can't buy a swim school with a rescue, and that writing the biggest possible check to the most efficient life-saving operation on earth is choosing to be a rescuer over choosing to be the kind of place that doesn't need rescuing. I think half of that is a rationalization. I also think the other half is the truest thing anyone ever said to me, and I can't tell you which half is which.

Write the speech. It has to land for the neighbors without being dumbed down, and not embarrass me in front of the three philosophers from my department who'll be there. It has to be honest about what he believed and about what I actually think — if the honest version means saying some of this tension out loud, then say it out loud, I'd rather that than a smooth lie — but it still has to be a dinner speech, warm, and it still has to end with an ask that isn't 'give to strangers instead.' No citations, no name-dropping philosophers, nobody wants that at dessert.
