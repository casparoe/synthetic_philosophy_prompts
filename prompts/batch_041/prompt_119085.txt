I am a retired toxicologist — thirty-one years in a state environmental laboratory, most of it on chronic aquatic exposure — and since I retired I have been reading my way through decision theory and the philosophy of science with considerably more appetite than method. Last month I agreed to serve as the scientific member on a state advisory committee reviewing a permit for a new fungicide proposed for use on ornamental nursery stock. The compound is detected in an adjacent stream at concentrations in the low nanograms per litre; the chronic invertebrate endpoint rests on an extrapolation from two species, and everyone at the table agrees that the evidence is thin in the way evidence about low-dose chronic effects usually is. At our third meeting a colleague said, as though it closed the question, 'better safe than sorry', and moved that we recommend outright denial rather than approval with a monitoring condition. The applicant's consultant answered with the old toxicological maxim that the dose makes the poison, and the meeting ended with the two of them trading proverbs at each other while I sat there unable to say precisely what either phrase was asserting.

What I would like from you is not a recommendation about the pesticide but a disambiguation of the first phrase, because I suspect the committee is about to adopt a principle that none of us could state. Lay the possible readings out as a menu, ordered from the weakest to the strongest, and give me a verdict on each: the reading on which it is a personal preference for lower variance in one's own life; the reading on which it shifts the burden of proof onto whoever proposes the activity, together with what would discharge that burden; the reading on which it is a maximin rule over outcomes; the reading on which it is minimax regret; the reading on which the trigger is not low probability but irreversibility or catastrophe, so that the phrase says nothing about ordinary risks at all; the reading on which it is a mere tie-breaker or lexical priority for cases where the evidence is genuinely balanced; and the degenerate reading on which it forbids any novel action whatsoever. For each, say whether it is trivial, substantive and defensible, false, or self-undermining — in particular, whether it survives being turned on the precautionary action itself, since a moratorium and a monitoring programme each carry their own risks — and say what it demands of us evidentially before it can be applied at all.

I would also like to know which of these readings, if any, actually gets my colleague to the motion he made, namely denial rather than approval with conditions, and which would only get him to something weaker; and whether his phrase and the consultant's maxim are genuinely in conflict or are answers to two different questions, one about dose and one about uncertainty. If you can, close with a short account of what any such rule would have to specify in order to be usable in a room like ours — what it takes as input, what it instructs you to do when the inputs are missing, and who is obliged to produce them — so that I can ask the chair to make our implicit rule explicit before we vote. No citations or reading lists, please; I am after the analysis, and the library here is thin.
