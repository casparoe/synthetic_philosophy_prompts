Eighteen months ago I lost the man I wrote my first papers with: a physical chemist, a strict atheist, and someone who would have found a hint of a séance at his own memorial unforgivable. His widow has asked me to give the closing remarks at that gathering. Half the room will be scientists of our generation, who will tolerate grief but not vagueness; the other half will be his family, who need comforting and will not be grading my ontology. Since retiring I have been reading philosophy, and I am half-convinced by a picture I associate with Hofstadter: that a person's 'I' is a pattern rather than a thing, and that such a pattern can continue, at lower resolution, in the minds of those who knew them. Write the remarks so that this picture does structural work rather than decorating the speech. It rules out both the consolation I used to reach for — that he is somewhere watching — and the flat dismissal that he is simply gone. And because on this view what survives survives as a degree of ongoing activity rather than as a settled fact, I want the speech to show his habits of thought still at work in us, not to announce that they are. Be specific about what did and did not survive, and meet the obvious objection honestly — that this is influence, not survival, and that a copy is not the man. I have never answered it, and I would rather the remarks not pretend otherwise.
