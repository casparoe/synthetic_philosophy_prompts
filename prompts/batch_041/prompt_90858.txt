I am trying to understand a difficult stretch of Hume's essay on suicide. Since I do not have the text in front of me, I will paraphrase the argument I am struggling with: Hume claims that suicide cannot be a violation of our duty to God, because the entire order of nature—including whatever power we have to end our own lives—is encompassed by divine providence; that it cannot be a violation of our duty to society, because no individual's contribution is so indispensable that one may never withdraw from it; and that it cannot be a violation of our duty to ourselves, because life can become a burden from which we may reasonably seek release. My difficulty is with the first claim. If divine providence encompasses everything that happens, including acts we ordinarily call crimes, then the claim that suicide is not a violation of duty to God seems to prove too much: it would equally excuse murder or betrayal. How is Hume's argument supposed to avoid this collapse? Does he rely on a distinction between God's permission and God's command, or between natural and moral evil, or something else? Please explain the structure of the argument in a way that makes clear what work the providence premise is doing, and then state what an opponent would have to establish in order to show that the argument fails.
