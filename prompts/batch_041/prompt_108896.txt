Last night my friend Dana and I spent like three hours arguing about whether you can cause something by doing nothing. It started as a dumb joke about me forgetting to water her plants and somehow ended up with us drawing diagrams on the back of a receipt: lifeguard asleep on the stand while someone drowns — is the cause the sleeping, or the not-swimming-in, or the drowning, or is there just no cause there and we're reading our own guilt into the world. Dana thinks causation by omission is basically a projection of our blame habits onto the furniture of the universe. I think there's something real under there but every time I try to say what it is I end up just redescribing the blame habits. She got me on one thing: she asked why, if omissions can cause, my forgetting her plants isn't "the cause" of them dying in the same sense the drought is, and I didn't have an answer that wasn't just about me feeling bad.

The only thing I got out of the whole night was a half-idea. Maybe the interesting cases aren't the pure inaction ones (bystander, nothing happening, no mechanism anywhere) but the ones where some process is already churning and the omission is a hole punched in it — the nurse skipping the dose, the brake that doesn't fire, the auditor who never looks. Like the omission borrows its causal oomph from the mechanism it interrupts. Dana says that's just the problem restated. Is it?

I need to do something with this or it'll eat another three evenings. I'm convening a small working group next term: me, five grad students, one person from the law school, one from the hospital ethics committee. Twelve weeks, no lab, no data, no money, just arguments and cases. Give me an agenda — which open questions about omission-causation actually matter most for a group like that, ranked, and why.

Defend the ordering. For each one, tell me whether it's upstream or downstream of the others (if we settled it, what gets easier or just dissolves), how tractable it is for people with no lab and one semester, and whether it's the kind of thing where the answer would change what anyone does or believes outside the seminar room. Flag the ones that feel deep but wouldn't move anything downstream even if we nailed them — I'd rather skip those. Also, does the picture change when the agent is a system rather than a person: a car that doesn't brake, a recommender that doesn't show the warning, something with no duties and arguably no agent. Is that a genuinely new question or the old one wearing a costume? And say what evidence or argument over the next year or two would bump a question up or down your list.

Dont give me a reading list, I know the literature better than I'd like to. I want your reasoning about what's worth the twelve weeks, not a syllabus.
