I am a novelist, and for the past several years I have taught a graduate seminar, cross-listed between the writing program and the cognitive science department, on the problem of style: what it is for a hand, a typeface, or a sentence to have one, and how much of it is available to the person or the program producing it. I spend my working life making sentences in a voice I could not define if asked, so the seminar is partly self-interested. This term we closed with the Letter Spirit chapter in *Fluid Concepts and Creative Analogies*, and the take-home midterm asked the students to work through the project's central distinction rather than summarize it. I am not a cognitive scientist by training and I do not want to penalize an answer for being written by a fiction writer, so I would like you to grade one of them on its content: say what it gets right, name each problem and point to the sentence that carries it, say whether the problem is local or whether it damages the answer's main line of argument, and then give a grade. The course uses plus/minus letter grades and I record a mark out of twenty alongside them, so please give me both. What I need is a point-by-point assessment rather than a general impression, and in particular I would like to know what a careful reader would write in the margin.

The question was this: "Letter Spirit is usually described as a program that designs typefaces, but Hofstadter treats the interesting thing as the pair of questions one can ask of any letterform: which letter is this, and in what spirit is it drawn? (a) Explain how those two questions are related in the project, and say what the role hypothesis is meant to do for each of them. (b) The program works only in the gridfont microdomain, where letters are built out of cells on a coarse grid, so that curves, serifs, and stroke weights are unavailable. Does that restriction make the categorization question trivial, or does it preserve it? Justify your answer. (c) The program drafts, perceives, and revises its own letters in a loop. Does that loop amount to autonomy in designing a typeface, or does something else do the real work? Take a position and defend it."

The answer I would like graded reads as follows. "Letter Spirit is built around two questions that can be asked of any mark on the page: which letter is this, and in what spirit is it drawn? Hofstadter keeps them apart because they vary independently — an 'a' can be an 'a' across a hundred typefaces, and one typeface's spirit shows up across the whole alphabet. The first question concerns identity, the second concerns appearance, and the gridfont microdomain exists so that a program can work on the second without being distracted by the first. The role hypothesis is the bridge between them. A letter concept is not a template or a stored image but a set of constituent roles, and a role is a part that every instance of the letter must have — the bowl of a 'b', the vertical stroke of an 'h', the crossbar of an 'e'. Because a letter is made of its roles, and because a style just is a way of filling them, style is not a layer painted over a shape; it lives at the deepest level, in how the parts are formed and related to one another. That is why the program needs perception rather than pattern matching: the letterform has to be segmented into its roles before anything else can happen. As for the grid, it strips away curves, serifs, and stroke weights — precisely the things that make real typeface design hard to formalize. Once a letter is a pattern of filled cells, whether a shape is an 'a' or a 'd' is settled by the cell pattern, so the grid buys the program a clean categorization step and leaves the genuinely creative work, inventing a spirit and carrying it across the alphabet, as the thing being modeled. On autonomy, I would say the loop gives the program a real independence. It drafts, perceives, and revises without a human in the loop, so the human's role shrinks to setting the problem, and the old division of labor between designer and tool no longer holds. The program's taste is only as good as its perception — a misread letter will be confidently polished into a worse one — but that is a question of how well the role model perceives, which is separate from whether the program is making its own decisions." Judge it on the content and not on the prose.
