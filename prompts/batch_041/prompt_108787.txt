I'm putting together a position statement for a graduate seminar on machine minds, and I want a consistency check before I circulate it. Here is where I've landed:

(1) Behavioral indistinguishability is the strongest evidence we could have that a machine is intelligent — stronger than any account of its internal organization.

(2) I'm an interpretivist about the propositional attitudes: for a system to believe or desire something is just for its behavior to be best predicted and explained by attributing those states to it.

(3) I take the Chinese room seriously, and I don't think it helps to reply that the whole room, rather than the man inside, is what understands — the system as a whole still only manipulates symbols, so getting the behavior right cannot by itself guarantee that thought is occurring.

(4) I don't think phenomenal consciousness is necessary for thought: a machine could think and be intelligent while there is nothing it is like to be it.

(5) I think there is no behavioral test for consciousness of the kind there is for intelligence — a system could be flawless in conversation and still be a zombie.

I suspect this set does not hang together, but I can't tell which tensions are real and which are artifacts of how I've phrased things. Please audit it: identify the pairs that genuinely pull in opposite directions, rank them by severity, and for each say what would have to give and what that revision would cost. Distinguish those from the pairs that only look incompatible, and explain why they don't actually conflict. Then tell me which single revision would clean up the most at once, and what I would be surrendering to make it. Please write your answer in American English.
