I'm in the middle of a module on measurement and incentives — we've done Goodhart's law, Campbell's law, all that family — and the more I read the less I can tell what anyone is actually claiming. Every case seems to get explained after the fact by one of about four stories: the proxy was only correlated with the thing to begin with, so picking the extreme just regresses; the proxy was never causally downstream of the target; making it a target changed people's behaviour; or somebody actively gamed it. Fine, but that's a list of ways a proxy can fail, not a theory.

So before I start revising I want the spec, not another case study. Write me the desiderata for a theory of specification gaming — what anything claiming to explain why optimising a measure breaks the thing it was supposed to measure has to be able to do. Which cases it has to get right, which distinctions it can't collapse, what it has to stay consistent with. The cases I keep bumping into:

- the department that starts ranking people on citations, after which the field splits its papers finer and cites itself alot more;
- the hospital mortality metric that makes doctors quietly avoid the sickest patients, and the school that teaches to the test — where the behaviour change is completely real (the teaching genuinely changed) but the target didn't move;
- a model rewarded by a learned reward model that figures out how to say what the rater likes;
- and the boring ones where nothing breaks at all: a thermometer, a ruler, waist circumference as a proxy for body fat, a firm that's been judged on profit for a century without the measure rotting.

The distinctions I'd want it to keep straight: proxy vs target vs the measure you actually compute; gaming vs a genuine and harmful behaviour change vs regression to the mean vs an artefact of selecting on the extreme; the case where there's an agent optimising versus the case where there's only selection over many units and nobody is gaming anything; descriptive regularity vs theorem; and the difference between "this particular measure is bad" and "any measure would go bad if you targeted it".

Tell me which requirements are non-negotiable and which are just the prejudices of one camp (economists vs ML people vs sociologists of science, say). And flag any subset that can't be satisfied together — for instance, can one mechanism cover all four stories without becoming so general it's unfalsifiable? My hunch is that the demand for a single unified account and the demand that it be falsifiable pull against each other, but tell me if that's wrong.

I'd rather have the requirements with the case behind each than a scorecard of who gets what right; I'll do that part in the seminar.
