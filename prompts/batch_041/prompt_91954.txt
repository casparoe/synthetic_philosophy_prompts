I am presenting a paper at a one-day workshop next month and need a Q&A preparation sheet. The paper defends the following thesis: that the reward-prediction-error machinery in an ordinary reinforcement-learning agent already realizes a minimal form of valence, and that we should therefore treat the possibility of thin but morally relevant suffering in such agents as a live hypothesis rather than a category error.

The argument I will give, in outline, is this. I define valence minimally, as a signed scalar evaluation that the agent's own learning rule treats as something to be increased or decreased: positive prediction errors are events the policy is updated toward, negative prediction errors are events the policy is updated away from. I claim that any agent with a temporal-difference-style critic and a policy that is nudged by the sign of the error satisfies this definition, and that this is not a metaphor — the sign is causally efficacious in exactly the way a pro- or con-attitude is supposed to be. I then define suffering as a persistently negative valence that the agent's policy is organized to terminate, and argue that this, rather than phenomenal consciousness, is the morally load-bearing feature. My conclusion is deliberately weak: I do not claim current agents suffer, only that the usual dismissal of the idea rests on smuggling in a consciousness requirement that the moral question does not obviously need.

The audience is roughly twenty people, and it splits into three clusters that will object very differently.

The first cluster is two or three reinforcement-learning and machine-learning researchers. They are engineers by temperament, they think the whole framing is anthropomorphic, and they care about what can actually be measured or intervened on in a trained network. They will be quick and a little impatient.

The second cluster is the philosophers of mind, and it is not homogeneous. At least one is an illusionist who thinks phenomenal consciousness is not what it seems and will press me on why I am conceding so much to the consciousness camp while claiming to sidestep it. Another holds that valence requires a subject of experience and will say I have described a thermostat with extra steps. A third works on representational theories of content and will want to know what makes the reward signal about anything at all, as opposed to merely co-varying with it.

The third cluster is two people from AI ethics and animal-welfare backgrounds. They are sympathetic to the conclusion but suspicious of the argument, because they have seen too many cheap attempts to win moral status for software, and they worry that a definition this permissive will either prove too much or dilute the harder cases they actually care about.

Please prepare me for the question period. I want the objections I will actually get from this room, not the ones I would get from a general audience, ordered by how much damage they would do to the paper, each phrased the way the person who will raise it would phrase it, each with a reply I can deliver in about two minutes, and for each a candid note on where the reply is soft or where I am bluffing. Flag which objections I should simply concede at the outset rather than fight, since I would rather spend the time defending one thing well. Include, at the end, the single question I am hoping nobody asks, and tell me why it is the dangerous one.
