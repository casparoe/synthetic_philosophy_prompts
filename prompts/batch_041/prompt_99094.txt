I run a weekly social choice reading group for graduate students in philosophy and economics, twenty-one of us, ninety minutes, and the room is always split between people who already know the standard results and people who have never seen a preference table. I want to open the term with a session in which the group produces the paradox of voting with its own hands, before anyone has heard the phrase. Please act as an expert in social choice theory who also designs classroom simulations, and think carefully before you answer: begin with a short brainstorm in which you work out exactly what preferences and what voting procedure would make the discovery unavoidable for a group this size, and then give me the finished design. Do not lecture at the start, and do not let the phenomenon be named until the debrief.

Here is the sketch I want you to improve. Before the session I prepare twenty-one cards, one per participant, each bearing a ranking of three concrete options for what we actually do in the final meeting of term: reading a paper together, screening a documentary, or taking a walk. Seven cards read paper over documentary over walk; seven read documentary over walk over paper; seven read walk over paper over documentary. Each participant holds only their own card, and the three options are the only ones on the table. Phase one: I take three majority votes by show of hands, paper against documentary, documentary against walk, and paper against walk, and let the group watch the results land. Phase two: I hand a chair the power to set the order of the votes, with the winner of the first contest meeting the remaining option, and I rotate the chair until someone notices what the chair can do. Phase three: I quietly swap cards with a few participants so that a majority now ranks a single option first, we re-run the votes, and the group has to explain why the instability vanished. Phase four: I announce a fixed agenda in advance and ask whether anyone now has a reason to vote for something other than their favourite in the first round. The winning option is what we really do at the end of term, so there is a genuine if small stake and no money involved. Give me the rules, the materials list, a minute-by-minute timeline, the exact point at which I should stop withholding the name, and a debrief that maps each phase onto what it is meant to teach: the cycling of majority preference, the agenda-setter's power, the difference between a group whose preferences cycle and individual voters who are being irrational, and the incentive to vote strategically under a fixed agenda. Then tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle it, including the participant who proposes a point system or approval voting and thereby dissolves the whole demonstration.

Be frank about the limit of the exercise: I suspect it cannot stage the other thing that gets called the paradox of voting, the one about whether a single ballot is ever decisive, and I want you to say plainly that the facilitator must supply that part, along with a suggestion for how briefly to do it without muddying the first lesson. Please take a definite stand throughout rather than laying out alternatives for me to choose from: commit to one arrangement, defend the choices you make about timing and card counts, tell me which phase you would cut if we ran short, and say where you are least confident that the arrangement will actually force the discovery rather than merely illustrate it.
