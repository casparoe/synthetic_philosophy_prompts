I'm a software engineer with no formal training in philosophy, and I've been asked to give the toast at the retirement dinner for my friend Arthur, who has programmed our town's repertory cinema for thirty years. The room will be roughly half cinema regulars — people who trust his canon and can still quote his program notes — and half family and old friends who know him mainly as the man who could be withering about a film everyone else enjoyed. I want the toast to land for both halves.

Here is the problem I keep circling. The easy toast is the relativist one: taste is subjective, he had his, you have yours, let's raise a glass. I don't believe that, and it would insult him. The other easy toast is the authoritarian one: he was simply right, and anyone who disagreed was simply wrong. I don't quite believe that either, and it would insult half the room. What I have been reading is Hume's essay on the standard of taste — the view that beauty is not a property of the object but a sentiment in the viewer, and yet some viewers' verdicts carry authority because of how their palate was formed: delicacy, practice, comparison, and freedom from prejudice. I want the toast built on that view. I don't want it to be a lecture about the view, and I don't want it to quote Hume. Work it in through the details of his life.

Two things I would like the toast to do, and I would rather it fail honestly at them than paper over them.

First, take a definite position on whether his verdicts were right or only his. Do not end on the idea that both are true in their own way. If you think the Humean line can support only something weaker than right — better formed, more authoritative, the verdict of someone who did the work — then commit to that weaker claim and let it shape the whole toast. But commit.

Second, deal with the circularity I cannot stop noticing: the standard of taste is the verdict of the ideal critic, and the ideal critic seems to be defined as the one who gets the verdicts right. I keep wanting to treat the ideal critic as a specification rather than a person — something you approximate through practices, not something you are. I would like the toast to do something like that. You have three concrete details to work with. He banned applause at the end of screenings, on the theory that applause is social contagion and corrupts the sentiment you actually had. He kept a notebook he titled Films I Was Wrong About, and he added to it until the week he retired. And he panned a film called The Glass Orchard the week it opened, in a program note that some people in the room still have not forgiven.

That last point matters. One of the guests, Nadia, has The Glass Orchard as her favorite film, and she is genuinely still a little hurt. The toast should make room for that without conceding that her taste is just as good as his, and without telling her she is wrong. I do not know how to do that, which is part of why I am asking.

Please write the toast — about five minutes, roughly 700 to 800 words — and then a short note, 150 words or so, on where the toast takes liberties with Hume or leans on a contested reading, so I know what I am committing to when I read it aloud. No direct quotations from Hume, and please do not fall back on the idea that beauty is in the eye of the beholder.
