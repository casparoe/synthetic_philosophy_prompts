I'm a philosphy professor who somehow ended up chairing the ethics committee at our teaching hospital, and we keep getting asked whether someone is dead yet. Here's our track record. A young man with a destroyed brain — no brainstem reflexes, can't breathe off the vent, heart still going on its own — dead. A woman a year out from a massive hypoxic injury, cortex basically gone, no awareness of anything, but her brainstem is fine: she breathes on her own, sleeps and wakes. Not dead. We told the team they could stop treatment, but we were clear that she's alive. A patient with a DNR order, family at the bedside, heart stops, nobody starts compressions: dead, and dead from the moment it was clear no one was going to try. Then a guy who collapses in the parking lot, a bystander starts compressions within seconds, the paramedics get him back, he walks out a week later: never dead, we said, not even while his heart was stopped.

Now the one I'm chewing on. A patient on a vent, not brain dead, family agrees to withdraw and to donate his organs. We pull the tube, his heart stops, we wait five minutes like the protocol says and declare him dead. Then the transplant team hooks the body up to a machine that pumps blood back through it to keep the organs fresh. Was he dead when they flipped the switch? I have a hunch what my committee will say, but I want to know what the pattern actually commits us to: what rule we've been running, what other rule fits all four of our verdicts just as well, what each one predicts here, and which case I'd have to put in front of them to tell the two apart.
