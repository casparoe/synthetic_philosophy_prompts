I'm teaching the opening session of a seminar on judging historical figures, and I need an instrument for it rather than a lecture. The topic is the "he was a man of his time" defense -- the move everyone makes about Jefferson, Churchill, Columbus -- and what I want is a classification of the positions people actually hold about it, laid out as a tree where every branching node is a question a student could answer without already knowing which position it lands them in.

Put the object of judgment at the top, because half the circularity in these arguements comes from one person grading a person while the other is grading a practice or an institution. After that, the branches should be the things that really do split people: is the defense meant to excuse the person or only to explain how it happened; is "his time" a claim about which beliefs were available to him or a claim about how universal the practice was (these two are seperate and I want the tree to make that obvious); is morality itself indexed to a period; does it matter that some people around him were already making the objection; is a statesman judged by the standards you'd apply to a private person; and is the individual even the right target, or is the target the institutions that made him possible.

For probes, use concrete cases and phrase them as questions about the case, not as "was X a bad person." I want Jefferson in there -- he denounced slavery in his own writing while owning people his whole life, so belief-availability cuts against him while practice-pervasiveness doesn't, and I want a question that shows those two defenses coming apart on the same man. Churchill and the Bengal famine, Columbus in the Caribbean, and as a control case where the defense looks strongest, a Roman senator with slaves.

For the leaves, give the position its name where there's a name in circulation and just say "unnamed" where there isn't. I expect the tree to end up with something in the neighborhood of: straight presentism; the strict historicist who thinks blaming anyone for what was done in another moral world is a category mistake; the culpable-ignorance view; the "they had abolitionists, so he could have known" view; the moral-fog line, the one that says everybody was in it and nobody could see out of it; the role-differentiated view about statesmen; the structural view that moves the target off individuals altogether; the relative-progress view that grades a person against the median of his contemporaries rather than against us; plus whatever else falls out of the branches. If two of those can't be separated by any question you can put to the cases, say so rather than inventing a distinction. And for the combinations nobody occupies, tell me which kind of emptiness it is -- incoherent, unattractive, or just undefended.

One more thing I care about, because my students keep doing it: grading the perpetrator by his period and the victims by ours. If the tree can catch that, great; if it can't, say so. Keep the whole thing to about a page, tree first, then the notes on collisions and empty cells.
