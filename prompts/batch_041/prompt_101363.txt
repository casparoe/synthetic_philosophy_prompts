Im a novelist, so I spend my days writing sentences like the man at the end of the bar about people who never existed, and I keep getting the nagging feeling that some philosopher sorted all this out a century ago and nobody bothered to tell the fiction writers. A big popular philosophy book I've been reading does a chapter on Russell's theory of descriptions, and here's the gist of it: Russell wanted to spare us from having to believe in shadowy nonexistent objects, so he showed that when you utter something like the present King of France is bald, you're not gesturing at a phantom king at all — you're really saying three separate things, that a king of France exists, that there's only one of them, and that he's bald. Since the first part is false, the whole sentence is simply false rather than meaningless, and that's how he demolishes Meinong's jungle. The punchline, the book says, is that a definite description turns out to be a kind of disguised name: the author of Waverley is just another way of naming Scott, the same as Scott names him, which is why Scott is the author of Waverley manages to tell us something even though both of those names are landing on the same fellow. He calls descriptions incomplete symbols because on their own they don't name anything, and the book adds that this is pretty much Strawson's point as well, that you shouldn't even ask whether a sentence with a failed description is true or false. I suspect the author is being breezy in places but I cant tell where. So break it down for me: what's solid, what's a convenience-simplification, what would Russell himself or a card-carrying Russellian actually push back on, and does the disguised-name bit survive once you fix it? And since Im the one who actually needs this: what does the theory say about my sentences concerning people who never existed at all?

Give it to me as exactly five numbered points, a sentence or two each, no preamble and no summing up at the end.
