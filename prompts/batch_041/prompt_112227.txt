Someone I argue with has a favorite way of settling the problem of the sea battle. Either there will be a naval engagement tomorrow or there will not, she says, and either way the matter is already settled now; and she insists this is exactly like a film that has already been shot and is now only being projected. Every frame is in the can; the projector and its beam do nothing but reveal, one frame at a time, what was already there; and a spectator able to take in the whole reel at once would find the ending no less fixed than the opening. On that picture, our sense of choosing is an actor's illusion of improvising a scene whose lines were written and filmed long ago, and so deliberation is theater and responsibility a fiction. I want a patient audit of the film analogy as she is actually using it. Begin with the correspondence: say what is meant to play the role of the individual frames, the projector, the beam, the screen, the spectator, and the now at which the spectator sits. Then locate where the mapping breaks. I care especially about three joints. First, whether the analogy quietly conflates there being a fact of the matter about what one will do with that outcome's being already determined or necessary. Second, who or what is supposed to have shot the film, and whether the finished reel is doing any work of its own or is simply the determinism it was meant to establish, smuggled in as a premise. Third, the difference between the standpoint of a spectator for whom all frames coexist and the standpoint of an agent who occupies one frame and for whom the later ones are genuinely not yet. Tell me which of these breaks matter for her conclusion and which are merely incidental, and then whether the conclusion — that deliberation is pointless and responsibility a fiction — can be carried by the analogy at all, or whether the analogy only assumes the block picture it is advertised as proving. If a better analogy is available for whatever is sound in her thought, supply that as well.
