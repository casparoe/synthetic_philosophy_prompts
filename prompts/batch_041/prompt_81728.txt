You are a philosopher whose work centers on collective responsibility, historical injustice, and the ethics of inherited advantage, and you are known for giving practical judgment rather than literature surveys. Please think carefully before you answer, and begin by setting out in your own words the distinct questions my situation actually contains — I suspect I have been running several of them together, and separating them may be most of what I need. I don't need a reading list; I won't be able to chase down sources.

My spouse is one of fourteen shareholders in a family manufacturing firm founded by her great-grandfather in 1902 in a mill town. The firm still carries his name and still pays dividends to all fourteen shareholders, a group that includes two people who married in, one who was adopted into the family as an adult, and several who were born long after the founder died. Last year the family commissioned a historian. The report is bad. For roughly the firm's first twenty years, the founder's mills ran on labor the report calls coercive: men recruited from distant towns under contracts they could not read, paid in scrip redeemable only at the company store, and pursued and brought back when they tried to leave. The practice was legal, common in the industry, and it built the firm's early capital. The workers' descendants today are a scattered and uneven group; some are comfortable, some are not, and nobody can say precisely who belongs on the list.

Four proposals go to a vote next month. First, a trust paying a substantial sum to descendants of those workers. Second, a public apology issued in the company's name. Third, removing the founder's name from the building. Fourth — the uncle's counter-proposal — endowing a needs-based scholarship in the town, open to every applicant regardless of ancestry, on the grounds that this does good without pretending to be guilt.

Here is how the family argues. The uncle, a lawyer, says only debts survive a person, not guilt: debts attach to assets, guilt attaches to wills, and since we hold the assets but not the wills, we owe only what the assets can be traced to and nothing further — pay the minimum, call the account settled, and stop moralizing. A cousin replies that the firm has been one continuous actor since 1902, so this is not inherited guilt at all but the firm's own present conduct, and that keeping the name on the building is a present-tense act of honoring. A third shareholder says the wrong was done to people now dead, so paying their descendants simply moves money between people who had nothing to do with it; on that view the only defensible ground for payment is that some descendants are still poor, which makes it a welfare question rather than a question of justice. My spouse says she personally did nothing and resents being treated as though she had, yet she cannot shake the sense that the dividend checks are not clean. Those dividends currently pay for our son's schooling, and the trust would suspend them for eight years.

I want three things. First, tell me which of these positions tracks something real and which conflate things that should be kept apart — in particular whether the uncle's debt/guilt dichotomy is exhaustive, whether the cousin's claim of one continuous actor can bear the weight she puts on it, and what the fact that no living person was wronged at the time does, if anything, to the obligation. Second, tell me concretely how to vote on each of the four proposals, including whether the scholarship is a genuine discharge of the obligation or an evasion of it, and whether I should arrange for our son to inherit shares at all. Third, be explicit about how confident you are in each claim you make, and single out the one point in your answer you are least sure of, with your reason.

If these positions have names and standard replies, tell me which is which as you go. I would rather have a firm answer that may be wrong than a survey of considerations: if some of these proposals are required and others are mere gestures, say which is which.
