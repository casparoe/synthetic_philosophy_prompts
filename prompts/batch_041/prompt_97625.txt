I host a small podcast on philosophy of biology, and next month's guest is a philosopher of biology who has spent much of her career defending a revised version of the Gould and Lewontin critique of adaptationism — the 1979 spandrels paper, which used the triangular spaces between the arches of St Mark's Basilica, and Voltaire's Dr Pangloss, to argue that biologists too readily assume every trait is an adaptation and too rarely take seriously that it might be a byproduct of something else. Her revision, as I understand it, runs like this. She thinks the paper is usually read as a metaphysical claim — that a great many traits are in fact non-adaptive — and that this reading is exactly what made the critique so easy to wave away. On her view the real target was an inference pattern: reasoning from a trait's current utility to its historical origin. The critique, she argues, survives only if it is recast as a claim about an asymmetry of evidence between the two kinds of hypothesis. An adaptive hypothesis, on her account, can always be defended by redescribing the trait or its environment, whereas a byproduct hypothesis can be stated in a form that is genuinely testable. To make that concrete she proposes a criterion she calls the counterfactual robustness test: a trait counts as a byproduct only if we can show that the same developmental or structural cause would have produced it across a wide range of selection regimes.

I want eight to ten questions that press her on the content of that position rather than inviting a general tour of the debate. Please include at least one question on each of the following, and for each of those a follow-up keyed to the answer she is most likely to give:

- the difference between calling a trait non-adaptive and calling it not-yet-explained, and how her criterion keeps the spandrel label from becoming an all-purpose name for our ignorance;
- whether her asymmetry claim is itself testable, given the standard objection that it is constraint explanations that are unfalsifiable and that the critique amounts to Panglossianism in reverse;
- which of the several readings of the original paper she takes to be the right one — I have seen it read as (i) a call for stricter testing of adaptive hypotheses, (ii) a call for pluralism about explanation, and (iii) a claim that non-adaptive explanations are positively to be preferred — and what each of the other two readings gets wrong.

I would also like one question on the distinction between a trait's current utility and its historical origin, and whether that distinction does any independent work once her test is on the table; and one on the charge that the San Marco analogy is factually shaky, since some critics say the shapes in question are pendentives rather than spandrels at all. Please end with a question about what evidence would lead her to abandon the constraint-first reading in favour of a straightforwardly adaptationist one.
