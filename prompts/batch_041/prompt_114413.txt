I run a 90-minute session in a graduate seminar on the ethics of emerging technology (roughly 18 students, a mix of philosophers and engineers, no background in decision theory beyond the basics). I want an in-class game that makes them feel a structure rather than hear it described: several agents can each act unilaterally and independently; each agent's own evidence gives a decent but imperfect estimate of whether the action is safe; each agent's expected-value reasoning is sound; but because any single mistaken action is enough to bring about the disaster, the chance that someone acts on a mistake climbs toward certainty as the number of agents grows. The sting should be that nobody in the game has made an error of reasoning — the failure is a property of the aggregation, not of any player — and they should also feel the mirror-image case, in which the same multiplicity works in the group's favour, so that they leave with a sense of which feature of the situation decides between the two. I would like it played twice with the payoff structure as the only thing that changes, with private simultaneous decisions and no communication between players in either round.

Give me the rules, the materials (I can photocopy or hand out index cards), the payoff scheme in tokens rather than money, and a timeline that fits the 90 minutes. Include a debrief that maps specific moments in the play onto parts of the structure and that names the phenomenon only at the end — the structure is the one usually called the unilateralist's curse, but the rules should not use that name, and I should not have to lecture before the game starts. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle each failure, and be frank about what the game cannot reproduce and that I will have to supply in the debrief myself. Finally, say which element of the design is load-bearing: what change to the rules would make it stop illustrating the point, or illustrate the opposite one, and what you would expect to observe in a run that showed the design had failed.
