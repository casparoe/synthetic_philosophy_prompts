I am teaching a ninety-minute session in a graduate Science and Society seminar, and what I need from you is a plan for the teaching rather than the exposition itself. The room holds ten humanities doctoral students and two biologists, a whiteboard, and no laboratory. The humanists arrive already convinced that talk of genetic information is a metaphor doing ideological work and that any sentence of the form DNA contains the instructions for X is determinism in disguise; the biologists arrive expecting a defence of the textbook picture. Both groups know the central dogma as it is taught in school, that there is a code and that codons are triplets, and very little about what the code does and does not specify.

The session has to leave them able to say, in their own words, in what sense DNA is a code and in what sense it carries information, and what does and does not follow from that. Give me the order in which the components must be introduced, with the dependency made explicit at each step: what has to be in place before the next thing can even be stated, and which single component the rest hangs from. Give me the one example or case that should carry the whole session, and say what it must have and what it must lack in order to carry it. I have been assuming it should be the blueprint-versus-recipe contrast, since it is the one the students will already have met; tell me whether that is right, and if it is, what the example has to be stripped of so that it does not do the wrong work. Tell me, too, where in the sequence each predictable misconception should be headed off, and where I should let the room's objections run rather than pre-empt them. My guess is that the two that matter are that the code is only a metaphor, and that once they see the mapping is arbitrary they will conclude the content is arbitrary as well; say whether that guess is wrong and what the real one is.

Three pieces of material have to appear somewhere, and I want you to place each and say what work it is doing. The first is that the standard codon table is not a law of nature: in vertebrate mitochondria the codon UGA, which terminates translation in the standard table, is instead read as tryptophan, and other variant codes differ elsewhere. I want this to be the case that fixes whatever the correct notion of arbitrariness is, not a curiosity. The second is that some traits are inherited with no change in nucleic acid sequence at all, the self-propagating protein conformations described in yeast being the standard instance; I want it used to make them locate the informational claim precisely rather than abandon it. The third is the students' own suspicion, which I would rather treat as a position with a price attached than as a confusion to be corrected.

Finish with the check for understanding: one question, answerable in the room without notes, whose answer would distinguish a student who has grasped the structure of the distinction from one who has merely become suspicious in the right direction. Then give me the things I should openly refuse to settle at this level, and say so in the session. At the very end, list which sentences of your plan are metaphors that I should flag as metaphors when I use them, and which are technical claims that would survive the metaphor being dropped, since that is the distinction I most often get wrong in front of a room.
