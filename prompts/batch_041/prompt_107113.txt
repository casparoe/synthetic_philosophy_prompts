I'm the tech lead on a small team (eight of us) that builds a formal verifier for a safety-critical controller — the verifier does static analysis plus a proof check against a spec, and it's what stands between our code and the certification lab. Right now every release of the verifier itself has to be re-certified by an outside lab, which takes weeks and costs a fortune. What I want is to close the loop: version N of the verifier checks version N+1 and its outputs, and if that check passes we ship without the lab. Then N+1 checks N+2, and so on. One of my engineers, who reads a lot more logic than I do, says this runs straight into something called the Löbian obstacle and that I should stop trying. I half believe her and half think it's a philosopher's problem that won't bite real engineering. So what I want is a procedure, not a lecture. This comes up every six weeks or so when we cut a release; getting it wrong either ships a controller nobody really verified or stalls us for a month; the safety lead signs off, and the outside lab is on retainer for exceptions only. Assume my engineers know the code but no proof theory, and assume none of us can look anything up.

Here's my understanding, tell me if it's wrong. In any system strong enough for arithmetic, if the system is consistent then (a) it can't prove its own consistency, and (b) for any statement A, if the system proves "if A is provable in me, then A", then the system already proves A — so the trust move, "I have a proof of A, therefore A", only ever gets you things you could already get, and a system that could prove that schema for every A would prove its own consistency and therefore be inconsistent. What I think that means for us: v_N proving "whatever v_N+1 certifies is correct" seems to require v_N to already be able to prove everything v_N+1 will ever certify, which is exactly what I was trying to buy. But I know there are supposed escape routes, and I don't know which are real: a stronger external system can prove the consistency of a weaker one, so maybe the chain just has to start higher up; maybe v_N can only certify a v_N+1 that's strictly weaker in some specific way; somebody mentioned there are odd weak systems that can prove their own consistency because they've been built so they can't do the diagonalization trick, at the cost of losing something we'd probably want; maybe there's a restricted or bounded reflection principle that's sound and enough for a chain; or maybe I should stop treating this as a proof problem and treat the verifier as a fallible physical process with a track record and monitoring, and call the result a risk argument rather than a proof of safety. Which of those actually work, and which are hand-waving?

What I want from you: a decision procedure my team can run on a proposal of the form "let v_N certify v_N+1 for release." One page, about an hour, no proof theory required. Ordered questions, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops with a hard no, and where it hands the decision to a human because what's at stake is an assumption we're choosing to make rather than a fact we can check. Mark clearly which steps are real constraints and which are judgment calls dressed up as procedure.

Then run it on three proposals. (1) v_N+1 adds a faster solver but keeps the same trusted core — same kernel, same axioms, same proof format. (2) v_N+1 replaces the kernel, the small trusted checker, with a rewritten one, and the argument for the new kernel is that the old kernel verified it. (3) My favorite plan: v_N+1 carries an embedded proof of its own soundness, generated by v_N, so that any later version can just check that proof and be done forever. For each, say what the procedure decides and what we'd actually have to do next. I suspect you'll shoot down (3) and I'd rather hear it plainly than have it softened.

Also tell me what the procedure can't do — the cases it will misfire on, and the calls it simply can't make. And be explicit about where your reasoning is weakest and what would change your mind: if some restricted reflection principle turns out to be sound for a system like ours, or if our verifier is weaker than I think, say which of your conclusions flips and why. One last thing: everything has to be usable by someone who's never heard of Gödel, so no "hence by the second incompleteness theorem" without saying what that buys us here, and don't send me to a reading list.
