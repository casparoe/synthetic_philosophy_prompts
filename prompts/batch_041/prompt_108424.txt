Next month I am giving a paper to our department's logic and philosophy of mathematics reading group, and what I want from you is not a defence of the paper but a Q&A preparation sheet for the question period. The paper argues that the axiom of choice is not an extra assumption we lay on top of the iterative conception of set, but a consequence of reading that conception as a maximality or plenitude principle: the hierarchy contains every set there is, so a candidate universe that omits a choice function for a family of nonempty sets is by that very fact not maximal. I concede in the paper that the usual indispensability argument for choice proves too little, since much of the mathematics I actually care about goes through on principles strictly weaker than full choice, and I use that concession to motivate the maximality route instead. I will have about thirty minutes of questions.

The audience is small but not friendly, and none of them is a general philosopher. There are two constructive mathematicians who work in Bishop-style analysis and in type theory and who accept countable choice at most; a set theorist who works in descriptive set theory and is happy to assume determinacy hypotheses when they earn their keep; a colleague who writes on conceptions of set and who, I expect, has no patience at all for maximality talk; a platonist about sets who agrees with my conclusion and will think my concessions are a sellout; and a computability-minded logician who will want to know what my choice function computes and why I take a non-constructive existence claim to be contentful. Give me the eight to ten objections I will actually get from these people, ordered by the product of how likely each is and how much damage it does — a probable objection that costs me nothing should sit below an improbable one that costs me the paper — each phrased the way the person who raises it would phrase it, with a reply I can deliver in two minutes, a candid note on where the reply is soft, and the follow-up they will fire if I answer that way. Tell me what to concede before anyone asks, identify the single reply that has to land for the paper to survive the hour, and give me the question I am hoping nobody asks — the one aimed at the weakest joint in the maximality argument — together with the best holding answer I have.

Do not confine yourself to conceptual objections; this room will also press the mathematical ones. I expect Banach–Tarski and the non-measurable sets, the undefinability of any well-ordering of the reals, the pull of determinacy in descriptive set theory, the fact that in intuitionistic set theory choice yields excluded middle (so my constructivist colleagues cannot adopt my line and keep their logic), the two-dart symmetry argument that is normally aimed at the continuum hypothesis and that I am never sure how far to push against well-ordering itself, and the suspicion that Fraenkel–Mostowski style permutation models in which choice fails are counterexamples to any plenitude principle. I also need the weak-choice bookkeeping sorted out, because I have been sloppy about it in the paper: for each example I lean on — a basis for every vector space, a maximal ideal in every nontrivial ring, Hahn–Banach, Tychonoff for compact Hausdorff spaces — say plainly whether it is equivalent to full choice or follows from something weaker, and what the audience member most likely to know the answer will say if I get it wrong.

Return the sheet as a single JSON object, with these keys and nothing else. thesis_in_one_line: a string. concede_up_front: a list of strings, each a concession together with a sentence on why conceding it costs me less than being caught not conceding it. objections: a list of objects, each containing rank (an integer, 1 for the most likely-and-damaging), from (a string naming which audience member raises it and in what vocabulary), objection_in_their_voice (the objection as that person would actually put it, framed as a question), severity (one of fatal, serious, cosmetic), reply_2min (the reply, written to be spoken aloud), soft_spot (where the reply is weakest), and their_follow_up (what they say next if I answer that way). reply_that_must_land: a string, with one sentence on why everything else depends on it. question_i_hope_nobody_asks: an object with objection, why_it_hurts, and holding_answer. traps_to_avoid: a list of formulations I should not use in this room, because they will cost me the audience.
