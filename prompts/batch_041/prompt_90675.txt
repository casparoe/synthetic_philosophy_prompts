I've been going back and forth on the whole fight over what natural selection actually acts on -- selfish genes, whole organisms, groups, species -- and it strikes me that everyone's arguing about the answer before anyone says what the answer even has to do. So before I read anything else, I want the spec first: what does any theory of the "unit of selection" have to get right to count as any good? Give me the list of requirements, each with the case that motivates it. Stuff like: it has to make sense of meiotic drive, where a gene spreads even though it's bad for the body carrying it; it has to handle eusocial insects where individuals seem to throw themselves away; it has to say what to do with selection happening inside a single body, like a tumor lineage evolving against the rest of you; and it has to keep straight the difference between a unit selection acts on and a unit that just gets dragged along. Now here's the case I care most about, which I thought up myself and can't find anyone discussing: suppose a bird picks up a gut worm, and the chicks reliably inherit a fear or a call from the worm rather than from the birds' own genes -- so the trait tracks the worm's lineage, not the bird's. Does your spec force the worm to be a unit of selection, or does something in the list rule it out? I want to know whether my requirements are even consistent with each other, which ones are load-bearing and which are just taste, and which single requirement does the most work in ruling out candidates. If two of them can't both be satisfied, say so, because that's the part I actually want. Take a definite position here -- don't just lay out the options and let me pick.
