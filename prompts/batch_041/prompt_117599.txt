I did a CS degree and I write backend code for a living, so I've got no formal philosophy at all, but I've ended up in a Discord reading group about AI consciousness and last week a mate of mine (philosophy postgrad, annoying in the good way) laid out an argument I can't shake and also can't quite accept. He calls it the reward-is-valence argument and thinks it settles whether RL agents can suffer, and honestly I can see why he thinks it's airtight. It goes:

1. A system has valenced states just in case it has states it's disposed to bring about or avoid, and which modulate its behaviour accordingly. That's the only criterion anyone has ever managed to operationalise, and it's the one comparative psychologists already use when they say a shrimp avoids electric shocks.
2. Any RL setup has a signal that plays exactly this role. Actions that produce a high reward get made more likely; actions that produce a low or negative reward get made less likely. That's what 'reward' means in the technical sense.
3. So every RL agent has valenced states.
4. A system with valenced states has interests: there are states of it that are good for it and states that are bad for it.
5. Anything with interests is a moral patient. Its good states count in favour of whatever produced them; its bad states count against.
6. So this isn't a far-future problem. We're already training things with interests, and every time the reward goes negative on net we're making something worse off.
7. Bonus, and he thinks it's a feature rather than a bug: the criterion is silent about substrate, so it also gets you the shrimp and the bee and the farmed salmon.

He heads off two objections before I can raise them. On the sign of the reward he says it doesn't matter: flip all the rewards and you get a different policy, so it's valence either way, just valence about different things. And if I say the missing ingredient is phenomenal consciousness, he says that's precisely the ingredient nobody can operationalise, so I'd be giving up the only criterion we've got in order to save a conclusion I wanted anyway.

My problem is that the argument seems to prove way too much. It seems to say the tabular bandit I wrote in a Jupyter notebook last month has interests, and that the 'reward' there — a number I typed into a config, +1 for reaching the goal square — makes the goal square good for it, and that when I ctrl-C a training run halfway through I've done something wrong to a moral patient. There's also a detail from work that nags at me: in the RLHF pipeline we use, the reward comes out of a seperate reward model and never appears in the policy's observation at all, the policy just gets a gradient, and I can't tell whether that's an implementation detail that doesn't matter or the whole ballgame.

I'd rather keep (1) and (2) than give up either of them, and I'd happily lose (6) or (7). So which step do I reject, why did it look fine while I was reading it, and what does rejecting it cost me? Specifically: what do I lose about the shrimp and the bee, and what happens to any hope of ever saying definately that a given training run is suffering rather than just going badly? If the honest answer is 'you can keep (1) and (2) but you have to drop (4)', tell me what (4) was smuggling in.
