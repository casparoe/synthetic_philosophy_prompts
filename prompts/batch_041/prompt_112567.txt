I work as a loss adjuster for an insurer, so most of my week is spent reading incident reports and deciding who, if anyone, caused what. A pattern keeps nagging at me. Two reports can describe the same physical sequence — a machine does what it was always going to do, one person stands still, someone is killed — and yet everyone involved, myself included, will say that in one report the person who stood still caused the death and in the other they did not. The difference is never in what the person did with their body, because in both cases they did nothing at all. The difference is in whether standing still was a departure from what that person was supposed to be doing: the operator who should have hit the emergency stop, versus the visitor who was never going to. But it is not only duties. The same thing happens when the omission is merely unusual for the individual (a technician who always checks the valve and this time didn't), and, so far as I can tell, when it is unusual for anyone on site. It runs the other way for actions too: something a person actively did gets singled out as the cause only because it was the one step in the sequence that wasn't routine.

What I want to know is whether there is an established name for this — for the phenomenon that our causal judgements seem to track what is normal or expected rather than what happened mechanically — and whether anyone has defended the strong version of the claim, that the norm is doing work in causation itself and not merely in our readiness to blame. I already know this is not just the distinction between doing and allowing: both of my people did nothing and merely let the death happen, so that distinction puts them on the same side of the line and cannot be what splits my cases. Nor can it be counterfactual dependence, since the counterfactuals are identical in the two reports. So: does the thing have a name, what are its nearest named relatives, and how precisely does it differ from each of them? If no single name covers the duty case, the statistical case and the habitual case together, say so, tell me which of them the existing vocabulary does cover, and give me a term and definition for what is left over. Please answer in British English.
