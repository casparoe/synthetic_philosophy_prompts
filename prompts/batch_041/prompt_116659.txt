I have been arguing with a friend about when a story stops being a legend and starts being history, and I want the principle that actually governs my judgments, not a survey of what historians happen to say. Here are my verdicts; find the rule behind them, or tell me there isn't one.

- The Trojan War as the Iliad tells it: legend. That a settlement at Hisarlik was destroyed around the twelfth century BCE I would call history; that Greeks besieged it for ten years over a kidnapped queen I would not.
- King Arthur: legend. The earliest surviving mentions of him come centuries after the period he is supposed to have lived, and I would not count even a hypothetical sixth-century war leader as history.
- The Exodus: legend. No Egyptian source records it, and the narrative is shaped at every point by the purposes of the people who told it.
- Caesar's crossing of the Rubicon: history. I have no doubt he did it, though every source for it is later, Roman, and partisan.
- Socrates' trial and execution: history, though our sources are a few contemporaries with axes to grind and there is no document from the court.
- A migration that a people's oral tradition places six hundred years in the past, and that matches a pattern in their language and their genes: history.
- A twelfth-century chronicle that invents a seventh-century battle to burnish its monastery's prestige, when a hoard of coins from the battle's date is later dug up in the right place: history.
- A shipwreck recorded only in one survivor's memoir, published forty years afterward, with no wreck found and no second witness: not history.

Here is what bothers me. The Rubicon and the Exodus have the same evidential shape — one community's later literature, and nothing that survives for reasons indifferent to the story — yet I put them on opposite sides, and I am unwilling to give up either verdict.

My own attempt at a principle: a claim is history when the evidence for it would survive the disappearance of the community that tells it, that is, when it rests on traces that do not care whether the story is true. I have been calling such traces sediment, and a community's own records keepsakes. By that test the Rubicon is not history, which I reject; and every loosening that lets the Rubicon back in seems to let the Exodus in with it.

What I want:

1. The principle, or two or three competing principles, stated precisely enough to apply, with the verdict above that forces each clause. If you think I am asking the question at the wrong level — about sources when I should be asking about claims, or about claims when I should be asking about the community's reasons — say so and relocate the question.
2. Your verdicts on cases I have not listed: a battle known only from the victory monument the winners put up; a martyrdom whose miracles are plainly legend but whose execution is mentioned in passing in an unrelated official's correspondence; and a story that an entire community believes and that archaeology neither confirms nor contradicts.
3. Which of my eight verdicts your principle cannot save, and whether I should abandon the verdict or add a clause. If two clauses you need cannot be jointly satisfied, say so and tell me which to drop.
4. What would change your mind: which counterexample or consideration would move you off the principle you recommend, and where you think your reasoning is likeliest to fail.

No reading list, please — I want the argument, not the literature.
