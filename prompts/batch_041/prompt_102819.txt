I grew up in a small river town where most people worked with their hands or in small businesses, and I was the first in my family to go to college; after fifteen years as a professor in a large city, I have moved back for a year to help care for my father, and my old high school has asked me to give this spring's commencement address. The invitation has become personally difficult in a way I did not expect. The audience will be split: half are people I grew up with—farmers, nurses, machinists, contractors, people who stayed—and half are colleagues, graduate students, and friends from the university who have driven out for the day. The graduates themselves are going in every direction: some to universities, some to trade programs, some into the military, some into jobs in town. I want to give a speech that is honest about a question I have been unable to stop chewing on since I came back: whether the rural and working-class outlook I was raised in, or the urban and professional-class outlook I now live in, is more defensible—or whether that question is somehow rigged.

I have been trying to state the contrast in a way that does not turn either side into a caricature. The outlook I was raised in treats a good life as largely a matter of doing what needs doing, well and without fuss: keeping your word, not putting on airs, staying close to the people who need you, and not asking why the whole arrangement is worthwhile, because asking can feel like a kind of betrayal. The outlook I now inhabit treats a good life as a matter of choosing well: the best school, the best city, the most fulfilling work, the partner who helps you become more yourself, and being able to give reasons for every major commitment, because a life you cannot justify looks unexamined. I have been calling these, in my notes, the ethic of the done thing and the ethic of the justified thing. Each has a real claim on us, and each has a characteristic blindness: one can mistake inherited obligation for wisdom and call any questioning a betrayal; the other can mistake mobility for freedom and call every rooted attachment a failure of ambition. The speech should make an argument, not just survey the two. It should say what each gets right, what each cannot see, and whether one has a stronger claim to being defensible—perhaps by distinguishing defensible to whom from defensible for what. It should also take seriously the thought that the very demand for a defense may already be a professional-class demand, and that asking whether either outlook is more defensible may be like asking whether a hammer is more defensible than a song. But it should not use that thought as an excuse to say nothing; it should decide how much of the question can be answered and how much should be refused.

The occasion is a commencement address of about six minutes. Please write the speech itself, in a voice that can be spoken aloud by someone who is neither a comedian nor a preacher, and that will not lose the room when it becomes abstract. It should use one concrete example from the town and one from the professional world to carry the argument, and it should address the graduates directly without telling them whether to stay or leave. There should be no citations, no reading list, and no quotations from other authors; the philosophical work should be done in plain language. It should not flatter the rural and working-class audience by treating their outlook as noble simplicity, nor flatter the professional audience by treating theirs as enlightenment, and it should not simply declare the two incommensurable. End in a way that both halves of the room can applaud without feeling that the other half has been sold out. Please state the speech's thesis early and return to it at the end.
