I'm in a seminar on aesthetics and we've just started Hume's Of the Standard of Taste, which is eighteenth-century and much harder going than I expected. I went in assuming it would be about whether beauty is in the eye of the beholder, and instead the first several pages are about moral vocabulary and about Mahomet, and I genuinely can't tell what any of that has to do with beauty. The passage I keep rereading is this one:

The admirers and followers of the Alcoran insist very much on the excellent moral precepts, which are interspersed throughout that wild performance. But it is to be supposed, that the Arabic words, which correspond to the English, equity, justice, temperance, meekness, charity, were such as, from the constant use of that tongue, must always be taken in a good sense; and it would have argued the greatest ignorance, not of morals, but of language, to have mentioned them with any epithets, besides those of applause and approbation. But would we know, whether the pretended prophet had really attained a just sentiment of morals? Let us attend to his narration; and we shall soon find, that he bestows praise on such instances of treachery, inhumanity, cruelty, revenge, bigotry, as are utterly incompatible with civilized society.

Just before this he claims that in matters of opinion and science men differ more often in their general terms than in particulars, and that this disagreement is less real than it looks, whereas in matters of taste the reverse holds. But then the Mahomet case seems to show that in morals the agreement on general precepts is cheap and the real disagreements only surface in particulars, which sounds to me like the taste side of his contrast rather than the morals side. My friend thinks the opening is just Hume clearing his throat and that the real action is later, with the true judges and their joint verdict; I think it has to be doing work for the argument about whether there's a standard at all, otherwise why spend so much of the essay on it.

So can you walk me through this passage closely: what the Mahomet example is supposed to establish, how the point about words like virtue already implying praise is meant to operate, and whether the same trick carries over to aesthetic words like elegant or beautiful. And what does this stretch of the essay commit him to about whether beauty is objective — does it push toward a standard or away from one? I don't need secondary sources or citations, just a careful reading of the passage itself, and I'd like to know whether the claim that disagreements about opinion are mostly verbal is even plausible, because it feels like a big empirical assumption he just asserts and moves on from.
