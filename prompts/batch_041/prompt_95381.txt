Our school debating society has tabled a motion on whether the United Kingdom should abandon the criminal prohibition of cannabis in favour of a regulated legal market, and the briefing note circulated by the proposing side leans almost entirely on one historical parallel. The argument runs like this: the United States prohibited alcohol for a period in the early twentieth century and then repealed the ban, because prohibition had produced organised crime, illicit stills, adulterated liquor and no great reduction in drinking; alcohol is now sold openly, heavily taxed and reasonably well managed. Since the prohibition of other drugs has produced the same familiar symptoms — criminal supply chains, unpredictable potency, violence over territory, and a state spending more on enforcement than on treatment — prohibition must be the wrong instrument altogether, and the sensible course is to legalise and regulate, treating drugs roughly as we treat drink. The note adds a subsidiary claim I found striking: that prohibition does not merely fail to suppress a drug but actively strengthens it, since anyone who accepts a fixed risk in smuggling a given volume has every reason to smuggle the most concentrated form they can, so a ban predictably yields stronger drugs rather than weaker ones. This is offered as confirmation that the parallel is exact.

I should like to know how far the analogy really holds. I suspect it is carrying more weight than it can bear, but I cannot articulate why. Could you begin with a table of correspondences — what in the alcohol case is meant to map onto what in the drug case: the prohibited substance, the consumers, the suppliers, the enforcement apparatus, the surrounding culture, and the political coalitions that secured the ban and later ended it? Then set out where the mapping breaks down, separating the disanalogies that matter for the conclusion being drawn from those that are merely colourful. I am particularly interested in whether the alcohol case was the clean natural experiment the note presents it as — for instance, whether it began from a legal, industrialised and culturally entrenched drinking culture rather than from a small and marginal base, and whether the verdict that it simply failed is as settled as the note implies — and whether the potency claim is a genuine consequence of prohibition or a separate empirical matter on which the analogy can tell us nothing.

Third, I should like to know what the analogy quietly imports that the drug case may lack. What does it assume about how a legal market would be built, and does "regulate it like alcohol" actually specify a policy or merely gesture at one? It would help to test this against something concrete, so please consider the package our side has drafted: possession for personal use handled administratively rather than criminally, on the Portuguese model, with supply left a criminal offence; but for cannabis specifically, licensed and taxed retail sale to adults, with a cap on potency, a ban on advertising, plain packaging, and a minimum price set high enough to discourage heavy use. Is the alcohol parallel an argument for that package, against it, or indifferent to it? Portugal is often invoked in the same breath as the alcohol case even though it decriminalised use while leaving supply illegal, and tobacco is a lawful product whose use has fallen under precisely the sort of restrictions I have just listed. I should be grateful if you could say which of the three — alcohol, Portuguese-style decriminalisation, or tobacco-style restriction — is the better analogy for what we are actually proposing, and why.

Finally, and most importantly for the debate, I want to know whether the conclusion survives the audit. If the disanalogies are real, is the case for legalisation and regulation weakened by that, or does it rest on arguments that never needed the alcohol parallel at all? Please be explicit about which parts of the case are carried by the analogy and which would stand without it.
