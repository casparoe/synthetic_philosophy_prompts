I want an essay on a question in the foundations of computability that I have never managed to settle to my own satisfaction: what is the Church–Turing thesis actually a claim about, and what is its epistemic status? Argue rather than survey, and end with a verdict rather than a both-sides summary. I know what a Turing machine is; I have no library at hand, so do not cite works or supply a reading list, and do not lean on the history of Hilbert's programme except where it does real work in the argument.

To keep the discussion honest, I am going to fix a distinction that I want you to maintain throughout. The single phrase "the Church–Turing thesis" is used for at least three different claims, and they do not stand or fall together:

(i) the mathematical thesis: that the informal notion of computability by an effective procedure has the same extension as computability by a Turing machine;
(ii) the physical thesis: that no physical system, actual or merely possible, computes a function that no Turing machine computes;
(iii) the cognitive thesis: that no procedure a human being can execute by rote, without insight and without auxiliary devices, exceeds the Turing limit.

For each, address the question of status: definition, empirical hypothesis, theorem in disguise, or something else again. I am most interested in three pressure points.

First, the definitional reading. If an effective procedure just is whatever a Turing machine does, the thesis becomes true by stipulation and therefore, it seems, empty — and yet it does not behave like an empty claim: it forbids things, it predicts the failure of every proposed machine, and it was used to identify the recursive functions as the correct extension of a pre-theoretic notion that already had a life of its own before anyone formalised it. Is there a reading on which the thesis is both true by stipulation and substantive, or must one choose?

Second, the empirical reading. State as precisely as you can what observation would refute the physical thesis, and then say whether the apparent impossibility of such an observation is a defect in the thesis, a feature of any very general claim, or evidence that it was never an empirical claim to begin with.

Third, the strategy of proving as much as can be proved: give axioms for what counts as a mechanical step or procedure, then prove that anything meeting those axioms is Turing-computable. Say exactly what such a result establishes, what it leaves untouched, and whether the residual gap is one that further argument could ever close or one that has to be closed by decision.

Then bring a case to bear. Imagine a machine with an ordinary finite instruction set but a clock that doubles its rate after every step: the first step takes one second, the second half a second, the third a quarter, and so on, so that infinitely many steps are completed within two seconds. Suppose it is given a program that never halts in the ordinary sense. By the two-second mark it has executed every step of a non-terminating computation. A colleague concludes that the machine decides the halting problem, and that the physical thesis is therefore false. Assume the physics is unimpeachable — I am not interested in whether the thing can be built. I want to know what, if anything, has gone wrong in the inference, and, if nothing has, precisely what the defender of the physical thesis must give up. Say what this case reveals about the content of the thesis, and whether the three theses above come apart here.

Finally, the verdict I want: which of the three theses is the substantive one, and what is its status? If your answer is that the thesis is a definition after all, say what work the definition does that a mere abbreviation would not, and what you are giving up by treating it that way.

Give the essay under the following headings, in this order, at roughly these lengths:

- The three theses (about 300 words): the distinctions, and why the three do not collapse into one.
- The definitional reading (about 450 words): the case for it, the case against, and your interim assessment.
- The empirical reading (about 450 words): what would refute it, and what the failure of refutation shows.
- What can be proved and what can only be stipulated (about 450 words).
- The accelerating machine (about 400 words): the diagnosis, and what it costs the defender of the physical thesis.
- Verdict (about 300 words): which thesis is substantive, what its status is, and the price of your position.
