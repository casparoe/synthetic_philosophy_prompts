I've spent fifteen years in financial reporting. I know the standards cold, I think in terms of recognition, measurement, materiality, and judgment, and I've spent most of my career on the rules-versus-principles boundary — whether a principles-based standard leaves too much to the preparer or a rules-based one invites structuring around the letter. My legal theory is at the level of someone who has read the standard arguments and stopped there. I want the debate between textualism and purposivism restated in my vocabulary as faithfully as it can be, and then I want the residue report.

Take each side as its ablest defenders put it. The textualist: the meaning of a provision is fixed by what its words conventionally mean in context and by the structure of the instrument, not by what the enacting body hoped to achieve; a legislature is not a single mind, so its collective intention is either a fiction or beside the point; and an appeal to purpose is an invitation to substitute the interpreter's policy for the enacted one. The purposivist: a provision is to be read so as to advance the object it was enacted to serve; literalism produces results no one contemplated and no one would have accepted; and when a rule meets a case the drafters never imagined, the reason for the rule governs, not its perimeter.

Render both in terms of what I actually work with — the stated objective of a standard, its requirements and their basis, materiality, the difference between complying with a standard and reporting faithfully, and the way a principle is meant to be applied by judgment rather than by enumeration. Do it honestly: where you find yourself importing accounting's own commitments to make the translation go through — that there is a fact of the matter about the transaction, that the standard-setter's rationale explains rather than binds — flag the import rather than letting it pass as neutral.

Then the residue. Which features of the legal debate translate cleanly, which survive only as distortions, and which cannot be stated in my vocabulary at all? I have two hunches I'd like either confirmed, refined, or dissolved. First, that in accounting the words of a standard are instrumental — they exist to yield a faithful representation of something that is there anyway, a transaction with an economic substance — whereas a statute does not represent an obligation but creates one, so that the textualist's question and mine may not be the same question wearing different clothes. Second, that in accounting there is a right answer to be found, and a regulator and an underlying transaction to check it against, whereas a statute's final reading is whatever the authoritative interpreter says it is, so that the legal debate is partly about who decides in a way mine is not.

Close with one or two concrete stress cases — a term of art whose technical sense diverges from its ordinary one, or a provision applied to a case no one anticipated — and show what my framework would do with each and where it runs out. Then tell me which framework comes out looking worse on the residue, and whether what is left over is a gap in my vocabulary or a confusion in theirs.
