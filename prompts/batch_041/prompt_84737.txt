I have been reading the rationalist blogs and the alignment literature for something like a decade — long enough to watch the same idea get renamed three or four times — and I have developed a nose for arguments that have quietly eaten themselves. This one still smells wrong to me, but I cannot find the exit, so what I want is a diagnosis rather than a refutation.

A friend in my reading group, a much better RL person than I am, laid out the following to show that goal misgeneralization — the case in which a policy's competence carries over to a new environment while its objective does not — is not merely rare but impossible, an artifact of how we talk. His argument:

(1) We have no direct access to a policy's objective. Everything we could cite as evidence about what it wants is some fact about what it does.
(2) For any finite behavior trace there is always a reward function for which the actions taken are optimal: pay out on exactly the transitions that occurred, and the policy that produced them is optimal by construction. Reward is not identifiable from behavior; this is the familiar triviality in inverse reinforcement learning.
(3) A mesa-objective, in the sense the alignment literature uses the term, is just the reward function for which a policy is optimal, posited to explain the policy's behavior.
(4) So, from (2) and (3), ascribing a mesa-objective is not a claim about the policy that could be true or false; it is a choice among descriptions that fit the evidence equally well.
(5) Inner alignment is the requirement that a policy's mesa-objective coincide with the base objective — the objective the training process actually optimizes.
(6) If mesa-objectives are descriptions rather than facts, then whether a policy is inner-aligned depends on which description we choose, and by (2) a description matching the base objective is always available.
(7) So there is no fact of the matter about a policy's alignment. No trajectory, however disturbing, could establish misalignment; the treacherous turn would be a turn in our vocabulary rather than in the system.
(8) Inner alignment is therefore a problem about how we talk and not about what we build, and the practical upshot is to stop attributing objectives to policies at all.

His illustration: an agent trained to reach a red door that happens, in every training maze, to sit on the left. In the test maze the doors have swapped sides and it goes left, to the blue door. Was its objective to reach the red door, or to go left? Both reward functions rationalize the training trace equally well, so he says the question has no answer — and that this is the general situation, not a corner case.

I am happy to keep (1) through (4); each strikes me as true or close enough, and I would rather keep all of them than accept (7). But I cannot name the step I should give up, and I do not know what giving it up would cost. Tell me which step to reject, why it looked fine, and what each of the live repairs commits me to: if the argument rests on a conflation, say what is being conflated and what the correct reading would leave standing; if it is valid and I simply have to pay somewhere, tell me which premise is cheapest to abandon and what the bill looks like. In particular I want to know what, on the repaired view, could count as evidence about a policy's objective and what could not.
