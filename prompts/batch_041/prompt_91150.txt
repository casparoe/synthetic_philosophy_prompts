'Epiphenomenalism is the view that the brain makes the mind but the mind can't do anything back. Pains, afterimages, the taste of coffee — all of it is caused by events in the brain, and none of it causes anything in turn. Consciousness is like the hum a fridge makes: it's genuinely produced by the machinery, and it even tells you the machinery is running, but if you could switch the hum off the fridge would go on cooling exactly as before. It follows that no mental state does any work. Your beliefs, your decisions, your weighing up of reasons are all just along for the ride, and if you want to know why someone did something you look at their brain and nowhere else.

The obvious objection is that the view is self-contradictory. If my pain doesn't cause my belief that I'm in pain, then that belief is a lucky guess rather than something I know, and the same goes for anything else I might think about my own experience. But the theory itself is supposed to be something we know about our experience. So epiphenomenalism saws off the branch it's sitting on, which is why nobody defends it seriously any more.

What's interesting is that the same objection can't be turned on illusionism — the view that there are no phenomenal properties at all and introspection simply misrepresents things. An illusionist never has to say that experiences cause anything, because on that view there's nothing there to be caused. So the self-refutation charge that kills epiphenomenalism leaves illusionism completely untouched, and I think that's one of the strongest arguments for illusionism.'
