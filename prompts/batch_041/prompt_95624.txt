For two years a colleague and I have been arguing about slurs, and we have stopped moving. She holds that a slur's derogatory component is carried by the act of uttering it — something you do in saying the word, not something the sentence says — while I hold that it belongs to the word's contribution to truth conditions; each of us has a stock of cases the other's account handles badly, drawn from the same small set: the reclaimed in-group use, the slur under negation, inside a conditional, quoted in reported speech, and the speaker who uses one while disavowing its force. We also disagree about whether the derogation is entailed, presupposed, or neither. I suspect we are not disagreeing about the data, and possibly not even about the same question. Diagnose the impasse: is it a verbal dispute about how broadly to use the word meaning, a substantive disagreement about what mechanism carries the derogation, two camps answering different questions under one heading — one about lexical content, one about what utterances do — or a disagreement about which intuitions count as data at all? Ground the diagnosis in what each position actually commits its holder to, rather than surveying the literature, and consider whether the dispute would look different if the unit of analysis were the practice of slurring rather than the word. Then say what would count as progress — a distinction, a decisive case, or an argument that no single account can do both jobs — or explain why none should be expected. If a third vocabulary (taboo, presupposition, speech acts, social practice) redraws the disagreement more honestly, say which and why. Assume I know the standard positions and need no introduction to them. End with a final line beginning "Verdict:" that states your diagnosis in one sentence.
