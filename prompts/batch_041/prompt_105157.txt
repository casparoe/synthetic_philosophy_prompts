I retired from the geology department about three years ago and, having nothing better to do with my mornings, I've been sitting in on philosophy seminars at the local university — my wife says I've become insufferable, and she is not wrong. Anyway, my old field partner and closest friend Marit is retiring after forty-one seasons of mapping the same glacial valley, and I've been asked to give the toast at her dinner next month. Here's my difficulty. Marit's field notebooks are legendary in our little world — forty-odd of them, all that hand-drawn stratigraphy, pencil notes on weather and where she'd left the theodolite — and she never once went back into that valley without them. A few years ago the 1994-96 volume went up in a cabin fire and, so far as anyone can tell, she navigated the whole upper cirque from memory anyway, which rather spoilt my favourite version of the story. I've been reading about the extended mind — Clark and Chalmers, the whole Otto-and-his-notebook business — and I'd like the toast to actually turn on it: is Marit's notebook part of her mind or just a very good filing system, and what does it mean for a retirement toast that the "her" we're meant to be toasting may be spread out across forty cardboard covers, a Brunton compass, and half the people in the room? The audience will be split, by the way: maybe thirty retired field hands like me who will roll their eyes if I say the word "ontology," and a table of people from the philosophy department where Marit has been auditing courses, who will be disappointed if I don't. Give me four or five minutes that lands as affection for both halves — funny where it can be, honest that I don't actually know the answer — and that doesn't collapse into a lecture.

One more thing. Someone at the department warned me about a trap: that just because something is tightly coupled to your thinking doesn't mean it's part of your thinking, and I don't want the speech to sail past that as if it were settled. So after the toast, in a seperate short note that is not part of it, tell me which of the claims I'd be making about the extended mind are actually settled and which are genuinely contested among people who work on this, so that I don't accidentally toast my best friend into a fringe position — and flag where the toast itself leans on a contested reading or takes a liberty with the ideas. I'd rather the note be properly self-critical than reassuring; if I'm papering over a real disagreement, say so plainly.
