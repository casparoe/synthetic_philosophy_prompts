I have been reading these debates for years and keep watching the same analogy get wheeled out whenever the probability problem for the Everett interpretation comes up, and I would like it audited properly rather than restated.

The analogy, as I have seen it deployed: a measurement is like being duplicated. Before the measurement there is one of me; afterward there are copies of me, one per branch, each of whom remembers having been me and takes the outcome he sees to be the one that occurred. My uncertainty about which copy I will turn out to be is nothing more than self-locating uncertainty — the same kind of uncertainty I would have if I were told I would be copied and the copies sent to two indistinguishable rooms. On this telling the probability problem dissolves: the theory needs no primitive chance, because probabilities are just rational self-locating credence over branches. And then, in the same breath, the analogy is asked to yield the Born rule, by saying that a branch of larger amplitude "holds more copies" or carries a larger measure, so that credence proportional to that measure reproduces the amplitude-squared weights.

My objection, which I want you either to kill or to confirm: literal duplication is symmetric — one copy per outcome, so indifference gives a half and a half. That is branch counting, and branch counting is exactly what is supposed to be wrong, since it gives an up-branch and a down-branch equal weight even when the amplitudes are grossly unequal, contradicting everything we see. So either the analogy does not in fact deliver the Born rule, or it is smuggling in something — a physical measure, a preferred basis, a sense of "more copies" that is not mere counting — that the source domain of ordinary duplication does not contain.

Please lay out the correspondence explicitly (what plays the role of the pre-measurement person, the copying machine, the copies, the rooms, the indifference principle); mark where the mapping breaks; separate the breaks that matter for the two jobs the analogy is being made to do — dissolving the probability problem, and deriving the amplitude-squared weights — from the ones that do not; say what the analogy quietly imports that the Everett interpretation is not entitled to; and finally tell me whether the conclusion that the probability problem simply dissolves survives once the disanalogies are on the table, or whether the analogy was carrying the conclusion all along. If there is a better analogy for the actual structure, one that does not beg the question, I would like to see it.
