You are a political philosopher who works on historical entitlement theories of justice and, more unusually, on the institutional design problem of applying them: what it takes to turn a claim about how holdings come to be just into a decision that a non-philosopher can run on a case file. Begin with visible preliminary work. In a few paragraphs, set out what a Nozickean entitlement framework actually commits a decision-maker to — what it says about acquisition, transfer, and rectification, and about the difference between assessing a holding and assessing a distribution — and mark which parts of that framework are substantive commitments and which are placeholders the framework never filled in. Think carefully, in that preliminary section, about the difference between a procedure that is faithful to the framework and one that merely borrows its vocabulary. Only then write the procedure.

I chair a body — call it the Historical Holdings Board — created to hear petitions from families and communities claiming that an asset now held by someone else is not justly held. The assets are ordinary: parcels of land, shares in firms, a manuscript collection, a pension entitlement, occasionally an entire business. There are roughly two thousand petitions, we have eighteen months, three staff (one of whom is a historian), and a statutory mandate that directs us to take account of hardship, to publish our reasons, and to decide in a way that will survive judicial review and press scrutiny. I have no theory of my own and I want to adopt the entitlement framework as the Board's working doctrine, but a doctrine is not a procedure, and my case officers cannot be asked to read political philosophy.

Write the decision procedure we can actually run: a numbered sequence of questions in the order they must be asked, and for each question what would count as a sufficient answer, what evidence would settle it, and what happens when the evidence is simply absent; where the procedure stops with a denial, where it stops with an order to restore or compensate, and where it must be handed up to the Board as a whole because what remains is a legislative or evaluative question rather than a historical one. It must be usable by someone with no background in the theory, and you should mark which steps only look mechanical but are in fact judgment calls.

Then run the procedure in full on three petitions. First, an asset that left a family's hands under legal duress during a period of persecution and has since been bought and sold twice by purchasers who had no reason to suspect anything. Second, an asset whose current holder obtained it through an unbroken chain of ordinary voluntary transactions from a baseline nobody disputes was just at the time, where the petitioner's real complaint is not about any transfer but about the shape of the resulting distribution — the case the framework illustrates with a star athlete whose contract gives him a small cut of every ticket sold, so that once a million people have freely bought tickets he holds a fortune far larger than anyone else's. Third, briefly, an asset first acquired from no one at all, where the petitioner's community argues that the acquisition left them worse off than they would otherwise have been. At least one of these must come out in a way I will not like; say which, and give me the argument for accepting it anyway.

Finally, tell me what the procedure cannot do: the questions it will never answer, the petitions it will systematically mishandle, and the point at which the Board is no longer applying the framework but substituting something else under its name. I am particularly interested in the case where the framework's own rectification principle appears unworkable because the record of the original injustice and its downstream effects is missing, and in whether the Board may substitute a rougher rule in that situation, and if so whether that rule is still an entitlement rule. Note every place where the statutory hardship mandate and the framework are in outright conflict, and say which one the Board should break, and how it should record in writing that it has broken it.

Two constraints on how you write. The reasons the procedure generates must be honest about their own basis: if a denial rests on an absence of evidence rather than on a finding that the holding is just, the written reason has to say so. And you have no library and no database, so do not cite books, editions, or page numbers, and do not attribute any particular formulation to any particular writer; where you rely on a feature of the framework that you are not certain of, state the version you are using and flag it as an assumption.
