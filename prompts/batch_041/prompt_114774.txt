I'm a novelist — three books, none of which you'd of read — and I've somehow ended up teaching a first-year seminar called "Decisions, Bad and Otherwise," which is mostly me getting paid to think out loud about why characters do what they do. Finals are coming up and I need one essay question for the exam. I want it to be a real question, not "list the expected utility axioms and define each term."

Here's the setup I want to build it around. There's this game a student brought in — I think it's from some philosophy paper, I've never tracked it down — where you toss a fair coin until it comes up heads, and if the first head shows up on toss n you get paid (−1)^(n+1) × 2^n / n. So a head on the first toss pays you 2; a head on the second toss costs you 2; third pays 8/3; fourth costs 4; fifth pays 32/5, and so on forever. Every single outcome is a finite amount, the coin comes up heads eventually with probability 1, and nothing ever pays infinity.

If you add up probability-times-payoff in the obvious order you get 1 − 1/2 + 1/3 − 1/4 + ..., which converges to ln 2, about 0.69. Which is where the student says: great, the game is worth about 69 cents, so you should be willing to pay up to that to play it once.

I know enough to know that's not obviously right. That series only converges conditionally, and I know you can reorder a conditionally convergent series to get any total you like, so "the expected value is ln 2" looks like an artifact of the order you do the adding in. I've thought about this alot and I still can't articulate cleanly what's actually gone wrong, or what a good student should say instead. I also want them to notice that my character only gets to play once — one toss, one life, no reruns — and to say whether that matters.

One more thing. A colleague of mine, a mathematician who teaches in the same program, has a rule he's very proud of: if a game's payoffs aren't absolutely summable, you declare its expected value undefined and you refuse to compare it with anything, ever, full stop. I'd like the question to make a student say something about whether that rule is any good.

So here's what I need from you:

(1) The essay question itself, written the way I'd hand it out — one question, with whatever sub-parts it needs, 20 marks total, doable in about 45 minutes. No citations, obviously; it's closed book and half of them would just invent sources.
(2) A model answer, around 600 words, the kind of thing I could read aloud in class the next day.
(3) A marking scheme: the named moves an A answer has that a B answer dont, with the marks each move is worth, adding up to 20.

And if I've got something wrong in the setup above, or there's a place where a student could go badly off the rails that I should be warned about, tell me.
