I sit on the curriculum group for a student wellbeing programme that our university counselling service has licensed and is preparing to roll out, and I have been asked to sign off on the content before it goes live. The programme presents itself as applied Stoicism, and its contents are these: an opening session on the dichotomy of control, according to which some things are up to us — our judgements, intentions, and reactions — while everything else is not, with a worksheet that asks students to sort their worries into two columns; later sessions on negative visualisation (deliberately imagining the loss of the people and things you care about, in order to value them properly), on what the facilitators call the reserve clause (committing to a goal while adding in advance that you will pursue it unless something outside your power prevents it), and on keeping a nightly journal in the manner of Marcus Aurelius. The programme is sold to us on measurable outcomes — lower anxiety scores, better sleep, improved academic performance — and the facilitators are trained to say that Stoicism does not ask anyone to suppress their feelings, only to refuse to be carried away by them.

Two features of this strike me as not obviously coherent, and I would like the philosophical commitments of the practice laid out before we commit to it. First, the programme tells students that the only thing genuinely theirs is their own judgement, and that health, reputation, friendships, and grades are not goods to be pursued for their own sake; yet it recruits them with promises about sleep and grades, and it asks them to imagine losing their friends in order to love them better — which seems to require that those things are goods, and considerable ones. Second, the worksheet treats being up to us as though it meant being causally within our power: students are told to stop fretting about the exam outcome because they cannot control it, and to concentrate on what they can. But on the ancient doctrine the exam outcome, the effort they put in, and the state of their own nerves are all equally outside the will; what is up to us is assent rather than influence, and the dichotomy appears to have been grounded in a cosmos in which everything except assent is fated and providentially ordered. Strip out the providence and the thesis that virtue is the only good, and I cannot tell whether anything survives except a piece of management advice about focusing on your sphere of influence. A colleague in classics tells me the modern programme has quietly swapped the doctrine for something else; I want to know what, exactly.

Please reconstruct the theory this programme presupposes. What view of value does it need — that virtue alone is good and externals are indifferent, or the rival view that externals are genuine goods to be held loosely? What view of agency and control does the two-column worksheet commit it to, and is that view the ancient one or a causal-power reading that makes the dichotomy either false (almost nothing is fully controllable) or trivial? What theory of emotion does the reassurance about not suppressing feelings presuppose, given that the Stoics treated the passions as judgements, and is that reassurance a tacit concession to a rival account? Tell me which of these commitments the practice strictly requires and which it merely fits comfortably with, which rival views it silently rules out (an Aristotelian or Epicurean axiology, ordinary cognitive-behavioural reframing, the three-way split between what is fully, partly, and not at all up to us that some popularisers propose as a repair), and where the practice is philosophically underdetermined, so that several incompatible theories would license the same curriculum. Finally, say what would have to change in the programme — in the worksheet, the marketing, the facilitator script — if we replaced the presupposed theory with one of its rivals, and whether a fully secularised version of this practice can be made coherent at all.
