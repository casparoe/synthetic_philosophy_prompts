I serve on the bylaws committee of a housing cooperative of roughly four hundred members, and we are revising our charter. The document contains provisions that have produced a genuine tangle. Article 12 allows amendment by a two-thirds vote of a general meeting, provided twenty-one days' notice is given. Article 13 provides that neither Article 12 nor Article 13 itself, nor the list of member rights in Article 4, may be amended. Article 4 guarantees, among other things, a member vote on any sale of cooperative property. At last spring's general meeting, a properly noticed two-thirds majority passed Amendment A, which repealed Article 13 outright and rewrote Article 12 so that amendments need only a simple majority. At a special meeting two months later, a bare majority passed Amendment B, which removed the member vote on property sales. Our board must now decide whether Amendment B is in force, and the argument has turned philosophical: one faction holds that the charter is sovereign over its own amendment and the later vote therefore governs, while another holds that an entrenched clause cannot be repealed by the very power it limits, so that both amendments are void.

In preparing for the next meeting I have collected six resolutions, mostly from a legal theory reading group I attend, and I would like them ranked rather than merely described. (i) The chain of validity terminates in a norm that no one enacted: the original charter is valid by presupposition, so self-amendment raises no special problem and the puzzle is dissolved rather than solved. (ii) Whether B is law depends on whether officials — board, staff, arbitrators — converge on treating it as law; validity is a matter of an existing social practice of recognition rather than of logical derivation. (iii) Validity is judged by the rules of change in force at the moment of enactment: A complied with Article 12 as it then stood, so A is valid, and by the time of B Article 12 required only a majority, so B is valid as well, the entrenchment having lost its grip when it was repealed. (iv) Article 12 confers a delegated power to amend, while Article 13 marks a limit belonging to the founding moment that the delegated power cannot reach; A therefore exceeds the amendment power and B falls with it. (v) The entrenchment is addressed to amenders as a political promise, so breaching it makes them culpable without voiding the act; B is valid law and the remedy is political. (vi) The question has no answer until someone is authorized to give the last word, so what makes B valid is the decision of a designated final decider, and our task is to appoint one.

Please sort these six into tiers, from best to worst, giving a sentence or two of justification for each placement. Rank them by how well each handles five demands: it should yield a determinate answer in our actual case; it should leave the charter's authority grounded in the charter itself rather than in an outside act or sovereign; it should avoid circularity or regress rather than merely relocating them; it should leave the entrenchment clause doing some work, so that entrenchment still entrenches something; and it should be stable under its own amendment, in the sense that a future majority could not use the candidate's own logic to gut it. Then tell me which single candidate you would adopt, which one of the five demands you weighted most heavily in ranking, and whether any two of the six are the same position in different dress or whether one of them is not a rival at all but a change of subject. If you think the puzzle itself is ill-posed, say which candidate best exposes that and let it govern your ranking.
