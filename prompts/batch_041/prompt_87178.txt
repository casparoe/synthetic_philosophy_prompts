Next month I'm giving a talk at a joint session — half the room is my dept's philosophy of perception reading group, the other half is the vision science lab we share a building with (mostly psychophysicists, plus two people who build Bayesian/computational models of perception, one of whom is a hardline "one generative model explains everything" type). I'm defending a disjunctivist line, but not the usual one, and I want a Q&A prep sheet out of you.

Here's the gist of what I'll be saying, in case you need it to aim the objections:

- The argument from illusion (and its hallucination cousin) only gets going because it assumes that the good case and the bad case are subjectively indistinguishable *and* that this indistinguishability has to be explained by a shared experiential kind. I want to say that's a non sequitur. Indistinguishability is a fact about what the subject can *do* — no discrimination procedure they can run separates the two — not a fact about what the two states have in common. If you stipulate that the hallucination is a perfect copy, then of course nothing separates them; that's the stipulation doing the work, not a discovery about experience.
- So I replace the demand for a common kind with what I'm calling an asymmetric explanatory demand: the good case has to explain the appearances (why things look the way they do, and why the subject is thereby answerable to how things actually are), while the bad case only has to explain why the subject can't tell — and that explanation is allowed to cite facts about the subject's access and the state's etiology rather than shared content. No common kind needed. I claim the asymmetry is principled and not a dodge: it tracks the difference between explaining an appearance and explaining an incapacity.
- And I stick my neck out empirically. I claim this isn't just bookkeeping — it predicts a dissociation. Perceptual learning, adaptation, and attention manipulations that sharpen veridical discrimination should not transfer to hallucinatory episodes matched for report; and things that induce hallucination-like states shouldn't shift discrimination thresholds for the corresponding veridical judgments in the same way. So "matched for subjective report" doesn't mean "matched for what's doing the work." I have a pilot design for this and it's the part I'm least sure about.
