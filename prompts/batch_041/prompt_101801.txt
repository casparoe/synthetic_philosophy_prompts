OK so I keep getting into arguements about lending algorithms and I've realized my own gut reactions don't obviously hang together, so I want to write them down and see if there's a principle behind them or if I'm just making it up as I go.

Setup, keep it simple: a bank scores loan applicants from two groups, A and B. In A about 5% default, in B about 15%. Nobody's litigating why the base rates differ, that's just the world we're in.

(i) The model is calibrated inside each group — of the people it puts at 20% risk in A, ~20% default, and same in B. That feels right. If you slap a number on someone, the number should mean what it says.

(ii) Because the base rates differ, this model ends up approving a smaller share of B than of A. Honestly I think I'm fine with that? If the scores predict equally well for everyone, the algorithm isn't doing anything wrong, it's just telling you what's true.

(iii) Now a model that ignores group entirely. It's calibrated overall, but break it down and at the same score a B applicant is actually riskier than an A applicant. This one bugs me more than (ii) does, which I can't fully justify since by the outcome measure it's the "fairer" one.

(iv) And here's the one that really gets me. Two applicants, identical file — income, debts, history, all of it — except one's in A and one's in B, and the model scores them differently because group got in there somewhere. Same file, different answer. That's just not on.

(v) Where I tie myself in knots: it looks like getting (i) might force me to accept something like (iv), i.e. letting group membership nudge an individual's score in order to keep the scores meaning the same thing in both groups. And someone told me there's a proof that if I want (i) AND equal false-positive rates AND equal false-negative rates across the groups, it's flat-out impossible once the base rates differ, so I'd have to give one up. I don't know if that's right or if I'm misremembering it.

So: is there a principle that gets (i)–(iv), or close? If not, which one do I have to drop, and what does the surviving principle say about a case I haven't put down — a model that's genuinely worse at predicting for B but equalizes the error rates between the groups by giving up some overall accuracy? Please also say how confident you actually are in the principle you land on, and single out the one step you're least sure about.
