The data-protection and public-integrity commission I sit on must issue an opinion next month, and I would rather it be defensible against our own published record than against first principles. Some background you may need: we are a statutory body in a jurisdiction whose data statutes were drafted by borrowing in roughly equal measure from the German proportionality tradition, from the American presumption in favor of open records, and from the Chinese model of integrated administrative data. The commission has never formally adopted a single test. Majorities have been assembled case by case, and the three blocs — call them the proportionality bloc, the openness bloc, and the integration bloc — have each written dissents, so our unanimity, where it exists, has been unanimity of outcome rather than of reasoning.

Our six opinions to date, each decided on the merits and each published, are these:

1. A statute requiring real-name registration for social-media accounts was rejected.
2. A regime of police body cameras was approved: automatic deletion after thirty days, no biometric or facial analysis, and judicial authorization required before any footage could be reviewed for investigative purposes.
3. A requirement that employers report their employees' off-duty political affiliations and union memberships was rejected.
4. A national statistics database linking census and tax records was approved: records irreversibly de-identified, individual re-identification prohibited, use confined to statistical purposes, and the whole holding vested in an independent custodian rather than in any enforcement agency.
5. A scheme logging all web-browsing metadata of public employees was rejected: no warrant, no retention limit, no purpose limitation, open-ended reuse.
6. A duty on banks to report large cash transactions to a central unit was approved, but only in the version requiring prior judicial authorization for each report.

The proposal now before us is this. A statute would require every licensed commercial freight and delivery operator to transmit to a single state enforcement unit, in near-real time, the vehicle telemetry and route logs of its drivers — odometer, position, speed, stop duration, hours behind the wheel — limited to working hours and to company-owned vehicles, with personal devices and passenger data expressly excluded. The stated purposes are enforcement of the mileage-based road tax and detection of unpaid overtime. Records would be retained for ninety days. Neither collection nor querying would require judicial authorization, and the unit could query records without individualized suspicion. An independent ombudsperson would audit access and publish aggregate statistics; drivers would have the right to inspect and contest their own records; there would be no opt-out and no means of withdrawal.

I want four things, in this order. First, what should the commission decide if it wishes to remain consistent with its own record, and what is your best guess as to the outcome, with the reason you prefer it. Second, which of the six precedents is the closest analog, and why the precedents that look comparably close are not in fact the governing one. Third, which competing rules our record is equally consistent with, what each of those rules predicts about the new proposal, and which single further case — one we could actually put to a vote — would separate them. Fourth, if the precedents genuinely underdetermine the outcome, state plainly what principle we would be adopting by approving and what principle we would be adopting by rejecting, and say which of the two you take to be the smaller departure from what we have already committed ourselves to.
