I'm in a second-year early modern phil course and we just finished Leibniz, and now "the best of all possible worlds" is getting thrown around in section like it's one single obvious thing. How it went down: someone said the problem of evil is basically dead on arrival, because God had to create the best of all possible worlds, so whatever suffering there is must be outweighed or required by something better — you can't fault God for picking the optimum. Then someone else said that's just wordplay, since if "best" means "whichever one God picked" the claim is empty, and if it means "better than every other world God could have made" it's a substantive but untestable comparison of worlds nobody has ever seen. Then a third person said you can't even do it that way, because you can't just add a happy person to a world and call it better — worlds aren't buckets you pour happiness into, and adding something good might wreck the arrangement of what's already there. The TA said everyone was "conflating different readings" and moved on, which was maddening, and I have office hours Thursday.

And for each reading, tell me whether it's strong enough to actually shut down the problem of evil, or whether the objection survives it. Also — is it right that on Leibniz's own view God is maximizing a score over a set of options, rather than satisficing or just picking something good enough?
