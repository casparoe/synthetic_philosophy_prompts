I'm TA-ing a second-year philosophy of mind course and I'm working thru a stack of short-answer exams. I'd like your read on one of them before I commit to a grade. The question was worth 10 points:

Q: L. A. Paul distinguishes experiences that are epistemically transformative from experiences that are personally transformative. (a) Explain the distinction. (b) Explain why she thinks transformative choices — the decision to become a vampire is her central example — resist the standard expected-utility picture. (c) Say how she thinks we should approach such choices, and whether you find her proposal convincing.

Student's answer:

"Paul is attacking the ordinary picture of rational choice, on which you list your options, assign each outcome a value and a probability, and pick whichever option has the highest expected utility. She thinks the picture falls apart for a certain class of decisions.

An experience is epistemically transformative when having it gives you knowledge you couldn't have gotten any other way — you find out what it's like from the inside. The vampire is her example: before you're turned you can't know what vampire experience is like, because it isn't like anything a human has experienced. An experience is personally transformative when it changes your core preferences and values, so that the person afterwards is in some sense a different person from the one who made the decision. Having a child is her other main case.

For transformative choices both things are true at once, and that's the difficulty. Expected utility needs a value for each outcome, but the value of being a vampire just is what vampire experience is like for you, and you can't know that in advance. You also can't put a probability on the outcome — you don't know the odds that you'd end up liking being a vampire — so there's a second gap in the calculation. Paul stresses that there are two layers to this, because it isn't only that the values are hidden from you; the experience can also change your preferences, so the values you'd have assigned beforehand might be completely different from the ones you'd assign afterwards. Since the point of the decision is to be guided by your preferences, and the decision may replace your preferences, there's no stable standpoint from which to compare the options.

Paul's conclusion is that these choices can't be made rationally at all: the standard model doesn't apply, and she doesn't think there's any procedure that tells you what to do. So in the end it's a leap of faith, which is fine, but we shouldn't dress it up as rational deliberation. I don't buy this. It seems obvious that you can still decide using the preferences you have now — if you want to have a child, that's a reason to have one, and the fact that you'll change later doesn't take the reason away."
