I am a second-year PhD student in intellectual history, and I am writing a chapter on moral revolutions. I would like your comments on the following plan for a short essay. The essay argues that the abolition of slavery and the end of dueling are better explained by shifts in honor and status competition among elites than by moral argument or economic interest. It distinguishes three mechanisms: first, arguments that change first-order moral beliefs; second, interests that make a practice costly to maintain; and third, honor codes that make a practice dishonorable or status-lowering for those who engage in it. The essay claims that the third mechanism does most of the explanatory work in both cases, and that this has uncomfortable implications for the view that moral progress is primarily rational. It also suggests that the honor mechanism explains why abolition was often led by elites who did not directly benefit from it, and why dueling persisted longer in some societies than in others despite similar moral arguments against it. I am especially interested in whether the essay overstates the case, whether the honor mechanism can be separated from interests, and whether the argument conflates explanation with justification. Please comment on the accuracy of the historical reasoning, on whether the thesis is interesting, and on which specific parts need revision. I am not asking for a reading list or citations; I want to know whether the argument holds up and how it could be improved. Answer in American English.
