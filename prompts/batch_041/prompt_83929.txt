I retired from a physiology department a few years back and have been sitting in on a philosophy of mind reading group ever since. Last week I asked whether there's any real sense in which the visual system can just be wrong about the world — my examples were a radiologist missing a hairline fracture, and my own habit of misreading a plot because I've already decided what it shows. The grad student running the session said 'perception is controlled hallucination' as if that settled it, and everyone nodded. I don't think it settles anything, but I couldn't say why, and I suspect that one phrase is doing at least four different jobs in that room.

So: lay out the distinct precise claims 'perception is controlled hallucination' could be making, from the boring to the radical — the version that just means top-down effects exist, the version about generative models and priors, the version where there's no principled difference between seeing and hallucinating at all — and for each one tell me whether it's a platitude, a real empirical claim, or something that sounds deep but isn't, and roughly what would count against it. Then say which of those readings would actually license dismissing my question, and which reading the student probably meant. The part I really want is this: on the strongest reading, is there anything left for 'prediction error' to be an error about? If the system only ever gets scored against its own expectations, I can't see what keeps it honest about the world rather than just internally tidy, and I'd like to know if that objection has a name and an answer or if I'm confused. Make the verdicts explicit — I want a list I can bring to the next meeting — and if there's a reading I've missed that would genuinely change my mind, tell me that too.
