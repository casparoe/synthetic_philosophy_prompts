My friend and I spent the evening arguing about whether there can be reasons without any reasoner, and I want a consistency check on what I found myself defending. Here is the position. (1) Explaining a bit of behavior by the reason it would be good to act on — the wasp hauling its prey to the burrow, the seedling bending toward the light — is a real explanation, not a decorative restatement of the mechanism. (2) Reasons are normative: having a reason is a matter of something's counting in favor of an action, and nothing counts in favor except relative to a standard that can be met or missed. (3) The only sources of such standards are minds and histories of selection; there is no third source of normativity in nature. (4) Attributing beliefs and desires is a predictive strategy, and I take it to commit me to nothing about inner states. (5) Whether a bit of behavior has a rationale is something I discover rather than project — it is not up to us which rationale a trait has. (6) Rationales of this kind are everywhere, not only in animals: in plants, in single cells, in the pivot a sorting routine chooses.

The objection I could not answer came from my friend's own code. He wrote a caching layer for a service for one reason only, speed, and it happened to make the service survive a whole class of upstream failures. Nothing selected for that, he never had that reason, and no one has ever entertained it — yet he says the design has that rationale, survive this class of failures, and that this is as much a fact about the code as its running time. If he is right, (3) is false. If he is wrong, I want to know which of (1), (2), (5) and (6) I have quietly given up.

So please audit the set: which of these are in genuine tension, which only look like they are, and for each genuine tension how severe it is and what the cheapest repair would cost. Rank them rather than listing them as equally bad, and tell me which single commitment, if I dropped it, would leave the fewest scars.
