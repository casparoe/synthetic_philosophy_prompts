I've been assembling a position on the epistemology of markets and I'd like it audited before I commit it to paper. It's six commitments. I've stated each in the strongest form I can defend, which is partly why I suspect they don't all fit together. I don't have a preferred casualty, and I'd rather not be told to drop whichever is least convenient.

(1) Prices as aggregators. Market prices are the best available summary of the facts they're about — not infallible, but better than any individual participant and better than any non-market institution I know of. The reason is the usual one: the price system lets dispersed, privately held information get into a single number without anyone having to hold it all.

(2) The no-trade and agreement results. I accept the standard results as correct: given a shared prior, an allocation that is already efficient to start with, and disagreement traceable only to private information, trade cannot occur; and posteriors that become common knowledge must coincide. I also treat these results as a substantive constraint rather than a curiosity. Any account on which trade is driven by disagreement owes an account of where the disagreement came from, and "people just have different priors" is not by itself an account.

(3) Permissivism about priors. Priors are not the sort of thing evidence can compel. Two agents with the same evidence may rationally hold different priors and go on disagreeing indefinitely; making each other's reasoning common knowledge need not move either of them, and I don't regard that as a defect.

(4) What actually drives trade. Most of the volume in liquid professional markets is sincere disagreement about fundamentals between well-informed participants. Two desks take opposite sides of the same bond because they genuinely differ about the default path, not because one of them needs the cash.

(5) An objection to liquidity and noise explanations. I'm suspicious of explanations of trade that appeal to "liquidity needs" or "noise traders." In practice they're unfalsifiable: name any trade and it can be redescribed after the fact as liquidity-motivated or noise-driven, and no observation will ever count against the redescription. I don't want a theory of markets with that shape.

(6) Prices as sufficient statistics. The right idealization of an efficient market is that the price is a sufficient statistic for the information in the economy: an outsider who knew the structure of the market and saw only the price could, in principle, recover everything the participants know in aggregate. That is what I take "the price contains the information" to mean.

What I want is an audit, not a survey of the literature. Begin by saying whether any of the six is really two commitments wearing one coat, and split it if so. Then, for every pair, say whether the pair is independent, mutually supporting, only apparently in tension, or genuinely in tension. Then take the genuine tensions, rank them by severity rather than listing them as equally bad, and for each say what would have to give and what giving it up would cost elsewhere in the set. Where a pair only looks inconsistent, say what the appearance rests on — my guess is that it is usually an unstated assumption carried by one of the two. One tension correctly located is worth more to me than six flagged as fatal, so please discriminate.

Finally, tell me which single revision to the set cleans up the most, and what that revision costs me.
