I have drafted the short essay below for a graduate seminar on theories of history, and I would like your comments on it. Three things interest me in particular: whether the characterizations of the four thinkers are accurate and fairly drawn, whether the framework I propose does any real analytical work or merely relabels a familiar taxonomy, and whether the argument is genuinely interesting or is a restatement of something well worn. Please be direct about weaknesses; I would rather have the strongest objection than reassurance.

Draft:

Three Axes of Cyclicity, and Why "Cyclical versus Linear" Is the Wrong Question

Most disputes about whether history is cyclical or linear founder because they compress three separate questions into one. A view of history can be cyclical or linear along an ontological axis — whether the historical process literally returns to earlier states, as in doctrines of eternal return, or constitutes a single irreversible sequence; along a typological axis — whether the same forms recur, such as the rise and fall of regimes or the consolidation and dissolution of solidarities, irrespective of whether the underlying sequence repeats; and along an axiological axis — whether the direction of the whole is good, bad, or neutral.

Once these axes are separated, the canonical figures look less like a family and more like four distinct positions on a grid. Polybius holds typological cyclicity: constitutions pass through a fixed sequence — monarchy, its corruption into tyranny, aristocracy, its corruption into oligarchy, democracy, its corruption into mob rule — and the mixed constitution of Rome arrests this rotation rather than abolishing it. Ibn Khaldun holds typological cyclicity as well, though keyed to a different mechanism: solidarity is generated in the hard periphery, conquers the soft center, is diluted by luxury over roughly three generations, and yields to the next wave. Spengler and Toynbee, by contrast, are usually read as holding ontological cyclicity, on the model of organisms with a determinate lifespan: a culture is born, matures into a civilization, and expires on a schedule of roughly a millennium.

My claim is that the first two positions are stable and the latter two are not, and that the instability is instructive. If recurrence is only typological, the substrate can be perfectly linear: the Rome of Polybius does not repeat, it resists; the dynasties of Ibn Khaldun succeed one another within a single, undated, and ultimately providential sequence. But the moment recurrence becomes ontological — the moment the cycle is a clock rather than a pattern — the thinker owes us a period, and that period cannot be derived from the materials of history without circularity. Spengler's millennium is not measured; it is stipulated and then used to measure. Toynbee, to his credit, retreats from this: the breakdown of a civilization comes from within, but it is a failure of response rather than the expiry of a term, which renders his cycles a matter of agency rather than of ontology.

What I take from this is that the interesting variable is not cyclicality but the phase relation among cycles of different periods. Constitutional cycles, demographic cycles, and cycles of solidarity have no reason to share a period, and the phenomena we care about — revolutions, collapses, declines — are plausibly phase-lockings or interference patterns rather than the completion of any single cycle. On this view the cyclical/linear question is a category mistake: every historical process is linear in its substrate and cyclical in its forms, and what varies is only the coupling between the two.

Specifically, I would like to know: Is my reading of Polybius and Ibn Khaldun as merely typological defensible, or does their cyclicality run deeper than I allow? Is the charge of circularity against Spengler fair, or does he have a principled way of dating a culture's phases that escapes it? Is my placement of Spengler and Toynbee on the ontological axis itself secure, or have I misclassified one or both of them in a way that undermines the argument that depends on it? And finally, does the phase-relation proposal amount to a thesis, or is it a hedge that dissolves the original question without answering it? Concrete suggestions for sharpening the argument would be welcome.
