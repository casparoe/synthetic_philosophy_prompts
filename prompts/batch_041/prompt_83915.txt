The question on the exam was: Kant's 1797 essay replying to Benjamin Constant is usually taught as the classic statement of the view that lying is always wrong. Reconstruct Kant's argument in that essay as it applies to the case of the murderer at the door, then explain how a Kantian should answer the objection that this position makes the truthful person complicit in the murder. Be sure to address (a) what kind of duty Kant takes truthfulness to be, (b) what he means when he says that the person who lies bears responsibility for the consequences, and (c) whether the murderer's question makes any difference to the duty. (I know, it's a mouthful — the course convener writes these.)

The student's answer runs about a page, and here's my paraphrase of it. Constant's example, he says, is that a murderer asks whether your friend is hiding in your house, and Constant thinks you may lie, because the duty to tell the truth is conditional on the other person having a right to the truth. Kant refuses that move. For Kant, truthfulness is a duty of right rather than a duty of virtue, so it isn't the sort of thing that can have exceptions built into it: allow an exception for benevolent lies and you destroy the universality that makes it a principle at all, and you injure humanity in general by corrupting the source of all right, even if the particular person you lie to comes to no harm. The student then says that Kant holds the murderer to have forfeited, by his own criminal intent, any right to a truthful answer, and that this forfeiture is why the lie to him doesn't violate the duty — he presents this as Kant's own central move. On (b), he says Kant is making the point that lying is a gamble that tends to backfire: if your lie sends your friend out of the house and into the murderer's path, you'll have caused the death yourself, whereas if you tell the truth you at least haven't made things worse. So he reads Kant as saying the liar bears responsibility for the consequences in the sense that the consequences are likely to be worse and he'll have himself to blame. On (c), he says the murderer's question doesn't change the duty, since duties of right hold regardless of the other party's intentions, and that Kant therefore says you must tell the truth.
