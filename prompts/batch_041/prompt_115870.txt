A multinational firm requires its managers to attend workshops built around country profiles, in which each nation is scored on dimensions such as individualism, respect for hierarchy, and tolerance of ambiguity, with the instruction to adapt one's management style to the local score. The same firm's code of conduct forbids employees from treating colleagues differently because of their nationality, and its recruiters are trained to disregard national origin entirely. What does this pair of practices presuppose about national character? Reconstruct the implicit theory of what a country score is a fact about — whether it summarizes the distribution of individual dispositions or attributes a property to a group over and above its members — and what it assumes about how much can be inferred about an individual from a group mean. Then say which rival views of culture it quietly rules out (that culture is purely situational, that the nation is the wrong unit of analysis, that generalizing about peoples is meaningless or inherently wrongful), and distinguish what the workshops strictly require from what they merely fit well with: could a purely pragmatic, as-if reading of the profiles license exactly the same practice? Finally, is there a real tension between the two policies, or does it dissolve once we separate generalizing about a group from judging an individual; what does the reference-class problem do to a single-axis national profile; and what would have to change in the workshops if one of the rival views were adopted?
