I serve on the ethics commission of a religious congregation that has houses and schools in a country where, eighteen months ago, the elected presiding officer of the legislature dissolved that body, suspended the constitution, and has governed by decree since. He retains a faction of the officer corps, and his decrees have closed two of our schools and detained four of our members without charge. The commission has been asked to recommend a course of action to the superior general, and three proposals are on the table. The first is a campaign of public non-cooperation, refusal of permits and registration, and systematic documentation of abuses, run by our members and their parish networks. The second is material and logistical assistance to a group of former officers, including the former chief justice's supporters, who intend to remove the leader by force and have asked us for a vehicle, a safe house, and medical supplies; some of the younger members regard this as the only realistic path and have already been in contact with the group. The third is a recommendation that no member take part in anything, on the ground that the last attempted coup was followed by reprisals that killed more people than the regime's ordinary violence has, and that the schools we still operate serve children who would lose them.

I need the written opinion I will circulate at Thursday's meeting. It should recommend one concrete course of action, with a time frame and the specific steps the superior general should authorize or forbid, and it should give the reasoning in a form the members who disagree can actually engage with. Two features of our situation make this harder than a general discussion of resistance would suggest. First, our own rule provides that the superior general is bound to follow a unanimous recommendation of this commission, but that where unanimity fails, existing policy stands, which means no involvement, and the question returns to us in thirty days; so the opinion has to say what we should do if we cannot agree, and not merely what the best option is. Second, the man in question was not a foreign invader or a general who seized the state from outside. He held an office under the constitution before he destroyed it, which makes it genuinely unclear whether he is a usurper with no title at all or a legitimate officeholder who rules unjustly, and some members think that distinction decides the whole question.

Reason this through in the tradition of Aquinas's political and moral thought, and do not treat it as a matter of proof-texting. Address, at minimum: his account of sedition, including the claim that disturbing a tyrannical government is not sedition unless the disturbance brings greater harm than the tyranny did, and the further claim that the tyrant is himself the seditious party; his treatment of killing wrongdoers, and the restriction of that power to those charged with the care of the community rather than to private persons; and the disputed reading, which one member has put in a dossier, according to which Aquinas held that someone who frees the community by killing a usurper acts praiseworthily. Say what turns on that reading being correct or incorrect, and whether the permission, if it exists, extends to an officeholder who turned against the constitution he held office under, or only to one who never had title.

The opinion must be honest about which of Aquinas's premises are load-bearing. Two members are pacifists who reject his premise that public authority may put anyone to death at all, and one member is a consequentialist who believes the greater-harm proviso is the only criterion worth using and the rest is decoration. The memo should state which premise does the most work, what follows for our recommendation if it is rejected, and whether the recommendation survives on premises the pacifist and the consequentialist could each accept. Treat the strongest case for the option you do not recommend, including the case for doing nothing. Do not include a bibliography or citation apparatus; refer to positions and arguments by description, since the memo will be read by non-specialists.
