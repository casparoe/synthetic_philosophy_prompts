I've got no academic background (I do admin for an insurance firm) but I've been chewing on this on and off for about two years, and I've finally found a small foundation that hands out grants to people outside universities to do independent research. The application needs a proposal of about 1500 words: a clear question, why it isn't already settled, what you'd actually do, and what you'd have at the end. I'm on the committee for our block's shared back garden, which is part of where this comes from, so I'm not totally divorced from the topic.

Here's my half-formed idea and I'd like you to turn it into the proposal.

Everyone knows the story about the shared pasture where each herder sneaks on one more animal and ruins it for everyone, and everyone knows the two fixes: sell the whole thing to one owner, or get the government to regulate it. Then there's the counter-story that real communities manage their shared stuff fine on their own, with rules they make and enforce themselves. Both sides seem to treat "the commons" as one thing with one kind of problem. But I've noticed the pasture story only works if the herders can't talk to each other and can't agree on rules. Let them talk and it stops being a trap and turns into a negotiation. So I want to ask whether "the tragedy of the commons" is really a single thing at all, or a bunch of different situations lumped together under one scary name — a prisoners' dilemma here, a mix-up about who's supposed to do what there, a case where everyone's fine so long as they trust the others to pitch in somewhere else — with the scariest assumptions quietly baked in.

Second half, and this is the bit I care about more: the "public goods" vocabulary bothers me for the same reason. Something counts as "non-excludable" supposedly because of the nature of the good itself, but a lot of the time it's non-excludable because nobody's built a fence yet, or because we chose not to charge at the gate. So is non-excludability a fact about the world or a decision somebody made? If it's often a decision, then a pile of arguments that go "therefore only the state can provide this, or else nobody will" are doing something sneaky.

So the question I want to nail down is roughly: does "commons" name a distinct phenomenon, or a family of situations held together only by an assumption about communication and rule-making that's contingent and often false — and what happens to the "public goods" idea if non-excludability is usually chosen rather than given?

What I need from you:

- State the question so it actually has a determinate answer. Right now mine is mush and I know it.
- Explain why the standard answers on both sides leave it open — including where the privatise-it camp and the community-manages-it camp are agreeing more than either would admit.
- The method. This is where I need the most help, because I genuinely don't know what "research" means when you're not running experiments or digging up dead languages. What would I actually do, month by month, over a year of doing this part-time around a job? What are the steps, in what order, and what would count as having made progress versus just having restated my own intuition in fancier words?
- What a finished thing looks like, and honestly what would count as the project failing or turning out to be a dud.
- A realistic scope for a year part-time, and where I'd be overreaching if I promised too much.

Please don't give me a reading list or citations — the foundation doesn't want them at this stage and I'd rather you didn't invent references. Just talk about the ideas and the positions themselves.
