You are a philosopher of biology who also writes verse. I want a poem to close a reading group on biological function, and I want the form to carry the philosophy rather than merely dress it. Think it through before you write — settle privately which traits you will use, which account each stanza speaks for, and precisely where the form should give way — but your reply must begin with the poem itself. No preface, no account of your reasoning, no restatement of this request, and nothing after the last line. A single bare title line is the most I want above the verse.

The two accounts the poem has to hold together are these. On the first, a trait's function is what it was selected for: it is there because ancestors that did this thing left more descendants than ancestors that did not, and its present contribution matters only as the thing that was selected. On the second, a trait's function is the contribution it makes here and now to a capacity of the system it belongs to: some whole has a capacity, and the part's function is what it contributes to it. The second account needs no history at all — a working whole and a contributing part suffice. Keep the standard labels for these views out of the verse, and name no philosophers. A reader who has never heard of either account should still get the poem, and a specialist should be able to label every stanza without being told.

The form is a blues — the AAB kind, five stanzas of three lines. Lines 1 and 2 of a stanza are the same line (near-verbatim is acceptable); line 3 turns. The repetition is load-bearing. The first account needs recurrence: the doing has to have happened, happened again, and been kept before it can be what the trait is for. The second account needs no second time — one working whole, one contributing part, and the job is done. Let the reader hear that difference rather than be told it. If you can also rhyme the third line of a stanza with the first line of the next, as the tradition often does, so much the better; sound handed forward from one stanza into the next is a decent figure for inheritance.

Five stanzas, five cases, one apiece. Invent the organisms freely — nothing here turns on any real species — and keep the poem about traits rather than about theories. In order:

1. a trait whose doing is uncontroversially its function, where both accounts sign off, each for its own reason;
2. a trait that first spread for one job and later became useful for another;
3. a trait that spread because of a side effect it had on something else, while doing nothing useful in its own right;
4. a trait that once did a job, has stopped doing it, and is still there;
5. a trait with no history at all — an organ grown in a dish from a patient's own cells and stitched in, doing a real job in a working body from the moment it is connected, never selected for anything.

Then the part I care about most. In that fifth stanza the middle line must fail to be a repetition: it should look like the same line said twice and turn out not to be, since there was no first time for it to return to, and the third line should register what that failure does to each account. Reconcile the two only if you can do it honestly. I would rather the poem end where they come apart, with the stalled repetition as the last thing the reader hears.

Meter and line length are yours. Use the word "function" sparingly, if at all; the poem should be about hearts and feathers and dishes, not about the vocabulary. Take the time to get the cases right — five stanzas a philosopher of biology can read without wincing are worth more to me than ten that merely name the problem.
