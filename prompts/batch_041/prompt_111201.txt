I'm grading the final exam in a philosophy of cognitive science course and would like a second opinion on one script. The question and the student's answer are below. Assign a grade out of 10, and give me the specific comments that justify it — a point-by-point assessment of what earns credit and what doesn't, organized around parts (a)–(c), rather than a general impression. The script is anonymous to me and I have no stake in the outcome, so don't soften the comments.

Exam question:

An agent is trained by reinforcement learning in an environment containing a state S such that (i) every trajectory passes through S no matter which policy the agent adopts, and (ii) S carries a large negative reward. The learned policy nonetheless minimizes the agent's dwell time in S whenever the environment permits. A colleague writes: "The agent suffers in S. The negative reward just is the badness of the state for the agent, and the policy's avoidance of S is the evidence for it." Reconstruct the strongest version of the colleague's claim, say precisely what it presupposes, and state what someone who denies that the agent suffers would have to establish. Address (a) the difference between a reward signal's role in shaping a policy and the ascription of valence, (b) whether avoidance behaviour can count as evidence of anything experiential, and (c) whether any conclusion about the agent's moral status follows from (a) and (b).

Student's answer:

The colleague's claim is best reconstructed as an inference to the best explanation. The agent's policy is not indifferent to S: it leaves S sooner when it can, and it could only have learned to do that because the reward function penalized S. So the penalty does real explanatory work, and the colleague proposes that the best explanation of the penalized, avoided state is that S is bad for the agent in the way a pain is bad for us, rather than merely bad in the designers' ledger. What the claim presupposes is that the agent represents S as bad: for the avoidance to be the agent's own rather than a fact about the environment, some internal state of the agent must encode S's disvalue, and that state must be what drives the policy. Someone who denies that the agent suffers must therefore hold that valence requires a biological substrate, so that only systems with the right kind of wetware can have experiences with a felt quality. That is a strong claim, and I don't think it survives: we routinely ascribe states to systems whose substrate is irrelevant to the ascription, and the substrate view looks like an unargued prejudice in favour of carbon. Against the colleague, though, I would press that representing S as bad is not the same as S's seeming bad — a thermostat registers temperature without feeling it. Here the value function assigns S a low value, so the agent does represent S as bad, and the colleague's presupposition is met. But that is still a fact about the training setup, and avoidance behaviour is evidence for the policy rather than for anything experiential. Since we cannot settle whether the agent suffers, nothing is owed to it, and we may continue to use it as we like.

Finally: tell me what would change your mind about the grade — which reading of the script, or which argument, would move it up or down — and where your own assessment is most likely to be wrong.
