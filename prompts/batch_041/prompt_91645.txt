I am a third-year student in a course on social choice and democratic theory. We have just covered the Gibbard–Satterthwaite theorem, and I am trying to write a seminar response on it. As we stated it, the theorem says that any social choice rule over at least three alternatives, with unrestricted strict preferences and a range that includes every alternative, that is strategy-proof in dominant strategies -- no voter can ever do better by reporting a preference order other than her true one, no matter what the others report -- is dictatorial: there is a single voter whose top-ranked alternative is always the unique social outcome. I built the following argument around the theorem, and it now strikes me as proving far too much.

(1) A voting rule is legitimate only if it is strategy-proof in dominant strategies.
(2) By Gibbard–Satterthwaite, any rule over at least three alternatives, with unrestricted strict preferences and a range that includes every alternative, that is strategy-proof in dominant strategies is dictatorial.
(3) Therefore any non-dictatorial rule over at least three alternatives is not strategy-proof in dominant strategies.
(4) If a rule is not strategy-proof in dominant strategies, then there exists some preference profile and some voter who can improve the outcome by misreporting.
(5) A voter who can improve the outcome by misreporting will do so when she knows the rule and the other voters' reports.
(6) Hence every non-dictatorial rule over at least three alternatives will be manipulated in any election in which voters are informed and strategic.
(7) An election that is manipulated does not aggregate the voters' true preferences.
(8) Therefore no non-dictatorial rule over at least three alternatives can aggregate true preferences.
(9) Democratic legitimacy requires the non-dictatorial aggregation of true preferences.
(10) Therefore democratic legitimacy is impossible.

Our student union uses instant-runoff voting, which is non-dictatorial, so the argument implies that its elections are illegitimate no matter what. I would like to keep (2) and (9) if I can. Which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost me? Our professor warned us that the theorem is often overread, and I suspect my argument is an instance of that, but I cannot see where. In class we also distinguished dominant-strategy strategy-proofness from Bayesian incentive compatibility, where truthful reporting is optimal only relative to a prior over the other voters' reports; I am not sure whether that distinction is relevant here.
