I teach a 90-minute session in an upper-level seminar on autonomy and personal identity — sixteen students, a mix of philosophy and medical-humanities people, no set reading for the week and no clinical knowledge assumed. What I want them to feel rather than merely recite is the problem of the authority of the earlier self: that an advance directive is a claim staked by someone who will not be present to enforce it, against someone who may have no reason at all to honour it, and that the whole weight of the earlier self's authority therefore has to be carried by a piece of paper. I have been sketching an activity and would like you to build it out properly into something runnable. Keep or discard my sketches as you see fit, but I would rather you kept the general shape: a two-round role-play in which students first draft a binding instruction and then have to argue against one — their own — as the person it was written about.

Some specifics, so that the thing doesn't collapse into vague discussion. The condition should be fictional and stipulated rather than a real diagnosis, so that the group cannot litigate the medicine and nobody with a family history is put on the spot: something like a progressive syndrome in which present-directed preferences remain coherent and articulate, long-range projects and evaluative commitments are transformed or lost, the ability to engage with one's own earlier commitments is impaired but not absent, and the person remains capable of contentment. I want the directive to have four fields, because I suspect the interesting action is in the last two: a substantive instruction; a trigger specifying what counts as the condition's having taken hold; a defeasance clause saying whether the later self's contemporaneous wishes may override the instruction, and who adjudicates if they conflict; and a meta-clause governing how the directive itself is to be interpreted when matters are unclear. My guess is that most students will write a meta-clause that renders their own directive undefeatable — along the lines of 'any apparent rejection of this directive by my later self is itself evidence that the trigger has fired' — and that they will find it monstrous when someone applies it to them in the second round. I would also like a device that turns the third-person/first-person asymmetry into something the group can see rather than merely feel: my thought was that each pod must decide its cases by announcing a general rule, which goes up on a public board and becomes a binding precedent for later cases, so that the reasons they used to override a stranger's directive come back to govern their own. And I want one small real stake in the room, so that self-binding isn't purely fictional — my sketch is that everyone also signs a sealed slip committing themselves to something trivial in the final ten minutes of the session, which is opened and enforced at the end.

Give me the rules, the materials (I want a list I can photocopy in five minutes), the roles, a minute-by-minute timeline for sixteen participants in 90 minutes, and the debrief. The debrief should map what happened onto the philosophical terrain: what is owed to the earlier self as against the present one; whether the earlier self's authority is derived from the later self's own autonomy or is independent of it; the difference between a directive's authority and its mere predictive accuracy about what the later self will want; and whether the earlier self can coherently set the terms of its own defeat. Then tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it — not generic facilitation advice, but the specific ways this design fails, for instance what happens when students draft jokey or vague directives, or when the later-self roles get played as a question about competence rather than about values, or when a Round A pod is too lenient to generate any precedent worth citing. Finally, be honest about what the activity cannot reproduce and what I will therefore have to supply from the front of the room.
