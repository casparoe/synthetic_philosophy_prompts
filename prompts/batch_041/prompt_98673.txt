I'm a marine neurobiologist, and last fall I got roped onto our university's animal care and use committee (the IACUC) because they wanted someone who'd actually held a crab and knows what its nervous system looks like. That was fine until this month, when the protocol that landed in my queue turned out to be my own postdoc's. She wants to test whether shore crabs show motivational trade-offs — whether a crab will put up with a brief noxious stimulus (a mild shock to one leg) to get into a preferred shelter, and whether having been shocked before changes how it trades the two off. It's a clean design and honestly I'd have waved it through five years ago. But our committee has been arguing for a year now about whether decapods belong on the vertebrate track — analgesia, humane endpoints, enrichment, the whole apparatus — or the minimal-protections track we use for insects and other things we've quietly decided probably don't have experiences.

The committee is split down the middle. One side runs the no-brain-no-pain line: a crab has no cortex or anything we'd recognize as functionally analogous, so there's nociception without any suffering, and we're wasting everyone's time and money pretending otherwise. The other side says the behavioural evidence — centralized processing, opiate receptors, motivational trade-offs, guarding an injured limb — has piled up enough that the burden of proof has flipped, and anyway we can't verify it either way, so we should act under uncertainty instead of pretending the question is settled. For the last few years we've been scoring protocols against a home-made checklist (centralized brain, nociceptors, opioid receptors, trade-off behaviour, self-protection after injury, whether the animal will work for relief) and no one can agree weather that's a test of sentience or just a list of things that happen to correlate with it. Someone on the subcommittee keeps saying we should be assigning credences across competing theories of consciousness and picking whatever action is least bad across them, which sounds right to me but also sounds like a machine for never having to say no to anybody.

Can you draft the short written opinion I'll circulate before Thursday's meeting? It should recommend a concrete course — approve, approve with conditions, or send it back for revision — with a time frame and the specific conditions spelled out (dosing, trial caps, endpoints, whatever you think is actually needed), and it should engage the no-brain-no-pain position on its own terms rather than just intoning the precautionary principle at it. I also need a paragraph on whether I should recuse myself, since it's my own lab's protocol and I genuinely don't know where the line is. Aim for something one to two pages, in the register of an actual committee document rather than an essay — my colleagues have to vote on this.
