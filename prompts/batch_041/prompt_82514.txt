For the past six years I have supported myself as a novelist, and for the last year I have also taken on work writing other people's books. A publisher has now offered me a substantial sum to write the memoir of a wellness entrepreneur: my name nowhere on it, hers on the cover, the audiobook read in her voice, copyright and all subsidiary rights assigned to the publisher outright. The money is roughly three times what my last novel earned, the interviews are scheduled around my own writing hours, and nothing about the arrangement is hidden from anyone involved. My agent calls it the easiest decision of my career and says that anyone who grumbles is being sentimental — I am paid above my usual rate, by my own choice, for work I can do well. I was ready to sign. Then I described the terms to an old friend who teaches in a political science department, and he said, flatly, that I would be doing something wrong. I told him that complaints of that kind only bite when someone is underpaid or has no choice, and that neither applied; he said I was confusing a wrong with a hardship, and that the fee I agreed to is beside the point, because what the publisher is buying is not my hours but my capacity to produce a thing it will then own and profit from indefinitely, at a rate set by someone else — and that this is so whether the fee is generous or insulting. When I pressed him, he added that the money was not actually the interesting part: what would bother him in my position is that the book would belong to someone else from the moment it existed, that I would never get to decide what it was, that I would spend a year building a voice and a self that were not mine, and that he took this to be a different complaint from his first one, which people tend to muddle. I did not understand the distinction he was drawing, and I still don't.

Some further detail, since it may bear on the advice I am asking for. The contract lets the publisher bring in another writer to finish or rework the manuscript if I fall behind, keeping whatever I have produced; it also bars me for five years from publishing fiction with a protagonist resembling the subject. My sister does comparable writing on salary for a game studio and says she would trade places with me tomorrow, since she has less control over her days than I would have over mine. I also notice that I felt a version of this same unease when I signed my first novel contract, which everyone — my friend included — agrees was an ordinary and decent arrangement, and which I have never regretted. And I notice that part of me wants the fee precisely because it buys me two years to write the books I actually want to write, which makes the whole thing sound less like a trap than like a trade.

What I want is not a verdict on my friend's politics but a way to think about this specific decision. If I take the money, am I doing something wrong — and if so, is it wrong in the way he first said, that someone is taking more out of the arrangement than they are putting in, or in the way he said afterwards, that the work puts me at a distance from what I make and from myself? Are those two complaints genuinely distinct, and could either be true while the other is false? Does it matter that I would be good at this, that I would enjoy stretches of it, that the subject is agreeable, or that I would be doing it in order to fund work that is entirely my own? And if you think there is something off about the arrangement, tell me what I could actually change — a royalty, a shared credit, a shorter term, a right of refusal, a reversion of rights after some years — that would address the real problem rather than merely making me feel better about the fee.
