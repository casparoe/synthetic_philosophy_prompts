A framing that circulates in tech ethics, and that a colleague of mine defends at length, is this: manipulating someone is to legitimate persuasion as hacking a computer is to handing them a document to read. Persuasion gives a person something to evaluate; manipulation exploits the machinery of their mind to produce an outcome the manipulator wants, and that difference in kind is what makes manipulation presumptively wrong and autonomy-undermining. He leans on the analogy to treat dark patterns, microtargeted political advertising, and casino loyalty programs as a single class of wrong, distinct from ordinary advertising and ordinary argument. How far does it actually hold?

Lead with your verdict in the first sentence, then give the correspondence table — what plays the role of the system, the bug, the exploit, the payload, the legitimate user — and then the disanalogies, separated into those that matter for his use and those that do not. Three in particular interest me. First, computer systems have designer-specified proper functions and hacking is defined against them; does the mind have anything of the kind, and what does the analogy lose if it does not? Second, what corresponds to using a feature rather than exploiting a bug — a true but cherry-picked statistic, or a good reason that happens to flatter the listener's vanity? Third, if cognition is one integrated process rather than a set of separable rational and non-rational channels, is the analogy repairable, or does it collapse into the claim that all influence is manipulation? Close by saying whether his conclusion — a difference in kind, and presumptive wrongness — survives the disanalogies, and, if it does not, what analogy would carry it better.
