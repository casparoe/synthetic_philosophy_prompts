I teach a graduate seminar on conditionals, and for two terms running a colleague in the department has been pressing an analogy at our colloquium that I have not been able to answer to my satisfaction, and which my students have now begun to repeat as though it settled the matter. His line is that a conditional is nothing over and above a bet: in asserting that if A then C, one offers odds on C that are simply called off if A turns out not to obtain. The fair price of such a bet, he says, just is the speaker's conditional credence in C given A, and anyone who buys or sells at another price can be arbitraged; so whoever accepts the picture is committed to the thesis, usually credited to Adams, that a person's degree of belief in a conditional equals their conditional probability for the consequent given the antecedent. From that he draws a conclusion he regards as devastating to a great deal of the literature: that indicatives and counterfactuals are not two semantic kinds at all, but one thing wearing two moods. Both express conditional credences; the felt difference between the sentence about whether someone else killed Kennedy and the one about whether someone else would have is pragmatic, a matter of conversational background rather than of what the sentences mean. He knows perfectly well about the triviality results, which show that no proposition can in general have as its probability the conditional probability of the consequent given the antecedent, and he takes them as confirmation rather than refutation. I suspect the analogy is doing more work than it can bear, but I would rather find out than keep gesturing at my unease.

What I want from you is an audit of the analogy, not a survey of theories of conditionals. Begin by making the correspondence explicit: what plays the role of the stake, the counterparty, the event that calls the bet off, the settlement, and the odds, and what each of these correspondences contributes to the step from the analogy to the conclusion about indicatives and counterfactuals. Then tell me where the mapping fails, and be specific about it. I am particularly concerned about the case in which the antecedent is already known to be false. On the strict version of the picture the bet is called off precisely there, so a counterfactual wager looks void from the outset, and the analogy threatens to fall silent at exactly the point where my colleague needs it most; the natural repair is to keep the bet live and settle it at the nearest world in which the antecedent holds, but that repair appears to smuggle in the possible-worlds apparatus the analogy was supposed to help us do without. I also want to know what the picture imports without argument: that the object of a bet must be a proposition with truth conditions; that the stake can be valued independently of the content of the bet; that the calling-off condition is always a well-defined event; that a speaker's conditional credences are always defined, antecedents of probability zero included; and that conditionals take their credentials from our opinions, when we ordinarily take counterfactuals about the past to be true or false quite independently of anyone's betting dispositions. And I want to know what the analogy cannot deliver even at its most charitable — whether it can say anything at all about embeddings and compounds, about conditionals whose antecedents are irrelevant to their consequents, or about the gap between a high conditional credence and an assertable conditional.

Finally, separate the disanalogies that matter for his conclusion from those that do not, and tell me plainly whether the conclusion survives once they are on the table. Does the betting picture, taken at its most defensible, actually license the claim that indicatives and counterfactuals form a single semantic kind, or does it at best establish something about the epistemics of conditionals — about how we ought to reason with them — while leaving their semantics untouched? If a better analogy for the relation between the two kinds is available, I would like to see it, with its own correspondences and its own points of failure; and if the honest answer is that no analogy in this vicinity can carry the argument, then say so, and tell me on what other ground the question has to be fought.
