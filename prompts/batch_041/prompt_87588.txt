I am preparing a seminar on Cicero's Cato Maior de Senectute and want to pair it with Ingmar Bergman's Wild Strawberries (1957). The obvious affinities — an old man taking stock on a journey toward an honour he is not certain he deserves — are not what I need. I want a reading organised around an asymmetry that I suspect the film exposes in the dialogue.

Both works are, in a sense, ventriloquisms. Cicero wrote in 44 BC, in his sixty-third year, and was killed within two years; he gives the case for old age to a man in his eighty-fourth year, and the work is addressed to Atticus, to whom he concedes that the sentiments are his own rather than Cato's. Bergman wrote the screenplay in his late thirties and put it into the face and voice of Victor Sjöström, himself an aged former director. In each case the voice of old age is supplied by someone who has not yet arrived there, and the speaker's authority is doing at least as much work as his argument.

That is my first pressure point. Cato's defence is that old age is not miserable, and he answers four charges — that it withdraws a man from affairs, weakens the body, ends sensual pleasure, and stands on the verge of death — by showing that the goods of age are substitutes and remainders: counsel instead of command, memory instead of appetite, the recollection of a life well spent instead of the living of it, and the nearness of the soul's release instead of the fear of annihilation. Every one of these consolations presupposes a standing that is social rather than personal; the dialogue's premise is that Scipio and Laelius have sought Cato out because his old age is admired. Isak Borg has the offices and the honours — the professorship, the jubilee doctorate at Lund — and the film lets the ceremony fall flat; his daughter-in-law travels with him out of obligation rather than affection, and his son is a stranger to him. If you take away the admiration and leave the offices, how much of the Ciceronian defence survives? Is Borg's misery precisely the failure of character that Cato says is the fault of the man rather than of age, or is he a counterexample showing that Cato's position was always parasitic on other people's recognition?

The second pressure point is memory. Cato denies that memory decays — his proof is the cheerful observation that no old man has ever forgotten where he hid his money — and his happiness is retrospective, a harvest already gathered that the old man's task is to audit. Borg's retrospect is not an audit at all: it arrives unbidden, in dreams and reveries, and it judges him rather than rewarding him. Consider the examination dream, where he is asked to read an inscription he cannot decipher and is told that a doctor's first duty is to ask forgiveness. What does this do to a theory of old age whose goods are all stored in the past? Is a harvest only as good as the memory that holds it, and does Bergman show that the Ciceronian old man depends on a faculty — involuntary, unreliable, indifferent to his wishes — that the argument quietly assumes?

I would like a reading that takes both the dialogue and the film seriously enough to let each correct the other. Concretely: tell me which scenes to put in front of a class and what each one shows; say where the Ciceronian frame genuinely illuminates the film and where the film resists or refutes it; adjudicate the ending, which is usually read as reconciliation — is it earned, or is it the last and most seductive of the film's consolations?; and finish by saying what the film adds to the philosophical discussion of old age that argument alone cannot supply — or, if the answer runs the other way, what Cicero sees about age that a film made by a man not yet forty cannot show. I would rather have the argument than a survey of the secondary literature.
