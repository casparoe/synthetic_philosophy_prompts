I am a high school student in a small philosophy-and-cognitive-science reading group, and last week we spent a session on Escher's Drawing Hands and the idea of a tangled hierarchy. As it was presented to me: the two hands draw each other, and neither hand can be the reason the picture exists; what actually does the work is the sheet of paper, which is not itself a hand. Generalizing, any loop in which a system takes itself as its own object is possible only because something stands outside the loop — the paper under the drawing, the rules of a formal system, the machine running a program. The group called this outside level the inviolate level, and I think I followed that much. What I did not follow was the argument a friend produced at the end of the session, which felt to me like it proved far too much. Here it is, written out as carefully as I can manage:

(1) A self-referential structure — two hands drawing each other, a sentence that speaks about itself, a mind that thinks about its own thinking — is possible only because there is a level that is not itself part of the loop and that does the work of making the loop happen. (2) If a system could represent its own inviolate level from inside itself, then that level would be drawn into the tangle with everything else, and the representation would simply require a new inviolate level above it. (3) Therefore no system can represent the level that makes its own self-representation possible; at best it can represent a level beneath itself. (4) A mind that reflects on its own thinking is such a system, and the level that makes that reflection possible is whatever machinery is doing the interpreting — the cognitive apparatus itself. (5) Therefore a mind cannot represent the machinery that does its interpreting. (6) Any psychological theory the mind arrives at is itself produced by that machinery, so by (5) the theory can never capture the interpreter. (7) So a complete science of the mind is impossible in principle, and introspection is never evidence about the interpreter — only about what the interpreter happens to display.

I would very much like to keep premise (1), because it seems plainly true of the drawing and of the self-referential sentences we examined, and I would give up almost anything to avoid (7), which strikes me as a conclusion that would sink most of what I want to believe about self-knowledge. Please act as a careful philosopher of mind and logic and help me find my way. Begin by brainstorming the places the argument might be repaired — is some step invalid only under one reading of its terms, or is the argument valid and the real question one of cost? — and only then give me your verdict. Tell me which numbered step you would reject, why that step looked so natural that none of us noticed it, and what accepting your rejection would force me to give up elsewhere. If the trouble is a slide between two senses of a word, or a shift in what is being represented at which level, name it precisely. I would also like to know whether the same diagnosis would let a formal system, or an AI that reasons about itself, represent its own outside level; if it would not, I suspect my friend's argument is doing something other than what it claims.
