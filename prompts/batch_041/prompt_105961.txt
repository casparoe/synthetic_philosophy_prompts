Last night I had a long, slightly wine-fuelled argument with a friend about our councils new green electricity scheme, where everyone gets auto-enrolled onto a renewable tariff and you have to ring up and opt out if you don't want it. The council is very smug about the whole thing because, as they keep saying, it's not a ban, it's not a tax, nobody is being forced, they've merely changed the choice architecture. My friend says that's just coercion wearing a nice jumper. I half agree with her, but I also think there's obviously some difference between banning something and just putting it first in the list.

The bit I actually want to think about, though, is the councils own rules for this sort of thing, because they've got a little nudge unit and it runs on two rules. Rule one: no bans, no fines, no fees, only rearrange how the options get presented. Rule two: no nudges that exploit a bias, only ones that work with how people already tend to decide. So they happily approved the default tariff, but they turned down a different version of the scheme that would've made the non-green option sound like a loss, on the grounds that that was playing on loss aversion rather than respecting how people actually think.

What I want from you is this. What does that pair of rules presuppose about the people being nudged? Specifically, does it require there to be a fact of the matter about what a person really wants, or would want if they were paying proper attention, so that working with that is fine and exploiting a bias isn't? Or can you hold rule two on some other basis entirely, like the nudge being transparent, or being one that a reasonable person wouldn't object to once it was explained to them? Reconstruct the implicit theory of the person and of autonomy that makes those two rules look like a coherent pair, tell me which bits the practice actually needs versus which bits it just happens to sit well with, and say where the practice is underdetermined, i.e. where a few different theories would all licence the exact same rulebook. And then, if someone ditched the real-preference idea altogether, which of the two rules would have to go, and what would the nudge unit have to start doing differently?
