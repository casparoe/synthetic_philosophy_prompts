I'm in a small reading group that meets Tuesdays at a pub near campus, and last week we got thoroughly stuck on the Euthyphro dilemma — we all knew the standard line about whether things are good because God commands them or commanded because they're good, and we all knew the usual replies, so within twenty minutes we were deep in the weeds and then someone knocked a pint over the only copy anyone had brought and we went home annoyed instead of enlightened. The thing is, the whole puzzle now feels to me like a multiple-choice question I've answered too many times, and I've been thinking a story might do better than another argument. So: could you write me a short story, 1500 words or so, set in a small coastal town where for about two hundred years a light has appeared over the water once a month and spoken a single instruction — sometimes about who may marry whom, sometimes about which boats to repair, sometimes about who gets turned away at the gate. The town treats these as binding and has built its whole moral vocabulary out of them. There's an elderly woman who keeps the record of every instruction and can recite them from memory; a younger woman who has started to suspect the light was wrong at least twice; and a council that argues for weeks over what a garbled instruction actually meant, since I'd like the question of who gets to fix the meaning in there too.

The part I actually want dramatized is what our group couldn't get past. One guy kept insisting the dilemma is a trick about definitions — that it slides between what makes an action right and what our word right means, so you can say God is the source of moral facts without saying God invented them. Another kept saying the real third way is that the light's commands are good because the light's own nature is good, so the commands can't be arbitrary. I want both of those moves to have bodies in the story, and I want them to cost something. Give me a townsperson who holds the second view sincerely and then has to live with an instruction that looks monstrous: show me exactly what he says to himself. Does he decide his own judgment is the defective thing, and if so what does that do to him over the following months? Or does he quietly back off, and if he does, what was the view ever worth? And give me someone who holds the first view — that the light's words only report a good that would hold even if the light went dark — and then let the light go dark for a season, so we can see what the town is actually left holding. Please don't end with a scene where one character explains the answer to the others; I'd rather have two or three concrete scenes — a funeral, a trial, a night watch, whatever earns its place — and characters who are each partly wrong.

After the story, add a short note of a few paragraphs, still not preachy, saying which position each main character was actually living out, whether the story lands on one horn or suggests the dilemma is falsely posed, and what you deliberately left unresolved. I'd also like the note to name at least one thing the story couldn't capture — some version of the problem that needs no god at all, if there is one — and to say whether that version bites just as hard.
