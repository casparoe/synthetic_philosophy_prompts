Philosophers have sometimes proposed that dispositional claims are disguised conditionals: to say that a thing is disposed to M when S is simply to say that if S were to occur, M would occur. That proposal is usually thought to be embarrassed by counterexamples of two kinds. In the first kind, S occurs and M does not, even though the thing seems, in some sense, to be disposed to M — either because the occurrence of S itself destroys or suspends the structure that would produce M (cases usually called finkish), or because some independent factor intervenes to block M while that structure remains intact (cases usually called masked). In the second kind, S occurs and M occurs, though the thing seems not to be disposed to M at all, because the manifestation is produced by something extrinsic to it (cases usually called mimicking).

I want an essay on what these counterexamples really establish, organised around a case of my own devising.

Imagine a material — call it krysite — with two features. Its internal lattice is of a kind that shatters under a sharp blow. It is also strongly piezoelectric: a sharp blow strains the lattice, the strain generates a charge, and the charge rearranges the lattice into a form that does not shatter. The rearrangement is faster than any crack can propagate, and it reverses itself once the strain is gone. A krysite rod that is struck therefore never breaks.

Now consider four rods.

Rod A is an ordinary krysite rod. Struck, it never breaks.

Rod B is a krysite rod cut from the same crystal, of the same size and the same lattice. It is fitted with a fine grounded metal mesh that drains the charge away the instant it appears, so the lattice never rearranges. The mesh is a separate object and leaves the impact otherwise unaltered; apart from the mesh, Rod B is intrinsically identical to Rod A. Struck, Rod B shatters.

Rod C is made of a different and intrinsically tough material, which on its own would not shatter under such a blow. A device bolted to its outside shatters it whenever it is struck. Struck, Rod C shatters, though the rod itself is not brittle.

Rod D is an ordinary rod of brittle glass, with no piezoelectric response of any kind. It is held in a snug rigid steel sleeve that bears the load of any blow. Struck, Rod D never breaks.

Write a single sustained essay that weighs the arguments for and against each of the following and reaches a verdict on each: that Rod A is disposed to shatter; that Rod B is disposed to shatter; that Rod C is disposed to shatter; that Rod D is disposed to shatter; that dispositions are intrinsic properties of their bearers; and that the simple conditional analysis can be repaired rather than abandoned. Where a rod's disposition and its counterfactual profile come apart, be explicit about which step in the argument for that conclusion is doing the work, and say what notion of intrinsicality you are relying on. Say whether you take Rod A's response to be finkish or masked, and whether the distinction earns its keep. Do not end on a non-committal note: defend a position, and state what it costs you.
