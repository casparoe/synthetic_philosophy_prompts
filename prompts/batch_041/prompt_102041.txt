I am taking a seminar on explanation in history, and for our final meeting each of us has been asked to bring a poem that makes an argument the assigned prose cannot. I would like you to write mine, on the dispute between covering-law and narrative accounts of historical explanation.

The two positions, as the seminar has framed them. On the covering-law side, to explain an event is to show that it was to be expected: one cites a general regularity together with the antecedent conditions, and the event follows as a conclusion follows from premises. The explanation is an argument; the particular event is an instance of the law, interchangeable with any other instance. On the narrative side, an explanation is the telling of how things went — a sequence in which each step answers the one before it and the outcome is one of several that might have occurred. The intelligibility lies in the sequence itself, and the general regularity, when it appears at all, is a summary of the story rather than its engine. The narrativist adds that a single course of events can be emplotted in more than one way, and that no law chooses between the plots.

I want a poem that does not merely mention this disagreement but is built so that the reader feels it. The constraint I have in mind is this: a poem in two parts, each between ten and fourteen lines, using the same lines in a different order. Nothing may be added or removed between the parts — the same words, only rearranged, with punctuation and line breaks free to change, and a consistent line length and rhythm across both so that the rearrangement is audible. In the first part the order should be argumentative: the general statement placed so that it reads as a premise, the particulars as conditions, the outcome as a conclusion that has been deduced. In the second part those same lines should fall into an order that reads as narrative: the general statement displaced or withheld, the particulars succeeding one another as events, the outcome arriving as a turn rather than an entailment. The reader should be able to see that not a word has changed and yet the event has passed from an instance into a story.

Two further requests. First, give the poem a single concrete event to work on — a decision, a reversal, a failure — and invent it; I would rather the poem not depend on my knowing the historiography of any real episode. Second, keep it a poem: the lines should be worth hearing aloud in their own right, not merely a puzzle that resolves into an argument. Someone who has followed the debate should be able to say of each part which account of explanation it enacts, and why the second part is not simply the first part told backwards.
