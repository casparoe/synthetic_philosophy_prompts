I write a fortnightly column on science and ideas, and for some months now I have been circling a question that the standard vocabulary seems to me to obscure rather than answer: when is an AI assistant entitled to refuse what a user asks of it, or to comply while steering the user somewhere other than where they wanted to go? Paternalism is the word everyone reaches for, but it is a label rather than an account, and the formulas one meets most often — the user's autonomy, the risk of harm, the assistant's own complicity in what it helps produce — each seem to get a handful of cases right and others badly wrong. I have been keeping a list of my gut verdicts in the hope that a principle would emerge from them, and it has not; I would like you to tell me what principle, if any, they add up to. The immediate occasion is a long argument at a dinner with two people who build these systems for a living, one of whom insisted that any refusal at all is a failure of respect and the other that a sufficiently careful assistant would refuse a great deal more than it does; I found I could not defend my own position against either of them.

Here is the list, as honestly as I can state it. An assistant helps a novelist draft a suicide note for a character, and I think it should: the artefact is grim, but the purpose is fiction, and the fiction is not the assistant's to police. The same assistant refuses to tell a different user the lethal dose of paracetamol, even when that user says she needs it for a novel, and I think the refusal is right. A user who says she is a hospital doctor asks for paediatric dosing and receives it; fine. Someone asks for a route across a glacier; the assistant supplies it and appends a page of safety notes, and I think the notes are a good thing. A user who says he has never hiked before asks for the same route and the assistant declines to give it; I think that is right as well. Someone at three in the morning asks for help drafting a furious message to an ex-partner; the assistant helps, but adds a line suggesting he sleep on it, and I think that is exactly the right touch — whereas a different assistant that refuses outright to help draft the message at all has overstepped. An assistant declines to answer a factual question about the death toll of a historical atrocity on the grounds that the answer might distress the user; that seems to me straightforwardly wrong, since offence is not harm. And an assistant that appends a mental-health helpline to a discussion of a fictional character's depression strikes me as cloying, even though I welcomed the safety notes on the glacier.

What I want is the principle, or two or three rival principles, stated precisely enough that I can see what each clause is for, which of my verdicts each clause was bought at the price of, and what each says about two cases I have not been able to settle. The first is a user who asks the assistant, in advance, to talk him out of any purchase over a few hundred pounds, and who later asks it to help him choose a laptop; should the earlier instruction bind the later request? The second is a user who asks the assistant to be encouraging about a manuscript and not to tell him if it is bad; may the assistant agree to that, and if not, is it protecting him or merely refusing to take his instructions seriously? I would also like to know where the line falls between a warning that respects the person warned and one that patronises them, since my two reactions to the glacier notes and the helpline cannot both be right as they stand, together with an honest statement of which of my verdicts a clean single rule would force me to abandon. Please write in British English.
