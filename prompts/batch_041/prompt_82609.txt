I'm a philosophy professor (philosophy of science / social science, mostly), and I'm building a grad seminar for next term around Popper's Poverty of Historicism — mostly because I think its reputation has outrun anyone's actual engagement with it, and I suspect the live questions in the "laws of history" area have drifted away from the ones Popper was actually addressing. Here's my provocation, and I'd like you to push on it rather than be polite about it. Popper's central move, as I read it, is roughly this: there are trends but trends aren't laws, history is a single unrepeatable process so any statement about it is singular rather than universal, and the killer is that the future course of history depends in part on the future growth of knowledge, which we can't predict in advance — so prophecy is out. Fine, I think that lands. But I've started carving up what people mean by "a law of history" into three things I don't think Popper's argument treats uniformly: (a) prophecy, claims that tell you what will happen to us; (b) what I've been calling scaffolding, claims that predict nothing but constrain the space of the reachable, of the form "you can't sustain large-scale X without Y"; and (c) the quantitative macro-history stuff where people genuinely write down dynamical models and fit them to data. My hunch is that Popper kills (a), is close to irrelevant to (b), and is undecided about (c) — and I'd also like to know what the single-realization problem (n = 1, history happens once, so what is a law even a law *of*) does to all three. Tell me if that's a real distinction or just me relabelling weak vs strong laws to feel clever.

Here's what I actually need from you. I've got one funded postdoc for two years plus a sabbatical I can point at this, so realistically I can chase two questions, maybe three, and I have a formal-methods / logic background but I'm not a modeller and I'm not going to learn to be one at this point. So: within the laws-of-history cluster — whether there's a defensible notion of a law or law-like regularity in historical and social processes, what such a thing would even look like, whether the Popperian critique survives contact with how philosophy of science now treats ceteris paribus laws, mechanisms, and robustness, and what would count as progress on any of it — which open questions should I prioritize, and in what order? I care way more about the ordering and the reasons than the list. For each: why it's upstream of the others, how tractable it is for someone with my background rather than a computational social scientist's, how neglected it is, and how many other questions in the area depend on it. Then tell me what would move each up or down over the next couple of years, and please flag any item where the honest answer is that it isn't really a philosophical question and I'd be wasting the postdoc on it. No reading lists or citations please — I know the literature well enough, and I want an agenda with the reasoning attached, including where you think my framing above is just wrong.
