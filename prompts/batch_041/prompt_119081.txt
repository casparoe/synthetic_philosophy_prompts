For the last few years I've written the agony-aunt page in a small student magazine — 'Ask Aunt Cordelia', a deliberately old-fashioned column in which every reply is delivered in the tone of a woman reporting settled facts to a reader who simply hasn't been told yet. Our next issue is themed 'who knows you best', and for my contribution I want the column to take as its settled background the notion of a person's coherent extrapolated volition: not what she wants, but what she would want if she knew more, thought faster, were more the sort of person she wished she were, and had grown up alongside the rest of us — the further thought being that this extrapolated want is what a benevolent party ought to hand her, that where such wants converge we have something to aim at, and that where they merely pull apart the decent thing is to hold several doors open rather than choose. My instinct is that this makes for good comedy precisely because it is a serious position rather than a silly one, and because the aunt need not be a fool and must never wink: she should be someone who read the material, found it decisive, and is now applying it, in a level tone, to the postbag. It should be funny to someone who has argued about this for years and perfectly legible to someone who has never encountered the phrase, so work the idea into the letters themselves rather than assuming the reader knows it.

What I'd like is four or five short letters with her replies, which between them do the philosophical work. At least one letter should come from the person on the receiving end — someone whose spouse, employer or flatmate has extrapolated her on her own behalf and is now doing something she says she doesn't want, on the grounds that she would want it if she were better informed — and the reply should be a real attempt to work out whether that defence is available to him, and if so how far it stretches. At least one should come from a reader whose idealised self turns out to want something she finds repellent, and who wants to know whether she is bound to it anyway. Somewhere in the replies the aunt must have to say who does the extrapolating and from what starting point: whether a person's idealisation is fixed by what she is now or drifts with whoever is holding the pencil, and whether convergence between people is something we may reasonably hope for or merely something we assert because the alternative is untidy. Keep her consistent throughout — the advice must follow from the view rather than from ordinary decency wearing the view's clothes, and the funniest reply should be the one that follows the logic furthest, not the one that pulls up short. No real people: the correspondents are inventions, and the aunt is not a portrait of anyone living.

Roughly seven hundred words, in British English, as continuous prose — no headings, no bullet points, no bold, no tables — and please fold the letters into the running text instead of setting them off as separate blocks, the way one would write a long letter to a friend. No title or standfirst, and no sign-off beyond her name at the end.
