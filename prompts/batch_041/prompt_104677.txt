I am taking a course on the metaphysics of the social world, and over the term I have become convinced of a fairly unified picture of social kinds. On this picture, race, gender and money are all instances of one and the same structure: a kind whose members exist only because a community collectively accepts a constitutive rule that confers a status on them — some thing or person counts as something else only within a particular context, and only for as long as the relevant acceptance is sustained. What varies between the cases, on this view, is merely the content of the rule and the breadth of the community doing the accepting; the underlying mechanism is identical. The attraction is that it promises a single answer to the question of what it is for something to be a social kind at all.

I have a nagging worry that the picture is too tidy, and I would like you to tell me what would count against it. The worry comes from a distinction I associate with Ian Hacking, between kinds that are indifferent to how they are classified and kinds that are interactive — that is, kinds whose members are aware of the classification, take it up, resist it, or are otherwise altered by it, so that the label loops back and changes the very kind it was meant only to describe. Money seems to me to sit firmly on the indifferent side: the coins and notes in my pocket do not behave differently because economists have a theory about them. Race and gender, by contrast, look interactive through and through. If that distinction is genuine, does it merely add detail to the unified picture, or does it expose the picture as false — or, worse, as so thin that it cannot separate social construction from any other variety of social causation?

What I want from you is a map of the position's exposure. Which observations, arguments, intuitions or formal results would count against it, ranked from what would be fatal to what would be merely awkward? Which of these would a defender of the unified picture concede as counting against them, rather than dismiss as a misunderstanding? I am especially concerned with whether the appeal to collective acceptance has been qualified into unfalsifiability: if any social kind can be redescribed as collectively accepted after the fact, does the view predict anything at all that its rivals do not, and is that a genuine defect or simply the price of so general an account? Is there a case it cannot describe — a social kind, perhaps, whose members never encounter the classification at all? And does the position, once pressed, collapse into one of its rivals: a purely functional account of money, or a power- and hierarchy-centred account of race and gender, in which the mechanism is coercion rather than acceptance? Finally, tell me honestly which of these considerations I should personally find most persuasive, given that I was drawn to the view precisely because it unified the cases.
