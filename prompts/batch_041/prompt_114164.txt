Next term I am teaching a graduate seminar on the ethics of deception, and I have run into a pedagogical wall that I should like you to help me get over. My students are entirely at home in the distinction as the philosophy of language draws it: a lie as a false assertion made with the intention of being believed, misleading as a true utterance that nevertheless induces a false belief. They can run the usual moves about the gap between what is said and what is conveyed, about the difference between misleading by implicature and misleading by trading on a presupposition the hearer already holds, and they are happy to argue about whether the merely misleading speaker is more culpable than the outright liar or less. What they lack, and what I want to give them, is a translation of the whole apparatus into a vocabulary they do not yet have and that I happen to know rather well from my own training: the economics of information. I am fluent in it — types and mechanisms, incentive compatibility, the revelation principle, cheap talk, signalling and screening, sender–receiver games, and above all information design and Bayesian persuasion — well enough to teach it, though I am not a working economist and make no claim to be abreast of the frontier. A colleague in the economics department tells me the distinction is a distinction without a difference, since all that ultimately matters is the receiver's posterior; I want to know whether he is right, or whether he is simply describing the limits of his own formalism and calling them the limits of the subject.

What I should like from you is a systematic translation, undertaken in good faith, followed by an honest report of what the translation fails to carry. Begin with a translation dictionary: for each element of the ethical apparatus — the act of assertion, the content asserted, the hearer's uptake, the speaker's intention, the wrong done — give the closest counterpart in the information-design vocabulary, and say how tight the fit is. Then take the six species of deceptive act around which I organise the course and render each of them as a sender–receiver problem: (i) a flatly false assertion made in order to be believed; (ii) a literally true assertion whose implicature is false; (iii) a literally true assertion that trades on a false presupposition the hearer already holds, so that the hearer supplies the error; (iv) a truthful but selective presentation of evidence that moves the hearer's posterior without any false content at all, where the speaker knows in advance which comparisons the hearer will make; (v) deception by omission, in which nothing false is said but the hearer's default assumption — that a report is complete, say, or that a quoted price includes tax — is left uncorrected; and (vi) deception by making the world rather than the words, as when evidence is planted or an observation arranged so that no utterance occurs at all.

The residue report is the part I care about most, so please do not hurry it. I want to know which claims in the ethics translate cleanly, which translate only at the cost of distortion, and which have no expression whatever in the target vocabulary; and for each residue, whether it marks a genuine gap in the formal apparatus or a sign that the original claim was less determinate than it looked. My own suspicion, which I should like you to test rather than flatter, is that the lying/misleading distinction is causally invisible to the economics — that once the receiver's prior and updating rule are fixed and common knowledge, exploiting them is simply another channel, and the point at which error enters the causal chain makes no difference to anything the formalism can express — while being normatively central to the ethics, which is concerned with what the hearer is entitled to infer and with the recruitment of the hearer's own reasoning as the proximate cause of the error. If that is right, tell me what follows for the two disciplines; if it is not, tell me where the translation does better than I have allowed. Throughout, keep the translation honest: where you find yourself importing an assumption of the target framework — that only the posterior matters, or that the receiver's inference rule is always given — flag it as an assumption rather than presenting it as a result of the translation.
