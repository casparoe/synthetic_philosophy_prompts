Act as a philosopher of law who's spent way too long thinking about blame, standing, and hypocrisy. Think carefully, and maybe start by brainstorming the candidate views on the menu before you commit to one.

There's an asymmetry in criminal law that's been bugging me. In the same courtroom, the defendent's own record is fair game — the prosecution can haul up his past convictions to show he isn't credible when he testifies. But the state's own record is basically irrelevant: it doesn't matter, legally, whether the state, the judge, or the prosecutor has itself done horrible things; none of that is ever treated as a reason the state lacks standing to punish him. Same system, opposite treatment of "your own record." So what theory of standing to blame does the practice of criminal punishment presuppose? Does it assume standing has nothing to do with the blamer's moral record at all, or can you save it some other way — say, the state blames in a purely institutional capacity that's insulated from its own sins? Reconstruct the implicit theory, sort out which rival accounts of standing it actually rules out vs. merely sits comortably with (the moral-record/hypocrisy view, the relationship view, whatever else you'd put on the list), and tell me where the practice is just underdetermined, so that several theories would license the same legal practice. Don't just recite the usual positions — I want the reconstruction. Then, last thing: if we took the "clean hands" idea seriously and applied it to the state, what concretely would have to change in how criminal justice actually works?
