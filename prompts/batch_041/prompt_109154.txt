I'm a first-year PhD student who came to philosophy from physics, and I've recently found myself defending a position I didn't expect to hold: the strong principle of identity of indiscernibles — that necessarily, no two distinct entities share all their qualitative properties, where qualitative is meant to exclude impure properties like being identical to a particular thing. I was led here by thinking about the hole argument and about permutation symmetry for identical particles, both of which made me want a principled reason to deny that there can be distinct but qualitatively indistinguishable entities. Now I want to know what would count against the position, not just the stock objections.

Please map its exposure. Which observations, arguments, intuitions, or formal results would tell against it, and how decisive would each be? I'd like the defeaters sorted from fatal to merely awkward. In particular, I want to know which of them a committed defender of the strong principle would themselves concede as counting against them, and which they would dismiss as question-begging or as resting on a controversial account of properties. The two-spheres case is the obvious one, but I'm more interested in what happens when you vary it: does it matter whether the spheres are alone in the universe, whether they are particles in a symmetric quantum state, whether the world is symmetric under their exchange? Does the principle survive if we allow relational properties to individuate, and if so, has it been qualified into something trivial or unfalsifiable? I'd also like to know what the principle predicts that its rivals don't — what would the world have to be like for it to be false, and whether any of those possibilities are live rather than merely logically possible.

Please separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who know this literature, and don't present a contested reading as the consensus. I would find a concrete case that doesn't depend on controversial assumptions about the nature of properties most persuasive, but tell me if I'm wrong to weight it that way. Keep the whole answer to no more than 800 words.
