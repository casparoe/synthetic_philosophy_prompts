Write a dialogue between two historical sociologists who are jointly examining a single comparative case and who disagree about whether the culture of a community can figure in a causal explanation of its economic development, or whether talk of culture is at best a placeholder for interests and institutions that a completed explanation would cite instead.

The case they are both looking at is a pair of small nineteenth-century towns — call them Brückenthal and Neudorf — that were, by every material measure their historians can reconstruct, near-twins: comparable soils, comparable access to the same river trade, comparable populations, and the same legal status within the same principality. They nonetheless diverged sharply. Within two generations Brückenthal had accumulated capital, founded a savings bank and a machine-works, and developed a dense web of voluntary associations, while Neudorf remained a subsistence economy with almost no capital formation. The one salient difference the records show is religious: Brückenthal's leading families belonged to a Calvinist-influenced congregation that read worldly success as a sign of election, whereas Neudorf's belonged to a quietist sect that treated worldly striving as a distraction from salvation. Neither sociologist disputes that the correlation is real; they dispute what it is evidence for.

The first sociologist maintains that the difference in religious outlook is doing genuine causal work and that no complete account of the divergence can leave it out. The second maintains that the outlook itself stands in need of explanation in terms of the towns' prior institutions and material circumstances, and that once those are specified the religious variable drops out as a cause. Let each press the other hard. The first must say what it would even mean for a value-orientation to be a cause, and how such a cause could be distinguished from a mere correlate; she should also confront the objection that a culture is not individuated well enough to enter a causal claim at all, and that describing the difference as one of religious outlook rather than of economic strategy already reflects a value-laden selection on the historian's part. The second must say whether, by his own standards, any appeal to culture could ever survive, and must offer a concrete mechanism — credit networks, guild regulation, inheritance practice, or the like — rather than resting on the bare assertion that material factors come first; he should also answer whether his preferred explanation smuggles in a value-orientation of its own, for instance by treating the pursuit of material interest as the default that needs no explanation.

Both should be equally sharp, and each should answer the other's actual argument rather than restate a position. Let them reach genuine agreement on at least one point and remain divided on at least one, and let the disagreement at the end be sharper and better located than it was at the start. No citations are needed: have them refer to arguments and positions rather than to named works.
