I belong to a reading group that has spent a term on teleology — on whether it makes sense to say that a trait or a device is "for" something, and what kind of fact that is when it is true. One member has been defending a view she calls the free-floating rationale: a thing can have a rationale — there is a genuine, observer-independent answer to "what is this for?" — even though nothing anywhere entertains that answer, is guided by it, or is aware of it. The streamlined body of a fish is for fast swimming; the switch inside a thermostat is for keeping the room warm; and in both cases, she says, the "for" reports a real fact about how the thing came to be and what it does, a fact that would survive the deletion of every mind in the universe. Reasons, on this view, need not have reasoners.

That was congenial enough until she produced the following argument, which she says shows the view proves far more than its friends want. I have reproduced it as she stated it.

(1) To give a rationale for something is to answer the question "what is it for?" — to cite the point or purpose it serves. (2) A fish's streamlined shape is for moving fast through water; a heart is for pumping blood; the thermostat's switch is for keeping the room warm. (3) In each case the "for" reports a real fact, independent of any observer: it is because the shape helps with swimming that it was retained, because the switch keeps the room warm that it sits there. (4) A rationale is a reason; so in each of these cases there is a reason — a reason for the shape to be as it is, a reason for the switch to be where it is. (5) But a reason, to be a reason at all, must be a reason for someone or something: a consideration that no possible subject could take up, that could not figure in any deliberation or in any explanation framed in terms of a goal, is not a reason but merely a cause. (6) The shape's being good for swimming was not a reason for the fish (fish do not deliberate), nor for the biologist (the shape antedates him), nor for the genes (they do not deliberate either). (7) So either there is a subject we have not yet named — selection, nature, a designer — whose reason it is, or premise (5) is false. (8) And nothing in the argument is special to fish and thermostats: anything with a function qualifies — a kidney, an enzyme, and, once we start talking this way, a sunbeam that "serves" to warm the ground or an electron that "does its job" of being drawn toward a proton. (9) If we take the first horn of (7), then behind every functional fact there stands a reasoner; since function-talk is everywhere in biology and creeps into physics, reasoners are everywhere — panpsychism, or a cosmic designer. (10) If we take the second horn and deny (5), then any effect anything has counts as a reason it had, and "reason" has collapsed into "cause"; the free-floating rationale turns out to be no rationale at all.

So the view she favors seems to force a choice between panpsychism and vacuity, and she offers that as a reductio of the view itself. I am not convinced — I suspect something has been smuggled past me — but each premise taken on its own looks defensible, and I cannot decide whether the trouble lies in the move from (3) to (4), in (5), or in the slide in (8) from genuine biological function to any old effect. If I had to save one thing it would be the free-floating rationale itself, since without it I do not know how to state what natural selection explains. Tell me which step or steps to give up, why the offending step looked so plausible, and what each of the live options costs — in particular, whether a defender of free-floating rationales can keep (5) while blocking the road to (9), and if so, what she must say "for" means.
