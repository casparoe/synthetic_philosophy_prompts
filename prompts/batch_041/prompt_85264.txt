I teach a graduate seminar on value theory and rational choice, and a former student of mine — now under contract with a trade press for a book on decision-making — has sent me the chapter on value comparisons and asked for candid comments before he returns it to his editor. I owe him a real read rather than a shrug, but I find I cannot say precisely where his central section goes wrong. I am on the side of those who hold that transitivity is non-negotiable and that a spectrum argument has to be met by giving up something else, yet when I try to explain what his version is actually doing, I wave my hands. I do not need a reading list; I know the literature well enough. Here is the passage, trimmed of the surrounding anecdote:

CHAPTER SEVEN: THE SPECTRUM

Temkin's spectrum argument is the sharpest result in value theory since the repugnant conclusion, and it is simpler than its reputation suggests. Take a life of radiant contentment and a life of unrelieved agony, and interpolate between them a long series of lives, each differing from its neighbor by a single grain of sand's worth of quality; or run the same construction over a thousand performances of the same sonata, each a hair less expressive than the last. Between any two adjacent items the difference is one that no one could perceive, and a difference no one could perceive cannot count. So no item in the series is better than the one beside it. Now let transitivity do the rest: if nothing in the chain is better than its neighbor, then nothing in the chain is better than anything that comes after it, and the radiant life turns out not to be better than the agonized one — which is absurd. Taken at face value, then, the spectrum shows that our rankings loop and that 'better than' is not transitive. The received view among philosophers is that this is really just vagueness: 'better than' is a vague predicate with borderline cases, and the spectrum exploits them, so transitivity survives as a relation with fuzzy edges. What Temkin has really discovered, on this reading, is that values are incommensurable — that the goods we compare have no common measure, which is why no grain-by-grain comparison ever settles anything. This is just as well, since an agent whose values cycle can be turned into a money pump: he pays a little to trade each option for the next and ends up exactly where he began, only poorer. Since nobody thinks it is rational to be exploitable, value itself had better come out transitive in the end. And a cycle is no contradiction in a relation in any case: A can beat B at chess, B can beat C, and C can beat A, and nobody concludes that chess is incoherent. Parfit, who saw the same structure in his mere addition cases, was eventually forced to accept the repugnant conclusion; Temkin draws the bolder moral, that there is simply no fact of the matter about which of two lives is better.

What I need from you is an accuracy audit I can act on. Go through the passage claim by claim and sort it into what a careful philosopher working on the transitivity of 'better than' would accept as a fair statement of the argument, what is a popularization that would make a specialist wince, and what is simply wrong; where a defender of the argument would object to the author's way of putting it, tell me what the objection would be, and quote the passage's own phrases at the points where you are objecting to them. I am especially interested in four questions. First, does the argument, as he states it, establish what he says it establishes, or does its force rest on a premise he asserts rather than argues for — and if the latter, what would a defender of transitivity have to surrender in order to escape it? Second, is the diagnosis that the whole business is 'really just vagueness' and that Temkin has 'discovered' incommensurability a fair report of the state of play, or is a contested resolution being presented as settled, and are the terms 'vague', 'incommensurable', and 'intransitive' being used interchangeably when they should be kept apart? Third, does the money-pump consideration bear on the structure of value at all, or only on the theory of rational choice, and is the inference from exploitability to the transitivity of value as quick as he makes it? Fourth, does the chess comparison earn its keep, or does it trade on a difference between a relation generated by pairwise contests and a ranking of the items themselves? Tell me also whether the closing attributions — to Parfit and to Temkin — are fair to those authors so far as you can determine, since I would rather not let him publish a misattribution. Finish with a verdict I can pass along: once the misstatements are corrected, does the chapter's overall conclusion survive, and if it does not, what is the strongest thing the author could still say on the page he has already written? Please write in American English, in continuous prose rather than bullet points, and take his view seriously enough to argue with it rather than to score points off it.
