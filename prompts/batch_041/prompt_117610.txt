The question:

"Historians often treat Herodotus and Thucydides as the two founding fathers of the discipline. In the lecture I suggested we distinguish testimony — sources that mean to tell us about the past, like histories, chronicles, letters — from traces — remains that say nothing until we interpret them, like coins, inscriptions, pottery, shipwrecks. Using the Persian Wars and the Peloponnesian War as your two cases, assess the claim that Herodotus and Thucydides differ in method rather than in reliability, and say what each of them can and cannot establish about the events they describe. No reading required — argue from the sources we discussed in class."

The answer:

"Herodotus and Thucydides get treated as opposites: Herodotus the storyteller who repeats whatever he's told, Thucydides the cold analyst who weighs his evidence. I want to argue that the difference is one of method, not of reliability, and that it's less sharp than it looks.

Start with Herodotus. He's our only real narrative source for the Persian Wars, and he says openly that he reports what he's been told without necessarily believing it, which people take as an admission of credulity. That's unfair. He names his informants — the priests at Memphis, the Spartans — he flags the versions he distrusts, and he sometimes gives two or three competing accounts and admits he can't decide between them. Where he's weak is numbers and marvels, the size of the armies, the gold-digging ants, and that is a real problem; but it's a problem with the material, not with his honesty. And we're not stuck with him anyway: Diodorus, Plutarch and later writers all describe the Persian Wars, so we have several independent attestations of the main events, which is why nobody seriously doubts that Xerxes invaded.

Thucydides is a different case. He tells us at the start that he composed the speeches himself, making each speaker say what the occasion seemed to call for. That's a remarkable piece of honesty, but it also means the speeches can't be used as evidence of what was actually said — they're Thucydides' own views put into other people's mouths. The narrative of events is another matter. He was an Athenian general and an eyewitness to the war, so for the fighting he describes he's about as good as it gets, which is why his account of the Sicilian expedition is still the standard one.

Where the two really come apart is what they're doing with all of it. Herodotus wants to explain why Greeks and Persians came to blows, and he'll happily digress for a generation if the digression explains something. Thucydides wants the war's causes and its course, and he cuts whatever doesn't serve that. That's a difference in the shape of the explanation, not in how far you can trust them.

If I had to sum up: Herodotus gives us a world, Thucydides gives us a war. Both are doing something more interesting than either recording or inventing, and the testimony/trace distinction is useful here — their texts are testimony, and it's the traces, the Serpent Column, the inscriptions, that keep the testimony honest."
