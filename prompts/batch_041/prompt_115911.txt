Ok, I need a favour and it's a slightly weird one. I'm giving the opening remarks at a small two-day workshop next month on split-brain cases and the unity of consciousness — about forty people, and it's a mixed room in a way that makes me nervous. Half of them are philosphers of mind and vision scientists who've read all the standard commissurotomy work and will notice immediately if I say something loose. The other half are the nueropsych people who actually run the testing, who are a bit allergic to philosophers telling them what their data mean, plus two people from the foundation that paid for the whole thing. And one of the former patients and her husband are coming to the first session, which is the part I keep thinking about.

I've got eight minutes. I don't want to do a survey of the literature — they've all read it, and anyway I'd just be reciting. What I want is for the remarks to set up a distinction I've been chewing on, which I've been calling unity of control and unity of experience. Roughly: one agent doing one thing (or two agents doing two things) versus one subject having one experience (or two subjects having two). My own view is that the split-brain results are genuinely troubling for the second and much weaker against the first, and that a lot of the shouting in this area comes from people sliding between them without noticing. If the distinction is load-bearing for the whole two days, then when somebody says "the patient chose X" or "the patient saw Y", people will start asking which kind of unity they mean. That's what I want the speech to buy us.

Please write it. It has to land for the clinicians without sounding like a philosopher grading their homework, and it can't make the patient in the room feel like a specimen or like we've already settled something about her. Don't tell the room what the answer is — I genuinely don't know the answer, and I'd rather the talk be honest about that than tidy.

A couple of things about how to write it. Start with the speech itself, no preamble, no "here's your speech." Don't attribute views to particular living researchers by name — I'd much rather you write "one influential line of work holds that..." than have you guess at who said what and get it wrong in front of this room. Then, after the speech, put a short separate note (clearly not part of the talk) telling me which of the claims I'm making are actually settled in the literature and which are the contested ones I'm leaning on, since I really don't want to stand up there and present my own reading as if everyone agrees with it. Flag anything in there that takes a liberty with the science, or that would land badly on the patient and her husband.
