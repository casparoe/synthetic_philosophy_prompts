I am a third-year PhD student in philosophy, and I am putting together the opening session of our department reading group on the epistemology of deduction. The session centers on the old Achilles-and-Tortoise regress: Achilles wants the Tortoise to accept a conclusion by modus ponens, the Tortoise grants every premise he writes down but refuses to grant the inference, and each new conditional Achilles adds simply becomes one more premise for the Tortoise to accept. I want to open with a poem rather than a summary, because I think the regress is best felt as a kind of formal vertigo: the reader should experience the rule of inference sliding into the position of a premise, and then sliding again. Could you write a pantoum on this? Use four quatrains, with the standard pantoum repetition, and let the final quatrain return to the first and third lines of the opening quatrain so that the poem closes formally even as the argument cannot close. Those two lines should be a conditional and an imperative: one says what follows if the premises are accepted, the other tells Achilles to accept it. Their recurrence should do the philosophical work. The reader should hear the Tortoise treating the rule as one more sentence to be believed, and should feel why no further sentence can supply the binding force of a rule.

The audience will be mixed: some are logicians who know the regress cold, some are epistemologists who know it only as a puzzle, and a few are visitors from cognitive science. So the poem needs to be accurate enough that the logicians recognize the exact structure of the regress, including the distinction between accepting a conditional and being bound by a rule, without presupposing that everyone already knows the dialogue. It should not name the fallacy or explain the moral in a final couplet; the form should carry the argument. Please keep the diction plain and the tone unsentimental. The Tortoise should not be smug and Achilles should not be stupid; the poem should make the Tortoise's demand feel reasonable, or at least not merely obstinate, and it should leave the reader with the uncomfortable thought that the regress is not a trick but a real gap in the project of justifying deduction by further propositions. If it helps, you can give the Tortoise a closing line that is not a conclusion but a request for the next premise, so that the poem's formal closure is also the regress starting over.
