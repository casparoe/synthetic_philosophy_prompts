For my decision theory seminar I have to produce a one-page handout on the Allais paradox, and I would rather not write yet another list of the standard named models. What I want instead is a decision tree that a reader can walk through using only their own commitments, and that ends at a definite position on what the Allais pattern shows. Please write it.

Start from the fact itself. In the usual pair of experiments most people, myself included, take the sure million in the first and the gamble in the second — that is, they prefer a certain million to a lottery giving a million with probability 0.89, five million with probability 0.10 and nothing with probability 0.01, and then prefer the lottery giving five million with probability 0.10 and nothing otherwise to the one giving a million with probability 0.11 and nothing otherwise — even though the two pairs differ only by a common consequence and the independence axiom forbids exactly this combination. Build the tree so that each branch node is a question about what the independence axiom is supposed to be doing, not about which model the reader has heard of. Phrase every node so that it can be answered without knowing where it leads; no node should read like 'do you accept rank-dependent utility?'.

I want the tree driven by the following questions, and I want you to decide how they order and combine:

(i) does the pattern survive re-description — when the common consequence is changed, and when every probability in a pair is scaled down by the same factor so that no option is certain?

(ii) is certainty valued as a property of the prospect itself, or only derivatively, because of what it lets the chooser do?

(iii) are options to be evaluated solely by their consequences, or does the set of alternatives on offer, or the way the choice is framed, enter into the value of an option?

(iv) is the object of the axiom a preference, a disposition to choose, or a plan that can be committed to in advance?

(v) when the pattern is embedded in a sequence of choices so that a bookmaker can extract money from someone with these preferences, does that show the pattern is irrational, or only that a chooser who can bind herself to a plan in advance is not exploitable?

I would like the following three cases used as nodes, since I think they separate positions that otherwise look identical: the scaled-down version of the first pair, in which nothing is certain and the largest prize is only slightly more likely than the smallest; a two-stage version in which a fair coin first decides which of the two sub-lotteries you face and you learn the coin's result before choosing; and a version in which you are advising a stranger about a single one-off choice and would tell them to take the gamble in both pairs, so that your advice and your own choice diverge.

Aim for six to eight leaves. Label each leaf with the claim it makes in one sentence rather than with a school name, and add a familiar name in parentheses only where one genuinely fits. For each leaf give one sentence on its most awkward commitment. Then list the combinations of branch answers that no leaf occupies, and say of each whether it is unoccupied because it is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. Finally, flag any two leaves the tree cannot separate and say what further question would be needed. Keep the whole thing free of author names, dates and reading references, since it has to work for a reader who has not done the reading.
