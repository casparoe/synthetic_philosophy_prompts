I'm a doctoral student working on skepticism and selfhood in early Chinese thought, and I keep getting stuck on the closing stretch of the second chapter of the Zhuangzi (the "Qiwulun," or "Adjustment of Controversies," chapter in the received text). I want help with the argumentative structure rather than the imagery, which I think I understand well enough. I'm paraphrasing throughout rather than quoting, since I'd rather not lean on my memory for exact wording.

The material, as I reconstruct it: earlier in the chapter, the text worries about whether the self has a "true governor" (真宰) behind its various faculties, and remarks that we can affirm such a thing's activity while never finding its traces. It also insists that self and other, this and that, are mutually dependent — without an "other" there is no "I" — and yet it confesses not to know what makes this so. Then, at the very end, comes the dream: Zhuang Zhou dreams he is a butterfly, is wholly and unreservedly a butterfly, and on waking is unmistakably Zhou; the text then says he cannot tell whether he was Zhou dreaming he was a butterfly or is now a butterfly dreaming it is Zhou. Immediately after, however, it grants that between Zhou and the butterfly there must be some distinction, and it calls the whole affair the "transformation of things" (物化).

My difficulty is that this last move appears to disarm the doubt at the very moment it is raised, and I cannot tell whether that is a feature or a defect of the passage. Three readings strike me as live:

(A) The skepticism is local and diagnostic. The point is not that we cannot know whether we are dreaming, but that our confidence in a fixed, self-identical "self" is unwarranted; the closing insistence on a distinction between Zhou and the butterfly is not a refutation but a rejection of the question's presuppositions — the doubt only looks threatening if you assume selves must be either identical across states or not, whereas 物化 treats them as phases of a process.

(B) The skepticism is genuinely global and the closing line is concessive. Zhuangzi concedes only that dream and waking states differ, not that we can know which state we currently occupy; "transformation of things" names a boundary that is real but epistemically inaccessible, so the chapter ends in unresolved suspension.

(C) The passage is not about skepticism at all but about the fluidity of perspective and the impossibility of a standpoint outside transformation. On this reading the earlier "true governor" material is the key: the dream illustrates the failure of the demand for a single ruler or self behind experience, so the closing distinction is between perspectives, not between worlds.

What I want from you: help me understand the passage well enough to say which of these the text actually supports, and where each reading overreads. In particular, make the bearing of the earlier "true governor" discussion on the dream passage explicit, since I suspect the answer lies there, and state the counterarguments to each reading rather than gesturing at them. Please don't retreat into the claim that all three capture something; pick one, say what it explains better than its rivals, and name the cost it incurs.
