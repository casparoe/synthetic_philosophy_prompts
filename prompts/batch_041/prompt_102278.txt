I'm an undergraduate taking a decision theory and behavioural economics course, and our seminar has a tradition in which one student opens each session with a joke that has to be genuinely about that week's material, rather than a generic quip with a technical term dropped into it. My week is the Ellsberg paradox and ambiguity aversion, and I'd like you to write the joke for me.

Two things I want from it. First, I don't want the standard two-urn setup with a punchline that amounts to nothing more than people dislike not knowing the odds; the humour has to turn on the actual structure of the problem, so that a reader who knows the literature finds it funny precisely because it gets the details right, and a reader who doesn't still senses that something exact is going on. Second, I want it built on a particular variation our lecturer used to make the point that ambiguity aversion cannot be reduced to an aversion to risk, or to an aversion to compound lotteries. The variation is this: there is a third urn whose composition was fixed by a fair coin — if the coin landed one way the urn holds thirty red and seventy black, if the other way seventy red and thirty black — but which way the coin landed is hidden from you. A bet on red drawn from this urn therefore has an objective probability of exactly one half, identical to a bet on red from the familiar urn known to hold fifty of each, and yet most people still report the hidden-composition urn as the less attractive bet. I want the joke to exploit that gap between what the probabilities say and what the person is actually doing.

Concretely: keep it short enough to read aloud in about a minute; set it somewhere a person could plausibly be facing this kind of choice; and land it on a character whose preferences look probabilistically impeccable taken one at a time but give them away somewhere once you look at them together. Please don't make the punchline a pun on the word ambiguity or a joke about statisticians being dull — the comedy has to come out of the content. If it helps, you can close with one sentence naming the mechanism the joke is playing on, but the joke itself has to stand on its own.
