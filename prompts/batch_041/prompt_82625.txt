Im putting together a handout for a grad reading group on modality and I keep hitting the same wall: everyone has a story about how *I* can have properties in other possible worlds — that I could have been a doctor, that this table could have been made of ice — but nobody says up front what such a story has to get right before we start arguing about whether their story works. So skip the views for now and give me the desiderata: the full spec any account of how a particular individual (me, a table, a number) figures in modal claims has to meet.

I want the requirements themselves, each with the case or consideration that motivates it. Roughly what I'm after: which cases it must get right (the Humphrey case, the losing candidate who could have won; identity being necessary, i.e. if a=b then necessarily a=b; the case where I never exist at all; the case where I could have been slightly different from how I am in every single respect); which distinctions it can't collapse (de re vs de dicto; 'could have been' said about me vs said about whoever plays my role; the necessity of identity vs the contingency of my particular properties); and which it has to stay consistent with, given that it's supposed to be a story about *those* individuals and not just about roles or descriptions.

Then three things I really want from you:

1. Mark which of these are genuine requirements of the subject and which are just house rules of one camp. Is it really a requirement that the account make 'I could have been a completely different person' come out one way, or is that a prejudice? Same question for 'there's a fact of the matter about which possible individual I am' — real requirement, or not?

2. Flag any pair you think can't be jointly satisfied. My suspicion: the demand that these claims be about the very individual in question, and the demand that we be able to point to that individual's stand-in in another world *independently* of the properties we're asking about, pull against each other — you can't have both a fixed referent and a property-independent way of locating it. Tell me if that's right, and if it is, which one the popular views quietly give up.

3. The stuff people forget the account owes us: how it counts (is 'how many possible versions of me are there' even a well-formed question?), what it does when the stand-in isn't unique (two equally good candidates in the same world who differ on the very property in question), and what happens when the relation isn't transitive (my counterpart's counterpart isn't my counterpart — does that break anything?). Also the first-person case has to be load-bearing: whatever it says about Humphrey in the third person it has to say something sensible about me when *I* say 'I could have been a plumber,' and dont wave that off as a special case.

Finally, briefly, one or two lines each and no padding: how does counterpart theory do against the list, how does the plain identity-across-worlds picture do (rigid names, the same individual in every world it exists in), and how would a haecceity view do — each individual carrying a thisness that fixes it across worlds?
