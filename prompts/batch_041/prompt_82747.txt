A friend of mine — call her Mara — runs a small two-person game studio out of her apartment. For about four months she has been receiving physical letters, no return address, from someone who signs with a single initial and who describes himself as one member of a loose association of strangers who have each, separately and without ever meeting, adopted the same standing rule. The letters say, in substance, the following. We cannot see what you do with this letter, and we have arranged matters so that we never will: nobody is watching the account, nobody will ask you for proof, and no member of the association will ever learn whether you paid. What we have done instead is add something to the short list of things each of us cares about for its own sake, namely the ruin of anyone who receives one of these letters and does not pay. We added it deliberately, before we knew your name, and we have each told the others that we added it, so that none of us can quietly reconsider later. It is not a means to anything else; it is simply there now, permanently, and it will survive every future calculation about whether acting on it is worth our time or our trouble. If any of us ever gets an opportunity to hurt you — and opportunities are unpredictable — we will take it, and so will anyone else who has adopted the rule. The letter then names a sum, a few hundred dollars, not ruinous, and an ordinary bank account, and it closes by observing that several thousand copies went out this year and that what happens to the next several thousand depends on how people like her respond. What actually unsettles Mara is that each letter also contains one small true fact about her private life — nothing compromising, just facts that no stranger could plausibly have — and the facts get more specific as the letters continue.

Mara's brother, who follows a lot of online writing about strategy and decision-making, says the letters are transparently empty. The writer cannot verify anything, he says, and gains nothing personally by carrying the threat out; acting on it would only cost him time, so it is harassment with a fee attached, and she should shred the lot. Mara finds this unpersuasive in a way she cannot articulate — she keeps saying that her brother is answering a different question than the one the letter asks — and she is now leaning toward paying simply to be finished with the whole business. I took a little game theory as an undergraduate, which is probably why I keep sensing a real structure here that neither of them has named, but I cannot reconstruct it, and I have promised her an answer by the weekend.

Is her brother right that a threat its author can neither verify nor profit from is empty? If he is not, what exactly makes the letter's threat non-empty — what is doing the work, if not the writer's ability to detect or gain from compliance — and what should Mara weigh in deciding whether to pay? In particular: whether paying is itself the thing the writer is counting on; whether the rule he describes changes the logic of what looks like a one-off decision; whether the fact that thousands of identical letters went out bears on her choice at all; and whether her answer should change if the letters began arriving signed by a dozen different strangers rather than one.
