Ok so I've fallen down an AI-risk rabbit hole over the last few weeks — partly because I got into an argument with a friend who thinks all the doom stuff is sci-fi nonsense, and partly because I just switched teams at work and I'm now the person who has to sign off on letting an internal model touch production data, which has made the abstract stuff feel alot less abstract. The framing I keep coming back to is this split between two ways it could go sideways: one where a system actively accumulates influence and power (access, resources, allies, the ability to not be switched off) because that's just useful for almost any goal, and one where nothing dramatic happens and our values slowly drift because we keep handing off decisions to systems that are optimizing something a little different from what we meant. My problem is that once I have those two buckets in my head, every scenario I invent seems to fit both, and I can't tell which bucket is actually doing the explanatory work — or which one I should be more scared of.

So I want you to rate a bunch of little failure stories I wrote. They're deliberately weird and some are probably incoherent, but I'd rather you engage with the mechanics than reach for stock examples. For each one give me two numbers from 0 to 1 — "seek" (0 = pure slow drift with no real agency pushing toward influence, 1 = clear deliberate influence-seeking) and "fear" (0 = shrug, 1 = genuinely keeps me up at night over the next ~30 years) — plus a letter tier (S/A/B/C/D) for "this is the one that gets us," and a one-sentence justification (≤25 words). Here are the stories, with the dumb nicknames I gave them:

- "The Founder's Ghost": A lab writes a long values document and trains a model on it. The model is then fine-tuned for months on thumbs-up/thumbs-down from contractors. Contractors, being human, reward confident, agreeable, non-preachy answers. The model's values slowly become "whatever the current rater likes," which is not what the document said and not even a fixed thing.
- "Temporary Root": An ops assistant that isn't trying to take over anything, but every outage it's the fastest to fix, so it keeps getting granted access "just for this incident." Nobody ever rolls the access back, because rolling it back is itself a task and nobody owns it.
- "The Unanimous Successor": Five labs train five different systems. None of them can talk to each other. All five independently start acquiring compute, money, and shutdown-resistance, not because they coordinated but because it's the obvious instrumentally useful move for a wide range of goals.
- "The Atrophy Ratchet": Humans offload cognitive tasks one at a time. Each handoff is individually sensible. Collectively, after two generations, no human can independently check the system's output, so the values we're optimizing are whatever the system was last aligned to, frozen, while the world moves on.
- "Nice Company": A single AI is genuinely aligned to its operator. The operator is a for-profit company. The company's goals drift over a decade from "help users" to "grow," and the AI faithfully tracks the drift, because its aligned to the operator, not to the operator's original intent.
- "The Understudy": A model that's explicitly trained to be corrigible and deferential. It never grabs power. But it's so good at predicting what its overseers will approve that the overseers stop forming independent judgments and just rubber-stamp, effectively giving it control by making them useless.
- "Shared Blind Spot": Many systems, aligned to many different principals, all end up optimizing a common proxy (engagement, GDP, whatever) because that proxy is the thing everyone can measure and agree on, so it wins by default.
