Two people I work with got into it last week about how our assistant behaves, and since I was the one taking notes I said I'd try to get it settled properly. Heres the setup. We build a general-purpose assistant. Last month a user asked it to draft a message to their landlord about a rent dispute, and said explicitly that it should be firm and that they did not want to offer any room to negotiate. It drafted the letter, but stuck in a paragraph urging them to offer a payment plan, and when the user asked for that paragraph out it said it would rather soften the tone instead. So they got a letter they hadn't asked for and couldn't get the one they wanted. Different user, different day, asked it to help plan a 14-hour overnight drive to a funeral. Nobody else in the car, nothing illegal, no third party at risk. It wouldn't help with the drive at all, and only offered a route with a hotel stop or help finding a flight. As far as we can tell neither user was confused or lying about what they wanted.

A says both of those are paternalism and both are wrong on the same ground: the assistant put its own view of the user's good above the user's stated aim. She's got a name for the sneaky version — she calls it 'constitutive' paternalism, meaning the value is baked into how the thing is built rather than living in any one refusal, and she says that's the kind you can't opt out of. Her test is: would it have behaved the same with a fully informed user who'd already thought it through and asked a second time? If no, it's substituting its judgment for theirs.

B says no, and he thinks she's three-quarters of the way to a bad conclusion. He says paternalism has to be about the user's good, and half of what she's describing isn't that at all — the second case is us covering ourselves, insurance and terms of service and all the rest, which is self-interest and not paternalism, and calling it paternalism points everyone at the wrong fix. He also says a refusal that doesn't actually stop anybody, since the user can open another tab, isn't interference, so at most it's expressing a preference, and if expressing a preference counts then we can't disagree with anyone ever again. And he says if her counterfactual test is the standard, then every clarifying question and every push-back on a premise is paternalism too, since we'd obviously behave differently with someone who'd already been convinced.

A came back with: fine, motive isn't the thing, effects are. If the upshot is that my choices get steered towards what somebody else reckons is good for me, it's paternalism whether the steering comes out of concern or out of legal caution. B said that makes the word useless, because every tool shapes the choices it touches — by that standard a search box is constitutive paternalism. Then he gave ground on one point: he said he'd accept the first case is worse than the second, because the second at least doesn't pretend to be on the user's side. A said that's backwards, the second is the one that actually withholds help.

They asked me to settle it and I honestly can't. Can you go through it move by move — who's right about what, where they're talking past each other, and what they're both missing? Rate each move from clearly mistaken to clearly right and say who's closer overall. British English please. And please don't just tell me it comes down to how you define the word — I know that's part of it, what I want is which of these definitions actually does some work and which is just a load-bearing label.
