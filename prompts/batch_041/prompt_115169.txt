Next month I am giving a departmental colloquium in which I defend a qualified scientific realism against the pessimistic meta-induction, and what I need from you is a question-and-answer preparation sheet, not a survey of the literature.

Here is the argument I will be presenting, so that you know what the objections have to hit. The pessimistic meta-induction runs: past theories that were empirically successful — caloric, phlogiston, the luminiferous ether, Newtonian mechanics taken together with its full ontological commitments — were subsequently abandoned, so our present successful theories are probably also destined for abandonment, and realist commitment to their posits is unwarranted. I treat this as a base-rate argument, and I hold that a base rate is only as good as its reference class. Rather than fixing the reference class by theory or by entity, I propose to fix it by a single retrospective coding of the historical record: for each posit, how many evidentially independent routes converged on it before its successor theory appeared? My contention is that the abandoned posits are precisely the ones with few such routes, or with routes that only appeared independent because they shared a background assumption, whereas the retained posits — atoms, the electron, the gene — accumulated many. If that holds, the meta-induction loses most of its force, since its stock examples are exactly the low-convergence cases, and realist commitment can be reconceived as a graded credence that scales with convergence count: a defeasible, domain-relative bet on the deliverances of inference to the best explanation, and one that the history of science can in principle test.

The room will contain three constituencies, and I need to be ready for all of them. First, two historians of physics — one who has worked for years on the ether-to-relativity transition, one on caloric and the emergence of thermodynamics — who are allergic to philosophers mining their period for data points and who will treat my coding as amateurish and hopelessly retrospective. Second, a condensed-matter theorist of emergentist sympathies, who thinks effective field theory has already dissolved the realism question and that my proposal is a parochial hangover from particle physics. Third, two committed realists, one a structuralist and one who holds that the realist should commit only to those parts of a theory doing ineliminable work in the derivation of novel predictions; both will think I have conceded far too much to the anti-realist and turned realism into a heuristic with no metaphysical content.

Give me the objections I will actually get from this room, ordered by how likely each is and how much damage it would do if left unanswered, and labelled with the constituency that will raise it. Phrase each objection the way the person who raises it would phrase it, in their vocabulary. For each, give me a two-minute spoken reply, an honest note on where that reply is soft, and your judgment on whether I should concede, hold firm, or reframe — including which concession I should make in my opening remarks, before anyone asks for it. I am reasonably confident I can handle the two realists; I am not confident I can handle the historians, so be especially thorough there, and tell me where the same objection will be put differently by two different constituencies. Close with the question I am hoping nobody asks, and say plainly why I am right to be afraid of it. No citations and no suggested readings — I want the arguments and the words.
