I am a fifth-year PhD student in decision theory and the philosophy of rational choice, and what I want from you is an adversarial briefing on a position I hold, not a survey of the area.

My position is this. The independence axiom of expected utility theory is a genuine requirement of practical rationality, not a modeling convenience, and the choice patterns that go by the names of Allais and the common-consequence effect are mistakes, not data that an adequate theory has to accommodate. I take the sure-thing principle to be the engine of the requirement: it is what allows an agent to evaluate each act by the consequences that distinguish it from its alternatives, rather than by the menu of alternatives it happens to appear in. On this view the agent who takes the sure million over the gamble and then reverses when the same common branch is stripped away is not exhibiting an alternative conception of rationality; he is failing to hold fixed what the two problems genuinely share, and the failure is diagnosable as such. I also think the position has content: it predicts that the pattern should attenuate as agents become numerate, practiced, and reflective about the structure of the choice, and that whatever survives should be traceable to a describable source of error.

Tell me what counts against this, sorted by how decisive it would be for someone who already accepts a broadly expected-utility picture. I want at least these addressed, and I want you to say which are live and which are wishful thinking on the critic's part:

- The empirical record. How robust is the pattern to real stakes, repetition, market-like feedback, expertise, and to presentation manipulations that split or juxtapose outcomes that the axiom treats as identical? Some anomalies in this neighborhood have been shown to attenuate under such pressure and others have not. Which results, if the pattern survived them, would actually threaten me, and which could I absorb as further evidence of error?
- The relocation argument. There is a line of thought on which what the Allais choices violate is not independence but the reduction of compound lotteries, or the coalescing of equivalent branches, and that reduction is the more optional axiom, since a lottery with a preliminary stage is a different object from its collapsed form. If that is right, my position is false even if independence is untouched. How much weight should this carry?
- The normative argument for the axiom. Does any available rationale for independence actually deliver it, whether from money pumps, from dynamic consistency, from the representation theorem, or from the sure-thing principle itself? In particular, say whether the money-pump argument establishes the axiom or only something weaker once its assumptions about the agent's future preferences are made explicit.
- Rival theories. Rank-dependent and probability-weighted accounts, regret-based accounts, and configural-weight accounts all accommodate the pattern, and some of them are transitive, complete, and not obviously exploitable. Does the existence of a well-motivated non-expected-utility theory undercut the error diagnosis, or is being well-motivated not the same as being rational?
- The error theory. An error diagnosis owes an account of the error that predicts something beyond the pattern it explains. What would a successful one look like here, does one exist, and what would it have to get right?
- Insulation. Defenders of my position retreat to the caveat that the theory governs only small worlds, or to saying that deviations are costs rather than irrationalities. Is that a qualification that empties the claim, and if so, is that a problem for a view of this kind or just the normal price of a defensible one?

Throughout, separate what a defender of my view should concede from what only critics press. Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward. If any are live, say which and tell me what I should change my mind about. Take a definite position rather than laying out the options for me: tell me which line of attack you think succeeds, which is overrated, and which way you would bet. I do not need attributions or a reading list; I want the arguments and the evidence. Where your answer turns on empirical facts I may not know, flag that rather than smoothing it over.
