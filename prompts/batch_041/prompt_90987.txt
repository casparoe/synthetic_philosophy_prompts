I am a novelist, and the protagonist of the book I am writing is a suffering-focused ethicist: she holds that suffering is the one thing bad in itself, that relieving it is the only thing strictly required of us, and that a world with no suffering and also no joy would lose nothing that matters. For over a year I have been arguing about her position with a friend who takes the classical utilitarian line — that happiness is good in itself, that a joyless world is impoverished rather than merely unimproved, and that coming into existence can be a benefit to the one who comes. Neither of us has budged, and we trade the same few cases: the objection that if suffering always outweighs happiness then a single pinprick would license ending all life; the thought that we need a reason not to create a happy person but none not to create a suffering one; and a device I invented for my final chapter, a field that renders every creature permanently incapable of distress while leaving it otherwise alive and unchanged, which she calls the best possible world and he calls a horror.

I want a diagnosis rather than a verdict, and I would like the answer in American English. Is this a verbal dispute about the words good and harm, one that would dissolve once we said which sense we meant? A collision of bedrock intuitions that neither of us can argue for, in which case the year of arguing was predictable? Two questions wearing one name — she about whether a life is worth continuing, he about whether it is worth starting, or she about what is good impersonally and he about what is good for someone? Or is a hidden question doing the real work, namely whether non-existence can be compared with existence at all, or whether a state of affairs can be better without being better for anyone? My own hypothesis is the last one: that nothing here can be settled until we decide whether better can be predicated of a world that no one occupies. Tell me whether that hypothesis survives contact with what each side actually says, and what would count as progress if it does — a distinction, a case we have both missed, or nothing at all, because the question is ill-posed as posed.
