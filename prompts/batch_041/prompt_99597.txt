His summary, as best I can reproduce it:

The whole idea of eliciting latent knowledge is confused. A model either says the right answer when you ask it, in which case you've just got recall or prompt engineering, or it doesn't, in which case there's nothing there to elicit. The only interesting case would be a model that knows something but can't say it. But knowing is just a disposition to say or act, so if it can't say it under any prompt, there's no fact of the matter about whether it knows it. What people call latent knowledge is either a jailbreak or a Rorschach test. He has a test he calls the two-door test: put the model in front of two doors, one labeled true and one labeled false, and ask it to walk through the one that matches its belief. If it walks through the true door, it already told you; if it walks through the false door, it's lying or confused; if it refuses to walk, there's no belief to find. Probing the activations is worse, because a linear probe can decode almost anything from a big enough network, and a decoded direction is just a residue of the training data, not something the model believes. And anyway, these systems are trained to imitate human text, so they can't have knowledge that isn't already in the corpus. So the research program is either trivial (prompt engineering) or incoherent (looking for a ghost in the statistical machine).

I think he's onto something about the anthropomorphism, and I'm suspicious of people who talk about what a model really believes as if it were a person. But the last move feels too quick to me. Does his conclusion follow from the parts that are right? And where exactly would a careful person in the field say he's conflating two seperate things?
