I've fallen down a rabbit hole on the species problem and I keep getting hung up on the Neanderthals, so I'd rather work it out with you than reread the usual stuff. The facts I care about aren't the genetics minutiae: Neanderthals and early modern humans overlapped in time and in some of the same places, they're morphologically distinct enough that the default for a long time was to call them a seperate species, and we now know that non-African people carry a small percentage of Neanderthal DNA -- so there was at least some interbreeding, probably somewhere in the Middle East, and probably not very much of it. When the genome work came out, it seemed like it should have been a bombshell for the two-species view, and yet almost nobody changed their labels. What I want from you is a proper case analysis. Take the old textbook species concept -- a species is a group of populations that interbreed, or could interbreed, and are reproductively isolated from other such groups -- and tell me, on its own terms, what it says about this case. Does the actual interbreeding settle the question? Is the "could interbreed" clause doing any real work, or is it a hedge that lets the concept dodge every hard case? Then run it backwards: what does the Neanderthal episode show about the concept -- does it confirm it, embarrass it, or reveal that it was never built to handle fossils in the first place?

I've also got a distinction of my own I want you to stress-test. It seems to me a species boundary can be either a barrier or a gradient. A barrier is what the textbook concept seems to want: a causal wall keeping two gene pools apart, so that "same species?" has a yes-or-no answer sitting out there waiting to be found. A gradient is just a statistical difference -- the pools mostly don't mix, but the edge is fuzzy and the mixing is a matter of degree, not of kind. My hunch is that Neanderthals are a gradient case dressed up in barrier clothing. If that's right, tell me whether the textbook concept can even be stated in gradient terms without smuggling in an arbitrary cutoff; whether, on the gradient picture, calling them a species is a discovery or a decision; and whether that discovery/decision dichotomy is itself too crude. It also seems worth asking what role the concept is actually playing here -- a sorting rule for labels, or a claim about what's really out there -- and whether this case can tell us which.

One more thing to throw in, since I suspect this is where it breaks: imagine a lineage that interbred with us at, say, one percent per generation. Clearly not one species by any intuitive standard, but also not reproductively isolated in the strict sense. Does the concept have anything non-arbitrary to say, or does it quietly stop describing nature and start legislating vocabulary? And if there's something about the Neanderthal case that neither the barrier nor the gradient picture captures -- something about time, or about what we'd even count as the same lineage, or about the fact that we're the ones doing the classifying -- say so rather than forcing the frame to fit.
