I am putting together a handout for the second week of a graduate seminar on risk, uncertainty and public reason, where I want students to see that the precautionary principle is not one position but a family of positions whose disagreements are often obscured by the label. The familiar weak-versus-strong contrast is too coarse for my purposes. I want a taxonomy in the form of a decision tree that begins from a commitment students may already have—namely, that in some cases a lack of decisive evidence about harm should make a difference to what we are permitted to do—and then branches according to the questions that actually separate the positions. Please build the tree from the logical space first, not from a list of famous names: start with three distinctions I use in my teaching, namely precaution as a decision rule for choosing actions, precaution as an epistemic attitude towards incomplete evidence, and precaution as a device that shifts the burden or standard of proof onto those who propose an activity. Within that space, let the branch nodes be questions about the trigger for precaution (bare possibility, a plausible mechanism, a non-negligible probability, or a signal of irreversible harm), the object of precaution (the activity itself, the technology, the uncertainty, or the distribution of possible harms), who must bear the burden of proof, what standard of proof they must meet, and what remedy follows (moratorium, adaptive management, compensation, directed research, or nothing beyond ordinary regulation). Each leaf should carry either the name of a position—where a recognisable position exists—or the word unnamed, together with one sentence stating its most awkward commitment. I would like you to flag any pair of leaves the tree cannot genuinely separate, and to note which combinations of answers are unoccupied and whether that is because the view is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored.

To keep the exercise concrete, please make every diagnostic question answerable by someone who does not already know the labels, and anchor the branches in a small set of cases I shall supply: a gene drive intended to suppress malaria mosquitoes but capable of crossing into non-target species; a deep-sea mining licence for battery metals where the recovery rate of the seafloor community is unknown; a cosmetic ingredient with a weak but non-zero signal in a rodent assay; a stratospheric aerosol experiment that is cheap to start but costly to reverse; and a psychiatric drug that may help a rare condition but whose long-term effects are unknown. I want eight to ten leaves in total, and I would prefer the tree to distinguish positions such as ordinary cost-benefit analysis with a safety margin, a weak precautionary filter, a strong precautionary ban unless proven safe, an epistemic precaution that licenses research while restricting deployment, a burden-shifting precaution that permits the activity if the proponent supplies evidence, and a meta-precautionary view on which the principle governs which decision procedure we should use rather than which outcome we should choose. Please write the tree as a numbered or indented structure, with each branch labelled by the question that separates it from its sibling and each leaf carrying its one-sentence awkward commitment.
