So I got dragged to this long civic dinner thing my city puts on — they're floating a ballot measure to replace the city council with a randomly selected citizens' assembly, which is a whole saga here — and two people at my table basically ran the entire argument past each other between courses while the rest of us ate. I couldn't referee it then and I can't now, so I'm handing it to you. Cleaning it up from memory, it went like this:

Nadia opened with: elections select for people who are good at campaigning and fundraising, not at governing, and they force politicians into permanent responsiveness to donors and the loudest organized interests because they always need to get re-elected. A randomly drawn body of ordinary residents, given real time, staff, and expert briefings, would look much more like the actual population and be much harder to capture. Juries are the existence proof that ordinary people can handle a hard, unfamiliar job. Owen came back with two things. First, competence — legislation is complicated, you can't just hand it to a few hundred randoms. Second, accountability — if you can't be re-elected you also can't be punished at the ballot, so what stops the assembly from going off the rails or from being captured by whoever's briefing them? A politician's career is a crude discipline, but it's a real one; and juries decide facts under a judge, they don't set policy. Nadia answered that the assembly wouldn't draft the statutes anyway, it would deliberate and choose among options prepared by staff — jury-with-a-judge again — and that the citizens' assemblies Ireland and France have actually run show ordinary people handle complicated briefs fine when you give them time. On accountability she said re-election isn't the only discipline: you can add ratification by referendum, keep the assembly's mandate advisory, attach sunset clauses, or subject it to judicial review. And she added that "accountability" through elections is mostly accountability to the median donor, not the median voter, and that term-limited legislators have exactly the same no-future-election feature and nobody calls them unaccountable. Owen said fine, but referendum ratification just relocates the campaign-finance problem to a bigger stage — money dominates referendums even harder than candidate races — and an advisory assembly is toothless, so if it's toothless the capture problem you complained about just moves to whoever ignores it. He said the term-limit analogy actually cuts the other way, since lame ducks are famously more captured precisely because they have no future election to fear. And then his sharpest one: who picks the staff and the experts who prepare the options in the first place? That's where the real power sits, and you've hidden the agenda-setter. Nadia said the agenda-setter point is fair and she'd answer it with a second sortition — the staff and expert panels drawn or seated by lot and rotated, overseen by a sortition-selected body — and that you could regulate referendum spending or make ratification a second, differently-seeded assembly instead of a popular vote, and that the lame-duck evidence is mostly about individual legislators, not a whole body that knows going in that it's temporary. Owen closed with: now your design is just a hall of mirrors, assemblies all the way down, and at some point some body has to be the one that isn't drawn by lot — the one that writes the rules for the lottery — or the whole thing is ungrounded. And a body that can't be thrown out is exactly the body that eventually entrenches itself, lottery or not. That's the real worry, he said: not individual corruption, institutional self-preservation.

Can you go through it move by move — rate each move from clearly mistaken to clearly right and briefly justify each rating — and tell me who's right about which specific point and what, if anything, both of them are missing? Two things I especially want a verdict on: does Owen's closing regress point actually land, or is it a debating trick that proves too much, and does Nadia have a real answer to the agenda-setter worry or is she hand-waving? Please don't hand me "it's a rich debate with merit on both sides" — I want actual verdicts, point by point, even where you have to make a judgment call, and tell me who's more right overall.
