I've ended up in a reading group at the library that's working through early Chinese philosophy — my sister dragged me into it, she's doing a masters in media studies and she's very into the decolonizing-knowledge stuff, and she keeps telling me that logic is a Western invention and that other traditions had their own ways of reasoning that don't have to obey the law of non-contradiction. I'm a backend engineer, I spend all day writing if-then statements, so I've definately got a dog in this fight, but I also don't want to be the smug guy at the table saying the Chinese just never got logic. Last week the group did the white horse dialogue and I've been chewing on it ever since.

Here's what I think I understand, and why it's bothering me. Gongsun Long — or whoever's playing the sophist in the exchange — comes out and says a white horse is not a horse. Which is just wrong. A white horse is obviously a horse, it's a horse that happens to be white, and no amount of cleverness about words changes what's standing in the field. There's a bit in our translation where the other guy is told that if you're looking for a horse, a yellow or a black one will do, but if you're looking for a white horse it won't — and fine, that's true, but it seems to show only that the two phrases pick out different things, which nobody ever denied. So the dialogue reads to me as either a triviality dressed up as a paradox or a plain falsehood, and the School of Names starts to look like a bunch of ancient trolls who just enjoyed contradicting themselves for the hell of it. I don't doubt that people like that exist in every century, but I don't see how it's philosophy, and I really don't see how my sister gets from it to logic being a cultural construct. If anything it looks like the opposite lesson — they tried to break logic and logic won.

So my actual question, sorry for the rambling: what is that dialogue doing, if it isn't denying that white horses are horses? The more I poke at it the more I suspect I'm importing assumptions the text doesn't share — someone at the group mentioned that the School of Names isn't even something the people themselves would have recognized as a school, and that there were contemporaries of theirs doing serious work on argument and coming back at their arguments, and that English translations flatten distinctions the original is playing on. If that's right, I'd like to know what the actual disagreement was about, and what I can say to my sister that's neither caving nor condescending. The group is doing the hard and white one next week, so I'd like to be a lot less confused by then. Oh, and I have zero Chinese — I'm reading whatever translation the libary has — so if the whole thing turns on something that can't survive translation, tell me, because that would change how I read it.
