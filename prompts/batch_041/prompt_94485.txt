I have been trying to understand what remains of Gödel's rotating-universe argument for the unreality of time, and I would be grateful for your help with a difficulty that has been troubling me. As I understand it, Gödel constructed a solution to Einstein's field equations in which the universe rotates, and in which there exist closed timelike curves — paths through spacetime that return to their starting point in time. Because the model admits no global time function, he concluded that there is no objective lapse of time, and that time is therefore unreal. But the universe we actually inhabit is expanding and, on the largest scales, remarkably isotropic; it does not rotate as Gödel's model does. It seems to follow that the empirical premise of the argument has been falsified, and that we are entitled to go on treating time as real. I would like to know what, if anything, is left of Gödel's argument. Is there a version of it that does not depend on the actual universe being a Gödel universe, and if so, what does it actually establish about the reality of time? I am asking not as a cosmologist but as someone who teaches the philosophy of time and wants to know whether Gödel's argument still has any force for us, or whether it should be treated as a historical curiosity.
