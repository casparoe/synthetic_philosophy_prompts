So this is maybe a weird request, but I'm in a reading group on testimony and knowledge and we keep having the same fight, so I want to just lay out where my gut actually lands case by case and ask whether there's one principle behind all of it. I've been told the two things I'm circling are called testimonial injustice and hermeneutical injustice, and fine, but those labels don't carve my cases up the way they're supposed to, and I'd rather have a principle than a taxonomy. Anyway here's my list. (1) A resident tells her attending that a patient's pain is worse than the numbers suggest, and he waves her off in a way he never waves off the male resident saying the same thing — injustice, obviously, no argument there. (2) Same attending waves off a patient who is in fact hamming it up, and he does it because of the way she talks — I still want to call that an injustice even though he got the medicine right and nobody was hurt, which I can't fully defend. (3) He waves off a patient who has lied to him three times before about the same symptom — not an injustice, credibility is a resource and it's fair to spend it down when somebody's burned you. (4) A woman in a male-dominated trade has a feeling she can't put a name to — that the banter isn't banter, that getting handed the customer-facing work while the guys get the technical work is a pattern and not an accident — and since there's no word for it anywhere in her world she decides she's thin-skinned, transfers out, and spends five years underemployed — injustice, and I want to say the same kind as (1), even though nobody disbelieved anything she said and no hearer did anything. (5) Same story, except the vocabulary does exist, it's standard in a union newsletter two industries over that she simply never came across — not an injustice, or a much smaller one, which is a problem because the only difference from (4) is whether a word was sitting around somewhere.

Then some harder ones. (6) The women at the plant do have the term, a visiting organizer brings it to them, and they reject it on purpose because they figure using it will get them marked as troublemakers and make things worse — this feels like an injustice, but not one the organizer commits and not really one the women commit either, it's more like the injustice is in the situation itself. (7) A junior woman in a lab never reports a senior colleague's behavior, because she's watched exactly how that report goes for someone else, and her silence gets written down as no complaints, which later gets used against a second woman — injustice, but no hearer did anything wrong and there's not even a victim in the moment. (8) Going the other way: a charismatic guy in that lab gets believed about a result he's quietly fudging, and everyone's surplus of trust in him is a deficit for the careful woman who contradicts him — I think the injustice there is the deficit, not the excess, that the excess is only unfair through the comparison, which I can't square with (7) where there's no comparison at all. (9) The woman from (4), ten years on, now catches herself discounting her own read of a room before she's even finished forming it — injustice, and the strange part is she's the one doing it now.

Here's the principle I keep reaching for: an epistemic injustice happens when someone's ability to know something or to be believed about it is damaged by something other than how things actually are — a bias, a missing word, a group's habits — and the wrong is the damage itself, not anybody's ill will. Does that actually get (1) through (9)? Because it seems to make (5) and (8) come out wrong, and if I bolt on "and someone has to be at fault" I lose (4), (6), and (7). Is there a version that keeps (1), (2), (3), (4), (6), (7) and (9) while still getting (5) and (8) right, or do I have to give one of them up? And please tell me what your version predicts for a case I haven't listed: a patient who's been dismissed so many times that she now shows up with printouts and a rehearsed script and a rehearsed calm, and the doctor reads the rehearsed calm as anxiety and the printouts as internet hypochondria, so the very thing she does to be believed is what costs her. Which of my verdicts would you throw out, not just which one the principle can't reach — I'd rather lose a verdict than keep a clause that only exists to save it.
