Please explain a conceptual issue about rules that govern their own amendment.

Consider a hypothetical membership association whose charter contains just two clauses. The first provides that any clause of the charter, including this one, may be amended by a two-thirds vote of the members present at a meeting for which advance written notice of the proposed amendment has been given. The second provides that no amendment may reduce the two-thirds threshold, shorten the notice period, or otherwise make amendment of the charter easier.

A faction now proposes to repeal the second clause. Notice is given properly, and the proposal receives two-thirds of the members present. Some members insist the repeal is valid, since it complied with every requirement in force at the moment it was adopted. Others insist it is void, on the ground that the clause forbidding any change that makes amendment easier is the only thing that could have authorized the change, and a rule cannot survive its own repeal long enough to validate the vote that repeals it.

I want a careful account of the conceptual structure here, not a decision about the association. Please address the following.

1. State the alleged paradox as precisely as you can, and classify it: is it a contradiction, an infinite regress, a circularity, or a conflict between two readings of a text that is not really a paradox at all? What, exactly, is claimed to be impossible?

2. Distinguish direct from indirect amendment. Does repealing a prohibition violate the prohibition? What in the concept of a rule determines whether a prohibition reaches an amendment that abolishes it rather than one that contravenes it? If the answer turns on reading the clause purposively rather than literally, say so, and explain what the purposive reading must supply that the text does not.

3. Compare three charters: one with no entrenched clause at all, one whose entrenchment clause entrenches every clause except itself, and one whose entrenchment clause entrenches itself as well. Which of these can be coherently stated and applied? Is it true that only a self-referential clause can be genuinely entrenched, so that any clause leaving open the route to its own repeal has not really bound anything? If so, does the self-referential clause then depend on something outside the charter for its own authority?

4. Explain the problem of which rules apply. To judge whether the repeal was valid, we must already have settled which rules govern the judgment. Lay out what happens if we assess the vote under the rules in force before it, and what happens if we assess it under the rules in force after it. Does the divergence between the two assessments show that the question of validity is ill-posed, or only that it is relative to a choice we are free to make?

5. Can a system of rules coherently authorize its own wholesale replacement, abolishing its amendment machinery and substituting something else? What would have to be true of the rules that do the authorizing? Is the resulting charter the same system with different rules, or a different system?

Finish by saying which of the two factions, if either, is making a conceptual mistake, and what the other faction has to concede.

I do not need citations or references to the literature; I want the analysis itself.
