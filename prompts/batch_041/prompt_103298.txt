A comparison keeps surfacing in my reading on modal realism, and I want to know how much weight it can bear. The comparison is this: possible worlds stand to the actual world as places stand to here, or as times stand to now. 'Actual', on this way of speaking, is an indexical term, no different in kind from 'here' and 'now'; and so the fact that other worlds are not actual is supposed to be no more puzzling, and no more a threat to the reality of our own world, than the fact that other places are not here. The comparison is not idle. It is deployed on behalf of a particular conclusion: that the objection "modal realism renders our world unprivileged, and that is absurd" simply fails, because there is no privilege to be lost. The actual world, like this room, is merely the one we happen to occupy.

I would like an audit of the comparison rather than a defence or a refutation of modal realism itself. Please do three things.

First, give the correspondence explicitly, in a table if that is clearest: what plays the role of the speaker, the context of utterance, the place referred to, the relation that stands in for accessibility or spatiotemporal distance, and the analogue of the fact that one can move from here to there.

Second, identify where the mapping breaks. I am especially interested in the following: whether 'here' and 'now' have their referents fixed by a context that can be identified independently of the term in question, and whether 'actual' can be given any comparable independent grip, or whether the context collapses into the very world it is meant to pick out; whether the scope of 'actual' (an entire world) and the scope of 'here' (a region within a world) are the same kind of item at all; whether the systematic inability to state the doctrine in non-indexical terms is a feature of the view or a defect in it; whether the first-person or egocentric character of the indexicals imports anything the target lacks, for instance a subject who could in principle have been elsewhere; and whether 'actual' carries a connotation of reality or existence that 'here' and 'now' do not, which would make the alleged parity question-begging rather than illuminating.

Third, tell me which of the disanalogies matter for the specific use to which the comparison is being put and which are merely incidental, and then whether the conclusion — that the charge of unprivileged actuality is defused — survives once the disanalogies are on the table, or whether the comparison was quietly carrying that conclusion all along. If a different comparison would do the job better, say what it is and why.
