I work in environmental economics, and there's a pattern in modelling practice I keep running into that I don't have a word for. A model of a fishery assumes that every boat knows next season's prices. Nobody believes this. But when you relax the assumption, the interesting result doesn't just shift — it evaporates: the boom-and-bust dynamics give way to a smooth path, and there's nothing left to explain. So the false assumption isn't scaffolding that could be swapped for something truer; it is what generates the phenomenon the model exists to explain. And yet the same paper goes on to recommend a quota in exactly the setting where the assumption is most plainly wrong.

Here is the contrast that puzzles me. In other papers a false assumption is treated as harmless, because the result survives when you perturb it, and everyone takes that survival as the mark of a good model. So we have two assumptions, both admittedly false, one handled as innocuous and the other as indispensable — and it's the indispensable one that gets used for policy. Is there an established name for the first kind of assumption, or for the practice of building on it, and how does it differ from idealization, from abstraction, and from the old line that assumptions needn't be realistic so long as predictions hold? I care less about the label than about the carving: what makes an assumption load-bearing in this sense, whether that is a vice or a legitimate modelling move, and whether there is any principled way to tell when a result that depends on such an assumption may still be exported to the world.
