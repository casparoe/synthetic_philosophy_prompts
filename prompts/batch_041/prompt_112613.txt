Okay so this is a slightly odd ask and I've been putting off writing it for about a week. My oldest friend in Germany — I've lived here twelve years, she's half the reason I stayed — has finally done the thing she's been talking about for a decade and formally left the church. You go to the Standesamt, you pay a little fee, you sign a bit of paper, and that's it, you're out, and the state stops collecting the church tax off you, which I think is 8 or 9 percent of your income tax depending on the Land. She's throwing a dinner to mark it. Twenty-ish people. And she's asked me to give the toast.

Here's my problem. The room is a minefield. Her mother and her aunt are at one end of the table, proper Bavarian Catholics, mass every Sunday, and they love me but they're hurt, and if I make one joke that sounds like the church is a con or their faith a quaint old habit I lose them, and probably the friendship. At the other end her colleague Klaus teaches church law and will visibly flinch if I get any of the constitutional stuff wrong. Her two kids (17 and 20) grew up entirely outside all this and think the whole thing is a paperwork joke. And three or four people who left years ago will cheer if I say anything that sounds like a liberation.

What I want to say, roughly, is that this isn't really about the money and isn't really about belief either, and that leaving the church here is a stranger act than people think — because the church isn't just a building you stop going to, it's a public corporation and the state is its tax collector, so you can resign from it the way you resign from a club, except you can't, except you can. And I want to say something true about the difference between that and where I'm from, England, where the church is actually established, bishops in the Lords, the king at its head, and yet there's no tax to escape and nothing to resign from; you can stop believing and the arrangement simply doesn't notice. Which is somehow both more and less than what she just did. And France is a third thing again. I definately don't want to lecture anyone about laïcité over pudding though.

So: write me the actual toast, about five minutes spoken, funny in at least two places and not at anyone's expense, warm, and honest. It has to be sayable by a novelist who is not a philosopher and not a lawyer and doesn't want to pretend to be either. And it has to mean something to her mother. That last one is the hard constraint — the whole thing fails if the aunt has to look down at her plate.
