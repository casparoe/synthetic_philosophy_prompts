I'm a software engineer with no formal training in philosophy, and I've been trying to set down, as an explicit argument, a worry about democratic legitimacy that I can't shake. I've been going in circles on it, so I'd like someone to tell me which step to reject and what rejecting it costs.

First, three terms, so that we aren't talking past each other:

- A decision *binds* a person when the state will enforce its outcome against them whether or not they consent, so that going along with it is not optional for them.
- The *demos* for a decision is the set of people entitled to participate in making it.
- A *boundary decision* is a decision — a constitution, a naturalization law, an annexation, a ratification rule — that fixes who is in the demos for some range of downstream decisions.

The argument:

(1) A person has a standing claim to participate in a decision if and only if that decision binds them.
(2) A boundary decision binds people it excludes: they end up governed by the outcomes of a long series of downstream decisions in whose making they had no part, and the state will enforce those outcomes against them.
(3) Therefore people excluded by a boundary decision have a standing claim to participate in the very decision that excludes them.
(4) A decision is legitimate only if everyone who has a standing claim to participate in it actually does participate in it.
(5) A boundary decision binds everyone who is bound by any downstream decision it governs, because it determines who will make those decisions and therefore what they will be.
(6) The people bound by the downstream decisions of a modern state include, at a minimum, everyone within its territory, many people outside it (trade, war, emissions, capital flows), and people not yet born (debt, climate, entrenched constitutional rules).
(7) So a legitimate boundary decision would have to include in its demos everyone in the territory, everyone abroad who is affected, and the unborn.
(8) That demos cannot exist: the unborn cannot vote, and a global demos cannot make the ordinary day-to-day decisions a state makes.
(9) So no boundary decision is legitimate.
(10) Every decision a state makes is made under a boundary fixed by some boundary decision, and a decision made under a boundary that was never legitimately fixed inherits the defect.
(11) So no state decision is legitimate — not the ones I voted in, not even the ones that bind me and that I would have chosen anyway.
(12) But some state decisions are legitimate, and I cannot accept (11).

I would rather not give up (1), since it is the only reason I find democracy attractive rather than merely convenient, and I would rather not give up (12), which seems to me obvious. So I'm looking for a flaw in the middle — or for a principled reason to accept that the middle is sound and pay the price at the end.

Two things I've already tried, in case they're relevant:

- In distributed systems there's a standard trick for changing the set of servers that constitute a cluster: you don't let the old membership or the new membership decide alone; you require a joint configuration in which both must agree during the transition. I keep wondering whether a boundary decision could be legitimate in the same way, by being ratified jointly by the demos that is and the demos that would be. But the demos that would be has to be identified before it exists, which looks like the same problem one level up.
- I've tried weakening (1) so that people have a claim to have their interests considered rather than a claim to participate. But then I can't see what work the claim does, since every government already claims to consider everyone's interests.

Please tell me which step or transition to reject, why it looked fine to me when I wrote it, and what the standard ways of rejecting it each cost — especially any cost that lands on (1) or on (12), since those are the two I'd like to keep.
