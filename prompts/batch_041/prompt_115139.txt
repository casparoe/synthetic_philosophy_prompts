For several years now I have been trying to find out whether a particular structure has a name, and I have not managed to settle the question for myself. What I want is the carving rather than the label, so let me set out the structure, give the cases that puzzle me, and say what I have already looked at and found wanting.

The structure is this. Something I want is offered to me on condition that I genuinely intend to do something unpleasant later on. The offer is honoured the moment the intention exists, before the unpleasant thing has been done. The offer was my whole reason for being willing to do the unpleasant thing, so once I have it the reason for the act has evaporated, while the intention, if it was genuine, is supposed to carry me into the act all the same. In the standard version the offeror is a perfect judge of intentions; in some variants he is not, and must take my word or wait for my conduct.

Four cases:

1. A stranger offers me a large sum, payable at once, if I can sincerely form the intention of spending the coming year nursing a relative abroad. I want the money very much. If I can get myself to intend the year, the money is mine; and then the year looks pointless, unless the mere fact of having intended it is itself a reason to go.

2. The same offer, except that the money is to be paid to my sister rather than to me. The year abroad is still mine to spend and still wretched. Does the structure survive when the benefit lands on someone else, or is the whole interest of the case that the agent is buying his own advantage?

3. The benefit is not money but information. A specialist will tell me whether I am genuinely committed to a punishing course of treatment, but will do so only if I first form the intention of completing it. What I want is the verdict; the treatment is the price. Is that the same structure, or a different one wearing its clothes?

4. I discover that I can produce the required intention indirectly: by hypnosis, by a drug that makes me unusually suggestible, by a vow taken while drunk. Does that dissolve the difficulty, and if so, does it show that what was interesting all along was not the economics of the case but the fact that intentions cannot be adopted at will?

The reply I am given whenever I raise this is that if I abandon the plan once the money is banked, the intention cannot have been genuine, so nothing follows about what I now have reason to do. That seems to me to turn the case into a question about my sincerity at an earlier moment, and I do not see how a verdict on my past sincerity can supply a reason now.

Three things I have found that are close but, I think, not the thing itself. The first is the familiar business of binding oneself in advance, as the sailor has himself bound to the mast so that he cannot act on the longing the singing will produce: there the agent has a reason to act later and arranges to be unable to act on it, whereas in my cases the agent's later trouble is that he has no reason left at all. The second is promising: if I have promised my sister to go, then the promise is a reason to go after the money has been paid. But in my cases nobody has promised anything and there is nobody to be answerable to; everything hangs on what an intention can do by itself. The third is the suggestion that one might believe a proposition because it would be agreeable to believe it, which strikes me as the same difficulty displaced from action to belief.

I should add some further cases that I cannot classify at all and would like placed: where the thing to be intended is something I would have wanted to do even without the reward; where the offer is made repeatedly, so that a reputation for carrying through becomes valuable in itself; where the condition is on my believing that I shall act rather than on my intending to; where what I must intend is to refrain from something rather than to do it; and where the benefit was paid some time ago, in reliance on a prediction that I would form the intention, so that it is already spent.

What I want from you is therefore not a name to file all this under. I want to know whether there is an established name, and if there is, precisely what it covers; if the established name covers only the first sort of case, what the wider family should be called and how you would define it; which feature is doing the real work, whether the unobservability of the intention, the way the benefit destroys the reason for the act, or the impossibility of intending on demand; and which of the cases above belong to the structure and which merely resemble it. Where a case falls outside, I want to know what disqualifies it.
