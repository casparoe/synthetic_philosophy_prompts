I've been going back and forth with my roommate about jaywalking and I want a philosopher's take rather than another round of us both just asserting things. Here's the practice, as concretely as I can put it: in basically every American city, crossing the street mid-block or against the light when there's no traffic is technically illegal, but everyone does it, cops almost never enforce it, and nobody -- not even the person who'd never dream of running a red light in a car -- thinks you've done anything morally wrong. The same guy who'd call a driver reckless for blowing a stop sign will step off the curb into an empty street against the signal without a second thought, and if you made him wait for the light he'd feel like a sucker. Meanwhile the pedestrian who *does* wait, obediently, at an empty crossing at 2am, gets treated as slightly pathetic or foreign. So what I want from you: put on your philosopher-of-law / social-norms hat, think carefully, and tell me what this practice presupposes. Does it quietly assume the traffic rule is a mere coordination convention -- like which side of the road to drive on -- that lapses the moment it stops serving its purpose, so the real obligation is just a background duty not to endanger anyone and the signal is a crude proxy for it? Or does it presuppose something stronger, that the law does bind but gets outweighed, or that there's a genuine moral norm of obeying signals and we're all just hypocrites about it? Brainstorm the rival views first, then reconstruct the implicit theory, and be careful to seperate what the practice *strictly requires* you to believe from what it merely fits comfortably with -- my hunch is it's underdetermined between several theories and I want to know which ones.

Two extra things to fold in. First, I read somewhere that the whole concept of "jaywalking" was basically manufactured in the 1910s and 20s by car and auto-club lobbyists as a way to push pedestrians off the streets and shift the blame for traffic deaths from drivers onto the people they hit. Assuming that's roughly right, does the *provenance* of a convention matter to whether it has any authority? If a norm was invented to serve a powerful interest, does that deflate its claim on us, or can a convention become binding regardless of the grubby reasons it started? Second, the visitor question: a friend from a country where pedestrians really do wait, and where jaywalking is genuinely looked down on, is coming to stay with me. Is she obligated to follow the local practice (cross when everyone else does), the local law (wait), or neither -- and does your answer to the first part even let you say? Lay out the positions fairly, don't pretend there's more consensus than there is, and tell me what would have to change in the practice for a different implicit theory to be the right one.
