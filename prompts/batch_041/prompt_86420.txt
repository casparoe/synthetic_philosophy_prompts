I'm taking a political philosophy course this term, and we've just finished the unit on Nozick's entitlement theory and the Wilt Chamberlain example. Alongside the primary text we were assigned a chapter from an accessible introduction to libertarian ideas, written by someone plainly sympathetic to Nozick, and it made the whole thing click for me in a way Nozick's own prose never did — which is exactly why I've grown suspicious: it was so tidy that it probably smoothed something over. I'd like you to audit it for accuracy. Here is my reconstruction of the chapter's four central claims, close to its wording but not verbatim. First, the entitlement theory says that a distribution is just when everyone is entitled to what they hold, and that you are entitled to what you have earned, since earning something through your own labor or through voluntary exchange is what makes it deservedly yours. Second, the theory is historical rather than end-state: what matters is how holdings came about, not the shape of the final distribution, which is why Nozick rejects patterned principles, patterns being principles that judge a distribution by its shape at a moment in time. Third, the Chamberlain case: begin with a distribution everyone would agree is just — say an equal one — and suppose a million fans each pay a quarter to watch him play, so that he ends up with $250,000 more and each fan has a quarter less. Every transfer was voluntary and the starting point was just by hypothesis, so the result is just; but it is no longer equal. To restore the pattern the state would have to keep interfering with what people do with their own money, forbidding the payment or taxing it back, so liberty will always end up upsetting any pattern. Fourth, therefore the argument shows that redistributive taxation is simply theft, that taxing someone's earnings is a form of forced labor, and that a consistent Nozickian state would not tax anyone at all.

What I want is a clause-by-clause audit rather than a general essay: for each of those four claims, tell me what a careful reader of Nozick would accept as accurate, what such a reader would wince at as an overstatement or as a conflation of two things Nozick keeps apart, and what is straightforwardly a misrepresentation. I'm particularly interested in whether the problems in the first and second claims matter for the conclusion in the fourth or are merely loose labeling — that is, if I repaired the labels, would the argument for the minimal state still go through as the chapter states it, or does the chapter need premises Nozick never asserted? And a second question, since I may have to defend this in seminar: suppose someone grants the Chamberlain case in full — agrees that the transfers were voluntary and that the resulting distribution is unequal — but denies that it establishes anything about the justice of redistribution. What are the strongest things such a person could say about what the example does and does not show? I would rather come away knowing where the real disagreements lie than with a verdict about who is right.
