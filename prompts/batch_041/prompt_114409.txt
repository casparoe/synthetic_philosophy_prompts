There's a small pot of money in my department for one-term research projects, the deadline snuck up on me, and I need a two-page proposal out of an idea that's still pretty rough. Can you write it?

The rough idea: everyone who writes about collective harm — climate, meat, voting, antibiotic resistance — ends up fighting the same fight. My one burger, my one flight, my one vote makes no difference to the outcome, so in what sense is it wrong? And the standard repairs all tinker with the causal claim: thresholds, imperceptible increments, tiny probability bumps, being part of a sufficient set, complicity, and so on. My hunch is that all of those assume the harm is a discrete bad outcome sitting at the end of a chain, and that for the cases that actually keep me up at night that's the wrong picture. The thing I'm damaging isnt an outcome, it's a buffer. There's slack in the system — a stock of fish, a reservoir of antibiotic susceptibility, an atmospheric budget, a bank's capital cushion, a norm's reserve of goodwill — and my contribution doesn't move the outcome much, it eats the slack. What changes isn't the probability of the bad event so much as the fragility of the system: it takes a smaller shock to tip it over. If that's right, then the moral unit might be something like "making a system less resilient," which is a contribution to a standing property of a system rather than to an event, and a lot of the standard machinery is looking in the wrong place.

Turn it into the proposal. What I need in it: (1) the question stated so that it has a determinate answer — my first pass would be: when a harm is better modelled as loss of buffer than as an outcome, does the inefficacy objection lose its force, and if so on what grounds? (2) why the existing answers leave it open, argued rather than surveyed — I'll add the citations myself, so dont bother with a reading list. (3) the method: what I'd actually do, week by week, over twelve weeks, with no lab and no data collection, just me and a library. (4) what would count as the project failing — I want it to be able to fail, and the failure conditions should be specific enough that a reader could tell afterwards whether it did. (5) an honest note on scope, since I teach and I've got one term.

Two things while you're at it. Pick one concrete case and stay with it — a fishery, a shared antibiotic, a bank's leverage ratio, whatever you like — rather than gesturing at "climate change," which everyone has heard a thousand times already; I'd rather the proposal turn on a real structural question than on a famous example. And at the end, outside the proposal, tell me straight: what would change your mind about the buffer framing being a genuine advance rather than a fancy relabelling of threshold-talk, and where is the argument most likely to break? If the honest answer is that it collapses back into the standard view, say so.
