I'm a backend dev with zero formal philosophy training, and I keep getting into the same dumb argument online: someone says Buddhism denies the self, someone else says no, it just says clinging to a self causes suffering, and we all go in circles. I want to build a decision tree / taxonomy I can actually follow, one that takes me from a vague gut feeling to a named position on the table, so I can stop repeating whatever I read last and figure out where I actually sit. Sort of a "which view of the self do you actually hold" flowchart, but built so each branch is a real question I can answer without already knowing which camp it dumps me in.

Here's the angle I care about. I don't want a tree whose only spine is "is there a self, yes or no" -- that just splits into Buddhism vs everyone else and I already know that one. The distinctions I want it to actually carve are: (a) is the no-self thing a claim about what exists, or a technique for reducing suffering that doesn't commit you to any metaphysics; (b) if there's no self, what carries the continuity -- karma, memory, a causal stream, nothing at all; (c) does the self exist in a lesser or conventional sense even if it's not ultimately real, and does that move actually help or is it a dodge; (d) is the "self" a thing, a process, a story, or a useful error. Those four axes are the ones I want the branches to turn on.

Please make it a tree (or a quiz with a scoring key, whatever fits) with the entry point being roughly "I think the self is something the brain/person does, not something they have" -- so I'm already partway in and the tree has to sort me from there, not from scratch.

Some specific stuff I want in it:

- Each branch node should be a concrete question or a tiny case, not a label. E.g. don't ask "are you a reductionist?", ask something like: if you replace one of the five aggregates at a time -- say memory goes first, then dispositions, then the body -- at what point, if any, does it stop being the same person, and is that a fact about the world or about how we talk?

- Use at least a couple of cases that aren't the usual teleporter/fission ones, since I've seen those a million times. Since I'm a dev, feel free to lean on engineering-flavored ones: a long-running process that gets restarted but keeps the same name; a git history where every commit is by a different author and there's no repo owner; a service with no central node, just a message log. The point is I want cases where the "no owner, only continuity" idea is intuitive, so the tree can test whether I really buy it or just like the sound of it.

- These positions have to show up somewhere as leaves: (1) there's a real, unchanging self/essence underneath it all; (2) persons are just the aggregates, full stop, self-talk is a convenient fiction; (3) the self exists conventionally but not ultimately -- two levels of truth, and the no-self teaching only bites at the deeper level; (4) the "middle" view where the person is genuinely real but is neither the same as nor different from the aggregates (I've seen this described as the view some early Buddhist school got condemned for, and I want to know if it's actually incoherent or just unpopular); (5) no-self is purely a practical strategy for loosening clinging, with no ontological commitment either way; (6) the self is a narrative/story the brain constructs, real as a story, not as a thing; (7) continuity with no bearer -- the causal stream/karma keeps going but there's literally nobody it happens to.

- For each leaf, give the name of the position if it has one, or write "unnamed" if nobody really defends it, plus one sentence on the most awkward thing that position has to swallow.

- Where the tree leaves combinations empty, say so and tell me whether that's because the combo is incoherent, just unattractive, or merely unexplored. And if two leaves come out behaviorally identical -- same answers to every question -- flag that the tree can't separate them instead of faking a distinction.

- Keep it to somewhere around 8-10 leaves and no more than maybe 12 branch questions, and please write the questions so I could hand the thing to a friend and they'd get sorted too.

One more thing, and this matters to me: I know some of this is genuinely settled and some of it is Buddhists and philosophers still arguing with each other. So when you lay it out, seperate the parts that people who actually know the topic agree on from the parts that are live disputes, and don't hand me a contested reading as if it's the consensus. If a leaf only exists because one scholar reads it that way, tell me that. I'd rather have the tree admit its soft spots than pretend the map is the territory.
