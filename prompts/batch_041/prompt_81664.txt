I'm putting together an anachronism exercise for a small metaphysics reading group, and I want to use Leibniz for it -- he practically invented possible-worlds talk, so he's fair game. The modern debate I want to drop on him is counterpart theory vs. transworld identity: when we say Leibniz could have been a shoemaker, is that literally the same Leibniz in another world, just with a different property, or is it a mere counterpart, a different individual who closely resembles him? Give me a verdict rather than a survey, argue it out of what he actually held about individual substance and complete concepts, and tell me where his own commitments would have to bend to make the answer come out cleanly. One wrinkle I want taken seriously: there's arguably a third option lurking in his system, namely that the complete concept pins down every predicate, so there's no sense at all in which Leibniz could have been a shoemaker. If that's right, where does it leave the other two views? And a friend keeps insisting that God's choice of the best world settles the whole thing before Leibniz even opens his mouth -- is that a real objection or a confusion?
