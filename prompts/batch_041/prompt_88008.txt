I'm a novelist — historical stuff, mostly, though the history is usually a frame I'm lying about — and I've got a question I keep half-answering and then losing. When I write the middle of a book, the part that has to make a reader feel why things went the way they did, I'm doing something that feels a lot like explaining. Then I read a real historian and think, no, thats something else, they've got evidence and I've got momentum. Except I can't actually say what the difference is, and I've started to suspect nobody can, which is either a relief or a disaster for me.

So before I read any more of it I want the spec, not the theory. What would any account of historical explanation have to get right to be worth having? And I'll fix the purpose, since I know adequacy is relative to something: I want something good enough that I can tell when my causal story is doing real explanatory work and when its just a shape that reads well.

Give me the list of requirements, and for each one the case or consideration that puts it on the list. I want these specifically handled, because they're the ones that actually bite me:

- the contrastive thing. Why the mill shut rather than limped along another decade — same facts, different question, and it seems like you get different explanations. Does the account have to allow that, and does it have to say which contrast is the right one?
- absences. A letter that never arrived, a warning nobody gave, a vote that didn't happen. Can something that didn't occur be part of the explanation of what did?
- scale. Explaining why the empire went under, and explaining one general's decision on one afternoon. Do those two have to agree with each other? What if the close-up story makes the big story look like a fiction?
- hindsight. A well-shaped story makes the outcome feel like it had to happen. Is that a feature of explanation or a bias the account has to guard against?
- rival stories. Two historians, same evidence, two narratives that can't both be true, both defensible. Does a good account of explanation have to pick a winner, or is that the theory failing rather than the historians?
- the law thing. Whatever history has going for it, its not laws the way physics has laws. The account can't quietly need them.
- the deduction worry. You can deduce the flagpole's height from the length of its shadow, and nobody thinks the shadow explains the pole. So whats missing there, and does the account have to say?
- order. Does the order of the telling carry explanatory weight, or is it packaging? If order matters, what does a badly ordered explanation get wrong?
- significance. What makes a detail part of the explanation and not just atmosphere? This one is maybe the most important to me and the one I never see anybody address.

Then mark which requirements are non-negotiable and which are just preferences of one camp. Flag any pair you think can't both be met — I suspect theres at least one, something like the demand that the explanation be checkable by someone who has the evidence but not the historian's imagination, set against the demand that it capture the particular, once-only, unfolding character of the thing. If a pair can't be satisfied together, say so plainly, because thats the part I most want and I'd rather have a shorter list that holds.

You can spend a couple of lines at the end on how the covering-law picture and the narrative-is-itself-an-explanation people come out against the list, but keep it short, I care way more about the list. No reading list please, and no citations, I'm not going to go read Hempel, I want the spec.

Last line, exactly this form and nothing after it: \answer{...} — and inside the braces put the name of the one requirement that knocks out the most candidates, in as few words as you can.
