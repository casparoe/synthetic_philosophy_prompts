I keep getting pulled into an argument about historical explanation and I genuinely can't tell if it's a real disagreement or two sides talking past each other. The setup, as I understand it: the covering-law model says something is an explanation only if what's being explained can be derived from a law plus initial conditions, so history explains just to the extent that it's got laws behind it (usually unstated, usually trivial). The narrative crowd said no — historians explain by telling a story, by arranging events so the outcome becomes intelligible, and no amount of law-talk captures that. The famous embarrassments are all on the table (the pole and its shadow, the barometer and the storm), the standard replies are all on the table too (those aren't the relevant laws; the law is there but elliptical; you've confused explaining with justifying), and I've never once seen either side give ground. It's supposedly a dead debate, except it keeps coming back under new names — causal modelling, mechanism-talk — making the same moves.

Heres my suspicion and I want you to test it rather than just nod along: they're answering differnt questions. The covering-law demand takes a plain why-question (why did this happen at all?) and wants a proposition that stands in a nomic relation to the thing explained. The narrative demand takes a contrastive why-question (why this outcome rather than one of the other ones that were equally live at the time?) and wants a selection and ordering of events that makes one outcome stand out as the salient one. If thats right, a big chunk of the fight is about which question history is even in the business of answering, which isn't a logical dispute at all. I also suspect the narrative side is at least three claims bundled together under one word — narrative as mere style of presentation, narrative as the claim that chronology and connective tissue do real explanatory work, and narrative as the claim that events don't have their identities apart from the story they sit in — and that covering-law people keep knocking down the weakest of the three and declaring victory.

So diagnose it. Verbal dispute, dispute about the aims of a discipline, hidden empirical dispute about whether history has laws at all, or several disputes wearing one name? Use what each side actually says, tell me which of the sub-disagreements are real and which are artifacts of the packaging, and tell me what progress would even look like — a distinction that dissolves the thing, a concession someone could actually make, or a reason to think it's a dead end and we should all stop.
