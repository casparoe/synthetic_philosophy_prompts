Next June I am to give the commencement address at the small liberal arts college where I have taught for twenty years, and I would like you to write it. I am a tranquilist: the only thing that is intrinsically good is the absence of craving and suffering — craving here meaning the agitated, self-reproducing wanting to be in a state one is not in, as opposed to mere preference or direction — while delight, achievement, and love are at most useful and never good in themselves. The audience will be graduating students and their families, most of them expecting a speech about ambition and possibility, along with a few of my colleagues who will catch any lapse into a view I do not hold. Write the address: warm, about four minutes, and genuinely meant for the people in the room. It must honor the occasion without claiming that anything being celebrated is good in itself; it must not become a lecture; and it must not turn the absence of craving into one more thing to crave and achieve.
