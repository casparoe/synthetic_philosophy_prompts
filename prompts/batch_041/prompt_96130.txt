I don't need a line edit, I need to know whether it actually works.

It's a variant on the gradual replacement case, except I run it backwards. Standard version: you swap one neuron at a time for a silicon chip that does exactly the same job, connectivity and all, and at the end there's a silicon brain that remembers your childhood, loves your partner, hates your job, etc. Most people say the person survives, or at least that we can't tell when they stop; a few say the lights go out somewhere in the middle. Fine. My version: while you're doing the swaps you keep every organic neuron you pull out alive in a vat, in a maintenance solution, with its connections to the other removed neurons preserved — stipulate the tech is good enough that re-implanting them restores the original wiring, it's a thought experiment. So at the halfway mark you've got a half-silicon brain in the skull and a half-organic brain in the vat, and at the end a full silicon brain in the skull and a full organic brain in the vat. Now run it in reverse: put the organic neurons back, one at a time, in the reverse order. When it's done you've got the original neurons, in the original skull, wired the way they were. Here's my claim. If you think the person survives the forward process — which is what the psychological-continuity view says, since the causal chain is never broken — then you have to say they survive the reverse process, since it's the same chain traversed the other way. But then during the reverse process the person is also, by that same reasoning, the silicon brain sitting in the skull; and the person is also, by the end, the brain we've rebuilt. So somewhere in the reverse run identity has to jump out of the silicon and back into the organic stuff, even though every step of it is the mirror image of a step in the forward run where no jump happened. I want to conclude that the continuity view can't be right, or at least can't give a direction-independent answer, and that these cases only look determinate because we always narrate them from the start forward.

What I want from you: (1) is there a hole in this? The reply I'm most worried about is that the vat neurons were causally inert during the forward process, so the reverse run is really a case of two candidates for identity rather than one, and the jump I'm describing is just the mundane fact that a fresh stream starts when the organic brain is reactivated — but I can't tell whether that's a principled move or just a way of stipulating that identity follows whichever causal stream is currently running, which is the thing I'm trying to put pressure on. (2) am I misrepresenting the position I'm attacking — is the continuity view really committed to the forward verdict, or do its defenders already have a story about frozen duplicates that quietly handles this? (3) honsetly, is this interesting, or is it just the branching problem in a lab coat? If it's the latter, say so, I'd rather find out now. (4) any suggestions for tightening it, and a title that doesn't sound like a term paper.
