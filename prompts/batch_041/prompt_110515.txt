I have no academic training in ethics, but I have been trying to work out for myself when the everyday conventions of a place—how to tip, how to queue, when to cross the street, where to stand on an escalator—are things I am actually obligated to follow, and especially when they bind a visitor who did not grow up with them. I have a set of gut verdicts, and I would like to know what principle, if any, captures them. I suspect the answer will have to distinguish conventions that are load-bearing, in the sense that they allocate some burden or benefit among people, from conventions that are more ornamental, but I am not sure that distinction survives the cases.

Here are my verdicts. In a country where tipping is the norm, I am a visitor and leave no tip after ordinary service. I think that is wrong, though somewhat less wrong than if I were a resident. In a country where tipping is not the norm and may even be awkward or insulting, I am a visitor and leave a large tip. I do not think that is wrong, exactly—it may be gauche, but it is not a moral failure. In a place where people queue in a strict line, I am a visitor and accidentally join by standing at the side; someone points to the back, and I go. I think I did nothing wrong, but I would have been wrong to refuse to move. If I knowingly cut the line because I was in a hurry, I think that is wrong, and not much less wrong because I am a visitor. In a city where jaywalking is common and unenforced, I cross against the light with everyone else; I think that is fine. In a city where jaywalking is genuinely frowned on, I cross against the light as a visitor; I think that is wrong, even if no one is harmed and no one is even likely to see me. In a place where people stand on the right and walk on the left of an escalator, I stand on the left with a suitcase. I think that is wrong, but mainly because I am blocking people, not because I have violated a rule as such. In a home where guests are expected to remove their shoes, I keep mine on. I think that is wrong, and more clearly wrong than the escalator case. At a market where haggling is expected, I pay the first price. I think that is not wrong, just foolish. At a communal kitchen where no rule is posted but everyone cleans up after themselves, I leave my dishes. I think that is wrong, even though there is no sign. Finally, in a country where small payments to officials are customary for permits, I pay one. I think that is wrong even though it is the local convention, because it harms people who cannot pay or who are shut out. But I do not think a visitor is obligated to adopt every local practice that locals themselves treat as normal; something about the payment case seems different from the shoe case, and I cannot say exactly what.

What I want is a principle, or a small set of candidate principles, that makes sense of these verdicts. Please state it precisely, with the case that motivates each clause, and test it against the cases above, including the ones where I am unsure. Tell me which of my verdicts it forces me to give up. I am particularly interested in whether the load-bearing versus ornamental distinction can be stated precisely enough to do the work, and if not, what should replace it. I would also like it to say what it predicts for cases I have not listed: a visitor who speaks a different language and cannot read a posted rule; a local who violates the same convention in front of a visitor; a convention that is offensive to the visitor's own moral or religious convictions; a convention that everyone follows but no one can explain; and a case where the visitor's breach is harmless in itself but would become harmful if many visitors did it. Finally, please be honest about whether the principle is doing real work or whether it is just a restatement of my verdicts, and whether the visitor's status should matter at all, or whether it is a distraction from the underlying question of what the convention is for.
