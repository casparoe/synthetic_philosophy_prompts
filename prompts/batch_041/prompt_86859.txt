I am a novelist, and for the better part of a year I have been trying to settle a practical grievance by way of theory, which I suspect is a mistake and cannot stop making. Two readers of my most recent book have produced readings I am fairly sure were not in my mind when I wrote it, and I cannot decide whether I am entitled to call them wrong. One finds, in a minor character's habit of mispronouncing the name of her home town, an allegory of the immigrant's relation to language; I gave her the mispronunciation because a friend of mine talks that way and it made me smile, and until I read the essay I had not known the town's name could be mispronounced in two directions at all. The other holds that my last chapter is an act of cruelty toward the heroine, when I meant it as the only mercy I could imagine for her, and I have to concede that the cruelty is on the page whether or not it was in my head. The reverse has happened too: a reviewer once pointed out a recurrence of water in the book — rivers, baths, a flooded cellar — that I had not deliberately arranged, that I now think is the best thing in it, and that I would refuse to give up. There is also a translator who has made choices I would not have made and in one sentence made the book better, an editor who cut a paragraph I now believe was load-bearing, and a first ending that I replaced, so that I no longer know which of the two I am supposed to be defending.

What I want from you is therefore not a verdict on my two readers, and emphatically not a reading list or a set of names to look up. I want the specification first: what must any theory of what a text means, or of how it is rightly interpreted, be able to deliver before someone in my position should take it seriously? Set out the requirements with the case behind each — the cases the theory has to get right, the distinctions it cannot be permitted to collapse (I am suspicious in particular of any account that runs together what a work means with how good it is, or that treats an author's intention as evidence of meaning and as the standard of meaning interchangeably), the facts about how writing actually goes that it must not contradict, and the question it must answer about what its own object is, since I have two endings and a translator and cannot tell which of them belongs to the work. Then tell me which of those requirements are genuine and which are merely the prejudices of one camp in the old quarrel between those who say the author's intention is what the text means and those who say consulting it is a mistake. I assume I cannot have everything, so name the pair or pairs of requirements you think cannot be jointly satisfied, and say which single requirement does the most to eliminate the theories currently on offer. I care about this for more than vanity: I am beginning a novel in which a character is posthumously interpreted, and I would like to know what my characters are entitled to. Write it as continuous prose, please — no headings, no numbered lists, no bold, no tables; I read this sort of thing the way I read letters, and the shape of the argument should come from the sentences.
