I'm an undergraduate in a science-and-technology-studies seminar on the Anthropocene, and for our term project I've been paired with a geology student who sits on the opposite side of essentially every question we discuss. The argument we keep having is not really "when did it start" — we have both stopped pretending there is a single date either of us would defend — but something more structural. My partner holds the formalist line: if the Anthropocene is to be an epoch at all, it must be earned the way every other unit of the time scale is earned, by a single point in a single reference section, with a primary marker that is synchronous worldwide, and it must be ratified by the appropriate stratigraphic body; if the human transformation cannot supply that, the honest conclusion is that it is not an epoch, and the word should be retired from the time scale and left to the humanities. I hold the opposite view: that the thing the word names — the global reorganization of Earth systems by human activity — is intrinsically diachronous, beginning at different moments in different places and propagating unevenly, so that demanding one synchronous point mistakes a process for an instant; and that any boundary we draw will encode a judgment about whose activity counts and when, which makes the question "who decides" not an embarrassment but the heart of the matter.

We want to do what our professor keeps urging and produce the document our disagreement actually licenses, rather than two more position papers. So please draft the joint statement of an adversarial collaboration between these two camps — call them the stratigraphic formalists and the historical diachronists, or whatever labels make the positions recognizable — such that each of us could put our name to it. I would like a numbered section of claims we can both sign, covering what the Anthropocene is supposed to be for, what a definition is doing, what the competing markers actually show, and what neither of us is claiming; then the residual disagreements stated as precisely as possible, each written in wording its own side would accept; then the cruxes — the sub-questions where settling the matter would actually move one of us. I suspect one crux is whether the synchrony requirement is a genuine constraint of chronostratigraphy or merely a convention of the golden-spike procedure, and another is whether the definitional act is descriptive or performative. For each crux, give a case, argument, or thought experiment that both sides would agree bears on it, with each side's prediction recorded in advance.

Two concrete threads we have been circling, which I would like folded in. My partner floated the analogy with diachronous, or time-transgressive, boundaries in lithostratigraphy — objects that seem to be perfectly respectable geologically even though they are not synchronous — and I cannot tell whether that analogy rescues the diachronist or proves too much. Separately, I have been arguing that the mid-twentieth-century radionuclide signal is a suspiciously convenient marker precisely because it is a byproduct of weapons testing rather than of the human transformation of the Earth system itself, and I think my partner should either absorb that point or answer it. Please make the agreed-claims section non-vacuous — I do not want a list of truisms we could both shrug at — and where you conclude that part of our fight is verbal rather than substantive, say which part and why.
