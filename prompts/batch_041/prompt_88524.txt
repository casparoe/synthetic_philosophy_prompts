I'm on the student conduct appeals panel at a mid-sized state university. I'm not a philosopher, just a person who got drafted into this and has been reading about free will at night, so forgive me if I mangle the terminology. We have a case Thursday that has split the panel, and I have to circulate the written opinion before the meeting. A junior, call her R, submitted a paper that was mostly lifted from an online source. She admitted it right away, so the finding isn't in dispute. The standard sanction is a one-semester suspension. She's appealing the sanction. Her statement says she was raised in a chaotic home, is first-gen, had untreated ADHD, and never got the chance to become the kind of person who wouldn't cheat; her impulses and character were shaped by things she didn't choose. One panel member keeps bringing up Galen Strawson's basic argument, the one that says you can't be ultimately responsible for what you do because you would have to have chosen the character and reasons that led to the act, and that's impossible since it just pushes the choice back and back. He says we should drop the suspension entirely. Another member says that's a reductio, since it would abolish all responsibility, and we should just uphold the sanction and stop philosophizing.

I think both are missing something but I can't articulate it, and I've been thinking about it alot. I've been trying out a homemade distinction between blame-responsibility, which is about deserving blame or punishment because you are ultimately the author of your act, and answerability-responsibility, which is about being the person who has to answer for the act and take steps going forward, even if you aren't the ultimate author of your character. I also made up a thought experiment: two students with identical brains at the moment of cheating, one raised in stability and one in chaos. If the only difference is history, does that make one more blameworthy? And if it doesn't, what do we do with the fact that we still have to run a university? Please draft the short written opinion I'll circulate. It should recommend a concrete disposition, uphold, reduce, or replace the sanction, with a specific time frame and any conditions, and give the reasoning in a way a non-philosopher panel can follow. It should engage the Strawson point seriously, including the strongest thing to be said for the panel member who wants to drop all sanctions, and explain why we might still hold R answerable even if we doubt ultimate responsibility. Also say what, if anything, the panel should do about the general policy, not just this case, because we have three similar appeals next month and I don't want to wing it every time.
