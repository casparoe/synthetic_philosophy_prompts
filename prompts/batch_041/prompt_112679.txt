I'm a second-year PhD student working on early modern philosophy and the history of science, and for the last few weeks I've been arguing over dinner with a friend who does historical theology about how to read Scripture when it appears to conflict with established science. Somewhat to my surprise, I've found myself defending a position close to the one Galileo sets out in his letter to the Grand Duchess Christina of 1615, and I want to know how much it actually risks. Stripped to its bones, the position is this: Scripture is accommodated to the capacities and idioms of the audience it was first addressed to, and its purpose is to teach what is necessary for salvation rather than to deliver a natural philosophy; consequently, when a passage read in its plain sense collides with a conclusion that has been genuinely demonstrated about the natural world, it is the reading of the passage, and not the demonstrated conclusion, that must give way. I take the word 'demonstrated' seriously — I am not claiming that any fashionable scientific view gets to rewrite the sacred text, only conclusions that have been established rather than merely conjectured. I hold the view because the alternatives look worse to me: giving it up seems to leave only a choice between reading the Bible as a physics textbook and treating its natural claims as so much dead metaphor.

What I want from you is the case against it, and then an honest accounting. Play critic first: which objections should actually move me? I am especially interested in three I can't settle myself — first, the charge that the principle is question-begging, since deciding which passages are 'accommodated' already requires knowing what the science says, so it can only ever ratify a conclusion I have reached on other grounds; second, the cases where the natural claim is so entangled with the soteriological one that the two cannot be separated — the sun standing still over Gibeon is not merely an astronomical remark but part of how God is said to fight for Israel — and whether the principle has anything to say when the physics and the promise turn out to be the same sentence; and third, whether the principle is unfalsifiable, since any apparent conflict can be dissolved by reinterpreting the offending passage, so that no possible discovery could ever count against it. Sort these, and any others you think stronger, from decisive to merely awkward, tell me which ones a committed defender should concede rather than dismiss, and say what the principle predicts that its rivals do not. Then give me your verdict, and give it as a verdict: if the honest answer is that the principle has been defended into vacuity, say so plainly, and tell me whether that is a fatal objection to a principle of this kind or simply the price of any view that tries to mediate between two authorities.
