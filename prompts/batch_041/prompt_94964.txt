You are a philosopher specializing in the ethics of truth-telling and in Augustine's two treatises on lying. Think this through carefully before you write, and begin by setting out for yourself, in your own notes, what each of the two criteria below actually commits its holder to.

A friend of mine and I have been arguing for three evenings straight. He has adopted what he calls the strict Augustinian line, and he holds it as a package: first, that lying is never permitted, with no exception for protecting anyone from harm and none for kindness; second, that a lie is not a matter of false words at all but of a divided mind, so that a person who says what is false while believing it true does not lie, and a person who says what is true while taking it to be false, intending to mislead, does. He treats the two claims as independent. I think the second one makes the first land in places he has not noticed, and I want a case that puts pressure on exactly that joint.

Build the case to my specifications. One realistic scenario, no longer than a paragraph: no murderer at the door, no courtroom, no deathbed, no hypothetical machinery. In it, two people in comparable situations give the same answer to the same question, and that answer produces the same belief in whoever hears it; the only things that differ are whether the sentence is true in each case and what each speaker takes to be true. Arrange it so that the speaker whose words are true, but who utters them taking them to be false and intending to mislead, comes out as a liar on my friend's second claim, while the speaker whose words are false, but who utters them in the honest belief that they are true, does not; and so that a cruder criterion, on which a lie is simply an utterance that is false, yields exactly the reverse verdicts. Both answers must serve a benign or protective end, so that the absolutism cannot be defused by calling the case trivial. And the verdicts must fall out of the criteria rather than being stipulated by the setup: if the case works only because you told me it does, it has failed, and you should say so.

Then: state what each criterion says about each speaker and why; identify the single feature of the case that is doing the work; say what happens to the case if that feature is removed; and then defend the Augustinian as hard as you can — is the verdict on the honest speaker a reductio of the package, a tolerable cost, or evidence that my friend has mis-stated the criterion? End by saying which claim in the package you think has to give way, and why.
