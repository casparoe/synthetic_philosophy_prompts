An argument I've been trying to defeat keeps winning. (1) A proposition — say, that the cat is on the mat — is a complex whose constituents (the cat, the mat, and the relation of being on) are genuinely combined into a single thing, and it is this combination that lets the proposition as a whole be true or false. (2) Whenever several items are combined into one complex, there must be something that does the combining: a further item, distinct from the items combined, that relates them. (3) That combiner is itself an item, so it in turn must be combined with the items it combines, which requires a further combiner, and so on without end. (4) Since the series of combiners is never completed, the combination is never achieved. (5) So there are no propositions — and, by the same reasoning, no complexes of any kind, since every complex would need a completed series of combiners. (6) But there plainly are propositions and other complexes, so one of (1)–(4) must be false. I would most like to keep (1); tell me which of the others to give up and what giving it up costs. Please be explicit about which parts of your diagnosis are settled and which are genuinely contested among people who work on this, rather than presenting one contested view as the consensus.
