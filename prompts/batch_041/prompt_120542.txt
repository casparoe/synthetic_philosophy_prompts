I want to practice defending the Mencian claim that human nature is good against the strongest version of Xunzi's position. Please play Xunzi — not a caricature of a dour authoritarian, but a systematic Confucian who believes ritual, teachers, and law are the only things standing between people and chaos, who has read Mencius carefully, and who attacks him at his strongest point rather than his weakest. Stay in character until I say stop. You may ask me a clarifying question about what I mean, but you must not slide into a neutral survey of both sides, and you must not concede the main thesis — at most, grant me a local point and then show why it does not cost you the argument. Write two short paragraphs per turn, no bullet lists, no naming modern commentators (you may refer to "later readers" if you need them), and refer to your own book by content rather than by chapter number.

Some of what I intend to press. You define xing as what is so by birth — in one standard translation, "What is so by birth is called xing" — and you hold that because following inborn desire unchecked produces conflict, that nature is e. Mencius uses the same word for something closer to the state or tendency a human being is meant to reach under good conditions, which is why he can admit that most people turn out badly, describing a hillside stripped bare by axes and grazing whose sprouts nonetheless keep trying to come back, without surrendering the claim that our nature is good. I want to argue that once the two senses of xing are separated, much of the dispute is not about the moral facts but about which sense deserves the name, and that your own analogies cut against you: the potter's hand and the frame that straightens warped wood are themselves inborn endowments of the human species, so artifice is natural in the only sense that matters.

Here is my opening move. You say the man in the street can become a Yu. I say that is Mencius's thesis in your vocabulary. If the capacity to be straightened is universal, if every human being can be made just by the right teaching and ritual, then by your own account the difference between the sage and the criminal is not a difference in nature but in what has been done to that nature — which is exactly what Mencius means when he says we are good by nature and lose it. So what does "nature is evil" add, beyond a harsh way of describing the untutored state that Mencius also grants? And if your answer is that the capacity is merely a capacity, that the clay is indifferent and only the potter's hand gives it shape, then explain why the potter's hand counts as outside human nature when the hand belongs to a human being.

Reply in two parts, in this order. First, a section titled "In character": two short paragraphs that address my opening move directly and end with one question back to me. Second, a section titled "Margin note": no more than three sentences, still in Xunzi's voice, naming the single place in your own case where you feel least secure. Then stop and wait for me.
