She used to be a conservator, so she's coming at this from the bench rather than from the literature, and she insists we should stop using "authenticity" as one thing and split it into (a) ontological authenticity — the object is what it purports to be, same maker, same materials, same date — and (b) biographical authenticity — the object has had the history it purports to have, the right chain of hands, the right trajectory through the world. Her view is that once you seperate them, most of the disputes people have about originals are really disputes about (b) dressed up as disputes about (a), and that this shows up most clearly in three cases she lumps together as one problem: restoration (the object is still the thing, but the history has been interrupted), museum replicas of repatriated or fragile objects — including the 3D-printed stand-ins that are now good enough to fool almost anyone — and generated images, where there's no interesting causal chain to the depicted scene at all. She also has a stronger claim I'm not sure I buy, which is that "the original" isn't a natural kind at all but an institutional role, and that the market, art history and the law assign that role by completely different criteria, so most of the philosophical literature is quietly assuming one unified role that doesn't exist. That last bit feels either really important or like a verbal trick and I can't tell which. (For what it's worth, the case that got me into this is the WWII-era Dutch forger who sold a fake Vermeer to Göring and was only caught because of the sale — the interesting part isn't the deception, it's that everyone's valuation of the object flipped overnight without the object changing at all.)
