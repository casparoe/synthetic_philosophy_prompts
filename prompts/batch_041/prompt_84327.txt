Ok, this is going to sound like a weird thing to ask a philosopher, but bear with me.

Six weeks ago our co-op's board voted to lower the threshold for constitutional amendments — from two-thirds of the membership to a simple majority — and did it by a simple majority vote. Half the membership now says the change is void, because it didn't clear the old two-thirds bar. The other half says it's obviously fine, because the amendment rule is itself amendable by the procedure it sets out. Our bylaws, which where written in 1994 by people who are all long gone, say nothing about which amendment rule was in force for the vote that changed the amendment rule. We're rewriting the whole document from scratch, and I've somehow ended up as the "philosophy person" on the drafting committee. Four months, six meetings, and the last one ended with someone crying. (I know there's a board game version of this — Nomic? — but in ours people's rent is on the line.)

I don't want a reading list, I can chase sources down myself. What I want is this: which open questions about self-amending rules actually matter for what we're doing, and which are fun puzzles that won't change a single line of the document? Rank them, and tell me why in that order. Things that have already come up, so you can calibrate what I mean by "in the weeds":

- whether the amendment rule can be applied to itself, and if not, what is supposed to ground the authority of the first version
- entrenchment: one rule says "this clause may only be changed by unanimous consent of all members", a later rule says "any clause may be changed by two-thirds" — which wins, and what grounds the winner rather than the loser
- whether we can bind future members at all: a clause saying "no future clause may create a paid board" — can a future meeting just repeal that clause and then do what it likes?
- a clause that repeals itself (someone proposed a one-meeting sunset and we genuinely couldn't tell whether it had already expired)
- whether an amendment can retroactively validate the irregular vote that produced it (not hypothetical)

For each one I want four things: does it actually change what we write down, how likely is it to blow up in a real fight, is it tractable for six people who aren't lawyers, and how many of our other headaches sit downstream of it. Then tell me what you'd just decide arbitrarily and stop worrying about — I suspect a couple of these have no right answer and any rule works, but I keep getting pulled into arguments about them anyway. And if there's something we should be worried about that isn't on my list at all, put it above the ones that are.

Be straight with me if the premise is confused. If "real questions vs. toy paradoxes" isn't a distinction that holds up here, or if all five of those bullets collapse into one problem, say so rather than handing me a tidy ranked list — I'd rather find out now then after four months of meetings. And flag where you're guessing. I'd take "I'm not sure this one has a determinate answer" over false confidence, because I'm going to be in a room arguing about this on Thursday.
