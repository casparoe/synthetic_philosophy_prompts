I'm a software engineer with no formal training in philosophy, and I've been trying to read Jorge Luis Borges's story "Funes the Memorious" as a thought experiment about prediction, memory, and compression. I'd like a serious critical reading, not a summary.

The story gives us Ireneo Funes, who after a fall from a horse is left paralyzed and with a flawless perceptual memory: he retains every detail of every moment he has lived, and it bothers him that a dog seen from one angle at one moment should share a name with the same dog seen from another angle a moment later. He also invents his own numbering system, in which each number gets its own arbitrary name rather than a position in a structured sequence. The narrator's closing suggestion is that Funes was scarcely capable of thought, because thinking involves forgetting differences, generalizing, and abstracting.

I want you to read the story against the algorithmic-information-theoretic picture: Kolmogorov complexity as the length of the shortest program that outputs a given string, Solomonoff induction as the ideal predictor that weighs candidate programs by their description length, and the version of Occam's razor that falls out of that as a trade-off between fitting the data and describing the model. My working intuition is that a mind with perfect data but no compression is not a good predictor at all, and that Funes is the limiting case of a model that memorizes its training set and generalizes to nothing.

Please do several things. First, tell me whether Funes really is the failure case the story presents him as, or whether the story is trading on a confusion between holding a maximally detailed representation and being unable to discard detail. Kolmogorov complexity is a property of an object, not of a mind, so I want to know whether that distinction matters here, and whether a lookup table that fits every observation perfectly is a legitimate hypothesis with a merely enormous description length rather than a non-hypothesis. Second, take the numbering system seriously as a claim about representation: the story seems to say that a code with no structure is useless even when it is perfectly faithful, and I want to understand how compression is what buys you computation and prediction rather than just storage. Third, if it fits, bring in "The Library of Babel" as a companion case, a place that contains every possible book including the true one, and tell me whether it dramatizes the same idea or a different one. Fourth, be candid about where the story oversells its point or misleads a reader who comes to it from information theory, and about what it contributes to the technical debate that the formalism alone does not.

One in-the-weeds wrinkle I'd like addressed if you can: since the choice of description language only shifts complexity by an additive constant, simplicity is objective only up to that constant. Funes's numbering scheme is effectively a different description language. Does that change anything about the verdict on him, or is it a red herring?

Please don't quote passages from the story, and don't include a bibliography or list of readings; I don't have the texts in front of me and I'd rather have your reconstruction and argument. Write in American English.
