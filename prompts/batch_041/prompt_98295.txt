Ok so I spent 35 years as a physical chemist and I've lately been reading a lot of philosophy, mostly Leibniz and some stuff on personal identity, and something's been bugging me that I can't sort out.

Here's the thing. In statistical mechanics we're taught to divide the number of microstates by N! when the particles are identical, and in quantum mechanics we're told that swapping two identical particles doesn't give you a new state, you symmetrize (or antisymmetrize) the wavefunction and that's the end of it. This is basically how the Gibbs paradox gets resolved, mixing two samples of the same gas gives you no entropy of mixing, but mixing two different gases does. So the practice seems to be saying: if two particles agree in every property, there's no fact of the matter about which one is which, there aren't really two distinct situations sitting there.

But Leibniz's principle of the identity of indiscernibles seems to say something stronger, that if two things have all the same properties then they aren't two things at all, they're one thing. And then there's that old thought experiment with the two perfectly similar iron spheres in an otherwise empty, perfectly symmetric universe, where people say well they're still two, just mirrored. So I genuinely can't tell whether my stat mech practice is comitted to Leibniz's principle or not.

And here's where I really get confused. We obviously still say there are two electrons in the helium ground state. We count them. We talk about one being spin up and the other spin down. So the practice seems to presuppose they're two distinct individuals. But then the symmetrization says the labels don't mean anything. Are those two things even consistent with each other?

So what I'd like to know is what this practice actually presupposes about identity. Does it really need the identity of indiscernibles, or only something weaker, like that the difference isn't observable, or that the labels don't track any physical difference? Which rival views does the practice rule out (that there are genuinely two individuals distinguished by something over and above their properties, versus that identical particles aren't individuals at all) and which does it just leave open? And if you swapped the presupposition out for a different one, what would actually change in how we count states and what we'd predict? I have a feeling the physics is more underdetermined philosophically than my textbooks let on, but I'd like someone to lay out exactly where.
