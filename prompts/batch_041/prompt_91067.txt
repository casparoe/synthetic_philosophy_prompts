I hold the position that the no-free-lunch theorems, correctly interpreted, do not show the problem of induction to be insoluble; they show that no learner can be uniformly superior across all possible worlds, so any inductive method must encode a substantive, non-empirical assumption about which worlds are possible. On this view, Hume's problem is not solved but relocated: the real work is done by a prior or bias that cannot itself be justified by experience, and the theorem is best read as a formal statement of that predicament rather than as a refutation of induction. What would count against this position? I want the defeaters ranked from fatal to merely awkward, including formal results (such as convergence theorems for Bayesian or simplicity-based learners), cases where the theorem is misapplied, and distinctions between no-free-lunch results for learning and for optimization. In particular, would a universal prior that favors short descriptions, if it can be shown to dominate on the class of computable worlds, count as a genuine free lunch, or does it merely shift the unjustified assumption into the choice of that class? I am also interested in whether the theorem's averaging over all possible target functions, rather than over worlds we have any reason to think possible, does any epistemic work or is a formal triviality dressed as a skeptical result. Separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who know this literature, and do not present a contested interpretation as the consensus.
