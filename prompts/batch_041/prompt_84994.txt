The topic: whether a trained neural network actually *has* representations, or whether that's just a story we tell about it. Officemate #1 is a realist — she thinks that when a probe finds a direction that encodes, say, whose turn it is in a board game, and when you zero that direction out the model's behaviour changes in a targeted way, that's evidence there's a real internal thing there, not just a correlation between our measuring instrument and a pile of weights. Officemate #2 is the skeptic — he thinks the net is just a big stack of matrices doing multiplications, that any sufficiently high-dimensional activation vector can be post-hoc decoded for practically anything you like (including features the network demonstrably never uses), and that "the model represents X" has no truth condition independent of whichever interpretability tool we happened to point at it. He's not a crank, he knows the engineering better than anyone in the group; he just thinks representation-talk is a useful fiction that people have started mistaking for a finding.

What I'd like the dialogue to actually chew on: (a) their advisor keeps pushing a criterion — a representation is real to the extent that you can intervene on it and get a specific, predictable behavioural change and nothing else weird — and #2's reply is that this definately just redefines representation as any causally relevant internal variable, which is true of basically every direction in weight space, so the criterion can't distinguish anything. #1's comeback is that that's not true, because if you perturb a random direction the network routes around it or the effect is diffuse and task-independent, and *that's* exactly the distinction we were after. Let them fight about whether that saves the criterion or just smuggles the conclusion in. (b) The experiment they can't stop arguing about: two nets trained to the same task from different seeds (or a transformer vs. an MLP), find a feature in one, and ask whether it's the same feature in the other. #1 says the question has to make sense, otherwise we can't even say the two models learned the same function; #2 says we're projecting our vocabulary onto two physically different objects and calling the projection a discovery. (c) Whether the skeptic's own position is stable — does he need representation-talk to say what the network is doing when he claims it's "routing around" a perturbation, or does he have a way of saying all that in pure weight-space terms?

Some notes: neither of them should be a strawman, both are smart and should have real replies to each other's actual points rather than trading monologues, and no citations or name-dropping papers, they both know the literature.
