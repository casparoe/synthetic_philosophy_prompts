Bit of a weird one, so bear with me. I'm a backend engineer (Go, a bit of Rust), no philosophy or logic background beyond some LessWrong posts and about half of GEB. I've gotten weirdly into the open-source game theory stuff — the trick where instead of picking a move in a one-shot prisoners dilemma you each submit a program, and each program gets to read the other ones source before it runs, so you can end up with mutual cooperation even though it's one-shot and theres no reputation or repeat-play thing going on. My company gives senior engineers a small "weird project" budget (roughly $120k over two years — one contractor plus cloud) and my manager has ok'd me pointing it at this, partly because she thinks its cool and partly because it might turn into a product for letting two companies' automated agents coordinate without a trusted third party. So I'm effectively a one-person funder and I have to pick.

Here's the part I want the advice on. Everything I've read assumes the programs can read and run each other for free, and that reading the source tells you what the program does. Neither is true for anything I'd actually build. So the version I care about is: both sides have a fixed compute budget, reading or simulating the other program eats into it, and either side can make its own behavior expensive to figure out — obfuscation, randomized behavior, or just being a big trained model instead of readable code. Call it program equilibrium with a simulation budget. Related thing I keep coming back to: in practice the "program" is a trained model, so you don't read it, you sample it — or you notice that you're both fine-tunes of the same base model, which feels like it should matter a lot and I can't tell if it does. Like, does cooperation survive if figuring out what the other side will do costs more than the payoff gap between cooperating and defecting? That's the flavor of question I mean, not the whole list.

So: which open questions here are most worth the money, and in what order?
