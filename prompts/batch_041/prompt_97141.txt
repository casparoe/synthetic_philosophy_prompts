He said if you can fully explain, in functional and neural terms, why humans end up with that intuition, then the hard problem is finished — the intuition is a fact about us, not about the world, so explaining it is explaining it away, and there's simply nothing left over to explain. Dana said that's just the confusion between explaining a belief and evaluating it: if I can explain why someone believed the fridge light stays on when the door's shut, I havent shown that it does, and explaining why we have the experience-is-special intuition doesn't show experience isn't special. She said he'd answered a question about our reports and then declared victory over a question about the world.

Marcus came back with two things. First, that the only grip we have on the alleged extra fact is the intuition itself, so if the intuition is the output of a process you've already explained, there's nothing left for it to be evidence of. Second, that positing a further non-physical fact to do justice to the intuition is exactly the move you'd reject if someone posited a vital force to do justice to the intuition that living things are somehow special. Dana said both moves prove too much. The first one would debunk any intuition you can causally explain, including the ones that lead Marcus to his own view — his reasoning is also produced by his brain, so by his own standard it's under suspicion too. And the parsimony point cuts the other way, she said: the deflationist owes an account of why the illusion is so stubborn and so uniform, and if the only way to get that account is to assume there really is something it's like, then he's begging the question.
