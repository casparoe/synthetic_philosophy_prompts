ok, I've been tinkering with a gadget for interpersonal utility comparisons and I want an actual credence on whether it works, not a tour of the debate.

The gadget: take two people, A and B. Get each of them to rank lotteries whose prizes are all described in one shared public unit -- say days of a standardized headache, running 0 to 30 -- so the bottom outcome is a shared zero and some fixed mixture over the top and bottom serves as a shared unit. Each person's lottery-preference scale (the von Neumann-Morgenstern thing) over that dimension then comes out pinned to the same affine normalization, and I claim the ratio of their two scale values at their actual situations is a definate fact about how their welfare compares. I call it common-lottery anchoring: there's a fact of the matter about the ratio, and this procedure finds it. My credence is about 0.3. My friend, who thinks the whole thing is a measurement artifact dressed up as a discovery, is at about 0.75.

Give me your number and decompose it. Exactly five numbered items, one or two sentences each, no preamble and no wrap-up:

1. your headline credence and how the pieces combine into it;
2. whether the shared public dimension really supplies a common zero and unit, or whether it quietly assumes something about how people trade risk off against the goods that actually matter to them;
3. whether a scale over one public dimension fixes the intensities of all the goods in a life, or whether it comes apart the moment you leave that dimension;
4. whether ranking behavior tracks intensity of desire at all, as opposed to mere preference ordering;
5. which term you're least sure of, which sub-question most likely seperates me at 0.3 from my friend at 0.75, and what evidence would move you most.
