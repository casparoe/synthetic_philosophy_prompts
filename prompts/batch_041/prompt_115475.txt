I'm writing a long feature for the ideas pages on what the world's older philosophical traditions would make of the new questions we now ask about death. Suppose Zhuangzi — same temperament, same metaphysics, but with our statutes and our trade press in front of him — were asked to comment. What would he make of two developments in particular: first, the shift to defining death by neurological criteria, which is what makes organ retrieval from ventilated patients possible, along with the argument that a family should be entitled to refuse that determination on conscientious grounds; and second, the growing industry that trains conversational agents on a dead person's messages so that the bereaved can go on talking to them? I want his case, made in his idiom and on his own terms, not a survey of what scholars have said and not a list of sources. Say plainly which of his recorded attitudes you are extrapolating from, and be candid about where the modern question simply has no analogue in his world — that mismatch is the interesting part of the piece. If you can, bring him to a recommendation: what he would tell a legislator drafting the statute, and what he would tell a mourner who has been offered the recording.
