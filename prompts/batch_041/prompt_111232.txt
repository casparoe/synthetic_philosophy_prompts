I teach a graduate seminar on the ethics of markets, and this term I want to build a session around Kazuo Ishiguro's Never Let Me Go. The novel strikes me as a better test of the commodification critique than the usual examples of repugnant markets — kidney sales, price gouging, vote selling — because it strips away most of what those examples supply. What I want from you is a reading of the novel that uses that critique as a lens, tests it against the book, and reports honestly on where the lens sharpens and where it distorts.

The seminar has already worked through the objections to commodification in some detail: the exploitation and coercion objections, which focus on what is done to the seller; the corruption-of-meaning and blocked-exchange objections, which focus on what pricing does to the good itself and to the shared meanings we live by; and the dignity objection, which holds that putting a price on a person, or a person's body, treats them as a means rather than an end. Treat these as three distinct claims rather than one. My working suspicion is that the novel can only be read as a critique of commodification if at least one of them survives in a setting where nothing is bought, nothing is sold, and no one is deceived.

Here is the specific pressure I want you to feel. In Ishiguro's England there is no market in organs at all. The donors are not paid; no transaction occurs; the institutions that harvest them simply own the bodies they raised, and everyone involved understands from childhood what is coming. So the novel isolates commodification from exchange, and isolates the wrong done to the donors from the wrong of a bargain. If the objection to commodification is at bottom an objection to what markets do, the novel should have nothing to object to. If it does object, then the objection must be about something else — a way of regarding persons, a social meaning, a form of life — and I want you to say what.

A few constraints. Assume I know the book well, so do not summarize the plot. Paraphrase the scenes you use instead of quoting; I am not checking quotations against the text, and I would rather you flag a detail you are unsure of than invent one. Do not give me references, a reading list, or citations — I have the literature in hand and I care about the argument, not the bibliography.

Write in continuous prose, in the following sections and in this order:

"The critique, stated fairly" (about 350 words). Give me the strongest version of the anti-commodification position, separating the wrong done to the person, the wrong done to the good, and the wrong done to the rest of us who must live in a world where the practice exists. Say what each of these needs to be true, and give the standard reply — that where no one is harmed and no one objects, there is nothing left to object to — along with the best answer the commodification theorist has to it.

"The novel as a test case" (about 700 words). Read the novel against that framework. Work with the features that carry weight: what Hailsham was actually for and what its students were being trained into; the Gallery, and the students' attempts to prove they possess inner lives worth preserving; the rumor economy around deferrals, and the scene in which Kathy and Tommy try to claim one; the way the donors keep donating; the flat, affectionate, unsurprised register in which Kathy tells her own story. Show where the lens earns its keep and what precisely it makes visible, and be concrete about the texture of the book rather than gesturing at themes.

"Where the reading strains" (about 400 words). Be honest about where the lens fails or flattens. Three candidates, and I want you to adjudicate rather than list them. First, the novel may be concerned with mortality, memory, and the ordinary self-deceptions of a life rather than with commodification at all, in which case my framing is an imposition. Second, the donors' acceptance can be read as adaptive preference, which would make their calm evidence for the critique, or as a genuine evaluative stance, which would force the critique to explain why it knows their interests better than they do. Third, the objection the novel actually lodges may be upstream of commodification altogether — a wrong in the creation of persons for a purpose, which is neither exploitation nor a market wrong in any recognizable sense. Tell me which of these is a real difficulty and which is a critic's nervousness.

"Verdict" (about 250 words). Which version of the anti-commodification position does the novel vindicate, and which does it embarrass? Say plainly whether the book is a better argument for the critique than the critique is for itself.

"What fiction adds" (about 250 words). Say what the novel delivers for this debate that an argument cannot, and what the argument delivers that the novel cannot.

I will also be showing the 2010 film adaptation, so if its choices change anything that matters, note it briefly — but do not let it become a second exercise.
