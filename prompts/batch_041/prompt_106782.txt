Me and my friend got into a long, slightly wine-fueled arguement last night about whether history explains things in the same way the natural sciences do. She's a hardliner about the covering-law model -- the idea that to explain an event is to show that it followed from general laws plus initial conditions -- and I kept defending narrative explanation, where a historian just tells you how one thing led to another without ever stating a law. By the end she'd talked me into a corner with an argument that seems to prove way too much, and I can't find the bad step. Could you act like a philosopher of history / philosopher of science who's thought hard about explanation, and help me diagnose it? Think carefully, and maybe start by brainstorming the two or three most plausible places the thing could break, then commit to a verdict. Here it is, roughly as she rebuilt it:

(1) An explanation is an answer to a why-question. (2) Every why-question has a contrast class: it asks why P rather than some alternative Q -- why the revolution happened in France rather than in England, why the dam broke rather than held, etc. (3) A covering-law explanation answers a why-question by showing that, given the laws and initial conditions, the event had to happen. (4) But if the event had to happen, then no alternative Q was really possible under those laws and conditions. (5) So a covering-law explanation can at best explain why P rather than nothing, not why P rather than Q. (6) Historical narratives, by contrast, are thoroughly contrastive: they explain why this outcome rather than that one, often by tracing contingencies, choices, and accidents. (7) Therefore either historical narratives aren't explanations (because they don't meet the covering-law standard), or the covering-law standard isn't the general form of explanation. (8) But the covering-law standard is supposed to be the general form of explanation. (9) So historical narratives aren't explanations. Which is absurd -- I've read histories that plainly explain things.

I'd rather keep (1) and (2) if I can, and I definitely want to keep (9)'s denial, i.e. that narratives do explain. What I want to know is which premise or step is doing the damage, why it looked so innocent, and what it costs to give it up. If the fix is to say covering-law explanations can be contrastive after all, what has to change in the model? If it's to say contrastive why-questions don't require alternative possibilities in the way (4) assumes, what notion of possibility is left? And if I have to give up (8) instead, what do I replace it with so that explanation isn't just whatever we happen to like? Please don't just tell me the covering-law model is unfashionable; price the options.
