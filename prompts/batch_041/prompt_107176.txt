I teach a final-year module on philosophy of social science and I've got 16 students for a 90-minute session. I want to run an activity that makes them feel two things at once, because I think the tension between them is the whole point of the topic and it never lands when I just say it out loud.

Here's the idea. Big historical catastrophes -- civil wars, state collapse, the fall of dynasties -- can come out of slow structural pressures that nobody chose and nobody wanted. One of those pressures I find genuinely gripping, and it goes like this. The number of positions at the top of a society (offices, rents, seats, commands) is basically fixed. It's a positional good: you can't manufacture more of them without diluting what they're worth. But the number of people who've been raised, trained and socialised to expect one of those positions can grow and grow, and it grows fastest exactly when the society looks most prosperous, because prosperity is what lets more families push another son or daughter into the aspirant pool. So you end up with a swelling mass of educated, well-connected, disappointed contenders, every one of them with the connections and the grievance to make trouble, and all they need is a spark. Meanwhile the same growth squeezes the people at the bottom, whose surplus is being pulled upwards to keep the elite in the style they've come to expect, and it squeezes the state's own coffers at the same time. The bit that isn't obvious -- and the bit I want them to feel in their bones rather than hear from me -- is that the relationship isn't monotonic. A society with too few elites is weak. A society with a healthy number is stable. A society with too many is a powder keg. More capable, ambitious people competing at the top is not straightforwardly better.

I want them to run into that themselves, through the rules, before it gets named. And I want them, by the end, to have felt the pull of the opposite worry too: that this whole style of explanation -- quantify a society, find a cycle, predict the next turn -- is a model that can be bent to fit almost anything, and that our confidence in it might be an artefact of how we built it. I don't want them leaving thinking either "history is a physics problem" or "this is all unfalsifiable nonsense". I want them stuck in between, and a bit annoyed about it.

The setup: 16 students, one 90-minute slot, an ordinary seminar room, no computers, I can bring a whiteboard, paper, envelopes, tokens or poker chips, whatever you need. No real money. Some of them will definately be awkward about role-play, so it has to be playable by people who'd rather be a spreadsheet than a character.
