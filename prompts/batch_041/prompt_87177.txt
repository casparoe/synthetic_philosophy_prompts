For most of my working life I was a taxonomist — I spent the better part of four decades sorting beetles into species and arguing with colleagues about what a species even is — and now, in retirement, I find myself circling a problem I suspect belongs to philosophy rather than to biology. It is the question of what makes every 'a' an 'a'. I first noticed it while fiddling with typefaces: the two-storey 'a' of a printed book and the one-storey 'a' of my own handwriting are, I am certain, the same letter, yet they share no obvious shape feature; a mirrored or upside-down 'a' is plainly not an 'a' to a reader; a glyph poised ambiguously between 'a' and 'o' becomes one or the other only because of the word around it; and an 'a' in a wildly ornate script, or in an invented alphabet, still seems to be an 'a' in virtue of something. I would like you to build me a taxonomy — a decision tree — of the positions one can hold on this question.

Please treat it as a genuine classification instrument rather than a list of labels. Every branching node should be a question that actually divides the positions beneath it, phrased so that someone can answer it from the cases alone, without already knowing which view it commits them to: for instance, whether the identity of a letter is fixed by intrinsic shape or by its relations to other things, whether membership in the category is all-or-nothing or graded, whether the deciding fact is historical (a matter of descent) or present-tense, and whether it depends at all on what minds do. Use the concrete cases as the material of the nodes — the two-storey versus one-storey 'a', the mirrored 'a', the borderline shape disambiguated by context, the ornate or invented-script 'a', the distinction between the printed glyph, the sound, and the abstract letter.

I want the leaves to be the actual positions, each given a name and one sentence on its most awkward commitment. I would expect at least the following to appear somewhere: an essentialist view on which there is a set of necessary and sufficient shape features; a graded or prototype view; a systemic view on which a letter is what it is only by contrast with the other letters of its script; a historical-descent view; a reader-response view; and a conventional or stipulative view on which a community simply fixes what counts. Where the tree leaves an answer-combination unoccupied, say so and tell me briefly whether nobody is there because the view is incoherent, merely unattractive, or simply unexplored; and if any two leaves cannot be separated by any of my cases, flag that rather than papering over it.

One warning: I know the species-concept literature far too well, and I do not want it served back to me under new labels. Rebuild the structure from the letterforms themselves, and tell me where the analogy to biological classification breaks down. Please write the whole thing as continuous prose — no headings, no bullet points, no bold, no tables — the way one would set it out in a long letter.
