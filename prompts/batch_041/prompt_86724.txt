I spent thirty-five years as a population geneticist working on social evolution: levels of selection, kin selection, the conditions under which groups can be treated as units of selection, and the theory of major evolutionary transitions in individuality. I have recently retired and have begun reading philosophy. I have worked my way into the debate over individualism and collectivism: the liberal-communitarian dispute over whether the self is prior to its ends or constituted by the community it belongs to; the East Asian strand in which the self is realized through its roles and relations rather than standing apart from them; and the political form of the argument, over whether there are rights or values that a community may not override in the name of collective flourishing.

What I want is a translation, as faithful as you can make it, of the main positions in that debate into the vocabulary I actually think in: units and levels of selection, biological individuality, fitness, heritability, reproduction and lineages, superorganisms, cheaters and the policing that suppresses them, and the idea that a new level of individuality can emerge when lower-level entities surrender their independent reproduction. Treat the person as the candidate lower-level unit and the community as the candidate higher-level one, and work through the positions one by one — individualist, communitarian, relational — saying what the translation yields in each case. Do not merely relabel; where a claim resists translation, record the resistance rather than forcing it through.

Then give me the residue report. Which claims carry over cleanly? Which survive only in a distorted form, and what exactly is lost in the distortion? Which have no counterpart in the biological vocabulary at all — I would expect, for instance, that anything about what a community owes a person, or about dignity and rights, may fall outside it entirely. For each item of residue, tell me whether it marks a genuine gap in the biological framework, something about persons that selection thinking cannot represent, or whether it instead shows that the philosophical claim was never precise enough to translate in the first place. I know the biology cold and am a beginner in the philosophy, so unpack the philosophical terms as you go and do not assume I know the literature.

Finally, answer the question directly: does the biological vocabulary, applied honestly, make one side of the debate look like a category mistake, or does it dissolve the dispute into a merely verbal one? And is there a sense in which treating a community as an individual, in my technical sense of the word, is precisely what one of the sides is denying?
