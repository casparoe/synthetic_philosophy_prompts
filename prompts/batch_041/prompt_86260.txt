I trained as a physicist and spent the bulk of my working life building large optimization models for an electric power system — unit commitment, economic dispatch, and later the market-clearing software — which means that for decades I made my living computing, among other things, prices: the dual variables of a linear program are prices, and nobody in that world finds it strange that a machine produces them. I retired four years ago and have since been reading my way into philosophy and the history of economic thought, where I ran into the socialist calculation debate and had the slightly vertiginous experience of finding my old job described as impossible. I am now trying to understand what Mises and Hayek were actually claiming, what Lange's answer to them was, and why the dispute is so often reported as settled when the two principal objections seem to me to be of entirely different kinds. The objection I can state precisely is about computation. The other one, about knowledge that exists only in dispersed, local, and largely unarticulated form, is not obviously answered by faster computers, and Lange's construction — a central board groping toward the correct prices by trial and error while managers treat those prices as given — looks to me like an attempt to obtain the parametric character of prices without the market that produces it. My further impression is that the incentive-and-information strand of the argument, the one I gather later turned into mechanism design, is a third thing again, but I may be flattening a real distinction.

I have been asked to give the after-dinner address at the annual dinner of a small workshop on systems optimization — roughly sixty people, mostly engineers who model networks, plus a number of guests who are not technical at all, and one invited economist who works on mechanism design and who will know the literature far better than I do. Please write that address, about twelve minutes long. I would like the philosophical content to do structural work rather than decorate: the speech should be organized around the difference between the computability objection and the knowledge objection, and around the odd fact that a competitive firm takes prices as parameters while those prices are nothing but the upshot of everyone's decisions, including its own. If the distinction holds up, then the cheerful modern reply — that we now have the computing power to solve the planner's problem — answers only one of the two objections, and the speech should be honest about which half survives and why that is uncomfortable. Bring in the present-day revival of the planning idea, the people who argue that machine learning and modern computation make comprehensive coordination feasible again, because that is where the argument actually lives now and it is what the non-specialists will have seen in the press; I have not read that literature carefully, so characterize it fairly rather than adjudicating it. One concrete thing you might use, if it helps: the Chilean cybernetic project of the early seventies, Stafford Beer's control room with its telex links to the factories, which is the only serious attempt I know of to build a real-time nervous system for a national economy, and which failed for reasons that are themselves instructive. The non-technical guests have to enjoy and follow the whole thing, so the economics should arrive through a homely example rather than an exposition.

Finally, do not let the talk end with a verdict. Whenever I read about this debate, everyone is certain who won, and the certainty looks inherited rather than argued. If the case is genuinely asymmetric — one side's argument answerable by computation and the other's not — say so, and let the toast be a little uncomfortable. And if I have got the debate wrong in some way that matters, or if the address I am asking for cannot be written honestly because my framing is confused, tell me so at the top instead of writing around it. Since a speech has no verdict to give, end with a short private note to me rather than to the audience: where it takes liberties with the positions, what it leaves out, and which of its claims are contested by people who know the material.
