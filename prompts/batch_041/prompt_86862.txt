I sit on the animal welfare and ethics review body at my univeristy — we see half a dozen protocols a month, twenty minutes each, lay members included, and the chair wants us to stop deciding by gut feel. Someone on the panel keeps pushing a view she calls lexical threshold negative utilitarianism: suffering comes first, and below some intensity of pain there's supposed to be a threshold such that no amount of mild pleasure or other benefit, however large, can buy it off — while above that intensity things can at least be weighed against each other. I'd like a procedure we can actually run on a protocol, in order: the questions to ask, what counts as a good enough answer to each, where it stops with a reject, and where it has to go to a full vote because it's a values call dressed up as a fact. Two things it has to be honest about: whether pleasure and pain are even on one scale, so that any exchange rate between them is a bit of a fiction, and what we do when we can't put a number on either side. Then run it on three: (a) mice with a moderate but weeks-long painful condition, testing a drug that would probably help alot of people; (b) very mild but lifelong discomfort across a large colony, small scientific payoff; (c) short but severe pain in a few animals, with a big expected payoff — I suspect the procedure tells us to reject that one and I want to see whether it really does. Finally, where will the procedure misfire, and where could a determined applicant game it?
