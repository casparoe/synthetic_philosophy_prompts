I am an undergraduate in a cognitive science program, currently taking a seminar on computational theories of mind, and I would like help diagnosing a dispute that has stalled our class for several weeks. The computationalists in the seminar hold that the brain is a computer in a substantive sense: cognition is to be explained by representations and algorithms, and the brain implements them. The critics, several of whom are sympathetic to dynamical systems and embodied cognition, hold that this is either false or empty. Their central move is the triviality objection: if any physical system can be described as implementing some computation by choosing a suitable mapping from its states and transitions to a formal description, then the claim that the brain is a computer says almost nothing. The computationalists reply that the relevant computation must be discovered rather than gerrymandered, and that the critics are conflating a description of a system with an explanation of its mechanism. The critics respond that discovery is doing no work unless we already know what level of description is the right one, which looks like a matter of explanatory interest rather than a fact about the brain. A student in class then proposed a thought experiment, which I will call the two-maps case, in which a single physical system can be mapped onto two different computations, one that looks cognitive and one that looks trivial, with no fact of the matter about which mapping is the real one. The computationalists say the trivial mapping is not a serious candidate; the critics say that is exactly the point.

I suspect the impasse is not a straightforward empirical disagreement. It may be that the two sides are answering different questions under one heading: one side asks what mechanism produces cognition, another asks what form a satisfactory explanation must take, and a third possible reading treats the computer claim as a formal claim about implementation that is too permissive to be interesting. But I can also see a substantive disagreement about explanatory standards, namely whether representation and algorithm are the right level at which to explain cognition, or whether dynamics and embodiment are. I would like a diagnosis rather than a verdict. Is this a verbal dispute about the word computation, a disagreement about what counts as an explanation, a hidden empirical question, or two research programs talking past each other? Given your diagnosis, what would count as progress—a distinction, a formal result, a new experiment, a change in the question—or should we conclude that the debate is ill-posed? I do not need citations or a reading list; I need the structure of the disagreement made explicit and an honest assessment of whether the two sides can be brought into genuine contact.
