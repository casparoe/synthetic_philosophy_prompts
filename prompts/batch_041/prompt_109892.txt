I am preparing a seminar on Adam Smith's political economy and have been slowed down by a passage in Book IV, Chapter VII of The Wealth of Nations. Smith begins his chapter on the motives for establishing new colonies by contrasting the ancient Greek and Roman colonies with the modern European colonies in America and the West Indies. He writes:

"The interest which occasioned the first settlement of the different European colonies in America and the West Indies was not altogether so plain and distinct as that which directed the establishment of those of ancient Greece and Rome. ... The mother city, though she considered the colony as a child, at all times entitled to great favour and assistance, and owing in return much gratitude and respect, yet considered it as an emancipated child over whom she pretended to claim no direct authority or jurisdiction. The colony settled its own form of government, enacted its own laws, elected its own magistrates, and made peace or war with its neighbours as an independent state, which had no occasion to wait for the approbation or consent of the mother city."

I can follow the individual sentences, but I cannot see the argumentative purpose of the contrast. Why does Smith open a chapter about modern colonization with an extended account of ancient colonization? What does he mean by saying the interest behind ancient colonization was "plain and distinct," and what does that imply about the motives behind modern empire? More specifically, what is the force of the "emancipated child" image? Is Smith holding up the ancient practice as a normative model, or is he using it as a foil to expose the distinctive irrationality and injustice of the modern colonial system? I would like a close reading that explains the function of this passage within Smith's broader critique of empire, without merely summarizing the chapter's later arguments about monopoly and representation. I am especially interested in how this opening shapes the reader's understanding of what is wrong with the British empire: is the problem that modern colonies are not emancipated, or that the very idea of a mother country's authority over distant settlements is a category mistake?
