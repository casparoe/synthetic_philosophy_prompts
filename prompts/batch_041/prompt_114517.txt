After forty years in experimental physics, I have spent my retirement reading philosophy of religion and philosophy of science, and I keep returning to al-Ghazālī on causation. I would like you to draft the joint statement that two camps in that dispute could sign after a genuine adversarial collaboration. The first camp is a Ghazālī-style occasionalist: God alone is the producer of every event, and what we call natural causes are occasions or regularities, not productive powers. The second is a concurrentist: created things have real causal powers, but those powers are continuously sustained and activated by God's concurrence. Both camps are theist; neither denies divine creation or providence. Their quarrel is over created causation, not over whether God acts.

Please produce a document with two parts. First, a numbered list of claims both camps accept: about observable regularities, about the dependence of everything on God, about what it means to call something a cause, about human agency and responsibility, about the difference between natural and miraculous events, and about what could count as evidence for either side. Be exact enough that neither side would feel the wording smuggles in the other's metaphysics, but not so thin that the list is vacuous.

Second, a disagreements table. Each row should name one crux and give: the occasionalist's formulation, phrased so an occasionalist would accept it; the concurrentist's formulation, phrased so a concurrentist would accept it; the case, argument, or thought experiment both sides agree bears on the crux; and each side's prediction about it. I expect the cruxes to include at least: whether created things are genuine causes or only occasions; whether causal powers are real features of the world or theoretical shorthand; whether God's causation is immediate or mediated; whether natural regularities are grounded in divine habit or in creaturely natures; whether either view can preserve human agency and moral responsibility; and whether any part of the dispute is empirical or merely verbal.

To keep this from becoming a rehearsal of textbook positions, please introduce and use a neutral four-part vocabulary throughout: occasion, concurrence, production, and regularity. Then apply it to a paired-world thought experiment of your own construction: two worlds with identical observable sequences, one in which God produces each effect directly on the occasion of the putative cause, and one in which God gives creatures real powers that are continuously sustained. Ask what, if anything, could distinguish the worlds, and have each side say what it predicts and what it would count as evidence against its own view. Also apply the vocabulary to a radium atom's decay, to a person raising an arm, and to an event both sides would call miraculous. Where you conclude that a disagreement is merely verbal, say which part and why.

Do not give a verdict on which camp is right, and do not include citations, bibliographies, or quotations from texts. Keep the entire statement under 1,000 words.
