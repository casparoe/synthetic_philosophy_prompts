I am taking a graduate seminar on economics and the good life, and we have spent the past two weeks on Keynes — not the Keynes of the multiplier, but the Keynes of Economic Possibilities for our Grandchildren, who predicted that the economic problem is not the permanent problem of the human race and that his grandchildren would face the unfamiliar problem of how to fill their leisure; and also the late memoirist who looked back with some irony on the creed he had absorbed as a young man from G. E. Moore and the Cambridge circle around him, according to which the ultimate goods are certain states of mind — the enjoyment of love and beauty, the pursuit of truth, the company of friends — while everything else, including the entire apparatus of production and acquisition, is at best instrumental. What interests me in the seminar is a structural feature of that view rather than its particular list of goods: it separates sharply between the states of mind that are good in themselves and the material and social conditions that merely enable them, and it assumes that once the economic problem is solved the enabling conditions can be taken for granted and the goods themselves will flourish. Keynes himself seems to have half-doubted this. The same essay that predicts abundance also worries that people will not know what to do with it, and the late memoir suggests that the whole Moorean picture was too thin and too rationalistic, insufficiently alert to the irrational and to the fragility of the civilisation that makes such states of mind possible.

My book club is reading Virginia Woolf's To the Lighthouse next month, and I want to use it as a test case rather than as decoration. Could you read the novel through that specific structural claim — that the good life consists in certain states of mind and that the economic and social order is merely instrumental to them — and tell me what the novel shows, complicates, or refutes? I am especially interested in three things. First, Mrs. Ramsay: she is the character who most reliably produces the states of mind Keynes valued — the dinner party at which the disparate guests briefly cohere, the moments of stillness and of being, the sense that something has been made — and she does it at the cost of an exhausting and largely invisible labour, in a household sustained by money, servants, and a settled class order. If the production of the goods of life is itself a kind of work, does that undermine the clean separation between the goods and their merely instrumental conditions, or can Keynes absorb it? Second, Mr. Ramsay: he is a philosopher whose states of mind are exactly the ones on Keynes's list — the pursuit of truth, the life of the intellect — and yet the novel presents him as vain, needy, and at times cruel, and presents his abstract work as something that must be continually propped up by his wife's attention and his children's resentment. Does the novel show that intellectual states of mind are not sufficient for a good life, or that Keynes's list of goods is too coarse to distinguish the states worth having from the ones that merely resemble them? Third, the war and the long middle section of the novel, in which the house decays and the family is scattered: Keynes in the late memoir described civilisation as thin and precarious, held together by convention and the will of a few, and the novel seems to stage precisely that thought, while also insisting that the moments of value are not cancelled by their fragility. I would like the reading to be faithful in both directions: do not reduce the novel to an illustration of a thesis, and do not soften Keynes into a generic humanist. Where the novel resists the Keynesian framing, say so plainly, and end by saying what the novel contributes to the philosophical question that Keynes's essays raise but do not settle — namely, whether a life of leisure and abundance would actually be a life in which the goods Keynes named could be enjoyed, or whether the conditions that produce those goods are inseparable from the labour, hierarchy, and mortality that Keynes wanted to set aside as merely instrumental.
