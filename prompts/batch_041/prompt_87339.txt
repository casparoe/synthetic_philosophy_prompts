I sit on the methods and ethics panel at a public statistics body — I'm the lay member, everyone else is an economist or a demographer, so my job is mostly to ask "would a normal person read it that way?"

Next Thursday we sign off a flagship living-standards report. The headline finding is that the cohort born from the mid-nineties onwards will be the first in living memory to end up worse off than the one before it. The press office loves it. They want the whole thing framed around the usual labels — boomers, millennials, Gen Z — with cohort-specific recommendations on housing, pensions and student debt, and they've already briefed a friendly paper, so we're not deciding in a vacuum. The analysts are uneasy. Age, period and cohort are perfectly collinear, so the model cannot seperate "young people are poor because they're young" from "this cohort is poor because of what happened to it" without imposing a constraint, and the constraint they've used assumes period effects are flat across two adjacent decades. One of them described this to me as a way of writing down your prior and calling it a result.

There's a proposal to adopt a three-part test before we ever put a generational label in a headline: does the cohort share a mechanism — an institution, a shock, a technology — that makes the cluster more than a date range? Does the cluster license predictions about people we haven't observed yet? And does the finding survive moving the boundary years by five? I like it, but a colleague argues it sets a bar nothing would pass, that age bands are just as arbitrary, and that if we refuse to say "generations" we're quietly denying that the handover of housing wealth between cohorts is a real thing that deserves political attention. Nobody has said out loud what I think is actually at stake: whether a generation is a thing in the world or a spreadsheet convention that's taken on a life of its own.

I have to circulate a short written opinion before the meeting. Recomend a course of action — adopt the test, adopt something else, or run the release as drafted — with a clear bottom line for Thursday (including whether the press office may use the labels in the headline), and give my colleague's point the best hearing you can before you knock it down.
