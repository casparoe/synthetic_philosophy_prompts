I'm reading Frederick Douglass's speech arguing, against the Garrisonians, that the Constitution is anti-slavery rather than pro-slavery, and there's a move in it I can't get my head around. Here's the bit:

"The Constitution may be right, the Government is wrong. If the Government has been governed by mean, sordid, and wicked passions, it does not follow that the Constitution is mean, sordid, and wicked."

He backs this with an analogy a little earlier — government and constitution are "distinct in character as is a ship and a compass. The one may point right and the other steer wrong." And then he lays down a rule for reading the thing: "the intentions of those who framed the Constitution, be they good or bad, for slavery or against slavery, are so respected so far, and so far only, as we find those intentions plainly stated in the Constitution." So: text only, no peeking at what the framers secretly wanted.

What I don't understand is how these two halves fit together. If the Constitution is a compass that can point right while the ship steers wrong, then the Constitution's meaning seems to be doing no work at all in explaining what the country actually did for seventy years — so in what sense is it "anti-slavery" rather than just a document whose good parts nobody used? And there's a second thing nagging me: he tells us not to consult the framers' intentions or the convention debates, but then he himself appeals to the fact that the framers met with the doors closed and that the debates went unpublished for decades — which looks like exactly the kind of historical/intent evidence he just banned. Is that a real inconsistency or am I missing a distinction?

Can you walk me through what Douglass is actually claiming here, and whether the plain-text rule survives his own use of the framing history?
