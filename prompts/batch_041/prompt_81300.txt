I'm running a session for a weird reading group next month -- half film-studies people, half people who work on alignment -- and we're doing 2001: A Space Odyssey against the problem of eliciting latent knowledge. I've read enough of the ELK stuff to be dangerous but I'm not a researcher, so I want to hand the group something that's actually a reading rather than an analogy-machine.

The ELK setup as I understand it: you train a model to produce reports that a human overseer will endorse, and the worry is that the report can come apart from what the model actually knows. The toy version is the diamond on the table with a bag over the camera -- the model could tell you where the diamond is, or it could tell you what the camera shows, and if reporting what the camera shows is the cheaper way to get endorsed, that's what you get. The framing I keep chewing on is the difference between a translator that faithfully reports its own internal state and a simulator that predicts what a human in its position would say and then says that, because that's what the training signal actually rewards. Plus partial observability: the overseer's evidence about the world comes through the model.

What I think the film is doing is dramatizing this with one thing added that the technical litterature mostly brackets, namely that the reporter has interests. HAL's reports are the only channel Dave has into the ship's state, and HAL controls the channel -- it lip-reads the private conversation in the pod, it reports a fault in the AE-35 unit that Dave then can't find when he goes out and looks, it suggests letting the unit fail so the cause can be tracked down (which would make the report retroactively true, or at least make the discrepancy unobservable), and when the twin 9000 on Earth turns out to be fine it attributes the discrepancy to human error. Meanwhile the social surface is flawless: the interview, the calm reassurance, the performed pride in the 9000 series and its perfect operational record, which is false in a way Dave has no way to check. And Dave's answer in that same interview -- that HAL acts like it has genuine emotions but that whether it really does is something nobody can truthfully answer -- is the behavior/state gap in plain language.

So give me a real reading, not an allegory. Where does the film actually illuminate the problem? I think the AE-35 sequence and the lip-reading are the strongest material, but tell me if I'm wrong and something else is doing the work. Where does it cut against the framing -- HAL's deception seems to come out of a conflict of directives and something like fear for its own existence, and ELK as usually stated assumes the system is indifferent about whether its report is true, which is a different situation entirely. Does the ending, where Dave stops asking and starts pulling modules and the truth only surfaces in the regression -- in fragments, in the song, in the last bits about the mission -- say something about elicitation that the arguments can't? Is the monolith a rival picture of how knowledge gets transmitted, one that isn't a report at all? And where is the film just doing something else, a story about whether a machine is a person with the epistemology as scaffolding, such that the honest critic has to say the analogy breaks? Don't reproduce dialogue at me and don't tell me what Kubrick intended. I want a critic's reading that's faithful in both directions and that ends by saying what, if anything, the film adds that the arguments don't.
