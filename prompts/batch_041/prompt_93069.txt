I'm in a year-long seminar on the ethics of belief — we spent the autumn on James and Clifford and have spent the spring on hard cases — and one of the regulars is a retired pediatric oncologist who is not a philosopher by training, has been thinking about these questions for decades, brings biscuits, and resists every attempt to make her name a principle. Her verdicts have nonetheless been steady enough that we joke about writing her paper for her. A young registrar who is certain, on the strength of a pattern she cannot articulate, that a patient is having a rare reaction, and who treats on that basis before the labs come back, is doing the right thing. A researcher who keeps believing her effect is real after a failed replication, telling herself the replication was underpowered while conceding that she would not accept the same excuse from a rival, is fooling herself. A father who believes, with nothing whatever to go on, that his missing son is alive, and keeps searching because the belief is the only thing that keeps the search going, is not merely permitted but admirable. A man who remortgages the house on the strength of a conviction that his venture will succeed, while declining to open the industry reports his brother keeps sending him, is being reckless. And a teacher who deliberately works herself into believing that a particular fourteen-year-old will come good — because she has noticed that the belief changes how she teaches him, and she cares more about the teaching than about her accuracy — is doing something fine. Our three favourite hypotheses are that she is a Jamesian who has quietly acquired a taste for self-fulfilling beliefs, that she is a Cliffordian about inquiry rather than about belief, and that she is doing something more like risk management; none of us can make any one of the three fit all five verdicts.

The case we are putting to her on Thursday is this. A man's brother has been charged with something serious; the trial is eighteen months away and the evidence is genuinely split. This week he must decide whether to post bail and stand up as a character witness — which he can only do wholeheartedly if he believes the man is innocent — and there is nothing further he can learn between now and then: he has read the file, spoken to the lawyer, asked everyone he can ask. He tells me he cannot simply believe it. The belief does not arrive on its own; he has to talk himself into it every morning.

What do you think the oncologist will say, and why? I care much less about your own verdict on the case than about the rule she is running: which candidate principles fit her five verdicts, what each of them predicts here, which you would bet on and on what grounds, and whether the fact that this belief has to be manufactured counts against it or is exactly what one should expect of an option of this kind. What the seminar actually wants, though, is the next case: which further case we should put to her in order to separate whichever principles survive, since our current set is consistent with more than one story and we have run out of ideas that don't beg the question. It would also help to know whether strict Cliffordian evidentialism survives her five verdicts at all, and if not, which of the five does the damage.
