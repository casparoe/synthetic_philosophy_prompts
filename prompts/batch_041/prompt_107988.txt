im putting together the last handout for a course on conditionals and i'd rather close on a poem than another paper about the man who shot the president. can you write me one.

what i want it to do: take a single if-sentence and bring it back twice. the first time it reads as an ordinary open prediction — something like 'if the ferry's running we'll be in Oban by six' — and then somewhere past the middle we find out the ferry wasnt running, or the pier's shut, and the same sentence comes round again and can now only be heard as a counterfactual: 'if the ferry had been running we'd have been in Oban by six.' nearly the same words. the whole difference is a had. i want the reader to feel that.

i think a pantoum is the right shape for this — the form where the second and fourth lines of each stanza come back as the first and third of the next, so lines keep returning and meaning something new. use it against itself: hold the exact repetition strictly until the turn, and then, at the moment the antecedent turns out to be settled, let the returning line come back altered by two words. break the pantoum's own law exactly where the conditional changes kind. if the strict pantoum fights you, quatrains with a refrain will do, same trick.

one more thing i want in there, because its the bit that actually bites: the counterfactual isnt just the indicative with the tense pushed along. to say 'we'd have been in Oban by six' you have to decide what else to hold fixed — that we'd still have set off, that the car started, that we didnt stop for lunch — and nothing in the words tells you which. so the would-have is really a claim about what you're holding constant, which is why it can be arguable in a way the plain past-tense version isnt. even the boring ferry case should end up feeling contestable. and if you can work in that the ferry may already have been broken when we said it, and that this makes no difference at all to how the sentence sounded, do — the difference between the two kinds of if is in us, not in the world.

constraints: dont use the words indicative, counterfactual or subjunctive anywhere in the poem. no explaining the theory at the end, no moral, no Kennedy. it should be a poem first — someone who's never heard of possible worlds should get something out of it, and someone who teaches this stuff shouldnt wince. 20-30 lines, plain diction, dont let it rhyme itself into a corner. keep the idiom british, its for my students here.
