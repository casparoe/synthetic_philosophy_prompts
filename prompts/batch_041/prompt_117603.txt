I am a graduate student presenting my job-market paper at a small decision theory workshop next month, and I need a Q&A prep sheet. The paper argues that the standard cases for expected value fanaticism — the ones in which an enormous good rides on an absurdly small probability, so that maximizing expected value swamps every ordinary consideration — depend on an assumption nobody states: that the size of the payoff and the size of the probability can be varied independently, so that the payoff can be held fixed while the odds are driven arbitrarily low. I argue that this independence is an artifact of how the cases are written down. Whenever the enormous payoff is something a mechanism actually produces, the same mechanism constrains how probable that payoff can be; there is no way to hold the good fixed and lower the odds without changing what produces the good. I am not claiming that no coherent mathematical object has these features — one can write down a distribution with any payoff at any probability — but that the argument for fanaticism needs the prospect to be something an agent can do, and once you ask what the agent would actually do, the payoff and the odds are fixed by the same causal structure. I call this the coupling condition, and I use it to argue that we can keep ordinary expected value maximization without the Pascalian embarrassments.

The room will be about a dozen people, and I know who they are. Four work on the foundations of decision theory and will come at me through the axioms: they will ask which axiom I am rejecting, whether my argument is a claim about rational preference at all or a disguised claim about physics, and whether anything recognizable as expected utility survives the change. Two are formal epistemologists who care about the separability of credence and value and will press on whether I am offering a norm of rationality or a prediction about which gambles exist. One works on the long-term future and infinite ethics and will suspect that I am handing people a respectable excuse to ignore tiny probabilities of catastrophe. One is an economist who will bring up the St. Petersburg paradox, bounded utility, and the fact that decision theory was deliberately built to handle the kind of uninstantiated prospects I want to exclude.

Prepare me. Give me the seven or eight objections I will actually get from this room — not the generic objections to expected value maximization — ordered by how likely they are and how much damage they would do, each phrased the way the person who holds it would phrase it, each with a two-minute reply and a note on where the reply is soft. Tell me what to concede up front. And tell me the one question I am hoping nobody asks.
