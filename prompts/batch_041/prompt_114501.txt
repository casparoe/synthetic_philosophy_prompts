ok so I've got two freinds in my reading group who have been going at each other about humanitarian intervention for about three years now and I'm tired of refereeing. One of them is a liberal interventionist / R2P type: thinks Kosovo was a rare case of the West doing the right thing way too late, thinks the 2011 Libya campaign was justified when it started and got wrecked by mission creep, thinks the lesson of Rwanda is that we should have gone in, and thinks the lesson of Iraq isn't "never intervene" but "never do it on a fake pretext with no plan for the day after". The other is a restraint person: thinks the sovereignty norm is load-bearing, thinks these wars keep getting sold with atrocity stories that turn out thinner than advertised, thinks "we had to do something" is a machine for generating wars, and thinks the real lesson of Libya is that a resolution to protect civilians turns into a regime change operation the moment the interveners get bored of the mandate.

What I want is the document those two should have written instead of arguing in my kitchen. Neither of them gives up their view, neither gets strawmanned, but it has to be honest enough that both would actually sign it.

So:

1. A numbered list of claims they both accept — about the empirical record (Kosovo, Iraq, Libya), about what the fight is even about, and about what would count as evidence either way. Keep it non-vacuous; if a claim would be signed by literally anyone, cut it. And say which parts of their fight are just words — I suspect "humanitarian" is doing a lot of quiet work in both of their sentences, and I suspect they mean seperate things by "the intervention worked".

2. A disagreements table, one row per live disagreement. Column 1: the question, phrased neutrally enough that neither side thinks it's been loaded. Column 2: what the interventionist says. Column 3: what the restraint person says. Column 4: who bears the cost if that side is wrong — the interveners' soldiers, the civilians we were trying to save, the civilians on the other side of the line, the norm itself, whatever is actually accurate for that row.

3. The cruxes: the sub-questions where settling it would actually move one of them. I think one crux is whether they're judging the decision by what was knowable at the time or by how it turned out, and another is whether Security Council authorization is a condition of legitimacy or just a legal hoop. For each crux, give a case or argument that both of them would agree bears on it, and write down each side's prediction in advance.

4. One case neither of them has staked out yet, where they have to pre-register before they know the answer: a country with a credible mass-atrocity warning, no oil or other loot worth mentioning, no Western diaspora lobby, and one permanent Security Council member with a naval base on its coast. Make the details concrete enough that "it depends" is not an available answer, then give me each of their predictions and where the split actually lands when neither has a dog in the fight.

No bibliography, no reading list, just the document. And don't let either of them off the hook with "it depends on the circumstances" — I want the circumstances pinned down.
