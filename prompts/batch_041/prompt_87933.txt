I run a small logic-and-philosophy group out of my local library — nine regulars, mixed backgrounds (two retired programmers, a couple of schoolteachers, one bloke with a maths degree from decades back) — and I've got 90 minuites with them next month. What I want to do is Skolem's paradox: set theory, if it has any model at all, has a countable one, and yet inside that model you can prove that uncountable sets exist. I don't want to stand up and explain that, I want them to build the thing with their own hands and hit the wall before I ever say the word.

My half-formed idea, scrap it if you've got something better: teams get index cards and a short list of plain-English rules for a toy universe of sets. A set is a card with its members written on it, and I enforce one thing hard — if it isn't written on a card it doesn't exist, and if it isn't written on the card of a set it isn't a member of it. They're allowed to write things like "all the counting numbers" on a card as shorthand, the point being that everything in their universe is something you can pin down in a few words. Their job is to make three sentences true: there's an infinite set; every set has a set of its subsets; and there's a set S such that no pairing-up between S and the infinite set exists in the universe. They'll manage it, because the second rule only ever gets them the subsets that somebody actually wrote down. Then I swap teams, and the other lot has to pair every single thing in the universe up with the counting numbers — which they'll do easily, just by walking around numbering the cards. So the whole universe is countable from outside, while from inside S is uncountable and the pairing-up that would prove otherwise simply isn't there to be seen.

Can you turn that into something actually runnable: exact rules, roles, materials, a timeline for the 90 minutes, and a debrief that maps what they did onto the paradox and onto the fight about whether it shows something's wrong with set theory or only something about models? They shouldn't hear the name until the debrief. Also tell me what will predictably go sideways the first time — the cheating moves, the "this is just finite versus infinite" confusion, the person who announces we've proved a contradiction — and how to head those off without giving the game away. And be honest about what the activity can't do: mine is a toy, so say which part of the real result, and which part of the philosophical argument about it, I'll have to supply myself.
