I'm an anesthesiologist and I've just finished sitting on the panel that reviewed an intraoperative-awareness case — the patient remembered hearing us chat, and it honestly wrecked me, so when the committee asked me to write up our recommendation on whether to buy one of these new 'awareness monitors' I said yes before thinking it through. The sales pitch is that it can timestamp the exact moment a patient first experiences something, so you know the second you're underdosing. What's eating me is something a colleague said after the demo. He described an old experiment: you flash a blue dot near the top of a screen and then a red dot lower down, close together in time, and people swear they watched a single dot travel down the gap and flip from blue to red halfway — which means they report the colour change as happening before the red dot was even on the screen. His point was that this leaves two ugly stories. Either the brain lets you have the experience at the normal moment and then quietly edits the memory afterwards, so you only recall it in the tidied-up order (he called that the Orwellian option), or it sits on the experience, waits to see how things turn out, and then serves you a finished version all at once (the Stalinesque option). Then he said maybe neither is right and 'when exactly did the experience happen' just has no determinate answer — and thats the bit I can't shake, because our incident review and the vendor's timestamp both assume there is a when. Write me the recommendation to the committee as exactly five numbered points, one or two sentences each, no preamble and no wrap-up: what we should do about the monitor and why, and don't just tell me it's a muddle — if we say no I need an answer for the next family who asks why we turned down the thing that might have caught their mother being awake.
