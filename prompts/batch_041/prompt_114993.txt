Lucas's Gödelian argument and Penrose's are habitually mentioned together, yet they differ in their premises, in which of Gödel's results they lean on, and in how strong a conclusion they draw. Write an essay comparing them, setting out each in its most defensible form before assessing it.

Begin by fixing what each argument is a reductio of. Does it refute the mechanist thesis that a single Turing machine enumerates exactly the sentences the reasoner can come to accept, or does it establish only the weaker claim that no one formal system captures all of the reasoner's arithmetical reasoning? Keep separate what Gödel's theorems actually deliver from the further epistemic premise needed to turn them into an argument against mechanism, and note whether the conclusion is purely negative — the mind is not a machine — or carries a positive commitment, for instance that the non-computability of understanding must be underwritten by non-computable physics.

Then identify the machinery. Which of the two incompleteness results does the work in each argument, and what does an appeal to the second theorem, rather than to the bare existence of an undecidable true sentence, add and cost?

Next, isolate the epistemic asymmetry on which both arguments depend. Each requires the reasoner to know something about the system under consideration that the system cannot establish about itself: that it is consistent, or sound. Lay out the dilemma this generates. If the reasoner's own reasoning is captured by a consistent formal system, the second incompleteness theorem denies her a proof of that system's consistency, and the argument stalls; if her reasoning is not so captured, the argument risks presupposing its conclusion; and if she is inconsistent, she will prove anything, the relevant Gödel sentence included, and the argument collapses. Compare how Lucas and Penrose each attempt to escape the dilemma, and judge whether Penrose's appeal to a kind of mathematical knowledge that carries its own warrant does genuine work or merely relocates the difficulty.

Finally, confront the mechanist's most resourceful reply: that the reasoner is not any single formal system but an open-ended sequence of them, each extending its predecessor by the addition of the predecessor's consistency statement. Does this reply defeat the argument, and does the answer turn on whether the resulting progression can itself be generated mechanically?

Conclude with a defended verdict. Which argument, if either, survives the objections, and what precisely would have to be true of human mathematical reasoning for a Gödelian anti-mechanist argument to succeed? Commit to an answer and justify it rather than surveying the positions.
