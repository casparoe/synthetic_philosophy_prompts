I do a small philosophy-of-mind podcast — like two hundred listeners, half of them my old housemates — and next week's guest is a philosopher who's spent the last few years defending a view about dreams that I keep going back and forth on. What got me hooked was actually a dream of my own: I woke up with this whole elaborate scene in my head that I slowly realized was stitched together out of a book I'd been reading the night before and a noise from the street, and it made me wonder how much of what I "remember" from a dream was ever really there while I was asleep. Anyway, I want to interview her well, and I'm worried I'll just ask the same three questions everyone asks about the Malcolm/Dennett thing and get the same three answers. The background, in case it helps: Malcolm argued that dreams aren't experiences at all — that the very idea of undergoing something while asleep is confused, because 'dreaming' is tied to the waking report, not to anything going on in the sleeper; Dennett pushed back with a picture where the sleeping brain writes down something like a script and the waking mind reads it out. She thinks they're both wrong about the reason but Malcolm is closer on the verdict.

Her position, as I understand it: she agrees there are no dream experiences, nothing is undergone by anyone while you're asleep, but she thinks that's a claim about the mind's architecture and not about the grammar of dream reports. On her story the sleeping brain does throw up imagistic material, but the *dream* is something the waking mind does with it — it finds this unowned content lying around and, having no other candidate author, hands it back to itself as if it had lived through it. So dreaming, for her, is really an activity of waking up, a kind of retroactive authorship, and 'remembering a dream' is closer to finding a page of your own novel in your handwriting than to recalling a trip you took. The part I find most interesting, and maybe most vulnerable, is that she claims this makes a *testable* prediction that seperates her from both Malcolm and Dennett: if dream reports are rebuilt at waking rather than retrieved, then what you end up reporting should bend to waking conditions — the first question anyone asks you, the sound of your alarm, who's standing over the bed — far more than it should bend to whatever was happening in your brain during REM. That's a bold thing to say out loud and I'd like to make her defend it.

Could you draft me eight to ten interview questions that actually engage with this specific version of the view instead of letting her recite the standard debate? I'd like at least one question on lucid dreaming (she claims even lucid dreams aren't experiences, and I want to know how she squares that with dreamers who can supposedly signal in real time that they know they're dreaming), one on nightmares and trauma (if nothing is undergone, in what sense can a nightmare be horrible or leave a mark on someone?), and one on the incorporation of outside stimuli (why does my alarm clock end up *inside* the dream, with a whole story built around it, if the dream is assembled after the fact?). For each of those three, give me a follow-up that's keyed to the answer she's most likely to give, so I'm not just nodding along. And maybe close with one question that's really for me: what would actually count as evidence that she's wrong — not a stray anecdote, but something that should make her give up the view?
