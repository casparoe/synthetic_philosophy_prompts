I am preparing a position paper for an interdisciplinary workshop on intergenerational ethics, and I want my own commitments stress-tested before I circulate it. Here is the package I have ended up with.

(1) Temporal neutrality: the date at which a benefit or harm occurs is not in itself a reason to count it for less — a harm in 2300 matters as much as the same harm in 2030.

(2) I am untroubled by long-run policy analysis that uses a positive utility discount rate, provided the rate is generated by the probability that the later generation never exists at all (an extinction hazard) rather than by a taste for the near over the far.

(3) Person-affecting about harm: being brought into existence is not in itself a benefit, non-existence is not a harm, and a policy that merely determines which people come to exist wrongs no one.

(4) The non-identity problem is a real problem, not a verbal one.

(5) We nonetheless owe future generations a great deal. Long-term risks — nuclear, climate, engineered pathogens — are among the most urgent things we can work on, and cost-benefit analysis over horizons of three centuries or more is meaningful and should be quantified rather than left impressionistic.

(6) I find the repugnant conclusion genuinely repugnant: a very large population of lives that are barely worth living is not better than a much smaller population of flourishing lives.

I cannot tell whether these hang together. Please sort them for me. Which pairs are in genuine tension and which only look that way? For the genuine ones, rank them by severity, say exactly which commitment has to give and what it would cost me to give it, and name the cheapest repair. Do not just declare the whole package inconsistent — if some of these sit together comfortably, say so and say why, and if any pair is in tension only under one reading of one of the items, tell me which reading is doing the work.
