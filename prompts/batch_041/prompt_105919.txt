I am a novelist, and for some years I have kept a commonplace book on how the moralists of the past handle drink, chiefly because I keep writing scenes in which drink does work that I suspect no amount of drink can actually do. The current entry is on Plato, and I have drafted the essay below in note form. What I want from you is not an exposition of the Laws but a set of comments: first on accuracy, then on whether the reading is interesting, then on what I should cut or develop. I would much rather be told that I have made Plato say something he does not say than be handled politely.

Thesis: Plato's treatment of drunkenness is not a tolerated vice at the margins of the ideal city but a piece of educational machinery, and its logic runs opposite to the modern therapeutic one. Wine is not a medicine for the drinker; it is an instrument for the legislator, who uses it as a physician uses a drug, to make the soul soft and malleable and to find out what it actually is.

Notes toward the argument:

1. The drinker is made childlike, so drink does not create character but exposes the character that education has already produced. On this view a drunk scene is an examination, and the symposium is an examination hall with an examiner — a sober, older president presiding over a company of the right size, exactly as an army needs a commander who has not been drinking.

2. Hence the age rule, which I find the most arresting detail: the young are kept wholly from wine, the middle-aged drink in measure, and only the older man may drink deeply, because wine was given to make old age young again and to loosen its moroseness. The old man is permitted a return to childhood that the young man is forbidden. I take this to be a deliberate inversion, not a lapse.

3. The same god gives the dance and the wine. Plato places the drinking party inside the same scheme of festivals and choruses by which children are first trained in pleasure and pain, so that wine and music are not opposed forces — the one licensed and the other suspect — but two instruments of one pedagogy. He says of the training in pleasure and pain that it is "the discipline of pleasure and pain which, when rightly ordered, is a principle of education."

4. The tension I want you to judge: if the test is administered only inside a well-regulated symposium, under a sober president, among chosen companions, does it test anything but the symposium? A test that works only in a laboratory may be no test at all. Either Plato is describing an institution that manufactures the virtue it claims to discover, or he is claiming that the drinking party is a genuine sample of the soul's behaviour under loosened conditions. I cannot tell which he means, and I suspect the text will not settle it.

5. What this does to the novelist: the modern drunk scene is usually a device for revealing a private self to the reader. Plato's is a device for revealing a self to a magistrate. Those are different scenes, and I think I have been writing the wrong one for years.

Please tell me where the reading is wrong, whether the tension in point 4 is real or manufactured, and which of the five notes is doing the least work. No citations, please; I care about the argument, not the apparatus. Answer in no more than seven hundred words.
