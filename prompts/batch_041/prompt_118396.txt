Next month I am presenting a paper at a two-day workshop on rationality and agency, and I need a question-and-answer preparation sheet rather than a rehearsal of my own argument. The thesis I am defending is this. Money-pump arguments against intransitive or cyclic preferences are standardly taken to establish that such preferences are irrational: an agent whose preferences run in a circle can be led, through a sequence of trades each of which she welcomes at the moment she makes it, to a position she herself regards as strictly worse than the one she began in, and an agent who can be separated from her money that way is defective. I argue that the argument fails twice over. First, it presupposes without stating it that the agent's preferences are stable across the steps of the pump — that the preference she acts on at the third step is one she would have endorsed at the first. Without that assumption the sequence is not a pump at all but merely a record of changing tastes, and a record of changing tastes is not an argument against anyone. Second, even granting stability, the pump establishes at most that the agent is exploitable, and exploitability is a relational property, one that depends on the presence of a sufficiently clever counterparty, whereas irrationality is supposed to be an intrinsic defect of the agent's preferences. My positive proposal is that we should stop treating exploitability as a proxy for inconsistency and instead ask what a stable intransitive agent actually lacks. What she lacks, I claim, is a coherent completion: her preference structure admits no representation by a single ranking, and the money the pump extracts from her is the price of that lack rather than a proof of unreason. I call the view that the pump is an argument about completion rather than about consistency the completion reading.

The audience is mixed, and I want the objections I will actually receive from each part of it, in the words of the people who will give them. Present will be three or four economists of the revealed-preference stripe, for whom any talk of what an agent prefers in a sense deeper than her choices is unscientific, and who will want to know what choice behavior could possibly distinguish my position from the orthodoxy; two philosophical decision theorists who have worked on money pumps and who will insist that the pump can be run with no stability assumption whatsoever, by fixing the agent's preferences at each step in advance of the sequence; one philosopher of action in the planning tradition, who will say that the entire debate has missed the real issue, namely that diachronic agency demands a kind of self-governance that a merely exploitable agent lacks, so that the interesting question is not whether she is consistent but whether she is a unified agent at all; and one psychologist who studies preference reversals, who will ask what would count, empirically, as evidence for or against my view, and who suspects that the cases I need — stable, intransitive, and actually acted upon — do not occur.

Produce a preparation sheet. Order the objections by how much damage they would do to my position rather than by how likely they are; phrase each one the way the relevant audience member would phrase it; and for each supply a reply I could deliver in roughly two minutes together with a candid note on where that reply is weakest. Tell me what I should concede at the outset, what I should hold firm on, and which single objection has to be answered if the paper is to survive the session. Close with the question I am hoping nobody asks, and a sentence on why it is the one I am hoping nobody asks.
