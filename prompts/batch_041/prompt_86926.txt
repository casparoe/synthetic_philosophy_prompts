You are an expert in archaeological inference and in the theory of how beliefs and institutions can be reconstructed from material remains. You are especially good at the reverse-engineering task I am about to set you: taking a colleague's scattered verdicts on individual finds and recovering the implicit decision rules that generate them. Please think carefully and work step by step. Begin by brainstorming several candidate rules before committing to any one of them, and be willing to conclude that the evidence underdetermines the answer.

Context. I am a doctoral student working on later prehistoric Europe. My supervisor has a long record of published interpretations, and I now have to write up a new excavation in a way that either agrees with her or explains precisely where and why it departs from her. So I need to know not only what she will say about my deposit but which rule she is running, and how confident I can be that it is that rule rather than a neighbouring one.

Her track record, as compactly as I can state it. In every case the question was whether the deposit should be read as ritual or as the residue of ordinary practical activity.

1. A group of bronze weapons and ornaments, several deliberately bent or snapped, buried alone in a peat bog with no settlement or trackway nearby. Her verdict: ritual deposition.
2. A set of complete, undamaged vessels and a stone axe packed into a small pit, sealed with a slab, placed under the threshold of a house. Her verdict: ritual deposition.
3. A pit of potsherds and animal bones mixed in with ordinary domestic refuse in a settlement midden, no arrangement and no special location. Her verdict: ordinary discard, not ritual.
4. The same kinds of sherds and bones, but placed in a pit at the entrance to a settlement enclosure, the pit capped with a layer of clean clay and the bones laid out in a recognisable order. Her verdict: ritual deposition.
5. A burnt store of grain in a granary that had caught fire, charred beams fallen across it. Her verdict: accident, not ritual.
6. A burial in which the body was placed in a flexed position with grave goods, in a cemetery of dozens of similar graves. Her verdict: ritual, but she refused to say anything about the deceased's beliefs.

She also insists on a distinction she says her critics keep collapsing: between a deposit being structured (patterned in a way that ordinary formation processes do not produce) and a deposit being costly (valuable things permanently removed from circulation). She treats these as two separate questions and says that answering one tells you little about the other. She adds that from ritual she will infer practice, and only from practice, if at all, will she infer belief; she thinks most of our field gets this backwards.

My new case. It is a pit inside a settlement, in a domestic courtyard rather than at any boundary or entrance. It contains a large quantity of metalwork, much of it deliberately bent and broken, together with some complete vessels. There is no careful arrangement: the material is jumbled together with ordinary food refuse and ash, and the pit is an ordinary rubbish pit that was left open and gradually filled.

What I want from you:

- What she will most likely say about my pit, and how confident I should be.
- The candidate rules that fit her six earlier verdicts, including at least one that fits all six but is not the one she would articulate. State each rule precisely enough to make predictions, and note any plausible rule that the six verdicts rule out.
- What each surviving candidate predicts about my pit, and exactly where they diverge.
- Your best guess at her actual rule, with your reasons, and the strongest reason someone might prefer a rival.
- The single further find that would best discriminate between the leading candidates: the case where they come apart, described concretely enough that I could go looking for it.
- Finally, how much of her overall picture is a substantive claim about prehistoric religion and how much is a methodological policy about what archaeologists should be licensed to say. Keep the two separate, and tell me which of her six verdicts are doing the real work of the policy.

You need not cite any literature or name any schools or scholars; reason from the verdicts themselves. Where the track record genuinely underdetermines the answer, say so rather than manufacturing a rule that fits everything.
