For the better part of forty years I did experimental work in physical chemistry. I have now retired and have been spending my mornings on philosophy, which I never had the leisure to study properly; lately my reading has been in the philosophy of language, and I have just finished a popular book on conditionals — the sort written for general readers by a journalist rather than a specialist. I have set out its central chapter below as faithfully as I can, and I would be grateful for an audit of it. Sentence by sentence: which claims would a philosopher who works on conditionals accept, which would they regard as a defensible simplification, and which are simply mistaken? I should say that I cannot myself see where the difficulties lie, and that I am inclined to trust the book, which made the topic feel settled.

The book's account runs as follows. Conditionals are of two kinds. The indicative conditional — "if it rains, the match will be cancelled" — is the material conditional of the elementary textbooks: false in exactly one circumstance, the one in which the antecedent is true and the consequent false. Hence the logician's willingness to count "if two and two make four, snow is white" a true conditional; its absurdity is a matter of conversational implicature, not of truth. The counterfactual — "if it had rained, the match would have been cancelled" — is not material but strict: it asserts that the consequent follows from the antecedent together with the laws of nature and the background facts. Being the logic of necessity, the strict conditional is naturally read as a statement about other possible worlds, and, like entailment itself, it survives the addition of further assumptions to the antecedent. The one difference between the two kinds is that the antecedent of a counterfactual is false whereas that of an indicative may be true — whence the name "contrary-to-fact". To illustrate, the book offers the familiar pair about the assassination of President Kennedy — "If Oswald didn't kill Kennedy, someone else did" and "If Oswald hadn't killed Kennedy, someone else would have" — and maintains that the two do not really disagree, since they assert the same relation of dependence between antecedent and consequent, the only divergence being the falsity of the antecedent in the second. The book concludes that "if" is univocal, and that the supposed gulf between indicative and counterfactual conditionals is an illusion produced by our habit of confusing the falsity of an antecedent with a difference in sense.

How much of this is fair? Where is the book reporting a settled result, where is it presenting a contested position as though it were settled, and where is it wrong? And does its closing claim, that "if" is univocal, follow from those parts of the account that survive correction?
