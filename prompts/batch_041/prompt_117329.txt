I'm a backend engineer with no formal training in philosophy. For the past few months I've been sitting in on a consciousness reading group that a friend runs out of her lab, and last week's session was on heterophenomenology — the proposal, associated with Daniel Dennett, that a science of consciousness should bracket the question of whether the subject's experiences are real, treat the subject's spoken and written reports not as testimony about an inner realm but as a text whose author is describing a world, and then construct from those reports a heterophenomenological world that gets checked for internal coherence and for fit with everything else we know. One of the regulars keeps insisting the whole thing is behaviourism wearing a suit; I don't believe that, but I couldn't say why, and what I actually want out of this is a case that makes the method's commitments visible rather than a lecture about it.

So please design a thought experiment in which heterophenomenology, applied exactly as its friends intend, returns a verdict about a subject that the subject rejects on the basis of their own experience, and where the clash is not the usual first-person-versus-third-person standoff that everyone has already heard. Requirements: the subject is an articulate, cooperative adult; the setting is somewhere real (a sleep lab, a neurology clinic, a psychophysics booth, a teaching demonstration); the verdict has to be forced by the bracketing rule itself rather than by an empirical mistake about the subject's brain; and nothing may turn on the subject lying or on anyone deceiving them. Banned: philosophical zombies, inverted spectra, blindsight, split-brain patients, Anton's syndrome, locked-in syndrome, and any technology that does not currently exist. Write the scenario as a single paragraph of no more than 150 words. Then, in at most 500 further words: state the verdict and the steps that generate it; state the subject's own account and say precisely what licenses the method to override it; state what a first-person method would conclude instead and where exactly the two part company; and identify the one feature of the case that is doing the work, what happens to the case once that feature is removed, and the strongest line of reply available to someone who thinks the method handles this case perfectly well. If it turns out the method can absorb the case rather than being embarrassed by it, say so plainly instead of dressing it up as a counterexample. Lead with a single sentence stating what the case is meant to show, and keep the entire reply under 800 words.
