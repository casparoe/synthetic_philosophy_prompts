For about a year now a reading group I'm in has been circling the same question — whether an ant colony can have a mind — and we have reached the stage where each of us can predict the others' next three moves and nothing ever moves. I'm not asking you to settle the case; I want to know why we are stuck, and what kind of stuckness this is. The debate is old enough that it should have converged by now, and it hasn't, and I've started to suspect that the reason isn't that the question is hard but that we are not all asking the same question.

Let me put the two positions as concretely as I can. One side says the colony is an aggregate and nothing more: each ant does its small local thing, no ant is in charge, and when we say the colony 'decides' to relocate the nest or 'remembers' where the sugar is, we are speaking loosely, the way we say a river wants to reach the sea. On this view there is no further entity that has the states; the only subjects present are the individual insects, and colony-talk is a convenient bookkeeping device that we mistake for an ontology. The other side is unimpressed, and the case that started all this is the one I keep coming back to: the colony can carry a memory that outlives every ant in it. What the colony 'learned' about a food source or a threat is not stored in any head — it is stored in the pheromone trails and the architecture of the nest, so a colony can retain and act on information across complete turnover of its parts. Add that the colony regulates its own internal temperature and allocates foragers across patches in something that looks like collective estimation, and this side says the interesting question is not whether the colony has a mind but why we are so sure it doesn't. A friend in the group, who works on insect behaviour, insists the whole thing is empirical and that we will look back on our denials the way we look back on the claim that bees are little machines; another friend thinks it is entirely a dispute about the word 'mind' and that we should stop.

My own hypothesis, offered as a hypothesis, is that we are not disagreeing about ants at all: one side is asking whether there is something it is like to be a colony, and the other is asking whether the colony is an information-processing agent that earns cognitive description, and these are different questions that happen to share a name. A second possibility is that the real fault line is where the system boundary is drawn — whether the unit of cognition is the head or the head plus the trails and the nest — and that this looks like a discovery about nature when it is actually a stipulation we are each making and then reporting as a finding. I would like you to tell me which of these it is, or what it is instead, and to be specific about the structure of the impasse: is it verbal, or substantive and merely unresolved; do the sides differ over facts, over standards of evidence, over which cases count as data, or over bedrock intuitions that neither can argue for; is there a hidden empirical question doing the work; or are we simply facing a question that is hard? Then say what, given your diagnosis, would count as progress — a distinction, an experiment, a formal result, a change of question — or argue that none should be expected and why. I would rather you commit to one diagnosis and defend it than lay out a menu of options for me to choose from.
