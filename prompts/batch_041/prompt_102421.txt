For some time now I have been quietly troubled by a puzzle about choice, and I should like you to turn it into a short story.

Picture someone who is offered two decisions in turn. In the first, they may either take a certain £500, or take a gamble that gives them £500 with probability 0.85, £2,500 with probability 0.14, and nothing with probability 0.01. Suppose they take the certain £500. In the second decision, they may either take a gamble that gives them £500 with probability 0.15 and nothing with probability 0.85, or take a gamble that gives them £2,500 with probability 0.14 and nothing with probability 0.86. Suppose the same person now takes the second of these.

What nags at me is this: the two gambles in the second decision are exactly the two options from the first decision with the same 85 per cent share of £500 stripped out of each. It seems to follow that anyone who prefers the certain £500 in the first case ought, on pain of inconsistency, to prefer the corresponding gamble in the second — and yet I am fairly sure that I, and most people I know, would choose the other way round. I do not know whether this puzzle has a proper name, and I do not much care; what interests me is the shape of the thing.

Please write a short story in which a character faces precisely these two choices, in this order, and makes precisely this reversal: the certain £500 in the first decision, the £2,500 gamble in the second. I should like the story to take the reversal seriously rather than treating it as a simple blunder — to let us feel why the certain sum looks so different from the very same sum buried inside a gamble, and why the large prize looks so much more tempting once the sure thing has been taken away. It would please me if the story left the reader uncertain whether the character has been sensible, foolish, or simply human. There is no need for the narrator to lecture or to explain the mathematics; let the situation speak for itself.

Please write it in British English.
