I am putting together a session for a graduate reading group on the intellectual prehistory of AI risk, and the figure I keep circling back to is John von Neumann. He is useful precisely because he died in February 1957 — before the word "software" had settled into its present sense, before the internet, before recombinant DNA, before anyone had seriously proposed training a single model on a large fraction of written human language — and because he left behind a compact and unusually specific set of commitments that one can reason *from* rather than merely gesture at. What I want from you is a careful historical counterfactual: not "what would a clever man of his era have said?" but what von Neumann, given the arguments he actually made, would most plausibly have thought about developments that postdate him. Treat the following as fixed points, and correct me if I have mischaracterised any of them: his diagnosis that technological progress is outrunning the geographical and political units capable of containing it, set out in his *Fortune* essay of June 1955, "Can We Survive Technology?", where the industrial revolution's three gifts are more and cheaper energy, more and faster communications, and "more and easier controls of human actions and reactions"; his picture, in the lectures published after his death as *The Computer and the Brain*, of a nervous system assembled from unreliable components that buys reliability by statistical means, together with his suspicion that the brain's language is not the language of mathematics; his architecture for self-reproducing automata, in which a machine's own description is copied without being interpreted and an infinite regress is thereby avoided; his conviction that two-person zero-sum conflict admits a complete mathematical analysis while many-player games do not; and the Cold War hawkishness that made him, to a good many of his colleagues, the most frightening voice in the room. The remark for which he is now most often quoted — that accelerating technological change gives the appearance of approaching "some essential singularity in the history of the race beyond which human affairs, as we know them, could not continue" — reaches us only through Stanislaw Ulam's recollection of a conversation, published after von Neumann's death, so treat it as suggestive rather than as doctrine.

Then work through the following, giving for each a prediction, the specific von Neumann argument it rests on, and a confidence level, with the reasoning shown rather than asserted: (1) large language models, and the claim that general competence can be acquired by absorbing human text at scale — would he have seen this as the crossing of a complexity threshold of the sort he expected, or as a confusion about what computation is for? (2) the alignment problem — control of a more capable agent, a problem about values, an engineering problem of reliability-under-redundancy of the kind he had already solved in principle, or a question he would have judged ill-posed? (3) competition between states over artificial intelligence, read as a game he would have called zero-sum, and whether that reading survives contact with the modern literature on arms races, commitment devices, and unilateralist temptations; (4) surveillance and social media as instruments of exactly the "controls of human actions and reactions" he catalogued; (5) deliberate intervention in climate, which he was willing to contemplate as a serious prospect when almost nobody else was; (6) engineered biology, machine self-replication, and the copying of minds, models, or persons — three faces of one worry, on his own account of reproduction; and (7) the existential-risk idiom itself, and whether he would have recognised it as continuous with his own question about survival or dismissed it as a moralising distortion of a strategic problem.

Finally — and this matters to me more than any of the individual verdicts — tell me where the exercise breaks down. Where would his own commitments have made him a poor guide: his confidence that crises are manageable by rational design, his willingness to think in terms of preemptive strikes, his near-total indifference to distributive questions, or the reported return to religion in the last months of his life, which his friends and biographers have read in mutually incompatible ways? Where is the honest answer that the evidence runs out and we cannot know? Where would he simply have been wrong, in a way that a well-informed contemporary should say so plainly? And flag at least two places where my framing above has probably smuggled a post-1957 assumption into his mouth. No citations, no bibliography, no page numbers, no invented quotations — I would rather have "he argued something like this" than a phrase you cannot vouch for. Write it as an argued reconstruction rather than a list of verdicts, as long as the argument requires, and be willing to conclude that some of these questions would not have made sense to him at all.
