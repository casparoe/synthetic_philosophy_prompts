Bit of an odd one, this one. I've been reading about Otto Neurath — Vienna Circle guy, ran the Social and Economic Museum in Red Vienna, did all those little pictograms with Gerd Arntz, the Vienna Method of Picture Statistics that later got renamed ISOTYPE. His thing was a picture language for science and for the public: barrels and little men and ships instead of words, so a housing report or a trade table could be read by anyone in any country with no translation and no lecture. He wanted something universal and neutral, pictures that carry the facts without dragging their interpretive baggage along.

But Neurath is also the guy who said we never get to hold our claims up against bare reality — no first philosophy, we only ever compare statements with statements, and we're like sailors rebuilding a boat at sea, plank by plank, never docking.

So: give me an example of a conceptual issue that sits right in the seam between those two commitments. Take one ordinary scientific or statistical claim, describe in words the picture that supposedly just shows the fact, and then show me where the picture is already doing work that the fact doesn't contain — a classification decision, a convention, a bit of grammar that had to sneak in. Bonus if you pick one where somebody on the other side of the dispute would have to draw a different picture of the very same data, so that "just look at the picture" settles nothing. Then say wether Neurath has any way out of this, or wether the picture language is just ordinary language wearing a pictogram costume.
