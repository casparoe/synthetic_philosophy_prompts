I want a credence from you on a narrow claim in the philosophy of mind, together with the decomposition that produces it. State the claim precisely before you assess it, and give me a number rather than a survey.

The claim to be assessed: the paradox of phenomenal judgment is a sound and non-question-begging argument against global epiphenomenalism.

Fix the target before answering. Take global epiphenomenalism to be the conjunction of three theses: (a) every phenomenal property of a subject is necessitated by some physical property of that subject at that time; (b) no phenomenal property is a cause of, or causally relevant to, any physical event, including the physical events that constitute the subject's reports and judgments; and (c) the physical events that constitute a subject's phenomenal reports are caused by physical properties alone. The argument to be assessed runs from (a) through (c) via the observation that our phenomenal judgments — a subject's judgment that it is now in pain, for example — reliably track the phenomenal states they are about, and that this reliability cannot be explained if the phenomenal state contributes nothing to the judgment. I want your probability that this argument succeeds, where succeeding means that its premises are defensible, that no step begs the question against the epiphenomenalist, and that the conclusion that global epiphenomenalism is false actually follows.

Decompose rather than summarize. At minimum, give me a separate probability for each of the following, with one line of justification each, and then tell me how they combine into the final number:

1. Whether the reliability of phenomenal judgment requires a causal connection between the judgment and the phenomenal state it represents, or whether a non-causal necessitation relation can underwrite reliability just as well — for instance, the judgment and the phenomenal state both being necessitated by a common physical base, so that the judgment tracks the phenomenal state without the phenomenal state doing any causing. I want your probability that the stricter, causal requirement is the correct one.

2. Whether the psychophysical correlations invoked in (a) are nomic rather than accidental, and what a merely accidental correlation would do to the tracking claim.

3. Whether the zombie symmetry cuts against the epiphenomenalist. If a physical duplicate of me with no phenomenal states would issue all the same judgments, does that show my judgments are not sensitive to my phenomenal states in the way tracking requires, or is it a red herring because the duplicate is nomically impossible in any world where (a) holds?

4. Whether a deflationary or error-theoretic reading of phenomenal concepts is available and acceptable — one on which my judgment that I am in pain is really about the physical state, and the apparent aboutness directed at the phenomenal state is a confusion. If that reading is right, does the paradox dissolve, and is the price acceptable?

5. Whether the argument tacitly assumes a causal or tracking theory of content and is therefore question-begging.

6. Whether restricting epiphenomenalism to intrinsic phenomenal properties, while allowing representational properties to do causal work, escapes the argument — that is, whether the paradox targets phenomenal properties or only phenomenal concepts.

Then, in order: give the resulting credence; name the sub-question the final number is most sensitive to; name the one you are least confident in; and say what single piece of evidence or argument would move the number most — a case in which a subject's phenomenal judgments came apart from its phenomenal states, say, or a demonstration that reliability can be had without causation.
