I work in the people-analytics group of a mid-sized technology company, and I have been asked to write an internal explainer on the assumptions behind our performance-management system, largely because two of our practices have begun to look inconsistent to the engineers. The first is our annual calibration: managers must sort their reports into a fixed distribution — roughly the top twenty percent, a broad middle, and a bottom ten — and anyone who lands in the bottom band two years running is managed out, even when their absolute performance has not slipped and even when their manager would rate them as meeting expectations in an ordinary year. The second is our spot-award program, which pays a lump sum to anyone who ships something exceptional, with no cap and no quota; here the company insists it is simply giving people what their work deserves. The puzzle my colleagues keep raising is that the first practice seems to say that whether you deserve to keep your job depends on how your colleagues performed, whereas the second seems to say that what you deserve depends only on your own work.

I would like the explainer to reconstruct what theory of desert each practice presupposes, or could only make sense under — whether desert is comparative and positional (there must be a bottom, and whoever is at the bottom is by definition undeserving relative to the others) or non-comparative and absolute (you deserve some reward in virtue of your own contribution, whatever anyone else has done) — and to say which of the two the calibration system genuinely requires rather than merely happens to fit. I am especially interested in whether the firm can hold both commitments at once, and if it cannot, what precisely would have to change: whether the calibration would have to drop the vocabulary of desert in favor of the vocabulary of incentives, or whether the spot awards could be redescribed so that they too are implicitly comparative and rationed by the field rather than by the individual.

Please also address whether these practices presuppose that people deserve reward for effort, for talent, or only for the value of the outcome they produce, and whether the appeal to desert is doing any real work in the practice or is a decorative layer laid over what is really a story about competition for scarce promotions and limited budgets. I do not need citations or a literature review; I need the implicit theory set out plainly, the distinction between what the practices strictly presuppose and what they merely accommodate, and the tension either resolved or shown to be genuine.
