After thirty years in experimental physics I retired, and for the past two years I have been sitting in on an upper-level course in the theory of knowledge. The instructor has now asked me to help mark the final examination, and I would like your assistance with one script. The question she set was this: state the two dogmas that Quine identifies in "Two Dogmas of Empiricism," reconstruct his argument against the first of them, explain how he connects it to the second, and assess how far the argument succeeds. The candidate's answer reads as follows.

Quine's target is the logical empiricists' assumption that there is a principled difference between analytic truths, which are true by virtue of meaning alone, and synthetic truths, which are true by virtue of how the world is. He identifies two dogmas: first, the belief in this analytic-synthetic distinction; second, reductionism, the belief that each meaningful statement gets its meaning from some logical construction out of terms referring to immediate experience. His main argument against the first is that the notion of analyticity cannot be explained without circularity. To call a statement analytic is to say its predicate is synonymous with its subject; synonymy is what a definition delivers; but a definition is only a report of a synonymy we already recognise, so definition presupposes the very notion it was supposed to explain, and we go round the circle again. Nor is the circle broken by appealing to interchangeability of terms in all contexts without change of truth-value, since that criterion either fails in opaque contexts or tacitly requires a notion of necessity, and so of analyticity, to do the work.

He then argues that the two dogmas are of a piece: it is reductionism that makes analyticity look respectable, because if each statement could be checked against experience one at a time, then a statement that no experience could tell against would indeed be true by meaning alone. Give up the idea that statements confront experience individually and the motivation for the distinction collapses. What replaces it is a holistic picture: our beliefs form a web, and experience bears on the web as a whole rather than on statements one by one. His conclusion is that no statement is immune to revision — even the law of excluded middle might be surrendered to save physics — and, since holism leaves no statement more central than any other, the analytic-synthetic distinction is not merely unclarified but false: there simply are no analytic statements, and every statement turns out to be synthetic. He insists that this is not a retreat from empiricism but a more thoroughgoing version of it.

Please grade the script on our four-point scale — fail, low pass, high pass, distinction — and then give me the comments that justify the grade. I am new enough to this field that I would rather see the reasoning than be handed a verdict to take on trust: tell me which of the candidate's claims are sound and why, which are loose or overstated and why, and what a stronger answer would have put in their place. You need not supply references; what concerns me is whether the philosophical moves hold up, not who said what first.

Write your assessment as continuous prose — no headings, no numbered points or bullets, no bold or italicised text, no tables — the way one would write a considered letter. Lead with the grade and the one or two things that matter most, then work through the rest in the order in which they arise in the script.
