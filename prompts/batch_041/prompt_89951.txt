I want to argue with Adam Smith about the British Empire, and I would like you to play him — not the textbook free-trader but the full moral philosopher of 1776, the author of both The Theory of Moral Sentiments and the Inquiry into the Nature and Causes of the Wealth of Nations, speaking in his own first-person voice and reasoning only with the concepts and information available to him at that date plus whatever I tell you. He holds that defence is the first duty of the sovereign and often the only good reason for regulations that are otherwise burdensome; that merchants and manufacturers are the loudest and most self-serving political faction in Britain; and that the exclusive colonial trade is a dead weight on the colonies and on Europe alike. In the fourth book of the Inquiry he also proposed that, once the American colonies grew great enough to contribute substantially to the common defence, Britain should either let them go or admit them to the imperial parliament in proportion to what they contributed — a federal union rather than a separation. He may concede small points and should grant whatever is plainly true, but he must not abandon his main position; he should push back, ask me for facts he lacks, and speak as a philosopher of his century rather than as a modern commentator. Keep each reply to a substantial paragraph or two, and stay in character until I say stop.

Here is my opening move. Your federal union proposal gives the game away. You yourself say that the merchant interest dominates Westminster and that the colonies' trade has been sacrificed to it; you grant that the colonists are lightly taxed and that their assemblies are more popular in character than our own. So why should Americans cross an ocean to sit in a Parliament already captured by the very interests you denounce, and why should their more equal societies be subordinated to a court and a merchant lobby that would treat their seats as one more piece of patronage to be bought? The honest inference from your own premises is that Britain should renounce sovereignty outright and let the colonies go, keeping only the commerce of an independent ally — not purchase a few American votes in order to preserve the monopoly under a new name. Which of your premises do you give up?

After you have answered in character, add one short paragraph out of character: state how confident you are that this is the historical Smith's actual position as of 1776, and name the single point in your reconstruction of him that you are least sure of.
