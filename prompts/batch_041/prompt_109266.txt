I'm in a reading group that's been working through the vagueness literature and we've hit a wall. Two of us have been talking past each other for weeks now: one is a dyed-in-the-wool epistemicist (the Williamson line -- there really is a sharp cutoff for "bald," we just can't know where it is), and the other is a supervaluationist (borderline cases are truth-value gaps, "bald" comes out neither true nor false, and what we're entitled to say about a borderline case is governed by what holds on every admissible precisification). The weird thing is that they don't seem to disagree about the sorites argument itself -- both think it's valid and that some premise has to fail, both accept some version of tolerance, both think a heap of 10,000 grains is a heap and one grain isn't -- yet every attempt to pin down where the actual disagreement lives turns into a shouting match about higher-order vagueness, about whether "determinately" is doing any real work, and about whether the supervaluationist's "neither true nor false" is a genuine third status or just a fancy way of saying "we don't know."

So here's what I want: the joint statement these two could actually sign after a good-faith adversarial collaboration. Structure it as (a) a numbered list of claims both sides can endorse -- about what the sorites argument establishes, about what tolerance comes to, about how borderline cases behave, about what each view is forced to give up; (b) the residual disagreements, each one written in wording its own side would accept for itself, not strawmanned; (c) the cruxes -- the sub-questions such that settling one would actually move at least one of them; and (d) for each crux, an argument or case that both sides would agree bears on it, with each side's prediction about how it comes out recorded in advance. Two things I especially want it to confront head-on: the "no sharp boundaries" intuition -- why the epistemicist's cutoff feels so obviously wrong, and whether that feeling is evidence or just a fact about us -- and whether any chunk of this dispute is really verbal, i.e. two people using "true" and "determinately" for different jobs and then arguing about which job is the real one. If you think part of it is verbal, say exactly which part and why; if you think none of it is, say what would have to be true for the verbal diagnosis to be tempting.

Please keep the agreed-claims section genuinely non-vacuous, since I'll be presenting this to the group and "both agree the sorites is puzzling" would get me kicked out of the room. I'm also curious whether either side has to concede something embarrassing about linguistic competence or about why the cutoff, if there is one, should be unknowable in principle -- I defintely don't want a version where both sides get to keep everything they like.
