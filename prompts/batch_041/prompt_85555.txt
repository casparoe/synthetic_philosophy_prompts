I'm teaching a third-year option on philosophy of language this term and I've accidentally let the linguistic relativity unit eat half the module. The students arrive all very confident that Whorf was simply refuted, which misses the point — nobody serious defends the strong determinist line (language doesn't put a ceiling on what you can think), but the weaker claim, that the grammar you're obliged to use when you speak leaves habits behind, that it shapes what you bother to notice and what your memory bothers to keep, that one is much harder to kill off. Last week we got onto evidentiality, the languages in parts of Amazonia (and elsewhere) where you can't simply report that he went to the river — the verb makes you declare how you know: I saw it, I heard it, I inferred it, I was told it, I'm only assuming it. One of my sharpest students then asked the question I couldn't answer, which is what happens when the anthropologist writes the grammar down. Her sentences about the language are themselves second-hand, and English has no particle for that.

So I'd like to close the term with a poem, and I want the form to carry the argument rather than just decorate it. Could you write one — five short stanzas, 15 to 20 lines, rhyme optional — in which every line is tagged for its evidence, something like (saw), (heard), (inferred), (told), (assume), arranged so the poem opens in confident observation and ends somewhere much shakier. The speaker should be someone working out whether her language shapes what she can think, using the usual suspects — the two Russian blues, absolute compass directions in place of left and right — but the tags should keep tripping her up, so that by the end neither she nor the reader can tell which claims about other people's languages she actually knows and which she is merely repeating. Someone in the field should feel the poem gets the difference between the determinist claim and the weaker "shapes your attention while you speak" claim right, and someone who's never heard of Whorf should still find it a decent poem. Dont let it resolve tidily; the tags should leave the whole thing under a cloud, since thats honestly where my own view sits. Finish with a single line that starts In one sentence: and gives me one sentence on what the poem ended up doing.
