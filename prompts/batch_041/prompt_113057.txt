A friend of mine works on training chatbots and he described the standard setup to me: you pay a few thousand people to look at two model answers side by side and click the one they like better, you fit a single number-scoring function to all those clicks, and then you tune the model to score high. He calls this "just measuring what people want." I said it looks like it smuggles in a whole theory of what a preference is, and now we're argueing about it, so I want your read.

What theory of preference does that pipeline presuppose? A few specific things I want you to take a position on, because they're where I think the interesting action is:

1. The clicks happen in a strange little room — someone paid by the hour, staring at two chunks of text, with no stake in the outcome — but the preference is supposed to be about a chatbot doing your taxes or talking to your kid at bedtime. Does the method actually need those two settings to be the same thing, or can it get by treating the click as a proxy that gets read off in the wrong environment and then extrapolated? And if it is a proxy, what exactly is it a proxy for?

2. People flip their answer when you swap the order or reword the options. Average enough of them and you get a very stable number that no individual person ever held. Is that the practice measuring a real preference (a population-level fact) or manufacturing one? Does the practice have the resources to tell the difference?

3. Suppose the preference someone reveals is one they'd disavow on reflection — they click the flattering answer, and five minutes later say they wish they hadn't. The pipeline can't represent "what they'd endorse after thinking." Which of the two does it end up tracking, and does that count as a presupposition of the method or just a limitation of it?

4. It wants one scalar. Say what that rules out — incommensurable values, preferences that are lexicographic, preferences that are about the process rather than the outcome, preferences that only make sense relative to some other person. Are those just hard cases the method handles badly, or does it assume they don't exist?

Then pull it together: which of these are things the practice strictly needs to be true, which are conveniences it could drop without changing anything, and whether the package is even internally consistent. And if you swapped the implicit theory for the most plausible rival, what would the pipeline have to look like instead — different data, different objective, different deployment?
