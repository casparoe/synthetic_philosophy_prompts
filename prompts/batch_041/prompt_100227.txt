I'm putting together a grad seminar on conditionals and I've managed to confuse myself, so I'm hoping you can straighten me out — tell me where this reasoning goes wrong.

Lewis's trivialty results show that the probability of an indicative conditional can't be the conditional probability, i.e. Adams' thesis is dead. But Adams' thesis was basically the only thing that made 'if' sentences come out with the right probabilities — the material conditional gives you P(not-A or C), which is wrong whenever C is antecedently unlikely. So if the only route to getting the probabilities right is ruled out by triviality, and the material conditional is the only truth-functional reading going, it feels like I'm forced into one of two corners: either (a) indicative conditionals have no truth conditions at all, or (b) they're material conditionals and we just have to accept that everyone's probability judgements about them are systematically irrational.

Neither can be right — half the literature seems to carry on assigning probabilities to conditionals like nothing happened. So what am I missing? Is triviality just a much narrower result than I'm taking it to be, or was Adams' thesis never about truth conditions in the first place? And if it's the latter, then what is the probability of an indicative conditional the probability *of*, if not of a proposition?

Related: is there some third option I'm skipping between the material reading and the no-truth-conditions reading? I keep feeling the possible-worlds / variably-strict crowd (Stalnaker, Lewis) must slot in somewhere, but I can't see where they fit if triviality is supposedly blocking the probabilistic route. I'd rather not tell my students something I'd have to walk back next week, so I'd appreciate you being blunt about which of these moves I'm getting wrong and why.
