I've been roped into giving the comencement address at the small liberal arts college where I teach philosophy, and I'm trying to write something that isn't the usual 'follow your dreams' mush. Here's my idea, and I want you to write the actual speech. The twist is that I want to build it around the surprise exam paradox -- the one where the teacher says there'll be a surprise exam next week, the students reason that it can't be Friday (because if it hasn't happened by Thursday night they'd know it's Friday), then it can't be Thursday, and so on until they conclude it can't happen at all, and then the teacher gives it on Wednesday and it's a genuine surprise. Half the graduating class took my logic course and we spent a week on this, so they'll know exactly what I'm doing; the other half are parents and grandparents who've never heard of it and will be wondering why the philosophy guy is talking about exams at a graduation. I want the paradox to actually do the work of the speech, not just be a cute hook. My old logic professor used to say the students' mistake was treating 'surprise' as a fact about the exam rather than about their own state of knowledge -- they thought if they could prove it couldn't be a surprise, then it couldn't be a surprise, which is like proving you can't be caught off guard. I think there's something there about how we tell young people to plan their lives: we hand them a schedule and a set of predictions, and then we're shocked when the important things arrive unannounced.

Please write the address. It should be warm and funny in places, and it should land for the parents as well as the philosophy majors -- no jargon, no named philosophers, no quotes, and don't even tell them the name of the paradox, just let it unfold. It should not end with a prediction about their futures, because that would be the exact mistake the paradox is about. Maybe it can end by actually springing something on them, or by admitting that I can't know what's coming. It should be about ten minutes when read aloud. I don't want it to be a lecture on the paradox; I want the paradox to shape what the speech can say about the future, ambition, disappointment, and surprise. I've been avoiding writing this for three weeks and my partner is sick of hearing about it, so any help is appreciated.
