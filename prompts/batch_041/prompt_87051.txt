For most of my adult life I went by the surname I was born with, and a few months ago I changed it — paperwork and sentiment, nothing more — and I have been quietly astonished ever since by how much *information* people extract from the change. "Daniel Ruiz is now Daniel Ashworth" lands as news; "Daniel Ashworth is Daniel Ashworth" lands as nothing; and yet there is only one of me. I had met Frege's puzzle about identity before, in the usual astronomical dress, and had always filed it away as a curiosity about how we talk about the morning star and the evening star; now it has become, in a small and slightly embarrassing way, about me, and I find I want to think it through properly instead of reciting the textbook solution. I belong to a book club that has just put Stevenson's *Strange Case of Dr Jekyll and Mr Hyde* on the list for next month, and it strikes me that the novella might be a far better laboratory for this puzzle than the tired star example — the whole plot is the slow discovery that two names, the respectable physician and the murderous Hyde, have been picking out a single man all along.

What I would like from you is a reading of the novella through the philosophy of identity statements: Frege's distinction between the sense of a name and its reference, the notion of a mode of presentation, and the later direct-reference reaction to it (rigid designation, and the idea that some identities are necessary yet knowable only a posteriori). Concretely, I want you to press two questions. First, does the story vindicate the Fregean thought that the statement linking Jekyll and Hyde is informative precisely because the two names carry different senses while sharing a reference — or does it resist that picture, since the two names seem to track genuinely different properties (Hyde is smaller, younger, morally monstrous) and to be tied to different stretches of a life, so that we may be dealing not with one object under two modes of presentation but with something closer to two persons sharing one body? Second, is the identity in question necessary, or merely true of this man on these occasions — and does the novella itself take a side, or leave the metaphysics underdetermined? I would like the reading to be faithful in both directions: not the story reduced to an illustration of Frege, and not Frege bent to fit the story. Anchor the case in scenes you can name without quoting — the by-street door, the will that names Hyde as beneficiary, the trampling of the child, the murder of Sir Danvers Carew, Lanyon's account of the drawer of salts, and Jekyll's own final statement — and say which of those a reader who rejects your reading would lean on against you.

Please write this as criticism, not as a survey: no citations, no reading list, no quotations, since I have the text and the Frege already and want the argument rather than a bibliography. Finish by being hard on yourself. Tell me where this reading is most likely to overreach, or to trade on a loose analogy between a story and a piece of logic; and identify the one consideration — a scene, a line of argument, or a point in Frege or his successors — that would push you toward the opposite verdict, so that I can see what would have to be true for you to be wrong.
