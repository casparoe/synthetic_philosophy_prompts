I'm a novelist — three books out, a fourth that's currently a disaster — and I keep getting dragged into an argument with a friend of mine who actually reads philosphy. The argument is whether a morally rotten artist can still make good art, and separately whether a book with a genuinely nasty moral vision can be a good book, or whether the nastiness makes it worse as a book automatically. Some people seem to think the two ledgers are seperate: gorgeous prose, rotten soul, no interaction between them. Others think a moral flaw in the work just is an artistic flaw, full stop. Others think the rottenness can even be a point in the work's favor.

I don't speak that language. I speak workshop language. So take those positions and restate each one in the vocabulary I actually use — voice, narrator versus the person who wrote it, the implied author (the version of me the book makes you invent), what a character says versus what the book itself endorses, subtext, unreliable narration, whether an ending is earned, whether the book is in on the joke or not.

Then the part I really want: tell me what didn't survive the trip. I've got a suspicion my vocabulary can't even state the separatist position cleanly, because in my world "earned" and "the book knows more than its narrator" already sound like moral judgments wearing craft clothes — we don't just say the ending fails, we say it betrays the reader. So does that mean my whole way of talking is secretly moralist and I've been defending a position I don't have words for? Or does the leftover show the philosophers are drawing a line finer than anything a book can actually be?
