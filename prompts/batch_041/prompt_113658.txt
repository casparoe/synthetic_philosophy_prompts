Please act as a professional philosopher of science with a special interest in philosophical method — in the epistemology of thought experiments and in the use of intuitions as evidence — and think carefully and explicitly before committing to a verdict. I am a retired experimental physicist who has spent three years reading philosophy, and I have reconstructed an argument that I suspect proves too much but cannot fault.

(1) A thought experiment is a description of a case together with an invitation to judge it. (2) Such a description always underdetermines the case: it leaves open the parties' capacities, the surrounding world, which features are essential and which incidental, and what else is true. (3) So judging the case requires filling those gaps, and the only material available for the filling is the reader's prior beliefs about how things work. (4) The verdict is therefore a joint product of the description and of the beliefs the reader supplied. (5) A case discriminates between rival theories only if the theories yield different verdicts on it, and only if it is the same case for both. (6) But the beliefs that do the filling are exactly what the rivals dispute; each therefore supplies different gap-fillers and reads a different case. (7) Hence no thought experiment can discriminate between rival theories; it can only restate what each theory assumed. (8) So appeals to what we intuit about imagined cases have no evidential weight, and arguing from them should be abandoned.

I want to keep (1) through (4) — they seem like plain sense to me, and in my old trade nobody concluded from the theory-ladenness of observation that experiment was worthless. Where does (4) turn into (7)? Begin by listing the two or three most plausible places to break the chain and what each would predict about the rest; then commit to one, explain why it looked innocent enough to slide past, and price the alternatives, saying what I must give up if I keep that step and reject another instead. Two further things. First, the phrase intuition pump seems to name something narrower than a thought experiment: does the argument run together the case used to exhibit an unacceptable consequence of a theory and the case used to establish that something is possible at all, and does that conflation matter? Second, my own verdicts shift when I re-describe a case in the vocabulary of physiology rather than in that of the agent's reasons and intentions — does that support the argument or expose a gap in it? Close by naming the premise a defender of thought experiments should be least willing to surrender, and the price of surrendering it.
