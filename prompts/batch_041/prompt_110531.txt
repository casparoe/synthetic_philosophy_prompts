Next month I'm giving the guest address at the citizenship ceremony in our town hall — four minutes, squeezed between the oath and the tea and biscuits. The audience is about thirty new citizens, several of them from republics, one of whom is my friend Nadia, who asked me fairly pointedly why she has to swear herself to a man and his heirs rather than to the country or the constitution; plus the mayor and a few councillors who'll be listening for anything that sounds like a dig.

What I want the speech to actually be about is the gap between the person and the office — that the Crown isn't the human being, it's an office that never dies, a corporation sole I think it's called, whose powers are entirely real in law and unusable in practice — and that we ask people to promise themselves to a placeholder. I also cant get over the contrast with the countries that wrote the monarch's role down and emptied it out (Sweden and Japan, I think) while ours stays unwritten and formally full; that feels like the strange heart of the whole thing and I'd like it doing some work in the speech. My own half-thought, which you can defend or drop: promising yourself to a person is easier to keep than promising yourself to a text, because a person can be let down in a way that a text can't.

Don't let it turn into a civics lecture or a pamphlet, and don't settle anything by saying the monarchy is above politics as if that answered the question.

Format: the speech itself, roughly 600 words, then a section headed 'Why it's built that way', about 200 words, telling me which bits are load-bearing and where I'm most likely to get into trouble. British English please.
