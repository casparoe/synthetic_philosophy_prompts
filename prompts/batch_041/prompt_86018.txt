I'm writing a seminar paper on social ontology and I keep getting stuck on wether "generations" are real kinds or just a filing system, and my roommate and I have been going around in circles about it at the kitchen table for about a week. Here's the framework I've landed on and I want you to build a case that pries it apart. Take three ways a cohort could be a real kind rather than a convenient bin: (a) the members were shaped by a shared formative event — the same war, famine, or new technology arriving while they were kids; (b) the label itself does causal work — the name is in circulation while they're young and they end up acting like the thing the name describes, so the kind is at least partly performative; and (c) the philosophy-of-biology picture where a kind is real when a cluster of properties hangs together because of some shared underlying mechanism, rather than because somebody drew the boundary. I also half-remember an old sociology-of-knowledge distinction between a generation that's just a bunch of people who happen to occupy the same spot in the social structure and a generation that's an actual bonded group aware of itself as a unit, and I want that in the mix too — but don't worry about getting sources or attributions right, I care about the case, not the citations.

So: construct me a thought experiment with two cohorts that are alike in every single respect except one, and that one difference should be the direction of causation between label and behaviour. In the first cohort the label is already in the air while the members are young and it shapes what they do, so you get a genuine feedback loop running from name to conduct. In the second, the pattern of behaviour shows up first and the name gets stuck onto it afterwards by outsiders who are just trying to describe what they see. Both cohorts need the same shared formative event, the same amount of internal variation, the same level of self-identification (I want members of both saying yeah, that's us), the same cultural distinctiveness, the same size, the same everything — the label/behaviour arrow is the only thing that moves. Constraints: invent the cohorts and the country and the event; do not use Boomers, Millennials, Gen Z, or any real cohort or real historical episode, because I need the variable isolated and not confounded, and don't lean on any demographic numbers, since you can't look them up and neither can I. Keep it realistic — ordinary modern society, no sci-fi, nothing that couldn't happen in a mid-sized country over about thirty years. And please don't just stipulate the conclusion: the theories have to actually disagree because of the case, not because you told them to.

What I want back: lead with your bottom line in the first sentence — is the direction of the label/behaviour arrow the thing that decides whether a generation is a real kind — and then give me the reasoning. Give me the scenario, then walk through what each of the three positions above says about both cohorts and why, then the honest part: which feature of the case is doing the work, what happens to the disagreement if I delete that feature and make the two cohorts identical on the label question as well, and how the side that comes out badly would attack the case (I want the strongest version of that attack, not a strawman).
