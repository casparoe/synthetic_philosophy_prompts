I'm a first-year MA student in political theory, and I've been trying to get straight the three-way contrast between negative liberty, positive liberty, and the republican idea of freedom as non-domination. I think I follow the negative/positive contrast well enough — the one is about the absence of interference, the other about self-mastery and being one's own sovereign — but the republican ideal keeps collapsing in my hands into one or the other, and I can't tell whether that's my own failure or a genuine redundancy in the concept. When I read that to be free is to be one's own master and not to have one's life governed by the will of another, that sounds like precisely the self-determination the positive conception was after; and when republicans insist that what matters is not being subject to another's arbitrary power, that sounds like negative liberty with a hedge — freedom as the secure absence of interference, i.e. interference that is improbable, or that no one has the standing to impose. On that second reading the whole view looks like the old negative ideal with a modal qualifier bolted on, and I'm tempted to conclude that the only real difference between the negative and republican conceptions is that republicans fret about the likelihood of interference rather than its occurrence. A friend in my cohort insists it's obviously just negative liberty in more careful dress; another says it's a disguised perfectionism that smuggles in a particular way of living.

Could you straighten this out for me: is republican non-domination a version of positive liberty, a version of negative liberty, or genuinely a third thing? And whichever it is, what work is the notion of domination doing that interference-talk cannot do on its own — in particular, what does it let us say about cases where no one is actually interfering with anyone? I'd like you to lead with the bottom line: one sentence stating which of the three it is and why, and then the argument that earns it. Please keep the whole reply under 350 words.
