Last semester I signed up for an intro course on Indian philosophy mostly to fill a requirement, and I ended up weirdly obsessed with the Nyāya school — the ones who turn everything into a debate about what counts as a valid way of knowing (perception, inference, analogy, and the testimony of reliable speakers). We spent a week on their account of how you understand a sentence, and I've been chewing on it since. As I understood it, four things have to line up before words give you a cognition of what's meant: the words have to expect each other (ākāṅkṣā), so 'bring' leaves you hanging until you know what's to be brought, whereas a jumble like 'cow horse man' never hangs together at all; the meanings have to be compatable (yogyatā), which is why 'he sprinkles the plant with fire' is heard as defective rather than as a weird instruction, even though the grammar is fine; the words have to be heard close enough together in time (sannidhi), so if I say 'cow' now and 'horse' an hour later you haven't heard a sentence; and finally you have to get the speaker's intention (tātparya), which is what tells you whether 'the bank' is a riverbank or a place to keep money.

My problem is that I can't see why that's the right list, or why it has four items instead of two. It feels like the intention condition does all the real work, and the other three are just a description of what it is for some words to add up to a sentence in the first place — in which case the interesting claim is hidden in the last item and the first three are filler. The timing one especially bugs me; why should how fast someone talks matter to what their words mean? So what I'd like is for you to explain, as carefully as you can, how each of the four is supposed to earn its place — what goes wrong, on the Nyāya view, if you drop one and keep the other three, ideally with a case where the remaining conditions are satisfied but the dropped one isn't. If you think one of them is genuinely redundant, or that the four are doing work that one or two could do, then say so and argue it, instead of just restating the textbook line. I'm also curious whether this is basically the same animal as the Frege stuff I half-remember about a sentence's meaning being built out of the meanings of its parts, plus Grice on speaker's meaning — is the Nyāya list a different cut at the same thing, or is it tracking something the analytic accounts leave out? No need for references or a reading list, I'm just trying to get the shape of the position straight in my head.
