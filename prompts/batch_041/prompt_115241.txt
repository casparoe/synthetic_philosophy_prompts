I sit on the commendations and safety-review panel for our city's fire department — I'm the civilian member, I teach high-school chemistry, so I'm the one who asks the annoying questions — and I have to circulate a written opinion before our March 14 vote. The case is this. Back in November a captain, fifteen years on the job, clean record, went into a burning duplex through a rear window to pull out a nine-year-old who'd been left behind. He did it alone, before his crew had a line in place, and without logging onto the accountability board, which is two seperate rule violations, and both rules exist because firefighters die that way. The kid lived, the captain came out with a burned forearm and nothing worse, and now we have three motions in front of us: give him the department's top commendation; give him nothing (the safety officer's position — the outcome doesn't launder the method); or commend him and at the same time refer the violations to a disciplinary review, which is what the chief seems to want. A retired member of our panel who taught philosophy for thirty years keeps saying the real question is whether any of this was ever required of him in the first place, and that if we agree it went beyond the call, we can't turn around and punish him for how he answered the call. The safety officer keeps saying that the moment we pin a medal on it, every firefighter in the department learns that the rule is optional when the stakes are high, and that we've quietly converted something nobody had to do into something everyone will now be judged by. They're annoying in different ways and I think they're both onto a piece of it.

Also on the agenda is a draft beyond-duty recognition policy that a lieutenant wrote and that I have to say something about. It proposes three bins — required of the role, expected but not mandated, and beyond the call — with the rule that only the first bin can ever ground discipline and only the third can ever ground an award, plus a clause requiring any award in the third bin to carry a written statement that the conduct is not expected of other members. A fourth proposal floating around would add a bin called commended and corrected, where the citation itself spells out what the member did wrong. I can't tell whether the written-statement clause is clever or a cop-out, and I can't tell whether the policy even covers the captain, since he's arguably in the first bin (rescuing people is literally the job) while also breaking the rules that define how the job is done.

So please draft the opinion I'll read out and circulate: a concrete recommendation on all three motions and on the policy draft, with a time frame I can attach to whatever we adopt, reasoning I can defend out loud in a room with a chip on its shoulder, and an honest statement of the best case for whichever side I end up recommending against — including, if you think it's the stronger side, the argument that we should simply decline to reward the captain at all. Something I can read in ten minutes.
