I am presenting at a workshop next term on Hobbes's later mathematics, and what I want from you is a Q&A preparation sheet, not a defence of the thesis. The thesis is that the long campaign over the squaring of the circle — the claim in De corpore, the retraction, the replacement proofs, and the decades of pamphleteering against John Wallis running from the mid-1650s into the 1670s — is not the hobby of a philosopher past his prime but continuous with the programme of Leviathan: Hobbes needed geometry as the one available model of a demonstrative science whose conclusions compel assent, and his refusal ever to concede error to Wallis is of a piece with his refusal to let any credentialed authority stand above demonstration. I take his hostility to algebra and to infinite quantities to be the same commitment working itself out.

The room will be about a dozen people. Roughly half are Hobbes specialists and seventeenth-century intellectual historians, who know the political, theological and university contexts far better than they know the mathematics, and who will be quick to observe that Wallis was a Presbyterian, a university man and an anti-Royalist, and that Hobbes himself insisted the quarrel was not merely scientific. Two are historians of early modern mathematics, who will know exactly how badly the proofs fail and may think my account of his motives beside the point. One works on the institutional history of the early Royal Society, and is likely to be sceptical of anything that makes the mathematical dispute more than reputation-management within a self-selecting guild.

Give me the objections I will actually get from this room, not the ones a philosophy-of-mathematics seminar would raise. Order them by how likely and how damaging they are; phrase each the way the particular questioner I have described would phrase it; give me a reply I could deliver in about two minutes; and add an honest note on where each reply is thinnest, flagging what I should simply concede rather than fight. End with the one question I should be hoping nobody asks. No reading list, please.

Where your judgement of an objection's force is genuinely uncertain, say so, and tell me what would change your mind — which piece of evidence or argument would lead you to revise your ranking.
