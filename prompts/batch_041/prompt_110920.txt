I've been reading around the rationality literature for a seminar, and I keep running into the same tidy line — sunk costs are sunk, only marginal costs and benefits matter, anyone who "throws good money after bad" is committing a fallacy — and I've started to suspect the tidiness is hiding something. What I want from you is a case built to order rather than an illustration: construct a thought experiment in which honoring a sunk cost is the rationally required choice, and in which this is not because the past expenditure happens to carry information about the future (I know that move, and it feels like cheating — it makes the sunk cost relevant only as a proxy for something else, which concedes the textbook point). The relevance of the past investment has to be doing genuine normative work of its own, and it has to do so within a framework an ordinary expected-value reasoner would accept, not by smuggling in a primitive "waste aversion" or an unexplained duty of consistency.

Constraints, and please take them seriously because the whole value of the exercise is in the joint satisfiability: the scenario should be one a normal adult could actually face (a career, a relationship, a renovation, a research program — not a lottery or a science-fictional setup); it should fit in a single paragraph; and it must isolate a single variable, so that if you flip that one feature the textbook verdict returns and honoring the cost becomes a mistake. No appeals to irrational preferences, no assuming the agent is mistaken about the facts, and no assuming the future is somehow caused by the past. After the scenario, do the full demonstration: state the agent's options, walk through what a straightforward expected-value maximizer says and what your case says, and show precisely why the maximizer's verdict is wrong here rather than merely incomplete. Then tell me which feature of the case is doing the work, what happens to the case when that feature is removed, and how a defender of the textbook line would attack the case — I want to see the strongest version of the objection that the sunk cost is still, strictly speaking, irrelevant and that whatever is doing the work could be redescribed without it. Finish by saying whether you think the case shows the textbook rule is false, or only that it is stated too crudely.
