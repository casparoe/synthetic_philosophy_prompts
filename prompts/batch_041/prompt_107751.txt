I'm partway through a short essay for a grad seminar and I'd like your comments on my notes for it. The topic is that old idea that the self is a center of narrative gravity, i.e. that the self isn't some thing lodged in the head but a useful abstraction, the way an object's center of gravity is a genuine feature of the object even though you'd never find it by cutting the thing open. My hook is that the analogy is sneakier than people notice and that it actually cuts against the way it usually gets used. Here's the thought: a center of gravity is uniquely determined — given a mass distribution there's exactly one point where it sits, so if two people disagree about where it is, at least one of them is wrong. A life story doesn't work like that. Take a life — the same remembered events, the same habits, the same choices — and you can tell three or four stories about it that fit all the evidence equally well while contradicting each other about what the person is really like (the loyal one who keeps getting betrayed; the coward who keeps inventing excuses; the one who just has terrible luck with people). I'm calling this narrative underdetermination, and my claim is that it isn't a defect of self-knowledge, its the normal condition, which means the whole project of uncovering your true story is confused: you aren't excavating a fact, you're making a choice. I know someone will object that the stories can't be equally good because only one of them predicts how the person will behave next, but my reply is that the predictions only come out equal if you describe behavior thinly (she shows up when it matters) and the stories differ at the level of thick interpretation (why she shows up), so the predictive test can't break the tie. Then the practical bit I want to land: if that's right, a therapist who helps you swap one equally-well-fitting story for another isn't discovering your real self, they're co-authoring it, and I want to say that's fine, that's all a self ever was.

What I'd like from you is where this breaks down. Specifically: (a) is narrative underdetermination actually true, or does it prove too much — if every story fits equally well, how do I keep a good reinterpretation of my life apart from plain self-deception, which we normally define as believing a story that doesn't fit the facts? (b) my last paragraph probably overshoots and I'm not even sure the therapist conclusion follows from the underdetermination claim at all. And (c) is there a cleaner way to state the center-of-gravity disanalogy? I worry I'm just saying abstract things can be vague, which isn't interesting. Don't bother with polish, I mostly want to know whether the spine of the argument holds up or whether I should scrap it and write about something else.
