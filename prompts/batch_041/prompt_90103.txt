Draft: The Best of All Possible Worlds as a Constraint-Satisfaction Problem

The standard objection to Leibniz’s doctrine of the best of all possible worlds is that it cannot be reconciled with the amount and distribution of evil. If God could have actualized a world with less suffering, then this world is not the best. Leibniz’s reply is usually read as a cost-benefit defense: God had to allow some evil because it is a necessary means to greater good, or because the alternatives contain even more evil. I want to propose a different reconstruction. On my reading, Leibniz’s best is not a scalar maximum of net value. It is the maximal compossible set of substances under a constraint. God’s understanding contains an infinite set of possible substances, each with its own complete concept. Not every subset is compossible: some substances cannot coexist because their complete concepts imply incompatible predicates. A possible world just is a maximal compossible set. The best world is not the one that scores highest on a single scale of good minus evil; it is the one whose maximal compossible set realizes the greatest perfection, where perfection is a structural property of the whole — its order, variety, and intelligibility — rather than the sum of local utilities. Evil enters not as a price paid for greater good, but as a consequence of finitude and incompossibility: a world of finite substances cannot contain every possible perfection, and the exclusion of some perfections is experienced by creatures as privation, pain, and sin. On this reading, the objection from evil assumes an aggregative axiology that Leibniz does not hold. The objector asks why God did not choose the world with less suffering; Leibniz’s answer is that there is no such world among the compossible sets, because suffering is not a separable item that can be minimized independently of the structure that makes a world the best. The analogy I find helpful is constraint satisfaction. Imagine a set of formal constraints — each possible substance is a variable, and compossibility relations are constraints on which assignments can be jointly true. A solution is a maximal consistent assignment. The best solution is not the one that maximizes a weighted sum of satisfied preferences; it is the one that satisfies the constraints in the most elegant and comprehensive way. If this analogy is right, then the problem of evil is not a problem about God’s benevolence but about whether the constraints themselves are good. That is a different and harder question, and Leibniz may not have an answer to it. The essay’s thesis is therefore partly deflationary: the best-of-all-possible-worlds doctrine is not a theodicy in the ordinary sense, but a claim about the structure of possibility. It does not explain why this world contains these particular evils; it explains why any world at all must contain some exclusion. Whether that is a strength or a fatal evasion is what I want to know.
