I run a political theory reading group — 14 grad students, one 75-minute session, no prep from them — and next week's topic is who actually counts as "the people" when you say "the people decide." I don't want to stand up and explain the problem; I want them to walk into it themselves. Can you design the activity?

Here's the scenario I've been sketching, feel free to scrap it if yours is sharper: they're the founding assembly of a new settlement on a river delta. I hand out roles with real stakes (points they can win, or a prize I bring — the point is they should care how the votes go): eight-year residents, families who arrived two years ago, seasonal workers who live there half the year, the port company that pays most of the tax base, a few 16-year-olds, people who moved away but still own houses, and a delegation from the town downstream whose water the dam will change.

Mechanics I want in it: they have to pass five substantive decisions (build the dam or not, who pays for the school, that kind of thing), but before any of it they have to settle who gets to vote. Any change to the franchise has to pass by a simple majority of whoever is currently voting. And at least one proposal on the table would, if it passes, add a bloc big enough to swing every later vote — so they have to decide whether to admit a group they can already predict will outvote them. Then a founders' clause: the original residents hold a veto for ten years, and the charter can only be amended by two-thirds of the current voters. If the group's moving fast, add a round where one district wants to leave and the question is who decides that.

What I want them to hit: the bit where the first vote on who votes had to be held by somebody, and there's no non-question-begging answer to who; the bit where "anyone affected should get a say" keeps pulling in more people until it swallows everyone; the bit where a group can't be trusted to decide its own future membership because the decision is about its own power. The debrief should map each stage onto the structure — including the version of the problem that recurs after the founding (immigration, secession, naturalization), not just the founding moment. If you can, tie the entrenchment clause to the real phenomenon of rules that ordinary majorities can't repeal.

Two things I need besides the activity: an honest note on what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to patch it on the fly, and a frank statement of what the activity can't reproduce, i.e. the part I'll have to just tell them. Also flag which claims in the debrief are settled among people who work on this and which are genuinely contested, so I don't accidentally teach a contested line as the consensus.

Format: under 1,000 words, headings and bullets are fine, and write it in American English.
