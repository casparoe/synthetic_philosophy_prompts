I am a third-year PhD student in the philosophy of the social sciences, and I have spent the past several weeks in a reading group on Popper's critique of historicism — his case that the social sciences are wrong to aim at large-scale historical prophecy, and that the search for laws of historical development is misconceived. The disagreement I keep having with a colleague is not exegetical; we read the texts the same way. It is about whether the thing Popper denies actually exists. My own credence that there is at least one genuine law of history — not a trend, not a heuristic, not a mechanism sketch, but a temporally unrestricted, empirically well-confirmed generalization over social systems that licenses conditional predictions about where a society will end up rather than merely redescribing where it has been — is about 0.15. My colleague puts it near 0.65, and we cannot locate where we part company.

I want your own credence in that proposition, decomposed rather than asserted. To fix the target, treat a law of history as having to satisfy three conditions: it must be a regularity over social systems rather than over individuals; it must be invariant across a wide range of interventions on the relevant variables, not merely stable across the observed record; and it must yield non-trivial conditional predictions about the trajectory of large-scale social change. Two distinctions I would like you to keep separate, because I suspect our disagreement hides in one of them. The first is between trajectory laws — regularities governing the shape of a whole historical path, of the sort a dynamical system with an attractor would supply — and local laws, which govern transitions between adjacent social states and say nothing about the global shape. The second is between regularities that are silent about their own discovery and those that are not, since any regularity whose operation depends on what agents believe about it inherits the reflexivity problem Popper pressed under the name of the Oedipus effect. A concrete case to work with, proposed by my colleague and which I suspect is not a law but cannot articulate why: that the rate of institutional innovation in a polity declines monotonically as the share of the social surplus captured by incumbent elites rises. Whether that candidate survives matters less to me than what your handling of it reveals about your general position.

What I would like back is a single number for the general proposition, and then the sub-questions it rests on — at minimum, whether the social domain admits of exceptionless or even ceteris paribus regularities at all; whether the trajectory/local distinction is principled or merely a device for concealing the problem; how much weight the growth-of-knowledge argument deserves, given that we cannot predict in advance what we will come to know; and whether the persistent failure to find laws is evidence of their absence or only of the difficulty of the search — each with a rough probability and a single line of justification, followed by the combination with the arithmetic shown. Tell me which term your number is most sensitive to, and what empirical or conceptual result would move it furthest. Finally, since my aim is to locate a disagreement rather than win one: given that I sit at 0.15 and my colleague at 0.65, tell me which of those sub-questions we most plausibly differ on, and what each of us would have to concede for the two positions to converge.
