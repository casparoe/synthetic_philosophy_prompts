For the past forty years I worked as a field ecologist, and since retiring I have been working through the philosophy of language on my own. I have just finished a chapter in a general-audience book on meaning, and I would like an accuracy audit of the way it presents Quine's "gavagai" argument, because something about it strikes me as too neat and I lack the background to say why.

The book's central paragraph reads as follows:

"Quine asks us to imagine a linguist set down among a people whose language has no known relatives. A rabbit runs past and the natives say 'gavagai'; the linguist provisionally records 'rabbit.' But Quine observes that no possible evidence could decide whether 'gavagai' means rabbit, rabbit-stage, or undetached rabbit-parts, since every observable circumstance would be identical in all three cases. This proves that translation is impossible in principle: we can never genuinely know what another language means. And since the same reasoning applies to our own words, Quine concludes that meaning is a myth and that every translation manual is an equally arbitrary fabrication."

The author is clearly competent and the chapter is otherwise careful, so I assume the errors, if there are any, are subtle rather than gross. My own hunch — offered with low confidence — is that the paragraph has converted a claim about the evidence available to a linguist into a much stronger claim about what there is to be known, and that the word "impossible" is doing illegitimate work. But I did not want to convict the author on a hunch, and I have not read Quine's own text, so I may be the one who is confused.

Please go through the summary claim by claim and tell me, for each, whether a philosopher who knows this literature well would accept it, would regard it as a forgivable simplification, or would say it is simply wrong — and in the last case, what the accurate statement would be. I would especially like to know: whether the three purported readings of "gavagai" really are indistinguishable by the evidence in the flat way the author asserts, or whether the difference only surfaces once some further apparatus is held fixed; whether the conclusion the author draws about the impossibility of translation is one Quine held or one he explicitly rejected; whether the extension to our own language is a legitimate part of the view or an embellishment; and whether the closing claim about meaning being a myth and manuals being arbitrary follows from anything that survives correction.

Please also tell me how the author might defend the paragraph — which parts are defensible as popularization rather than error — so that I do not over-correct in the other direction.

Finally, I would like the response to state plainly what would change its verdict: which consideration, if it turned out to be the case, would move a claim from "misrepresentation" to "acceptable simplification," or vice versa, and where in its own reasoning it is most likely to be wrong. I would rather have the confidence level and its failure points than a verdict presented as settled.
