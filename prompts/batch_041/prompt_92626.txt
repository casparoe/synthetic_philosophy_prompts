I am a backend engineer with no formal training in philosophy, and I have lately been working through epistemic game theory on my own, mostly because the two-generals problem I know from distributed systems turns out to have a philosophical cousin. The part I cannot get over is that mutual knowledge and common knowledge are not the same thing, and that the difference is not a matter of degree. As I understand it, there is a result showing that two agents can each know the relevant fact, know that the other knows it, know that the other knows that they know it, and so on for any finite number of levels you like, and still rationally fail to do what they would do if the fact were common knowledge; and that when the levels finally do cross into common knowledge, behaviour jumps rather than sliding. If that is right, then any deterrence arrangement that depends on both sides knowing what the other will do is standing on something much thinner than it looks, and no amount of redundant signalling gets you there. I would rather have the idea handled carefully than have it attributed to anyone, so please do not worry about naming papers or scholars; I am not writing anything with a bibliography.

My friends and I have a fortnightly film night where one of us has to defend a philosophical reading of whatever we watched, and I drew Dr. Strangelove. The reading I want to test is that the film is really about common knowledge rather than about the Bomb. The war room scene where the doomsday machine is revealed seems to make the crude point first: Strangelove observes that the machine would only have deterred anyone if everyone had known about it, and the ambassador answers that the announcement had been scheduled for the Party Congress the following week. That is a failure of secrecy, not a failure of common knowledge, since one side simply did not know, and no level of mutual knowledge was reached at all; I suspect that if I present the film as an illustration of the two-generals problem, someone will rightly tell me I have read a subtle result into a simple plot point. But there are other pieces to work with. The machine fires automatically and cannot be called off, which removes the human decision and with it the credibility problem, while apparently leaving a knowledge problem untouched. Wing Attack Plan R delegates the launch to a commander who is out of contact, so the President's authority runs through a code that only one man knows and that has to be reconstructed from his doodles while he rants. And the whole deterrent logic on display assumes the other side will retaliate, which is exactly what common knowledge of everyone's rationality makes hard to believe, so the film has to supply a genuine madman rather than a credible policy.

I would like you to act as a philosopher of game theory and epistemic logic who also knows this film well, and to write me the reading I would give at film night. Start by sketching two or three candidate readings of the film in terms of common knowledge and rationality, then commit to the one that survives scrutiny. Be honest in both directions: do not bend the film into an illustration of the formal result, and do not soften the result to make the film fit. Tell me which scenes I should actually put in front of people and what each one shows, where the film dramatises the real distinction between mutual and common knowledge and where it only dramatises plain ignorance, and whether its satire of human irrationality amounts to an argument that common knowledge of rationality is unavailable in practice — and if so, what that does to a theory that takes it as a starting assumption. Do not quote dialogue; I care about the arguments and the scenes, and I will paraphrase anything I need. Finish with what the film gets that the formal treatment cannot, if anything.
