I chair the residents' association in a small seaside town, and on Thursday I have to open the public consultation meeting on the council's proposal to close our 1930s lido and sell the site for flats. Please write my opening address. The room will hold about a hundred and fifty people: campaigners who have been quoting Chesterton at each other for months, a chartered surveyor presenting the viability appraisal, older residents who learned to swim there and have no interest in philosophy whatsoever, two planning lawyers, the borough's heritage officer, and the developer's consultant, who has already said in print that the same argument would have saved the slums.

I want the speech to take Chesterton's fence seriously enough to be useful and not so literally that it turns into a defence of everything old. Concretely, I want it to do four things, in this order, and I want the order to be visible in the speech itself. First, state the principle plainly enough for people who have never heard of it: that it is a claim about where the burden of proof lies, not a claim that old things are good. Second, separate it from the weaker arguments that get attached to it — nostalgia, and the thought that if a thing still works there is nothing to discuss. Third, and this is the part I care about most, say what would actually discharge the burden in this case. If the answer is never, then the principle is worthless and the consultant wins, so the speech has to name the conditions under which I would accept demolition — what would count as understanding the fence well enough. Fourth, be honest about where my own case stops: I do not know whether the viability figures are right and I should not pretend to.

A friend has suggested the parallel between old institutions and biological traits that may be adaptive, vestigial, or mere by-products, which I think is genuinely illuminating, but I would rather it did not take over the speech: one sentence at most.

Some of the campaigners are quoting Chesterton as though he were a Tory, whereas I gather his own politics were a long way from a defence of the propertied status quo. If that can be turned into a sentence that does not smuggle in a cheap point, do it; if it would only annoy the room, leave it out.

Please write in British English — the address is going into the minutes and probably into the local paper. Six or seven minutes spoken, so roughly eight hundred words. It should be formal enough to read aloud at a public meeting without sounding like a planning document.

After the speech, add a short note for me alone, no more than a paragraph or two, in which you separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who know the topic — whether the fence principle is a conservative doctrine at all, whether Chesterton and Burke are really making the same argument, whether the principle can ever be given a stopping rule — and make clear which of those is contested rather than presenting any one reading as the consensus.
