I work on trust and safety for a mid-sized social platform, and I want to understand what the rule system I help administer is actually committed to. Here is the practice. Our Community Standards are, in principle, made by the community: a standing council of users drafts rules, the wider user base votes, and a rule takes effect once it has majority support. But our charter also contains a short list of behaviors that are barred no matter what the community decides; the council may not propose to permit them, and no vote can repeal the ban. The charter further draws a distinction I have never seen defended. It treats some norms as valid because they were endorsed by a majority, and others as valid because they express what the community already is -- values we are said to hold whether or not we have ever voted on them. The first kind can be revised by vote; the second is described as discovered rather than decided. Enforcement, meanwhile, is inconsistent in a way that nags at me. When we explain a ban, one script says the community has decided the behavior is not allowed, while another says the behavior is wrong and would be wrong even if most users disagreed. And we have at least one rule that passed a vote, was later declared by the council not to reflect our values, and is still enforced. One more detail: the charter itself was ratified by a user vote in the platform's early days, and we still cite that vote as the source of its authority, including for the norms we describe as discovered.

What I want is the theory this practice presupposes. Is our rule system committed to the view that a behavior is prohibited because the community prohibits it, or that the community prohibits it because it is independently wrong -- and can it hold both without contradiction? Which side does the charter's second category, the norms that are discovered rather than decided, actually commit us to, and is that a genuine third option or a restatement of one of the first two -- especially given that the charter's own authority traces back to a vote? Distinguish what the practice strictly requires from what it merely happens to fit: could several incompatible theories license everything we do? Finally, tell me what would have to change -- in the charter, the voting procedure, or the enforcement scripts -- if we committed cleanly to one side, and what we would lose by doing so.
