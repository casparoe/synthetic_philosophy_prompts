I am trying to determine whether my attitudes toward the future add up to a principle. I suspect that at least one of them will have to go. My verdicts, stated as flatly as I can manage:

- I would pay a great deal to avoid a week of severe pain beginning tomorrow, and much less to avoid the same week of pain if it began ten years from now. I do not take this to be a mistake about my own good.
- If the same week of pain were to fall on a stranger, I would not think the date mattered at all, once how well off that stranger is otherwise is held fixed. The world is not better for a stranger's suffering having been deferred.
- Forced to relieve either a stranger suffering now or a stranger who will suffer in a century, I would relieve the one now; but I take the reason to be that the later suffering is less certain to occur and the later person is likely better off, not that the date itself lightens the claim.
- A harm suffered a thousand years ago seems to me exactly as bad as the same harm now. I do not discount backward.
- I save for my own retirement at a rate that treats a unit of my future welfare as worth less than a unit of my present welfare, and I think this is at least permissible, perhaps required.
- A policy procedure that counts a life saved in 2150 as worth less than a life saved in 2050, for no reason beyond the date, strikes me as unjust rather than merely imprudent.
- I prefer $100 today to $120 in a week, and I also prefer $120 in fifty-two weeks to $100 in fifty-one weeks. I count the reversal as a defect in me; I do not count the bare preference for the earlier sum, at equal delay, as a defect.
- I do not think a good is better for coming earlier. I would rather a great work were made in my lifetime than in a thousand years, but I take that to be a fact about me and not about its value.

The best principle I have is this. Time preference is legitimate when it governs the distribution of goods within a single life, since an agent is entitled to give more weight to what is nearer to what he is now doing and caring about; it is illegitimate when it governs the weight assigned to different persons, or is built into a rule that allocates goods to persons existing at different times. Call the first perspectival discounting and the second absolute discounting, and say that only the first is defensible.

This saves the retirement case and condemns the policy case. What I cannot see is why the structure that licenses the first should be forbidden in the second. The distance from me to my own pain in ten years is a distance in time; the distance from me to a stranger's pain now is a distance in persons. If the first distance may be used to discount, why may the second not? And if neither may be, why am I so confident that my retirement saving is acceptable and the policy case is not? I also notice that once I subtract from my own time preference everything attributable to the uncertainty of my survival and to the likelihood that my later self will care about different things, I am no longer sure that any preference remains, and I would rather be told than guess.

So: state the principle, or the smallest set of principles, that yields as many of these verdicts as possible; for each clause, identify the verdict that forces it; tell me which verdicts I must surrender if I want a principle rather than a list; and then apply the best version to two cases I have not mentioned. First, a person who prefers that a good come to him later rather than sooner, because he likes the thought of a pleasure awaiting him: is that the same license running in reverse, or something else? Second, a case in which a cure can be given either to a person in 2050 or to a person in 2200, the two are equally ill, the benefit is identical and cannot be converted into anything else, and the later person is not better off in any other respect: what does the principle say, and does the answer embarrass any of my verdicts?
