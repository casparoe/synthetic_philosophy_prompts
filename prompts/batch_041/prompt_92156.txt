I am a novelist, and I am midway through a comic novel set largely in a computational-cognition laboratory, most of which takes place over one disastrous weekend. There is a scene I am stuck on: a departmental dinner, at a restaurant whose lazy Susan nobody is operating correctly, at which a character — a lapsed AI researcher, recently and somewhat unfairly denied tenure — is expected to be funny. He is not, generally, funny; the joke he tells is his one good one, and it has to land with the two colleagues across the table, both of whom have the technical background, while remaining at worst baffling and at best invisible to the visiting dean. I would like to write that joke properly rather than gesture at it, and I would rather it be built out of the actual content of a real problem than out of the ambient folklore about robots and common sense.

Here is the material I have been reading up on, and I would like the joke to be able to draw on it. First, the original puzzle in logic-based AI: you write down what an action changes, and then discover that nothing in your formalism licenses the conclusion that the ceiling, the colour of the wall, or the position of the chair stayed as they were, so you are driven to add frame axioms spelling out the non-effects of each action one action-and-property pair at a time. Second, the default rule that was supposed to rescue you — things stay as they are unless something changes them — and the awkwardness of stating such a rule in a logic whose conclusions only ever accumulate. Third, the small embarrassing scenario in which a gun is loaded, some time passes, and a turkey is shot: the formalisms that seem to capture the default rule either lose track of the loadedness of the gun or conclude something nobody wanted, which is why the eventual successor-state axiom, on which inertia falls out automatically rather than being stipulated, counts as progress. Fourth, the two neighbouring puzzles: saying exhaustively what must be true for an action to work at all, and capturing an action's indirect consequences, since moving the box moves what is inside it. Fifth, the wider philosophical reading, on which the frame problem is not really about frames but about relevance — how a system knows where to stop thinking — illustrated by the robot that either fails to notice the bomb being wheeled into the room or spends its final moments checking whether the bomb is relevant. What interests me is that these are not one problem wearing several hats, and a joke that treats them as interchangeable would be no better than one that puns on picture frames.

Please give me three candidate jokes, in different shapes: a one-liner he can deliver across the table; a short exchange between him and one colleague, with the colleague supplying the straight line; and a slightly longer, more elaborate telling that builds. For each, tell me which piece of the material above the punchline actually turns on, and why someone who has had to write a frame axiom would find it funny while someone who knows only that AI has trouble with common sense would not. Keep each to something that can be said aloud in under two minutes, avoid anything that requires the listener to know a name, a date, or a citation, and flag any candidate whose force depends on a detail of the problem that I have got wrong.
