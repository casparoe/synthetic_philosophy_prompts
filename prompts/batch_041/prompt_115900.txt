A colleague has talked himself into the conclusion that Gibbard and Satterthwaite proved democratic choice to be impossible, and I think his argument extracts more from the theorem than the theorem contains, though I cannot yet say where the surplus comes from. Reconstructed, it runs:

(1) With three or more alternatives, any voting rule that is strategy-proof, that can return each alternative as the winner under some profile, and that is defined on every profile of strict preferences is dictatorial.
(2) So every non-dictatorial rule with full range is manipulable: at some profile, some voter has a ballot other than her true preference that yields an outcome she prefers.
(3) A voter who has such a ballot uses it.
(4) Hence, under every non-dictatorial rule with full range, some voters vote insincerely.
(5) An outcome built from insincere ballots is not the outcome the rule would have produced had the voters reported their true preferences.
(6) A rule whose outcomes come apart from the preferences it claims to aggregate is not aggregating them.
(7) Therefore every such rule fails as an aggregator, and the only rules that escape are dictatorial ones or ones that cannot elect every alternative. Democratic choice is impossible.

I am confident in (1) and attached to (7), and I would rather not surrender (6). Which step has to go, why does it look so plausible while it is doing the damage, and what do I owe if I drop it? If the argument can be repaired rather than abandoned, tell me what the repaired version would have to concede.
