Ok so I'm a postdoc, about two years out of the PhD, and my whole shtick is the Dennett-ish line on consciousness: no Cartesian theater, no single moment where it all comes together and gets shown to the inner viewer, just a bunch of content-fixations happening in parallel at slightly different times, some of which get probed, written down, and later reported, and that's the whole story of what it was like for you. (Yeah I know that's compressed and unfair to somebody, but that's the gist.)

My paper got accepted with three reviews that all said some version of: fine, we're convinced there's no theater, now what? What actually follows, what do we do with this? Which is fair, and I've got two funded years to answer it, so I want to spend them on the right thing.

So: once you've actually swallowed the no-theater line, what are the open questions that are worth a person's time, and which one or two are most upstream of the rest? Here's the pile I've been circling, roughly:

- What grounds the difference between a content being *mine* and a content merely being processed in my brain? With no viewer, does that distinction still do work, or is it a leftover of the theater picture that should just be thrown out?
- The unity of experience. Is the felt "all at once, all mine, all now" a real achievement of the system, or a gloss the reporting machinery adds after the fact? I genuinely don't know if this is a deep question or a verbal one.
- Reportability. Is being reportable the mark of the conscious, or just the only handle we can get a grip on? I suspect the literature slides constantly between "reportable" and "conscious" and half the disputes are that slide in disguise.
- Falsifiability. Does the picture make any prediction that could actually fail, or is it compatible with everything anyone could ever find? If it's the latter, what do we even mean by saying it's true?
- Skilled action. When someone does something expert and then narrates a decision they demonstrably couldn't have made in time, does the model have to say the decision got made *after* the fact, or is the temporal talk here just a figure of speech we should stop using?
- Boundary cases. Split brains, and whatever else, do these sharpen the question of where one mind ends and another begins, or dissolve it?

And then my own pet thing, which may be dumb: I've been chewing on a cut between two ways a draft can matter. One is being *causally load-bearing* — downstream behavior actually depends on it. The other is being *reportable* — you can tell someone about it later. I think a lot of the literature quietly conflates these, and when you separate them some puzzles get easier and others turn out to be pseudo-problems. Is that the right cut? If it is, which of the questions above does it make tractable, and if it isn't, what's the better distinction?

What I want from you: rank these (and add ones I'm missing — I'd bet I'm missing the one that matters), say which one or two I should actually go after, and defend the ordering. Be explicit about the criteria — how upstream it is, how tractable it is for someone who does conceptual work and light modeling rather than running experiments (no lab, no scanner, no subjects), whether other people are already on it — and be honest about the trade-offs between those criteria when they conflict. Tell me which of these are overrated or secretly already settled. And tell me what would move something up or down the list in the next year or so, i.e. what I should be watching for that would make me regret the choice.

Not looking for a reading list, I know the lit. I want the agenda and the reasoning behind it.
