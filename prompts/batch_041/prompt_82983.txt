I retired three years ago after thirty-five years in field biology — my own work was on nest defence and mate desertion in colonial seabirds — and I have spent the time since doing what I always meant to do, which is read philosophy, chiefly at the margins where it touches on decision and behaviour. This has produced one recurring irritation. In my field we were trained to call it the Concorde fallacy: the animal that keeps defending a nest, or the government that keeps funding an aircraft, because of what has already been sunk into it, when only the prospects of the remaining course should count. The philosophers I read with will not let the label do the work. They point out that the money is gone but that the fact it was spent is evidence — about the project, and about the person who committed to it — so that an agent who suppresses evidence merely because it arrives attached to a cost is not obviously the rational one; that in repeated dealings a reputation for not being moved by bygones can be worth more than the bygones, so that honouring a sunk cost may be a commitment device rather than a mistake; and that the norm may presuppose an agent whose past commitments are detachable from who she is, which is not how anyone actually deliberates.

I have been rereading The Old Man and the Sea with this argument in view, and I cannot settle whether Santiago is the textbook case or the counterexample. Eighty-four days without a fish, a village reputation as the best of the fishermen, a remembered arm-wrestling match won in his youth, his hero DiMaggio playing through a bone spur — the man carries his past with him, and when the marlin finally takes the line he will not cut it, holding on for three days and nights, killing the mako that takes his harpoon, making a knife from an oar blade, and coming home with a skeleton that the woman tourist on the terrace takes for a shark's. What I want is a reading of the novella through the sunk-cost question that is a reading and not an illustration: the decision the frame genuinely illuminates; the scene where the book pushes back — if, say, what Santiago is invested in is not a return but a contest with a worthy opponent, or a self he cannot shed without ceasing to be that self; and whether the rational-defence arguments I summarised above have any purchase on him, given that his fishing is a repeated activity in a village that watches him, while the marlin is a one-shot contest that ends in the water and leaves him nothing but the proof of its size. If the novella has a verdict on him, state it; if it has none, say that too.

Give me exactly five numbered points, each a sentence or two, with no introduction and no conclusion. At least one point should name the scene that most resists the sunk-cost frame, and the fifth should say what the novella adds to the philosophical argument that the arguments by themselves do not.
