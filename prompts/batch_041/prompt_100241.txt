For about eighteen months I have been part of a neighbourhood group trying to get our city to explain and then change the scoring tool it uses to decide which buildings get inspected first. I have no technical training at all — what I know I have picked up in meetings and by building a spreadsheet of my own — and I want to be clear at the outset that I am not asking for a reading list, references, or names of papers. I could not use them, and I suspect my group's argument is not even the argument we should be having.

Here is the shape of it. The tool gives every building a risk score. The contractor's defence is that the score means the same thing everywhere: among buildings with the same score, the same fraction turn out to have violations, whichever part of the city they are in. Our side's complaint is that this is not enough — among the buildings that really do have violations, the tool catches a smaller share in the poorer, mostly immigrant quarter than in the wealthy one, so equally bad landlords over there go unvisited. Both of these seem to be true of the same score at the same time, and both sides restate them at every meeting. A friend then showed me, on the back of an envelope with invented numbers, that if the two halves of the city genuinely differ in how often violations occur, you can have the score mean the same thing everywhere, or you can catch the same share of offenders everywhere, but not both — and that this is arithmetic rather than a shortage of data. We proposed replacing the score in the poorer half with a plain lottery among all buildings, on the grounds that a lottery is obviously not biased; the contractor replied that this would make the tool worse at finding violations overall, which struck me as a different sort of objection from the fairness one. Since then the meetings have gone in circles: we demand that they fix the bias, they say the mathematics forbids it, we say that is a dodge, they say we do not understand the model, and nothing moves.

What I want is not a verdict on the tool but a diagnosis of the disagreement. Is this a substantive quarrel about which kind of error we are willing to bear, and therefore a political choice dressed up as a technical one? Is it verbal — are the two sides simply using "fair" for two different quantities and talking past each other? Are they answering different questions under one heading, one of them asking how well the tool forecasts and the other asking how a scarce good (inspection visits) should be handed out? Or is the arithmetic beside the point, with the real objection being about legitimacy — that the people who bear the errors never agreed to the scheme, so no adjustment of the score would satisfy them? I have a hypothesis, and I hold it loosely: I think the contractor hears a forecasting problem, our group hears a rationing problem, and underneath both sits a question about consent, so the impossibility result everyone keeps invoking is a red herring in the actual fight. Tell me whether that survives contact with what each side is really saying, and if it does not, what the disagreement actually is.

Then tell me what would count as progress given your diagnosis: a distinction we should draw, a piece of evidence that would settle something, a change in what we are asking for, or an honest statement that no progress should be expected here because we are not really disagreeing about the tool. Say explicitly which consideration would change your mind and lead you to a different diagnosis, and where your reasoning is most likely to fail — I would rather have your doubts than your confidence. End with a single final line of the form \answer{label}, where the label is one or more of substantive, verbal, different-questions, legitimacy, ill-posed, joined by "+" if more than one applies, and put a one-sentence summary of the diagnosis on the line immediately before it.
