My colleague in the department works on the Libet-style neuroscience of free will, and I'm on a panel with her next month about a new paper, so I want to know what she'll say about it before she says it. Her track record: the classic clock-watching result — readiness potential first, reported urge a few hundred milliseconds later — she treats as a non-issue, mainly because the early signal is a vague readiness that doesn't say what you're going to do; the studies where people successfully cancel a movement they'd already prepared she calls the most interesting work in the area and no threat to anything; she's fond of the result where subjects made a genuinely consequential decision and nothing in the brain predicted it ahead of time; and the one finding she's said should actually worry us is a study that predicted a choice from activity recorded before the person had even seen the options, so their stated reasons can't have been doing the work.

The new paper: subjects make a real, deliberative choice — they write their reasons down first, and real money goes to whoever they pick — and a classifier reads off which way they're going to go from about 1.5 seconds before they report having decided, well above chance. Their picks do match the reasons they wrote down, nobody offers them a chance to back out, and the design makes deliberation matter.

Predict her verdict. Then tell me which rules fit her track record, what each predicts about this paper, which one I should bet on and why, and what case I should put to her to find out which rule she's actually running. I care less about whether the study really threatens free will than about what her verdicts committ her to.
