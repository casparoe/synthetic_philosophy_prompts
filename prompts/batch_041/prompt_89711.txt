I'm a science journalist — I mostly cover food science and perception, which means I spend alot of time around flavor chemists and trained tasters, and lately I've been sitting in on a friend's philosophy-of-mind reading group because we kept arguing over beers about whether the taste of a thing lives in the thing or in you. The fight started over a story I did on a flavor house that reformulated a big seller: the lab ran the assays and swore the chemical profile was identical, but their head taster insisted the stuff tastes different to him now, and nobody — not the lab, not the other tasters, not the man himself — could say whether the change was in the liquid or in his mouth. The group told me this is basically Dennett's old coffee-taster example, the two tasters who both notice the coffee tastes different to them and can't agree whether it's the coffee that changed or their palates, and that Dennett used it to argue the whole notion of a quale is a mess. Fine. But then a grad student turned it into a clean argument on the whiteboard, and it's been eating at me, because it looks airtight and it also looks like it proves way too much. Here's his version:

(1) A quale is intrinsic — what it's like for you to have an experience is fixed by the experience itself and doesn't consist in any relation it bears to stimuli, to your behavior, or to your other mental states. (2) Physicalism: if two systems are physically identical, they're identical in everything that can make a causal difference. (3) A system's qualia aren't fixed by its physical or functional makeup, so two physically identical systems could differ in what it's like to be them. (4) So two systems could differ in what it's like to be them while doing, saying, and remembering exactly the same things. (5) So whatever a quale is, it makes no causal difference to anything — it's idle, epiphenomenal. (6) But our beliefs and reports about our experiences are supposed to be about those experiences and to track them; if qualia are causally idle, then nothing we say could ever be tracking them. (7) So either qualia are causally inert fictions, or they were never intrinsic in the first place — and either way, the thing the two tasters were disagreeing about just evaporates.

Here's what gets me. The same moves seem to kill things nobody in that room wanted to kill. Swap qualia for meaning and the argument says a thought's content is idle too: two people with matching brains but different histories can differ in what their words mean, so by these same steps meaning would make no causal difference, and then nothing anybody says means anything in the sense of causing anything. It also seems to say a burn's hurting plays no part in my yanking my hand off the stove, which I flatly don't believe. So I'm pretty sure something's off, but I've read the steps so many times they've gone smooth and I can't find the seam. I'd hate to give up (2), because I'm not about to stop being a physicalist, and I'd hate to give up the idea that there's something it's like to be me that's a real fact about me and not just a manner of speaking — though I realize I probably can't have both. Tell me which step to give up, why it looked so airtight in the first place, and what each option would actually cost me. No reading list needed; I just want the diagnosis.
