My cousin enlisted in the infantry back in March — no deployment yet, but its coming — and it's turned every family dinner into a fight, so I've been reading up on the ethics of war on the side (I'm a physio, not a philosopher, so go easy on me). Anyway, there's a pattern in those arguments that I've started noticing everywhere and I don't have a word for it. Ask people whether a soldier who fought in a war they think was unjust is a bad person, and almost nobody says yes: he was following orders, he didn't choose the war, he probably didn't know what the intelligence said, the alternative was a court martial, and he's the one who might come home in pieces. So the man who actually did the killing gets excused. Now step back one pace, to the drone operator with a live feed and eight hours to think, and the excuses get thinner. Step back again — the analyst who picked the target, the colonel who signed off, the minister who sold the war, the columnist who beat the drum, the people who voted for the government that did it — and by the end nobody is offering "he didn't know" or "he had no choice" anymore. So blame looks like it bottoms out exactly at the point where the harm is done, and climbs in both directions away from it, up the chain and out into the public. And what flips as you move away is precisely the stuff we normally use to let people off: knowledge, and room to do otherwise. The bloke with the rifle had the least of both; the columnist had the most. Which seems odd, since we also say the person most to blame is whoever knew what they were doing and could have stopped it.

I know there's a whole literature on the "moral equality of combatants" — whether the two sides' soldiers are on a par — but that seems to be about the horizontal thing, two guys in foxholes, and what I'm after is the vertical slope: why the excuse (or the equality) runs out as you go up and out. A friend suggested "diffusion of responsibility," but that seems to be about responsibility getting lost in a crowd, and here it isn't lost, it's re-concentrated on the people furthest from the act. Is there a name for this? And I don't just want the label — I want to know what the thing actually is, where its edges are, and which of the nearby ideas it's really a version of (if it turns out to be just moral luck, or just "ought implies can," tell me that, don't be polite about it).

Lay it out for me as: (1) "Straight answer" — three or four sentences on whether there's an established name, and if there is, the one thing that stops it from being a perfect fit; (2) "The neighbours" — a short paragraph each on the two or three closest existing ideas, saying what each one really picks out and the exact place where my pattern falls outside it; (3) "The carving" — a paragraph giving me either the best-fitting established concept in its precise form or a coined name with a definition, and in either case saying what sits inside the boundary and what sits just outside; (4) "Test cases" — five one-liners (the drafted conscript who hated it, the volunteer who enlisted out of conviction, the drone operator, the engineer who writes the targeting software, the voter who now regrets it) each with a few words on whether it's the same phenomenon or not.
