I'm currently taking a course on rational choice, and I've come around to the coherence argument for expected utility maximization: any agent whose preferences over options violate the expected-utility axioms is exploitable, in the sense that there is a sequence of choices, each of which it prefers to the one before, that leaves it strictly worse off than where it started, so such preferences are irrational and the axioms are genuine requirements of practical reason. I want you to tell me what would count against this view — not the familiar grumbles that people's preferences are odd or that the axioms are demanding, but the defeaters that a defender of the argument ought to concede, ranked from the ones that would sink it to the ones that are merely awkward. I'm especially interested in whether the argument really establishes as much as it is usually taken to, or whether it only tells against intransitivity while leaving the completeness requirement untouched.

To make this concrete, I'd like you to take seriously a case I constructed and cannot classify. Imagine an agent with a strict three-way preference cycle over goods that it cannot exchange for money or anything else divisible, which has also adopted a standing policy of refusing any trade that would carry it around the cycle. It never gets pumped, and it seems to act on a stable plan, yet its preferences are plainly intransitive. Is that a counterexample to the argument, a trick — and if so, exactly what is the trick — or a description of an agent that the argument simply doesn't reach?

More generally: which of the argument's assumptions are load-bearing — that preferences are the right object of assessment, that the agent accepts each trade as it comes, that it fails to anticipate where the sequence leads, that the sequence terminates, that there is some common currency in which the losses can be measured — what would the world have to look like for each to fail, and which of those failures are live rather than merely logically possible? Finally, has the argument been qualified into unfalsifiability, for instance by reading any pattern of choice as evidence of some hidden preference, and if so, is that a defect or a sign that it was never an empirical claim to begin with? I suspect I'd be most moved by a case of an agent that is recognizably rational, reliably gets what it wants, and still violates an axiom; if such a case can't be had, say so.
