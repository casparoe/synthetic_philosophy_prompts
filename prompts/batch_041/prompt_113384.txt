I'm building a dataset around the presentism debate in historiography -- the claim that historians can't really escape the standards, tools and values of their own time, and the pushback that you're supposed to try to see the past on its own terms (whatever that even means). I want to stress-test it on a real episode rather than argue it in the abstract. Take Heinrich Schliemann at Hisarlik, digging for Homer's Troy in the 1870s: he basically used the Iliad as a dig guide, cut huge trenches through the mound, announced a treasure he named for Priam (it turned out to belong to a much earlier layer than any plausible Trojan War), and generally read the Bronze Age through a romantic-nationalist 19th-century lens. Later archaeologists have been pretty scathing about the whole thing. I know the outline; what I want is the analysis.

So: run Schliemann through presentism. What does the view say he was actually doing -- producing history, or something else wearing history's cloths, and can presentism even distinguish "wrong for his time" from "wrong, full stop"? Then flip it: does the case support presentism, embarass it, or reveal that the interesting version of it is much weaker than the slogan? And address the obvious trap -- that our scorning of Schliemann is just our own present standards doing the judging, so it's presentism all the way down and nobody gets to be right.
