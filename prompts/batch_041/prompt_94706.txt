I'm a novelist, a couple hundred pages into a book whose best character is an old-school analyst — 1920s Vienna, cigarettes, the whole bit — and he keeps telling anyone who'll listen that religion is just a collective version of the rituals his neurotic patients can't stop performing. Private ritual, one patient; shared ritual, a whole church. Which is supposed to prove religion is a sickness we'll eventually grow out of, with his own work — dragging the compulsion into the light — as the cure. I love the line and I want to give it to him, but I don't want to be the novelist who lets a character win an argument with an analogy that doesn't hold up.

So take it apart for me. What's supposed to map onto what — the compulsion, the ritual act, the guilt, the resistance, the suffering, the ego that does the analyzing, the cure — and where does each piece actually hold, only partly hold, or fail? Then tell me what the analogy quietly imports from the neurosis side that religion may not have, and vice versa. And finally: once the breaks are on the table, does his conclusion survive — is religion a pathology, and is the analytic cure even the kind of thing you can apply to it — or was the analogy carrying the whole argument the way a metaphor sometimes carries a plot?

Give it back as JSON and nothing else, with these fields: "mapping" (array of objects, each with "neurosis_element", "religion_counterpart", "holds" = "yes"/"partial"/"no", "note"), "disanalogies" (array of objects with "break", "why_it_matters", "does_it_sink_the_conclusion"), "smuggled_assumptions" (array of strings — what the comparison borrows from the neurosis side and never earns), "verdict" (a paragraph: does the conclusion survive, and what would it need), "better_analogy" (a comparison that carries less unearned weight, or an honest note that there isn't one), and "his_line" (one sentence my analyst could definately say that survives the audit).
