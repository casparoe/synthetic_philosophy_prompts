I am a third-year PhD student in ancient history, working on the social history of the eastern Mediterranean, and I serve as the graduate representative on my university library's special collections acquisitions committee; I do not vote, but I draft the decision memo that the committee discusses before each vote. Next Thursday we will decide whether to accept a donation of 47 inscribed lead tablets from a private collector. The collector says the tablets were found in a sealed cave deposit in the eastern Mediterranean in the 1970s, but there is no excavation record; he bought them from a dealer in 1976, and his notes about the findspot contradict one another. If genuine, the inscriptions would be the earliest evidence for a particular religious association and would name several otherwise unknown officials. Two colleagues in my department have already published preliminary readings treating the tablets as authentic; three others have circulated a memo arguing that the inscriptions are pastiches, pointing to anachronistic formulae and a suspiciously convenient overlap with better-known literary sources. A radiocarbon date on a fragment of the wooden box that supposedly held them yields a range of roughly 150 BCE to 250 CE, consistent with the alleged date but also with a modern forgery that used old wood. The collector will donate the tablets only if we agree to catalog them as 'ancient documents' and to allow scholars to cite them as historical evidence; otherwise he will sell them at auction.

The committee is split. One faction says that our policy on unprovenanced antiquities forbids acquisition without a documented chain of custody, and that accepting the tablets would encourage looting and forgery; they add that authenticity is not the same as truth, and that even a genuine document can be tendentious. The other faction says that refusing the donation would deprive scholarship of potentially irreplaceable evidence, that provenance is often lost for innocent reasons, and that we can accept the tablets while labeling the uncertainty. A subcommittee has proposed a compromise: accept the donation, but adopt a 'warrant score' for each tablet, combining (a) the strength of the chain of custody, (b) internal consistency and conformity to known epigraphic practice, (c) corroboration by independent evidence, and (d) the base rate of forgery for comparable objects on the market; the score would be printed in the catalog and would have to accompany any scholarly citation. One member wants to add a 'criterion of embarrassment' (a detail is more likely authentic if it cuts against the interests of the community that produced the text), and another wants a 'criterion of multiple independent attestation,' but the subcommittee worries that these criteria smuggle in contestable assumptions about how texts and communities work.

Please draft the memo I will circulate before Thursday's meeting. Recommend a concrete course of action, with a clear bottom line, and if you recommend acceptance, specify the conditions you would attach. Engage the strongest argument on the losing side seriously—especially the worry that a warrant score lends a false appearance of rigor to what is at bottom an unverifiable guess, and the worry that cataloging the tablets as 'ancient documents' would be a lie of omission. The memo should be something the committee can actually vote on, and it should say what follows for how the tablets may be used, published, and cited. It should also address the general policy question, not just this donation: how should we decide, in a principled way, when an unprovenanced object or text can count as evidence about the distant past?
