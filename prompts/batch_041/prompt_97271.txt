I'm a science journalist at work on a long feature about scientific realism, and I keep running into the same wall in interviews: everyone reaches for the same three or four episodes — phlogiston, the luminiferous aether, caloric, the shift from classical mechanics to relativity — and the conversation never gets past the textbook version of them. For the piece I want a case that is engineered rather than borrowed, one I can hand to a philosopher of science and watch what she does with it. So please construct one. Invent an episode of theory succession in some delimited area of physics or chemistry: an earlier theory and a successor, both of your own devising, neither a disguised version of a historical theory. The predecessor must have enjoyed real empirical success; the successor must be strictly more successful, in that it predicts and then confirms at least one phenomenon the predecessor could not have reached; the successor must not be merely the predecessor recovered as a limiting case or a special case; and yet some substantial piece of the predecessor's mathematical structure must survive into the successor, described concretely enough that I can see exactly what carries over and what does not. The surviving structure also has to do exclusionary work — it must rule out some otherwise viable ontologies for the successor theory, so that "the structure survived" is not trivially true of anything. The episode should be one that scientists could plausibly have lived through — a bench, a priority race, a paper that settles the question — not science fiction, and no longer than a paragraph of narrative. I don't need citations, references, or a reading list; nothing in the piece will hang on the actual literature.

Then do the work the case exists for. State what each of four positions must say about this episode — the entity realist, who takes the predecessor's successful posits as reason to believe in the entities themselves; the epistemic structural realist, who thinks we can know only the structure and not the natures of the things that bear it; the ontic structural realist, for whom structure is all there is to say about the world; and the skeptic who runs the pessimistic meta-induction from the graveyard of past theories — and try to engineer the episode so that the two structural realisms come apart over it, giving genuinely different verdicts. If the episode cannot be made to do that without my stipulating the verdicts rather than the case generating them, say so plainly, because that impossibility would be as interesting to me as the case itself. Tell me which single feature of the case is doing the work, what becomes of each verdict when that feature is removed, and how the position that fares worst would attack the case — what its defender would say to defuse it. Finish with the honest note I always ask for: where this construction is most likely to break down, and which consideration or counterexample would lead you to give up the case and the verdicts you drew from it.
