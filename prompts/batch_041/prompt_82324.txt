Last night I lost several hours to an argument with a friend about population ethics, and we ended up exactly where we started, which I gather is roughly where the literature has sat for about forty years. My friend is a totalist: he thinks what makes an outcome better is more total welfare, so a sufficiently large population of lives that are barely worth living can be better than a smaller population of flourishing lives, and he accepts that with perfect equanimity. I put the usual pressure on him — that on his view a world of many barely-worth-living lives, plus a few lives of unremitting torture, can still beat a smaller world of excellent lives — and he replied, calmly, that our intuitions about populations that large are simply not to be trusted. So I turned the same pressure on the position that looks designed to avoid this, the one on which adding a life counts in the plus column only if it clears some threshold of wellbeing. It has a mirror-image cost: it can imply that it is better to add a small number of lives of unremitting suffering than a large number of lives that are barely worth living. His answer was that this is an artifact of a badly chosen threshold. Neither of us moved, and I notice that each of us spent the evening defending a cost that the other regards as flatly absurd.

What I want from you is not a verdict on which view is right but a diagnosis of why this keeps happening. My working hypothesis is that the stall is structural rather than a failure of cleverness on either side: I understand there are formal results to the effect that no theory of this general shape can satisfy all of a short list of conditions that each look almost too weak to deny — among them that adding a life worth living does not make an outcome worse, and that it is not better to add lives of suffering than lives worth living — so that every theory must buy one of these by surrendering another, and the real disagreement lives in the choice of which to surrender. If that is right, then I want to know which of the following the actual commitments of the positions support: (a) a genuine formal dead end, in which case the interesting work is identifying which condition has to go and what giving it up costs elsewhere in the theory; (b) a clash of bedrock intuitions, quantity against quality, for which neither side can offer an argument from anything more basic, so that progress could only mean a negotiated trade-off that nobody is obliged to accept; (c) not a disagreement about value at all but about whether different-sized populations can be ranked at all — whether 'better than' is even well defined in the cases the whole debate turns on — which would make the question ill-posed rather than merely hard; or (d) two camps answering different questions under one heading, one about what makes a state of affairs good and one about what we owe to possible people. Be concrete about what each side has already conceded, and do not give me a survey of proposed theories.

Then tell me what would count as progress given your diagnosis: a new condition plus an impossibility result showing the old ones were inconsistent for a deeper reason, a demonstration that one of the extreme cases has been misdescribed, a change of question, or nothing at all. For what it is worth, my own hunch is that the impossibility results are real but have been misread as a verdict on the subject rather than as a constraint on one particular formal framework, and that the productive move is to locate which of that framework's assumptions — that welfare is the only currency, that every outcome must be ranked, that 'better than' is transitive — is doing the damage. If that hunch is wrong, say so, and say why.
