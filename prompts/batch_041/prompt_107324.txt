I teach a 90-minute session of an upper-level ethics seminar with about 16 students, and I want them to run into — not be told about — the pressure on the doctrine of double effect's condition that the foreseen harm must not be the means by which the good is achieved. Please design a runnable activity with a debrief, not a lecture.

The spine I have in mind, which you should feel free to improve: students sit as a hospital or military review board that must return PERMITTED or FORBIDDEN on a planned action and write one sentence naming the feature that decided it, applying a four-condition test printed on a handout (the act is not intrinsically wrong; the harm is not the means to the good; the harm is not intended; the good is proportionate). Don't name the doctrine on the handout — let them meet the name in the debrief.

Then three rounds of case files:

(1) Two actions that are physically identical and differ only in the agent's aim: in one, the harm is the mechanism that produces the good; in the other, the same harm is a foreseen accompaniment of producing the good by other means. The verdict should flip on intention alone.

(2) The loop case: diverting a threat onto a side track where it strikes one person, and that collision is what halts the threat before it reaches five. The harm is plainly a means, yet most boards will permit it — the condition misfires.

(3) The same event under two descriptions, one locating the harm on the causal path to the good and one placing it off to the side, with the board asked which description is the description and who gets to choose it.

Mechanically: after each round, post the verdicts and have the room vote on whether a given board's verdicts are mutually consistent; reserve a consistency-auditor role for the final round, so someone must defend a board's whole pattern aloud.

Give me the rules, the case files in enough detail to run them, a timeline, and a debrief that maps each round onto its pressure point — intention as the only difference-maker, the means/side-effect line misfiring, the line moving with the description. Include what will predictably go wrong the first time and how to handle it, and be honest about what the activity cannot reproduce: it will show participants that their own verdicts conflict, but it cannot tell them whether the fault lies in the doctrine or in the intuitions, and I will have to supply that.
