I teach a graduate seminar on rational choice, and last week, while presenting what I take to be the standard defense of expected-utility maximization, I was pressed by a student until the argument came out looking less like a defense than a reductio. I have reconstructed it below as carefully as I can, and I would like you to diagnose it.

(1) A rational agent cannot be money-pumped. There is no finite sequence of trades such that at each step the agent prefers the position the trade would put it in to the position it currently occupies, and yet at the end of the sequence it is worse off than it began. To be led by one's own preferences into a position one prefers less is the clearest case of practical irrationality.

(2) Hence a rational agent's preferences contain no strict cycle: no A ranked above B, B above C, and C above A.

(3) The same requirement rules out gaps as well as cycles. An agent that has no ranking of A against B has no determinate basis for choosing between them; where it chose, its choice would be arbitrary; and arbitrariness, no less than a cycle, would leave it at the mercy of whatever option it happened to confront. So for any two options, a rational agent must either rank one above the other or count them equal.

(4) Preferences that are both acyclic and gapless can be represented by a real-valued utility function; and when the same exploitability reasoning is carried over from certain options to lotteries, the representation comes out linear in probabilities. An agent so represented maximizes expected utility.

(5) Therefore an agent that regards two options as incomparable — neither better nor worse nor equal — is irrational.

(6) And since no finite agent can have a determinate ranking of every pair of options it might ever encounter, including options whose character it cannot now anticipate, no finite agent is rational.

I cannot accept (6), and I am no happier with (5): I do not think someone who says of two careers, or of two lives, that they are simply not commensurable — that she can see no common scale on which to weigh them — is thereby guilty of a failure of rationality. Yet I am confident of (1) and (2), and I do not want to lose the money-pump argument against cycles. Tell me where the argument should be blocked, why the step you name looked compelling, and what blocking it costs. If (3) can be defended on some ground independent of exploitability — say, that to be an agent at all is to be disposed to choose — I want to hear that defense and its price. And if the honest verdict is that incomparability really is irrational and (6) must be swallowed, say so, and say what becomes of the thought that some choices are rationally underdetermined. I would also like to know whether the trouble is local to one step or is something the coherence program cannot escape.
