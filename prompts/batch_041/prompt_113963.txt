I'm a software engineer with no formal background in philosophy, and I host a small podcast where I interview researchers about conceptual puzzles. Next week's guest is a philosopher whose new book argues that beliefs and desires don't exist — that the vocabulary of propositional attitudes is a decaying folk theory we should retire rather than repair. Draft eight to ten interview questions that force her to commit to specifics instead of restating the thesis, with a follow-up keyed to her likely answer for each. I want at least one question pressing the self-defeat worry in its practical form (how can she argue for the conclusion without treating her audience as having beliefs that her argument could change?), with a follow-up ready if she says that asserting something doesn't require believing it; one asking what would actually count as evidence that the folk theory is false rather than merely rough, and whether the familiar comparison to phlogiston and caloric is doing real work or just decorating the claim, plus a follow-up if she retreats to saying the comparison is only illustrative; one asking whether her thesis is a claim about how brains work or about how we ought to talk, and whether defending the first while recommending the second is a bait-and-switch; and one built on a case from my own work — a neural network whose internal states I can probe and intervene on, but whose behavior I can only predict statistically — asking whether that is a model of belief without beliefs or merely a model of behavior. Also ask where she draws the line: if beliefs and desires go, what becomes of the posits she still uses herself, such as representations and computations, and what vocabulary is supposed to do the work afterwards? Close by asking what she thinks would concretely be lost if she turned out to be right.
