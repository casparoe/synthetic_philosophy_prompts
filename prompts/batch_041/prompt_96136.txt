I'm four years into retirement after a career as a palaeontologist — mostly fossil vertebrates, mostly teeth — and I've been amusing myself by sitting in on alot of philosophy seminars. Which is how I got into this argument.

The line I keep running into is that evolutionary psychology is just so stories. I've heard it from a grad student in a reading group and, more annoyingly, from a philosopher I respect, and both times it landed like a checkmate, the conversation was just over. I know where the phrase comes from (Kipling, 1902, the one about how the leopard got his spots), but knowing that hasn't told me what the accusation actually is.

So what I want is the menu. Lay out the distinct precise claims that 'that's just a just so story' could be making about a hypothesis. I can see that 'there's no way to test it' and 'it's a story invented after the fact to fit what we already believed' are two different accusations, but I don't know how many there are, weather some of them collapse into each other, or which one people are actually reaching for. For each reading, tell me whether it's a platitude, a serious methodological objection, or plainly false; tell me which reading the person deploying the line in a seminar probably means; and tell me whether that reading, made precise, really gets them the conclusion they're after — that the whole field is a sham — or only tells against a few hypotheses and leaves the rest standing.

For context, the last time it happened I'd been defending the fairly boring claim that our taste for sweet and fatty food is a hangover from a past when those things were scarce. That was the hypothesis that got called a just so story, and I couldn't get anyone to say why.

And here's the thing that nags me. My whole career was inferring the diet and behaviour of animals no one has ever seen, from teeth and bones, and nobody ever called palaeontology a fairy tale. So if the charge boils down to 'you weren't there and you can't run the experiment,' it can't be a special problem for evolutionary psychology — it would sink half the historical sciences. Tell me which of the readings survive that comparison and which don't, and what someone holding the strongest defensible version of the charge would actually have to show, in one concrete case, to make it stick.
