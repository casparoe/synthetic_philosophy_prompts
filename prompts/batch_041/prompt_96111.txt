I run a graduate reading group that has spent the term on self-binding — we started with the obvious Odysseus material and then drifted into constitutional entrenchment, and I keep running into the same frustration: two people who agree completely about the mast and the wax disagree about eternity clauses, and neither can say which of their commitments is doing the work. So please act as a philosopher comfortable in both action theory and constitutional theory, and treat this as a design problem rather than an essay question. Before you write anything, spend a few lines brainstorming the axes along which views about precommitment genuinely divide, and say which of the candidate distinctions you considered and rejected as doing no real work. Then build me a decision tree in prose that takes a reader from a starting commitment to a named position, with each branch node phrased as a question a reader could answer without already knowing which leaf it leads to, and each leaf carrying the name of a position plus one sentence on its most awkward commitment. I don't need citations, reading lists, or a survey of who said what — describe positions by their commitments; if a position happens to have a famous defender you may mention them in passing, but the tree must not depend on names, and where a combination of answers is occupied by nobody, label the leaf "unnamed" and tell me whether that is because the view is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored.

I want the tree organized around three axes in particular, and I'd like you to keep them distinct rather than collapsing them into a single "is precommitment legitimate?" question. The first is the mechanism: distinguish self-binding proper (the agent narrows its own future options), self-insuring (the agent leaves the options open but attaches a cost to taking them), and self-substitution (the agent transfers authority to another agent or body), and make the reader sort their own intuitions by mechanism before they sort them by verdict. The second is temporal authority: which moment is basic — the binding moment, the tempted moment, or the enforcing moment — and note that most familiar views are quietly committed to one of these. The third is revocability, including the awkward middle case of devices that are formally revisable but practically sticky. As the tree branches, it should be forced to take a stand on whether the political cases are continuous with the personal ones or only analogical, and I want that node near the top rather than buried, since it is where my group's disagreements actually live.

Certain cases must appear as branch nodes or leaves, not merely as illustrations: the mast; the smoker's deposit contract; a psychiatric advance directive in which someone in a lucid period authorizes treatment over their own later objection; a sunset clause; an entrenchment clause that a later supermajority wants repealed; central bank independence; and a promise made to someone who has since died. The leaves should include, at minimum, self-paternalism as legitimate, sovereignty of the present self, the view that binding is one temporal self's aggression against another, the deflationary view that "precommitment" is just ordinary intention plus a story, the proceduralist view that binding is legitimate only when it binds process rather than outcomes, the view that a constituent power cannot bind itself at all, and the agency-shifting view that only binding by another party counts. Aim for eight to twelve branch nodes and eight to twelve leaves. Finish by flagging any pairs of leaves your tree cannot separate, diagnosing the unoccupied combinations, and telling me which single node does the most work — and what happens to the whole tree if a reader flips their answer at that node.
