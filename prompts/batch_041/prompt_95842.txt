You are a philosopher with expertise in moral desert, in the comparative philosophy of religion, and in the construction of tight conceptual arguments. I am applying for a departmental summer research grant (two pages, roughly 1,000–1,200 words) and I need a proposal. Please think carefully before drafting: I care far more about whether the question is well posed than about the prose. Begin with a short brainstorm — three or four candidate ways of posing the central question — then commit to one, saying briefly why the others are worse. Do not ask me follow-up questions; where something is unclear, choose the reading that makes the project most tractable and flag the choice.

My half-formed idea is to take karma seriously as a theory of moral desert rather than as a doctrine to be reported or debunked: a candidate answer to the question of in virtue of what an agent comes to deserve her circumstances. Two features of the picture I want to work with make it unlike any secular desert theory I know, and I suspect they pull in opposite directions.

The first is that the mechanism is impersonal. On the version of the picture I want to work with, there is no judge, no keeper of accounts, and no one to whom the person who suffers can address a claim; consequences accrue to the agent the way a shadow follows the body that casts it. But a desert claim is normally a claim against someone — someone owes the agent something, or is answerable for how she fares. So we need to know whether desert without a claimant is a coherent notion or an equivocation on the word. Note that this is not the familiar worry that karma is harsh or disproportionate; it is the worry that there may be no one for it to be just or unjust to.

The second is that the picture is cyclical, and I think this generates a regress that is rarely pressed. The great attraction of a karmic account is that it answers the arbitrariness objection that dogs every secular desert theory: if your starting position in life looks unearned, karma says it was earned, as the yield of earlier action. But the earlier action was itself performed from a position that was earned from still earlier action, and so on. Either the regress is vicious and no one's desert is ever fully grounded, or it terminates in a first term that is itself unearned — in which case karma has exactly the defect it was meant to cure, merely postponed. I want the proposal to state this argument precisely enough that a reader can see where it bites and what would have to be true of the mechanism for it not to bite.

I also want to try out a distinction of my own and see whether it earns its keep. Ledger desert: a desert claim is a credit or debit that some authoritative process is obliged to honour. Constitutive desert: the agent's circumstances are deserved in the sense that they are the fitting expression of what she has made of herself, with no obligation-bearer anywhere in the picture. My hunch is that karma is defensible only under the second reading, and that critics and defenders alike have mostly been arguing about the first.

The proposal should contain, in this order: (i) the question, stated so that it has a determinate answer, together with the two or three candidate answers; (ii) why the usual religious-versus-secular framing of karma obscures rather than settles it; (iii) the method — what I would actually do, week by week across roughly ten weeks — including the regress argument, a small set of test cases, and a diagnosis of what work the word desert is doing in each; (iv) what a successful outcome looks like (a verdict, not a survey) and what would count as the project failing — I would rather be told in advance which results would show that my distinction collapses, or that the whole thing is a causal thesis with moral colouring; and (v) a realistic scope for the time available, saying explicitly what gets cut.

Two test cases I want the plan to accommodate, though you should feel free to improve on them: a case in which the fitting consequence arrives but no one could owe anything to anyone, and a case involving forgiveness, since forgiveness seems to presuppose a debt that someone can cancel and it is unclear who that someone is here.
