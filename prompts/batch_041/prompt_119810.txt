I'm not an academic, just someone who reads too much and thinks in the shower, and I've got stuck on the run on Northern Rock in September 2007. I know the basic story: the bank had lent long and borrowed short in the wholesale markets, those markets froze, it went to the Bank of England for emergency support, the news got out, and then queues formed outside branches — the first proper run on a British bank in living memory. The government ended up guaranteeing deposits to stop it. I dont have the maths for epistemic game theory, but I've been trying to get my head around it, and I want you to treat the whole thing as a game of that kind. Not the financial mechanics — the knowledge mechanics. So: who are the players, what are their strategies and payoffs, what does each know at each stage, and what does common knowledge of rationality predict should happen? Then tell me where the actual episode diverges from that prediction.

The thing I keep circling is this. Common knowledge isn't only something you build up logically from everyone being rational. It can be produced by public events. A queue outside a branch is a public event. A news report is a public event. Maybe the run wasn't a failure of common knowledge; maybe it was a horrible success of it — everyone came to know that everyone knew that everyone was frightened, and that knowledge changed the payoffs. But then the content of the common knowledge isn't the bank's solvency. It's other people's fear. And I've been tripping over the difference between common knowledge and common belief, where common knowledge has to be true and common belief doesn't. In a run, what becomes common knowledge might only be that everyone believes the bank is in trouble, not that it actually is. Does that distinction do any real work here, or is it a philosophers' quibble?

I also wonder about the almost-common-knowledge case. If every depositor has even a tiny doubt about the bank, and everyone knows everyone has a tiny doubt, does the whole tower of knowledge collapse? Does that explain why a small piece of bad news could trigger a full run? And what about the deposit guarantee — does it change the game by making my payoff independent of what others do, or does it only work if it's common knowledge that the guarantee will hold? What does the episode show about the limits of the theory? Does it miss the media, the queues, the fact that people aren't cold calculators, or the fact that the bank's balance sheet is itself a public object that everyone is trying to read? I want the analysis to run both ways: the theory illuminating the episode, and the episode testing the theory. Say what the theory explains well, what it has to stretch to cover, and where it's just silent. At the end, tell me how confident you are in the overall analysis and single out the one point you're least sure of.
