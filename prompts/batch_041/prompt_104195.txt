A comparison I keep encountering in discussions of whether legends can serve as historical evidence goes like this: an oral tradition is a game of telephone. Some real event occurs; a first witness describes it; the description passes along a chain of retellers, each adding a small distortion; the legend we inherit is simply the last and most corrupted message in the chain. On this picture a legend is degraded testimony -- noisy, but still traceable back to a signal -- and the historian's task is to work backwards through the garbling to recover the event. This is the picture that underwrites the popular claim that behind every great legend there lies a real war, a real king, a real migration, and it is often invoked to license reconstructing history out of myth.

I would like you to audit this analogy. Assume I am trying to decide how much evidential weight a legend can bear for the historical events it appears to be about.

1. Give me the correspondence table: what plays the role of the original message, the first speaker, each reteller, the channel, the noise, and the final utterance -- and what in the target domain is supposed to occupy each of those slots.

2. Identify where the mapping breaks. Consider, in particular, features of the source analogy that the target may lack or possess only by stipulation: that there is a single determinate original; that the chain is linear and single; that distortion is random and cumulative rather than functional and selective; that no reteller has a stake in what the message says; that the message has no use except to be passed on; that fidelity rather than utility is the measure of a good retelling; that the endpoint is a copy of one message rather than a selection among many coexisting variants; and that the point of the exercise is to recover a token rather than a type. Say which of these the telephone picture silently imports and which genuinely hold of oral tradition.

3. Separate the disanalogies that matter for the intended use -- treating a legend as evidence for a particular event -- from the ones that are merely picturesque. If some of the imported features are doing the real work of the conclusion, say which, and explain how.

4. Then test the analogy against three cases with very different evidential shapes: the Trojan War, the Arthurian legends, and the Exodus. For each, say which parts of the telephone picture fit, which fail, and whether the analogy's conclusion -- that the legend is corrupted testimony for a real event -- survives, is unsupported, or becomes vacuous. Note where the three cases diverge from one another, since a single analogy ought not to be permitted to cover them all in the same way.

5. Finally, say what a better analogy would be, if there is one -- one that keeps whatever the telephone picture gets right about transmission without importing the features you identified as doing illicit work.

Argue from the structural features of the cases and of oral transmission. Do not appeal to what scholars are generally said to accept, and do not give me reading lists, citations, or names of authorities, since I have no way to check them. I want the reasoning, not the bibliography.
