The key is that the usual bad-making feature — someone gets misled, harm is done — is missing. Version two: she says something that is true, but she intends it to deceive, and she knows it will deceive. Not a half-truth, not a misleading omission, not mental reservation or equivocation — just a complete true sentence used as a lie. 

More specs: one paragraph per version, present tense, third person. In version one, include a specific detail that makes the hearer's non-deception plausible without making them omniscient. In version two, the true sentence should be about something ordinary, like a project deadline or whether she liked someone's cooking, so the case doesn't turn on exotic facts. No appeals to what God secretly knows or to a character's supernatural insight.

The verdict has to fall out of the view that lying is intrinsically wrong, not out of the character being a cartoon. At the end, give me a confidence level for your main claims and flag the one spot where you're least sure about what the view actually entails — if there's a place where you're guessing, say so.
