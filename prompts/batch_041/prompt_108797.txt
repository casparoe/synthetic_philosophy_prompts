I'm drafting a chapter on money pumps and I want the criteria settled before I evaluate any of the candidate theories. Set out the desiderata that any adequate theory of diachronic rationality must satisfy: the cases it has to classify correctly (the two-option cycle in which a small fee is paid at each switch, the three-option cycle A ≻ B ≻ C ≻ A, the sequential pump in which every step is locally choiceworthy, the agent who escapes only by precommitting, the agent whose preferences change mid-sequence for good reasons, and the agent who simply refuses to complete a sequence she began), the distinctions it must not collapse (consistency of preference versus consistency of choice across time, sure loss versus mere exploitability, being pumpable versus actually being pumped, the rationality of a single act versus that of the sequence containing it, and diachronic requirements versus the synchronic requirements of the moment), and the substantive constraints it must respect — including, I think, that a theory which avoids money pumps only by making the agent's preferences immune to trade, or by licensing her to abandon a plan she has already begun, has renamed the problem rather than solved it. Be explicit about which of these are genuine requirements and which are artifacts of the expected-utility framework, and flag any subset you believe cannot be jointly satisfied; I suspect there is one, and that would be the most useful part of your answer. Then say briefly how resolute choice, sophisticated choice, and the acyclic-preference-plus-optional-stopping line fare against the list, and which single requirement does the most to exclude candidates. Finally, tell me which requirement you would surrender first if forced to, where your own reasoning is most likely to fail, and what argument would move you to a different verdict.
