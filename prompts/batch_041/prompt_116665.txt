I'm a phil grad student and I've somehow ended up as the unofficial referee in a fight between two friends that's been going on for almost a year, and I want to force them to actually write something down instead of re-litigating it at every party. Friend A is the rememberer: she thinks communities have a real ethical duty to remember, that we owe the dead and the wronged a kind of fidelity, that forgetting is basically complicity, and that "moving on" from an atrocity does violence to the people it happened to (she's always invoking Margalit at me). Friend B is the forgetter: he thinks most collective memory is selective, self-serving mythology that keeps grievances on life support, and that forgetting — not amnesia, but a deliberate, chosen letting-go — is often the more decent and more livable option, for individuals and for the polity (he counters with Rieff). The problem is they talk straight past each other: A hears "let's stop caring about victims," B hears "let's be haunted forever," and neither one will concede a millimeter.

So here's what I want from you. Act as the neutral chair of a proper adversarial collaboration between them and draft the joint statement the two of them could actually both sign. Before you write it, brainstorm for a bit in a scratch section I can delete — I want you to hunt for the places their positions secretly overlap, or turn out to be about words rather than substance, before you commit to anything. Then write the document itself: first, a numbered list of agreed claims (about what memory is for, what forgetting is, what's owed to the dead versus to the living, and what neither side has to deny to hold their view); second, the residual disagreements, each phrased in a wording that its own side would accept — so don't render the forgetter's position as "we should stop caring about victims," that's exactly the strawman I'm trying to kill; third, the cruxes, the sub-questions where settling one would actually move at least one of them. My own hunch is that one crux is whether the duty is backward-looking (owed to the dead, who can't be helped anymore) or forward-looking (owed to the living, to stop it happening again), and another is whether "forgetting" can be cleanly seperated from "denial" — but tell me if those are real cruxes or red herrings, and propose better ones if you've got them. There's also a distinction I can't get them to engage with: whether the duty attaches to content (remembering the facts) or to practice (the recurring ritual act of remembering), and I suspect that's load-bearing.

Fourth, and this is the part they'll hate: for each crux, name a concrete case, thought experiment, or piece of evidence that both of them would agree bears on it, and write down in advance what each side predicts about it. Use this running case so it's not all abstractions: a city — call it Calder — has just unsealed the archive of a former regime's internal security service, and the files name informants, some of whom are still alive and now quite old. The council has to pick one: publish the full list, publish a redacted version, seal it for fifty years, or destroy it. I want to see whether the cruxes actually cash out differently across those four options. Where you genuinely can't find a formulation that's neither vacuous nor slanted, say so out loud rather than papering over it — I'd rather have a gap flagged than a fake agreement. And since the whole point is that you might be wrong about one of them, close with a short note on which of your agreed-claims you're least confident both parties would actually sign, and what argument or consideration would tell you you've mischaracterized one side — what would change your mind, in other words. Take your time and don't smooth over the joints.
