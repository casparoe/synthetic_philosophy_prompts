I have come to accept the block-universe picture: past, present, and future are equally real, and passage is a feature of our perspective rather than of the world. What convinces me is the Rietdijk–Putnam style argument. Special relativity denies any privileged simultaneity relation, so if the events that are simultaneous with an event are as real as it is, then by chaining the judgments of differently moving observers we are forced to grant reality to events that lie in one another's absolute past and future, and from there to the whole four-dimensional block. The load-bearing premise, as I see it, is the denial of a privileged present. Tell me what would count against this — against the argument, and against the view it is used to support.

I want the defeaters ranked from fatal to merely awkward, and I want you to sort them into the empirical ones (a result that would unsettle the premise about privileged simultaneity) and the conceptual ones (a hidden assumption in the argument, a consequence eternalism is committed to that turns out unacceptable, a way the view collapses into a rival). Some things I would like pressed on, not as a checklist but because they seem to me to be where the action is: whether the argument needs a principle to the effect that what is real for something real is itself real, and whether that principle can be resisted; whether the conventionalist treatment of distant simultaneity blunts the claim that relativity rules out a privileged now, and whether a privileged present could survive as a brute fact leaving no local trace, as the neo-Lorentzian and privileged-frame defenders of presentism propose; whether general relativity, with its loss of any global time, makes the argument harder to state rather than easier to run; whether the argument is even needed for eternalism, so that a successful reply to it would leave the view standing; and whether rivals like the growing block, the moving spotlight, or a fragmentalist picture that rejects the tidy presentism/eternalism dichotomy do the same work with fewer commitments. I would also like to know whether eternalism predicts anything its rivals do not, or whether it is empirically indistinguishable from them by construction, and if so what that implies for a view of this kind.

I would personally be most moved by a defeater showing that the argument's conclusion depends on a reading of reality that already presupposes tenselessness, so that eternalism is obtained only by helping oneself to it; but I want your honest ranking, not a confirmation of my hunch. Please take a definite position and defend it: end by saying whether, on your assessment, the view survives the strongest thing you can throw at it, or whether it should be given up. If it turns out to be insulated by qualification — retreating, say, to eternalism as the best interpretation rather than a theorem — say so plainly and say whether that is a defect or simply what a view of this kind should look like. I do not want citations, reading lists, or references; I want the arguments themselves.
