After thirty-five years as an analytical chemist I retired, and for the past two years I have been teaching myself philosophy the way I was trained to learn a subject: by trying to build the phenomenon rather than read about it. I convene a fortnightly discussion group of eleven people — two of them lawyers, none of them professional philosophers — and at the next meeting I have one hundred minutes and the floor to myself. What I want the group to feel, rather than merely be told, is the trouble that arises when a rule licenses its own amendment: that a charter able to revise every part of itself, including the part that says how revision works, has no article in which its own authority can be located.

I have drafted the apparatus myself — I would rather not borrow anyone else's published rule set — and I would like you to turn my draft into a runnable session. The draft:

1. The Assembly is whoever is in the room.
2. A proposal is adopted when two-thirds of those present vote for it; abstentions are not counted.
3. Any article of this charter, including this one and Article 2, may be amended by the procedure in Article 2.
4. No proposal may name a member, or award points to a member by name.
5. Adopting an amendment scores its proposer two points and every member who voted for it one point; at the close of the session the highest score wins.
6. Any member may move to repeal any amendment already adopted, by the procedure in Article 2.

Article 4 is bait, and I expect it to be repealed within the first ten minutes, since voting yes pays a point. Please design the session so that this move, and the moves that follow it, are the substance of the exercise rather than an interruption of it.

I need materials and room setup; roles and the order of play; a timeline for the hundred minutes; and instructions that let me say as little as possible while it runs, including what I am to do when someone proposes something that would end the session, dissolve the Assembly, or bind me personally.

Then a debrief mapped to particular moments I can point at: the move from amending an article to amending the procedure for amending articles; why an entrenchment clause cannot entrench itself; what a unanimity requirement does to a single holdout; the difference the group will feel between an amendment being valid and its being legitimate, when a valid amendment takes something from them; and the fact that the session ends only because a clock outside the charter says so — what that shows about where the basic rule's authority actually sits. Give me a version for a group of six as well.

Tell me what will predictably go wrong on the first run and how to handle it. I anticipate three failures at least: trivial amendments eating the time; a member proposing to remove the end of the session; and my two lawyers objecting that real constitutions settle this with a core that cannot be amended at all, after which the group follows them into constitutional law and out of the exercise.

Be honest with me about what a hundred minutes of play cannot reproduce and that I will have to supply in my own voice. My guess is that a charter written in front of them can never carry the authority of one that has stood for two centuries, so the attitude the paradox depends on never arises and the whole thing strikes them as a parlor trick. In my old trade a calibration chain always terminated in a convention rather than a measurement, and everyone knew where it terminated; here nobody can say.

End with a final line beginning Core mechanism: followed by the one rule that produces the trouble, in under thirty words, stated so that I could reuse it in another setting.
