A guy in my economic-history reading group keeps insisting the whole Great Divergence boils down to one thing: Europe was a competitive market, China was a monopoly. Dozens of small states all bidding for merchants, skilled workers and capital, each scared of the others getting ahead — basically firms in a competitive industry, so anything that raised output got selected for and copied. China was one giant firm with a single owner and no rivals, so no pressure to try, and the emperor could just ban whatever he disliked. Hence fragmentation = Europe pulls ahead, unity = China stalls.

I think fragmentation mattered, but this analogy feels like its doing way too much work. Can you give me the mapping table — what plays the firm, the market, the price signal, entry and exit, the customer — and then tell me where it breaks? A few things nag me: states aren't firms (they fight wars, they can't really "exit," their "customers" can't shop elsewhere), monopolies innovate plenty sometimes, and China wasn't so much one firm as a market with its own internal competition. Which of those actually bear on the conclusion he wants, and which are just noise? Then take a side: does the conclusion survive once the disanalogies are on the table, or was the analogy carrying it the whole time?
