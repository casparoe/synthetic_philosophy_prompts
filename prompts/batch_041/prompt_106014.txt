ok, I'm making a little zine of poems about philosophy puzzles and I need one for Molyneux's problem — the person blind from birth who can tell a sphere from a cube by touch, and then gets their sight, and we ask whether they could tell which is which just by looking.

Write me a sestina. The important thing is the six end-words have to be doing real work: choose them so the form is itself part of the argument — the same small set of words coming back in a new order every stanza, never quite the same arrangement, so that by the last stanza the repetition reads less like the same object returning than like the same object under a description that keeps shifting. Something in the poem should make the reader feel the gap between seeing that this is a sphere and seeing that this is the one I was holding, because thats really where the problem lives.

The envoi should be the moment the two senses have to be brought together in one act, and it should land the actual crux instead of dodging it — that the question isn't about spheres and cubes, it's about whether same-shape even means the same thing when the information arrives through a different channel, and whether bringing the concept to bear needs something the senses alone can't supply.

Please dont let it settle into a tidy empiricist or nativist answer; the honest state of the debate is that it's still open and turns on what you think a concept is. But it should be accurate enough that someone who knows Locke's version recognizes it — the distinction between seeing that it's a sphere and seeing that it's the sphere you felt, the worry that the newly sighted person might not even have the right kind of experience to apply the word at all. And it still has to read as a poem, not a lecture with line breaks. No rhyme needed, sestinas usually dont, but the end-words have to be words you can actually build a line on — not "the" and "of".
