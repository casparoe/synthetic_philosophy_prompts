Write a dialogue between two equally competent interlocutors who sit on the advisory board of a medical charity that must distribute a fixed budget among patients whose conditions differ in severity. The first is a prioritarian: she holds that a fixed unit of health benefit matters more, the worse off the person who receives it, and she insists that this is a claim about the moral weight of benefits rather than about the value of equality as such. The second is a critic who presses the leveling-down objection — the charge that any view treating a distribution as better merely because it is more equal will license making the better off worse off when no one is helped — and who suspects that the prioritarian's supposed escape from that charge is either illusory or bought at too high a price.

Let the dialogue unfold as a genuine exchange rather than two monologues. The critic should open with the standard leveling-down case; the prioritarian should give the standard reply — her view is welfarist and monotonic, so it never counts a pure loss to the better off as an improvement. The critic should then press further, for instance by constructing a case in which a small gain to someone very badly off is purchased by a large loss to someone only slightly better off, and asking whether the prioritarian's weighting really diverges from the egalitarian's in any case that could arise, or only in cases that never do. The prioritarian must have a real answer rather than a concession, and the critic must have a real rejoinder; each should respond to the actual point the other has just made, not to a position the other does not hold. Have them work through at least one case with explicit numbers, and let them disagree about what the leveling-down objection ultimately shows — whether it exposes prioritarianism as an unstable compromise between utilitarianism and egalitarianism that inherits the costs of both, or merely shows that a very steep priority weighting is implausible.

The two need not reach agreement, but the exchange should end with the disagreement sharpened rather than restated: one of them should be left facing a specific case or dilemma the other has put and not yet defused. Neither should be a strawman, and neither should prevail by caricature. The arguments must stand on their own — assume the reader has not read the surrounding literature, so do not rest any move on a named article, book, or author, and never ask the reader to accept a claim on the authority of who held it. Roughly 1,000 to 1,300 words.
