I teach an upper-level epistemology seminar (mixed MA and 4th-year) and I'm writing the final. I want ONE essay question on the Gettier problem, 45 minutes, 20 marks, and I dont want anything that can be answered by reciting the fake barns and the stopped clock and then listing the usual menu of fixes — alot of my students have that down pat and it tells me nothing about whether they understand the problem.

The hook I want to use: last term a student proposed his own fix in seminar, roughly this — S knows that p iff (1) p is true, (2) S believes p, (3) S's belief is justified, and (4) nothing false is "doing work" in S's justification, where doing work includes stuff S never says out loud but just takes for granted, not only things she'd actually avow.

So the question should force them to (a) notice that condition (4) is ambiguous — read one way it's only about what S would avow, read the other way it's about every background assumption her evidence rests on; (b) build their own counterexample to the avowal reading, and they have to construct it rather than name a famous one: all of the subject's beliefs come out true, her evidence is genuinely good, and yet the truth of the target belief is secured by something entirely outside that evidence; (c) say what happens to the other reading — does it survive, and if it does, what does it cost, and if not, why not; and (d) say what the whole exercise shows about whether fixes of this shape can ever work, i.e. whether the Gettier problem is a heap of cases or a structure.

They won't be citing anything, and a model answering this offline shouldn't need names or dates either, so keep it clean of that.

Give me the question, a model answer of about 650 words, and a marking scheme: five must-hit points with marks totalling 20, one line each on how candidates typically blow that point, and a clear statement of what separates an A from a B — for me that's whether they see the general structure, not whether they manage to produce a counterexample.
