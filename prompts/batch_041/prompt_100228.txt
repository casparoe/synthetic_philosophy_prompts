I have been reading about the species problem and suspect the whole debate is largely terminological. If a species is simply the smallest diagnosable lineage on the tree of life — a segment of a branch, fixed by ancestry and descent — then species look like perfectly objective features of nature, and the older worry that they are mere human conveniences looks like a residue of pre-Darwinian essentialism. Once you abandon essences and define species by genealogy instead, doesn't the problem simply dissolve? Yet philosophers of biology continue to describe species as conventional, arbitrary, or mind-dependent, and continue to speak of a species problem rather than a solved question. Is that just disciplinary inertia, or is there a substantive disagreement I am failing to see? If there is one, I would like to understand what work the word conventional is doing that ancestry and diagnosability do not already do on their own.
