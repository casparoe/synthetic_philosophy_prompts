I am a software engineer with no formal background in philosophy, and I have been trying to think through the modern debate over wild animal suffering. The part that keeps tripping me up is the so-called predation problem: if the sheer number of wild animals is enormous and most of them die young from starvation, disease, or being eaten, then the natural world looks less like a harmonious system and more like a vast engine of suffering. Some people now argue that we should intervene directly—for example, by using contraception or gene drives to reduce populations of r-strategists, or even by genetically engineering carnivores to become herbivores—while others say that such intervention is hubris and that predation is inseparable from the ecological and evolutionary processes that produced us.

Suppose Charles Darwin were alive today, retaining his general intellectual temperament and his knowledge of natural selection, but also aware of modern biology and the scale of wild animal suffering. What position would he likely take on whether humans should intervene in nature to reduce that suffering? Would he regard such intervention as a natural extension of the sympathy he thought had been gradually extended to more and more beings, or would he see it as a dangerous misunderstanding of the struggle for existence and the web of life? Please reconstruct his probable reasoning in some detail, paying attention to how his views on evolution, human morality, and the place of humans in nature would shape his verdict on concrete proposals like predator suppression or carnivore engineering.
