I have been trying to pin down when someone who blames another for a wrong still has the standing to do so, and my gut verdicts on a run of cases refuse to settle into anything tidy. I should like you to find the principle, or the least cumbersome set of principles, that captures them; to say what it predicts for cases I have not listed; and to tell me which of my verdicts I ought to surrender if no single principle can save them all. I have a provisional principle of my own, but I already suspect it.

Here are the cases, with what I find myself thinking about each.

1. A man who has evaded tax for twenty years tells his brother, who is evading tax this year, that he ought to pay up. I think the brother's retort of 'you too' is decisive here: the demand simply does not land, and it is not merely that the blamer is a bad man.

2. The same man, having since repaid everything and now working for a tax-reform charity, says the same thing to the same brother. I think his standing is restored. The 'you too' is now idle, even a little cheap.

3. A woman who is serially unfaithful to her husband tells a colleague that embezzling from the firm is wrong. I think she has standing; her own wrongdoing is beside the point, and no 'you too' can be made against her.

4. A man who tells small social lies blames a friend for fraud on a mortgage application. I want to say he has standing — a small lie is not a mortgage fraud — but I notice that this concedes that a hypocrite whose own sins are smaller keeps his standing, which sits awkwardly with case 1.

5. A man who has just failed to declare a trifling sum blames his neighbour for large-scale evasion. I think he has standing: his own evasion is nothing beside the neighbour's.

6. A man evading a large sum blames his brother for evading a small one. I think he has no standing; the blamer is the worse offender.

7. A father who abandoned his own children years ago blames his brother for neglecting his. I think he has standing — the demand is made on behalf of the children, and his own record does not defeat it — even though, by the reckoning in case 6, his own wrong is the worse one and in the very same domain.

8. A stranger publicly rebukes a father for shouting cruelly at his child in a supermarket. I think the stranger has standing; the child's distress licenses anyone to speak, and the stranger's own parenting is neither here nor there.

9. A shareholder who for years voted with the board and drew the dividends blames the chief executive for the firm's pollution. I think his standing is diminished; the 'you too' works here, though in a causal rather than a purely conduct register.

10. A woman who cares nothing for the wrong she denounces, and blames a colleague for a real wrong solely to advance herself, nevertheless blames him for something he has actually done. I think she has standing, ugly though the blame is: insincerity of motive does not by itself strip standing. But this troubles me, because my reason in case 1 for saying the demand does not land was that the blamer is not committed to the norm he invokes, and she is no more committed than he.

The case I keep going back and forth on:

11. A woman who is herself evading tax, but only because she cannot afford to pay and would pay if she could, blames her wealthy brother for evading. My gut says she has standing — her evasion is excused, his is not — but 'you too' still seems available to him, and I cannot decide whether it lands.

My provisional principle is this: one has standing to blame for a wrong just in case one is not oneself guilty of a wrong at least as serious in the same domain. I can see that it saves 1 and 6 but mishandles 7, and that it says nothing at all about 9 and 10.

What I want from you: the principle, or the small set of candidate principles, stated precisely; for each clause, the case that forces it; the verdicts it delivers on cases I have not considered — in particular, whether a blamer's standing survives when he is guilty of a worse wrong in a different domain, and when the wrong he denounces is one he could not have committed himself; and an honest account of which of my verdicts the principle cannot accommodate. If I must choose between a complicated principle that saves every verdict and a simple one that overthrows one of them, say which and why.
