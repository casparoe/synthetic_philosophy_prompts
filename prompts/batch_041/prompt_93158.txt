Quick setup so we're on the same page (and so you dont have to go digging around for it): the rough picture is that Hegel treats states as the highest form of ethical life, standing to each other in something like a state of nature with no shared judge above them, so disputes between them get settled by war. He doesn't treat war as an unqualified evil — he gives it a kind of ethical seriousness, says it exposes the vanity of purely private and finite interests, and suggests a people can be kept from settling into stagnation by being forced up against its own finitude. He's also unimpressed by Kant's plan for a permanent league of peace, roughly on the grounds that there's no will standing above the states to make it stick. And history's real business — the growth of freedom, the recognition of persons and peoples as ends in themselves — gets done through, and often behind the backs of, the people actually doing the fighting.

Assume that sketch is close enough. Given it, tell me what he'd say about each of these, and be specific rather than oracular:

1. Nuclear deterrence. MAD, second-strike capability, a whole arsenal designed never to be fired. Does a weapon that's most powerful when unused fit anywhere in his picture, or does it just snap it?
2. Drone swarms and autonomous weapons — a war with no combatants on one side, maybe none on either. If mutual recognition between states was part of what war was doing, what's left of it when nobody can be killed and nobody has to look anybody in the face?
3. Humanitarian intervention / the responsibility to protect. He thinks a state is an individual with a right to its own actuality — does that rule out invading to stop atrocities, or does it somehow get him there anyway?
4. Cyber attacks that quietly degrade another country's infrastructure with no declaration and no visible violence. Is that war for him, or some third thing his vocabulary can't name?
5. Private military contractors: violence as a business rather than as a moment of the state's own will.
6. War crimes tribunals and an international criminal court — i.e. exactly the supra-state judge he said couldn't exist.
