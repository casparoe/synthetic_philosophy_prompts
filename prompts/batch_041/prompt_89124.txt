I'm a science and ideas journalist, and I'm working on a feature for a general-interest magazine — 4,000 words, educated but nonspecialist readership — about Karl Popper's late-career views on Darwinism and what the episode still teaches about how we argue over what counts as science. I've written the spine of the piece below as a set of notes rather than a draft. Before I start writing, I want a hard critique of the argument, not of the prose.

Please act as a philosopher of science with deep expertise in both Popper's epistemology and the philosophy of evolutionary biology. Begin by brainstorming, in a short list, the strongest objections that (a) a Popper scholar and (b) a working philosopher of biology would raise against my framing. Then work through those objections and tell me where the piece is wrong, overstated, or likely to be misread.

Some ground rules. Do not write the essay for me. Do not supply citations, reading lists, or references to specific papers — I have my own sources and will check everything myself; what I need from you is argumentative pressure. Where you think I am asserting something as Popper's considered view that is really my own reconstruction, say so plainly. Flag any claim that you believe is genuinely contested among specialists, even if you can't resolve it.

The notes:

Thesis. The Popper-Darwin episode is almost always told as a cautionary tale: the great champion of falsifiability declares that natural selection is not a testable scientific theory, gets hammered for it, and later admits he was wrong — so the demarcation criterion is too crude to survive contact with real biology. I want to argue nearly the reverse. The episode shows the criterion doing exactly the work Popper wanted it to do, and the revision was less a surrender than an application of the critical method he spent his life preaching. What makes the story get told as a flip-flop is that both camps in the evolution debate want Popper to be doing something he wasn't: anti-evolutionists want a famous philosopher's authority for the claim that evolution is unfalsifiable, and some of Darwin's defenders want a clean verdict that natural selection is a testable law on the model of physics.

The charge. Popper's original worry was vacuity. If fitness is measured by survival and reproduction, then the claim that the fit survive tells us nothing; and a theory that excludes no possible observation is not empirical. He was careful — or at least later insisted he had been careful — to separate the fact of evolution, which he took to be established, from the mechanism, which is where his doubt sat. And here is the point I think gets lost: when he classed Darwinism as a metaphysical research program, that was not, in his vocabulary, an insult. He held that metaphysics is the womb of science, that atomism was metaphysical long before it became testable, and that a research program can mature into testable science. So the interesting question is not whether Popper called Darwinism unscientific. It is what he thought would have to happen for it to become scientific, and whether that has happened.

The revision. In his Darwin College lecture in the 1970s he announced that he had changed his mind about the testability and logical status of natural selection, and he was visibly annoyed that his earlier formulations had been conscripted by anti-evolutionists. My reading is that what changed was not his logic but his estimate of how much independent content the theory carries. Fitness can be specified by heritable traits that predict reproductive success rather than being read off from it after the fact, and selection hypotheses can be tested by manipulation, by comparative prediction, and by the historical record. If that is right, the revision was substantive but narrow, and it does not license the moral usually drawn.

The twist I like most. Popper's own evolutionary epistemology treats the growth of knowledge as a selection process: problems generate tentative theories, theories are exposed to criticism and eliminated, and the elimination generates new problems. That is a selectionist program in his own sense. If selection is empty in biology, it should be equally empty in epistemology, where he plainly thought it was doing real work. Either the unity of his view tells against the tautology charge, or the charge proves too much. I want to use this, but I suspect it's the part of the piece most vulnerable to the objection that I'm trading on an analogy where Popper intended a literal continuity.

The harder problem, which I want to be honest about. Popper's criterion was built to sort empirical science from pseudo-science. The interesting feature of selection is not that it is unfalsifiable in the abstract but that it is hard to falsify locally: for nearly any trait, some selection story can be constructed after the fact. The fix, if there is one, is not that selection is a law with sharp predictions but that selection claims are constrained by population genetics, by the requirement of heritable variation, and by the demand that the posited selective pressure be independently measurable. That is a methodological point, not a demarcation point, and I want to say so rather than pretend the criterion settles it.

Ending. The lesson is not that Popper flunked Darwin. It is that he had two questions in play — is this testable, and is this fruitful? — and that the second is what he cared about in the end. A research program that generates testable questions is doing what science does even when its central principle cannot be falsified in isolation. That is more useful to my readers than another round of is-evolution-science.

What I want from you, specifically: whether the two-questions reading is a distortion of Popper or a fair reconstruction; whether the recantation was substantive or chiefly reputational, and whether there is any way to tell from the outside; whether the evolutionary-epistemology parallel earns its place or should be cut; which single claim in the notes is most likely to be quoted against me by a reader who wants to say evolution is unfalsifiable; and three concrete changes that would make the argument harder to misuse without making it boring. Think carefully before you answer, and be blunt about which parts of this are not worth defending.
