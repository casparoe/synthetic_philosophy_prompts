I sit on my state's sentencing standards commission, and the legislature has directed us to write the release rules for a new earned-release statute. I am the swing vote on Thursday, so I need a written opinion that does real work rather than splitting the difference between the two drafts in front of us.

The background you will need, since you cannot look any of it up: our commission sets a "desert anchor" for each offense class, a presumptive range reflecting the gravity of the offense and the offender's culpability at the time of the act. The new statute also requires an actuarial risk score at intake and at each review. Two drafts are on the table. The first makes the desert anchor binding in both directions: no earned release below the bottom of the range, whatever the risk score, and no confinement past the top of the range, however high the risk score. The second treats the anchor as advisory only, with release set by actuarial risk and demonstrated program participation, the anchor recorded but not binding, and no floor. That second draft also carries a provision I asked for and now worry I did not think through: an acknowledgment interview, under which a prisoner becomes eligible for earned release only after narrating the offense, its wrongfulness, and their own responsibility for it. Our research staff report that these interviews predict reduced recidivism modestly, that they are unreliable in the technical sense, since the same prisoner can pass one week and fail the next, and that a substantial minority of prisoners who are eligible on every other measure fail them for reasons having nothing to do with their conduct: limited English, a diagnosed autism spectrum condition, a documented incapacity to feel or express remorse following traumatic brain injury.

Three cases are in the file and will be the ones cited at the meeting. The first is a man serving a sentence for a single count of arson of an unoccupied commercial building, committed to destroy records of a debt he could not pay; his risk score is high, and the desert anchor for his offense class is three to four years. The second is a woman convicted of vehicular manslaughter; her risk score is the lowest the instrument produces, and her desert anchor is seven to nine years. The third is a man whose risk score has fallen sharply over six years in custody, a real change rather than an artifact of the instrument, and who will fail the acknowledgment interview because he maintains that the death he caused was an accident for which he does not deserve blame; the file contains a psychiatric finding that he is not capable of believing otherwise. He sits at the bottom of his desert range.

What I want from you is a decision, not a survey of punishment theory. Trace where the two drafts actually diverge, and do it at the level of what happens to a particular prisoner rather than in the abstract, using the three cases as the test. Then tell me what the disagreement between Daniel Dennett's compatibilism and Gregg Caruso's free-will skepticism, over whether anyone ever deserves to suffer for what they have done, actually changes in this decision and what it leaves untouched. My expectation is that it leaves more untouched than either man would like, and that the acknowledgment interview is one of the things the commission will have to settle on grounds the debate does not supply. If that is right, say so and say why; if it is wrong, say that instead. I would rather have a straight answer than a balanced one.

Please write the opinion in the following sections, in this order and roughly these lengths:

1. Recommendation — about 150 words. Which draft I should vote for, and whether I should move amendments or vote to send the statute back to the legislature.
2. Where the drafts actually differ — about 350 words. The operative difference between them, stated in terms of what each treats as the reason a person is confined, and traced through the three cases.
3. The three cases — about 450 words. Each case under each draft, including the one where the two drafts happen to agree, and whether that agreement is principled or accidental.
4. The acknowledgment interview — about 400 words. Whether eligibility should turn on it at all, given the reliability problem and the capacity problem, and what the commission should do instead if the answer is no.
5. What the Dennett–Caruso disagreement changes — about 400 words. What it settles here, what it does not, and which of the commission's choices are being made on assumptions neither position supplies.
6. The case against my recommendation — about 300 words. The strongest argument for the draft I am not choosing, and for my colleague who wants to strike the earned-release provision entirely.
7. Amendments and what I will say Thursday — about 150 words. Concrete language I can move, and the two or three sentences I should lead with at the meeting.
