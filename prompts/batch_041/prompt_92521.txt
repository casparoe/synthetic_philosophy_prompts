I am a novelist — four books, a slow career — and for the last several years a good part of my income has come from translating other people's novels, which is how I ended up on the board of a small nonprofit that runs a prize, a hardship fund, and a lobbying line on behalf of literary translators. Ten days from now the board votes on whether to put our name to a pledge that has been circulating in the profession since the spring. The pledge is narrow: signatories commit to refuse any contract in which the text to be delivered is a machine translation draft that a human is hired to clean up. There are no boycotts, no lists of offending publishers, no coordination of any kind — only a public, personal refusal, recorded on a website. The steering group behind it has been refreshingly blunt about the arithmetic. Refusals matter only if a large share of working translators refuses; they put the share needed to make publishers stop writing that clause into contracts at somewhere near forty percent, and they guess that perhaps fifteen percent of the profession would sign today. We are a few hundred members; the market is tens of thousands of people, scattered across a dozen languages. And the board is not being asked to instruct anyone: the other translators are strangers who will never hear our reasons, and we are deciding in parallel with them, on the same public evidence, in the same months. Signing would cost us concretely — two of our board members have already lost regular work for saying in public what the pledge says in writing, and I would probably lose a series I have translated for eleven years.

What I cannot settle is an argument that has split the board. One of my colleagues, the sharpest person in the room and a translator of poetry, says I have the question backwards. Whether our signature changes anything for anyone else is, he grants, not in our hands. But our signature is evidence about what people who reason as we do, facing the same choice on the same evidence, are going to do; if we sign, that is a small piece of evidence that the profession is drifting toward the threshold, and if we refuse, a small piece of evidence that it isn't. On his account the board should sign even though the pledge is, individually, a pure loss. Our treasurer calls this a pun dressed as a policy: other people's decisions are what they are, our signature cannot make them otherwise, and a board that votes to lose money on the strength of what its own vote tells it about strangers will vote for anything. I think the treasurer is right about causation, and I do not think my colleague is simply confused, and I cannot tell whether the disagreement between them is substantive or verbal. Please draft the memo I will circulate before the vote. It should recommend a course of action — sign, refuse, or sign under a stated condition — with a time frame, give the strongest form of the losing argument its due rather than dispatching it, and be written for a board of nine, most of whom are not philosophers and one of whom is a retired contract lawyer. No reading list; I need an argument I can defend at the table. Keep it under 700 words, and say somewhere in it how confident you are in your central claim and name the single point you are least sure of.
