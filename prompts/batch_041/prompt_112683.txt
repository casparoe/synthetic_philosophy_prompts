I'm a conservator at a small natural history museum, and I've gotten into a long-running argument with my friend Noor, who's a philosophy grad student and has very settled views about material constitution. We keep going in circles about whether a statue is the same thing as the clay it's made of, so I started keeping a list of her verdicts on other cases. Here's her track record: (1) the classic statue and lump of clay—two things, because the lump can be squashed and survive while the statue can't; (2) a bronze statue that gets melted down—the statue goes out of existence, the bronze doesn't; (3) a sandcastle and the sand—two things, because kick it over and the sand is still there; (4) a cloud and its water droplets—two things, because the droplets drift on after the cloud is gone; (5) a human person and their body—two things, because the body can outlive the person; (6) a word and the ink marks that spell it—two things, because the word can be rewritten in different ink. In every one of these, the "formed" thing and the "stuff" have different persistence conditions: one can survive the other's destruction. I'm definately not a philosopher, so I might be missing something, but that's the pattern I see.

Now here's the case I actually care about. I'm cataloguing a specimen that came to us from a private collection: a chunk of a rare mineral (call it "petriform") that erosion has shaped into a perfect seated figure. It's been on display as "The Seated One" for decades. The mineral is extremely brittle and cannot be reshaped, repaired, or partially destroyed. If it breaks, it shatters into dust, and both the figure and the chunk of mineral cease to exist at the exact same moment. There's no possible future in which the figure survives without the mineral, or the mineral survives without the figure. They came into existence together when the erosion finished, and they'll go out together when it breaks. So unlike all Noor's earlier cases, the persistence conditions match perfectly. My question: would Noor say the figure and the mineral are identical (one thing) or distinct (two things)? I can see at least two rules that fit her old verdicts: (A) constituted object and matter are distinct just when their persistence conditions differ, and identical when they match; (B) anything with a "form" or "shape" sortal—statue, figure, sandcastle, cloud—is always distinct from its matter, no matter what, while "stuff" sortals like "mineral" or "bronze" just name the matter. Both rules give the same answer on every case she's judged so far.

Which one is she actually running? What would she say about the Seated One? And what further case would tell the two rules apart? Please start by brainstorming the candidate rules and their predictions, then give me your best guess with reasons, and tell me what would settle it. You're a philosopher of material constitution, so think carefully—but no need for citations or a reading list, I just want the reasoning.
