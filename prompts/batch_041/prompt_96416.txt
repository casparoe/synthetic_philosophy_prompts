My friend and I have been having the same arguement for about two years now about whether the Nordic model can be exported, and we've finally agreed to stop shouting at each other and write the document we could both put our names to. I'm the skeptic. My line is that "the Nordic model" isn't a policy package, it's an equilibrium that fell out of a particular history: unions and employers' federations dense enough to bargain centrally (the Saltsjöbaden deal between LO and the Swedish employers' association in 1938 is my favourite example, mostly because the interesting part is the government backing off and letting the two sides write their own rules), unions that run the unemployment insurance so membership stays high, wage bargaining coordinated around a pattern set by the export sector, and the Rehn-Meidner idea that solidaristic wages should deliberately squeeze low-productivity firms out of business while active labour market policy shovels the workers somewhere better. Then the contingent stuff: Norway's oil, small open economies, the fact that Danish "flexicurity" is really cheap firing plus generous benefits plus retraining, and that all of these countries reformed hard after their own crises in the 90s. My friend thinks that's all romance. On their view the causal core is a boring policy bundle -- high taxes, universal services, cheap childcare, strong safety nets -- that any country can legislate, and the high trust everyone admires is downstream of the policies rather than a precondition for them.

So give us the joint statement.

1. A numbered list of claims we can both sign. What the Nordic countries actually do (be specific and honest -- a lot of the tax take is VAT and payroll, not just progressive income tax), what the outcomes are and are not, and what the historical record of attempted transplants can and can't establish. Keep it non-vacuous; I don't want a list of things like "institutions matter".

2. The precise points where we still disagree, each one phrased the way the person who holds it would accept it. If my summary of my own position above is a strawman, fix it and tell me you fixed it.

3. The cruxes -- the sub-questions where, if one of us turned out to be wrong, we'd actually change our mind. I suspect one is whether trust is upstream or downstream of institutions, and another is whether the right unit of analysis is "institutions" or "a bargaining structure that can credibly threaten its own members with unemployment". Say if you think there's a third one I'm missing.

4. For each crux, the kind of case or natural experiment we'd both accept as bearing on it, and both of our predictions written down in advance, plus your honest guess about which of these we will never get a clean test of.

Then, at the end, tell me which parts of our fight are verbal -- we probably mean different things by "the model" and by "exportable", and I'd like those pulled apart and the definitions separated.

Don't tell us who's right. And don't name countries, studies or scholars you're guessing at; if you're not confident of a specific example, describe the kind of case instead of naming one.
