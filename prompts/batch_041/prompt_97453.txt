I would like your credence, with the derivation exposed, in the following proposition, which I will label C:

(C) For a typical small-scale individual choice—the paradigm case being whether to spend five dollars on a coffee now or to direct the same five dollars to a highly effective charity—the expected consequentialist value of the difference between the two options is well-defined and falls within one order of magnitude of the difference in their short-run effects.

The interest here is not the coffee but the structure of the problem. The familiar difficulty, sometimes called cluelessness, is that a consequentialist must weigh the whole of an act's causal downstream, and we appear to know almost nothing about it. I have come to think that the dispute between those who treat this as a fatal problem for consequentialism and those who treat it as manageable, or even illusory, is really a disagreement between two unstated assumptions about how small causes propagate. I would like you to price the question with those assumptions named.

The two assumptions are these. The Cancellation Principle holds that over long horizons the causal contribution of an ordinary individual act to aggregate value is approximately zero-mean and roughly independent of the contributions of other such acts, so that as the horizon extends the expected value-difference converges toward the short-run difference and the surrounding uncertainty is noise around it. The Compounding Principle holds that social and historical systems are chaotic and path-dependent, so that a small act's causal footprint propagates into an outcome distribution of enormous spread, whose expectation may be dominated by low-probability, high-magnitude branches, or may fail to converge at all.

Two further considerations I want folded in rather than set aside. First, the ergodicity point as it has been developed outside moral philosophy: where value compounds multiplicatively rather than additively, the ensemble average over possible futures and the average along any single realized trajectory can come apart, and maximizing expected value is then not obviously the same as doing well by the actual world. Second, whether expected value is even the right decision-relevant functional here, or whether cluelessness, if it obtains, tells against consequentialism as a criterion of rightness, as a decision procedure, or both—and whether those two can come apart.

So: give me a single number for C, then decompose it. At minimum I want probabilities for (i) that ordinary acts have any non-negligible long-run causal footprint at all; (ii) that, conditional on a footprint, its expected value is centred on the short-run difference rather than dominated by tail branches; (iii) that the relevant expectation converges, and so is well-defined, given how the value distribution behaves in the limit; (iv) that expected value is the correct quantity to maximize here in the first place; and (v) any further term you think belongs. Show how the terms combine into the number, then tell me which single term the number is most sensitive to, which of the two principles above you are implicitly assuming when you assign it, and what argument or result would move the number most.

For calibration: I am at roughly 0.35 on C, and a colleague who regards the whole problem as a confusion is at roughly 0.85. If your decomposition can also indicate which sub-question the two of us most likely differ on, that is the part I will actually use.
