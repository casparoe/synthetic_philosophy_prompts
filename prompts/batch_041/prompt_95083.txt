Two responses to the St. Petersburg paradox are frequently run together, and I would like them separated and then compared. The first locates the trouble in the value of money: the utility of money is bounded above, or at least rises slowly enough, so the game's expected utility is finite, its fair price is modest, and nothing has gone wrong with the expectation itself. The second locates the trouble in the step from a divergent series to a price: the expectation of the game does not exist as a real number, and an infinite expectation is not a price any finite agent can sensibly pay, since it would license paying any finite sum whatsoever — including sums that would ruin the agent — for a ticket whose payoff is finite with probability one.

Please compare the two. Specifically:

1. Are they genuinely distinct, or does one collapse into the other? Does the second covertly require bounded utility, or does the first already presuppose a particular view about what an expected value is supposed to deliver?

2. In what respects is each one special — what does each claim that the other cannot, and what does each leave untouched? Is either of them a response to the paradox at all, as opposed to a redescription of it?

3. Which of the two survives, and which is embarrassed by, each of the following: (a) the mirror-image game, in which the losses double as the probabilities halve, so that the expected loss is infinite while the loss itself is finite with probability one; (b) a game with a bounded maximum payoff but infinitely many possible outcomes; (c) a game constructed so that its expected utility diverges under every unbounded utility function; (d) a version in which the ticket is freely tradable and the market clears at a finite price; (e) a version offered by a counterparty whose unlimited reserves and guaranteed payout are stipulated rather than assumed away.

4. State what each response predicts about an agent's willingness to pay as the agent's wealth grows and as the stakes are rescaled, then give your verdict on which response is the better one, naming the single variation above on which your verdict most depends.
