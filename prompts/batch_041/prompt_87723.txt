For about six years now I've been shooting medium-format film, so I have a fairly concrete feel for what blur actually is, and that's the backstory to a problem I've been chewing on since last spring, when I sat in on a graduate seminar on vagueness. One of the students there kept reaching for the same comparison every time the debate between epistemicism (there is a sharp cutoff in every sorites series, the sequence from clearly bald to clearly not-bald, but we can never know where it falls) and supervaluationism (a vague sentence has no truth value except where every admissible way of sharpening it agrees) came up. His line was that a vague predicate is like a blurred photograph: the blur is in the picture, not in the scene, the world is perfectly sharp, and what is fuzzy is only our representation of it. He took this to settle the question, on the grounds that there is no sharp boundary out there for the epistemicist to say we are merely ignorant of, so epistemicism is unmotivated and the supervaluationist's picture wins by default. I don't think it is that simple, and I don't trust my own reaction enough to state it cleanly, so I'd like you to audit the comparison properly rather than reassure me.

Give me the correspondence table first: what plays the role of the photograph, the lens, the scene, the resolution, the grain, the eye of the viewer. Then the places where the mapping holds, the places where it breaks, and what the comparison quietly imports from photography that vague predicates may not have at all, separating the breakages that matter for the conclusion he is drawing from the ones that are just scenery. Two things I want you to take seriously, because they are where my doubts live. First, the analogy seems to me to run the other way: a blurred photograph is a photograph of a fully sharp scene, so if vague language is blurred then the world behind it is sharp, which is what the epistemicist says and not what my classmate says. Second, blur is not the same thing as vagueness. As anyone who has actually focused a lens knows, a defocused image is a convolution of a sharp image with a point spread function: it is a weighted average over a whole family of sharp images, and the result is perfectly determinate at every point, since every pixel still has an exact value. Taken seriously as optics rather than as a loose metaphor for fuzziness, then, the analogy seems to point away from truth-value gaps and toward a view on which vagueness is a matter of degree, or of a spread of admissible sharpenings carrying different weights. Tell me which reading of the analogy is the right one, whether either version can actually support supervaluationism over epistemicism or the reverse, and where higher-order vagueness (the fact that the boundary between the clearly bald and the borderline is itself not sharp) shows up as a failure of the photographic image, since a blurred photograph has a perfectly determinate extent of blur. Finish with a definite verdict on which analogy, if any, you would use, and what you would say to my classmate. American English, please, and no hedging: I would much rather have a committed answer with reasons I can argue with than a survey of the options.
