My film group is doing Rashomon -- the Kurosawa one, 1950, the one that gave us the word for "everybody remembers it differently" -- and I got handed the introduction. Meanwhile I've been reading my way into Indian epistemology, the Nyāya school, and the thing thats grabbed me is that they treat testimony as a real, independent source of knowledge, one of four pramāṇas sitting alongside perception, inference, and comparison or analogy. Not a poor substitute for checking things yourself, but a genuine means of knowing. (I dont read Sanskrit and I'm working from a couple of secondary sources, so correct me if I've mangled the list or the reasoning.) In my old line of work we spent a lot of time on instrument error, and Nyāya seems to treat the witness as an instrument with its own calibration conditions, which is why the film suddenly looked different to me.

So: read the film the way a Nyāya philosopher would watch it. Four people describe the same afternoon in the grove, and it isn't just details that diverge -- the stories are morally incompatible, each one flattering its teller. The bandit makes himself a great lover and a fair fighter; the wife makes herself a victim who nearly drowns herself; the dead samurai, speaking through the medium, makes himself honorable. And the woodcutter, who is the only one with no reputation to protect and who was actually there, also lies, and only comes clean when the commoner pushes him. Then at the end he takes home the abandoned baby and the monk decides people are worth trusting after all.

I want the reading to take both sides seriously, so here's what I'm after:

- If testimony is a pramāṇa, what exactly is breaking down here? Nyāya seems to require that a witness be someone who knows the thing first-hand and intends to tell it straight. Each of the accounts looks to me like it fails a different one of those conditions -- or maybe they aren't testimony in the Nyāya sense at all, but something else wearing its clothes. Memory reports? Reconstructions built to make the teller look good? Secondhand reports at third hand? Say which, and why it matters.
- As I understand it Nyāya doesn't give memory its own pramāṇa status, because memory is parasitic on the original cognition. The whole film is people reciting memories hours or days later, and I have a hunch this is doing real work in the film, but I can't say what. Does it?
- The medium: the samurai testifies while dead, through a psychic. Does that clear the bar for being a witness at all, or is it testimony twice removed? And is the monk's reaction throughout a clue about what testimony is supposed to accomplish?
- The lazy reading -- nobody knows anything, it's all perspective -- seems too easy. Would a Nyāya philosopher buy it? Contradictory testimonies don't refute the pramāṇa, they just demand that you hunt for defeaters, so which account has the fewest, and does the film actually let us pick one? If it doesn't, is that an argument against the framework or just a nasty case?
- Now the honest part. Where does the film refuse the Nyāya reading and force you to admit it? My suspicion is that the film is about people lying to themselves before they lie to anyone else, and that each story is shaped by the kind of person the teller needs to be. Nyāya has error, doubt, and defeaters -- does it have anything for self-deception? If not, say so plainly rather than papering over it.
