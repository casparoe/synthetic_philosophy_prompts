I teach a seminar on commodification and I've finally started writing down what I actually think about markets in odd goods — partly because I'm tired of running the same kidney/vote/price-gouging drill every year, and partly because my students keep catching me contradicting myself. Verdicts below, straight from the gut. I want to know what principle, if any, they add up to.

1. A 24-year-old with medical debt sells a kidney to a stranger through a clinic for $9k. Legalize it. I'm not thrilled, but I'd legalize it.
2. A hardware store charges $60 a case for bottled water two days after the flood. Fine. Price is information.
3. A guy with a bot buys 40 tickets to a show and flips them at six times face. This one bugs me, and I'd have no complaint if it were banned.
4. Selling your vote in a state election for $200. No — and not for consequentialist reasons. It feels like a category mistake, like selling a memory.
5. Paying $500 for an A. No.
6. Paying someone $150 to sit in line overnight outside a congressional hearing so you can take the seat at 9am. I'm told this is a real service in DC. Fine. That's just labor, like hiring a mover.
7. Hiring a professional mourner to weep at your mother's funeral. Fine, and I'll defend it. Grief wants bodies in the room.
8. Paying a friend $100 to phone your brother and apologize to him on your behalf. Not fine, and I can't fully say why. Nobody's harmed, nobody's deceived, and yet. I've been going back and forth on this one alot.
9. A pharma company pays a healthy 22-year-old in a poor country $3,000 to swallow an untested compound in a first-in-human trial. Fine.
10. Paying a homeless man $50 to hold a sign at a rally saying he supports the nurses. Fine. Paying him the same $50 to look into a camera and say he supports the nurses. Not fine. I'd swear those are different, even though the sign and the sentence carry the same content.
11. A carbon offset purchase. Fine, obviously, and I know people think that's just inconsistent with 2 and 3.

Here's the principle I keep reaching for, which I've been calling the exit/standing thing in my notes: a market is OK when it lets someone buy their way out of a bad spot (the debt, the flood, the overnight queue, the empty funeral), and not OK when it lets someone buy their way into a standing they haven't earned (a vote, a grade, a solidarity, an apology). But then scalping should be fine, because the flipper is buying nothing but a seat, and it isn't fine. And #10 looks like it's tracking something else entirely — whether the money is buying a body's presence or a mouth's avowal — which isn't exit vs. standing at all. I can't seperate those two readings cleanly and it's driving me up the wall.

So: is there one principle that gets, say, ten of the eleven? If yes, state it as a test I could apply to a new case in under a minute, tell me which clause is doing the real work, and tell me which case forced that clause in. If no, give me the cheapest repair — I'd rather drop one verdict than carry a principle with five epicycles.

Then apply whatever you end up with to these four, which I haven't decided about: (a) a hospital sells naming rights to its cancer wing; (b) a man in a rent-controlled flat sells his place in the queue to someone behind him; (c) a parent pays their nine-year-old a dollar a page to read books; (d) hiring a stand-in to sit your bar exam.

Please write it up in this shape, with these headings:

- **The verdicts, tabulated** — a table, one row per case: my verdict, and the one-line reason you'd guess is actually doing the work in my head.
- **Candidate principles** — two or three, each stated as a precise test in a couple of sentences, each with the case that motivates it and the case it can't handle. About a paragraph each.
- **The hard pairs** — one paragraph on scalping vs. flood water, one on the sign vs. the sentence. Can anything keep both halves? What does it cost?
- **The principle I'd defend** — a paragraph, with the load-bearing clause named and the case that forces it.
- **Four new cases** — the ones above, a short paragraph each.
- **What I have to give up** — the verdict(s) no version can keep, and how much that should bother me.

And if the whole thing is one principle plus one exception, just say so plainly — that's a better answer than a unified theory I don't believe.
