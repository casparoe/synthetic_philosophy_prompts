Take Hobbes's late-life obsession with squaring the circle -- the years he spent trying to solve it, the increasingly bitter public feud with John Wallis over his attempted constructions, the Royal Society's collective exasperation with him, and his refusal to concede defeat before his death -- and analyze it through Hobbes's own account of geometric knowledge. On that account, geometry is the one science whose demonstrations we can be certain of, precisely because its objects are figures we ourselves construct: we know what we make, and the certainty of the demonstration flows from the construction rather than from any correspondence to an independent reality. This is the same account that underwrites his claim that civil philosophy can be demonstrated with geometric certainty, since the commonwealth is also an artifact we make. I want the case worked in both directions. First, the mapping: what does the constructivist account say Hobbes was doing when he built his quadratures, what counted as a demonstration on his own lights, and why did Wallis's objections not register as decisive -- was Hobbes simply stubborn, or did his epistemology give him principled reasons to treat a rival's construction as beside the point? Second, the testing: what does the episode show about the theory? If demonstration rests on construction, what explains why two competent constructors fail to converge, and does that failure undermine the maker's-knowledge program or merely reveal something about its limits? Then the payoff: if the analogy between geometry and politics is load-bearing for Leviathan's method, what does Hobbes's mathematical failure do to it -- does it weaken the analogy, or is the analogy independent of his competence in either field? Finally, I'd like an honest verdict on whether a different lens does better here -- proof as a social practice, or the epistemology of epistemic dependence on recognized experts -- and where each of those lenses is itself blind.
