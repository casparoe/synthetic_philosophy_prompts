I have been reading around the ethics of blame — specifically the question of when a person is even in a position to blame someone else — and there is a recurring case I cannot match to any of the labels I already know. The case is this. Someone criticizes a longstanding wrong: a citizen pressing their government to reckon with an old atrocity its institutions profited from, a shopper objecting to the labour conditions behind very cheap clothing, a man speaking against a sexist custom he was raised inside. The reply is not that the criticism is false. The reply is that the critic has forfeited the standing to make it, because the critic has benefited from the wrong — grew up in the country that gained, buys the clothing, was formed by the custom. This is not the hypocrite charge, because the critic need never have done the wrong and may have opposed it from the start. Nor is it quite the charge of complicity, because the critic did not take part in the wrong itself; they merely received some of its fruits. It looks like a third thing: standing lost through benefiting rather than through doing.

What puzzles me is a twist that shows up whenever I push on the objection. The objector usually adds that the critic could criticize if only they gave up the benefit — but the benefit is typically the kind of thing one cannot hand back. One cannot un-inherit a childhood, un-live in an economy one was born into, or return a security that came from an ancestor's gains. So the condition for recovering standing looks unsatisfiable, which makes me suspect the real work of the objection is something other than shielding the practice from unfair blame.

My question is whether this has a name. I know hypocrisy, and I know complicity, and I know that the you-too reply and whataboutism are nearby, but none of these seems to fit, because none of them turns on the mere receipt of a benefit. If there is an established concept for standing undermined by benefiting rather than by conduct, please give it to me and tell me exactly where it draws its boundaries: in particular, whether receiving a benefit one did not seek is supposed to remove standing at all, and whether the answer changes if the benefit could plausibly be refused or renounced. If there is no established term, I would like you to coin one, define it, and specify the cases it should and should not cover, so that I can tell it apart from ordinary hypocrisy and from complicity. A bare label will not help me; what I am after is the carving.
