I keep meeting the phrase "strategy stealing" in three quite different rooms, and I can no longer tell whether the people in them are making the same claim. In a combinatorial games reading group it was used to settle a game (Hex, as it happens) without anyone producing a strategy: the reasoning was that if the second player had a winning strategy, the first player could make an arbitrary opening move and thereafter play that same strategy, treating the extra piece as no burden — so the second player cannot win, and since the game admits no draw, the first player wins. In a footnote I read elsewhere, the same idea was called the strategy-stealing assumption, which made it sound like a premise about a game rather than a proof about one. And in a reading group belonging to a different department, someone invoked strategy stealing to argue that no group-level advantage can be stable, on the grounds that whatever strategy produces the advantage can be copied by individuals within the group.

What I want is a map rather than a verdict. Lay out the distinct precise claims that this phrase and its variants could be expressing, ordered from the nearly trivial to the ambitious, and for each say what kind of thing it is — a definitional triviality, a theorem about a restricted class of games, a substantive assumption about the game or about the world, a heuristic that usually works, or an overreach that is simply false. For the core game-theoretic reading, be exact about the premises the conclusion requires: symmetry of the available moves, the condition that an extra move is never a disadvantage, determinacy, the exclusion of draws, and anything further. Then tell me what survives as each premise is dropped: games that admit draws; games in which being obliged to move can hurt you (I take it zugzwang is the classic case); games whose starting position is made asymmetric by compensation or handicap rules; games with more than two players; games with simultaneous moves; and games that can continue forever. I also want to know what the non-constructive character of the argument costs. It delivers an existence claim about a strategy it never exhibits, and I would like to know whether it depends on classical logic, and whether it is a coherent thing for me to write in a paper that the first player has a winning strategy that nobody knows.

Finally, tell me which reading each of the three speakers most plausibly intended, and in particular whether the third claim — that any group-level advantage can be stolen by individuals — is the same claim wearing the same name, a different claim that happens to be true, or a confusion, and if it is a confusion, exactly where the disanalogy lies. Close with the single reading I should adopt if what I actually need, in a symmetric finite game with no draws, is the licence to assert that the first player wins.
