I've been going back and forth about hate-speech law with my flatmate, who's doing a master's in EU law, and I keep reaching for the marketplace-of-ideas picture that basically everyone in US free-speech debates leans on. The story goes like this: speech is a market. Speakers are sellers, listeners are buyers, ideas compete, bad ideas get outcompeted by good ones, and the way you deal with a defective product in a market is not to ban it but to let a better one undercut it. Hence the standard line that the answer to bad speech is more speech, hence why the US lets neo-Nazis march while Germany and France can put people in prison for it, hence the whole First Amendment edifice. My flatmate says the analogy is doing all the work in my arguement and that if you actually take markets seriously it cuts the other way. Real markets are crawling with failures — monopolies, externalities, buyers who can't inspect the goods, consumers who can't walk away — and a market with those features is exactly the kind of market we regulate. Her version: the people targeted by hate speech are captive consumers, they can't exit the way a shopper can leave a shop, and a captive audience is the textbook case for intervention. So now I genuinely don't know whose side the analogy is on.

What I want from you is a proper audit, not a history lesson and not a reading list. Give me the correspondence table first — what plays the role of the seller, the buyer, the good being traded, the price signal, the regulator, entry and exit — then tell me where the mapping actually breaks. Is truth the kind of thing that behaves like a price? Is there any clearing mechanism that does the work the price system does, or is 'the market' here just a metaphor for 'a lot of talking'? Can the people most affected opt out, and does that matter? Does competition between ideas impose anything like the discipline a market imposes on a firm, or is the feedback loop just missing? Then sort the disanalogies: which ones actually bear on the conclusion I want to draw (that the American approach is better, or at least that more speech is the fix) and which are just decoration. And tell me straight whether that conclusion survives the audit, or whether the analogy was carrying it the whole time. If it was, say so plainly — don't soften it — and tell me what a better picture would be, even if the better picture turns out to favour my flatmate. I'd much rather be told my whole framing is confused than get a tidy answer that keeps it breathing.
