So I'm in 11th grade and I run the small philosophy club at my school, and next month we're doing a session on whether you can really have an opinion about art you havent actually seen. I want to open with a quiz, like one of those magazine "which one are you" quizzes, because last time we did free will everyone just picked compatibilism because it sounded chill and we got nowhere. So the quiz has to sort people by what they actually say about cases, not by which label they like.

The obvious split is between people who think your friend telling you the new film is great can give you knowledge that it's great, and people who think you can't know that until you've seen it. But that split is way too easy, everyone in my club will land on one side in about two seconds. So I want questions that cut finer. The things I think actually separate people:

- whether it matters what kind of thing the judgment is about. Being told a joke is funny feels different from being told a painting is well composed, and being told a curry is delicious feels different from being told a novel is original. I want the quiz to find out if people treat those cases differently or the same.
- whether it matters what you were actually told. Someone saying "it's good" seems different from someone describing the thing in so much detail you can almost see it yourself. Do those two count the same?
- whether the question is about what you can know, or about whether the judgment is really yours, or about whether you understand the thing as opposed to knowing facts about it. I have a feeling alot of the disagreement is people answering different ones of these three.
- whether you could in principle go check for yourself later. Maybe testimony only works as a placeholder until you can see the thing.

Concrete cases I want in there, because they always make people argue: someone blind since birth who has read and listened to everything about paintings and says Guernica is a masterpiece; a friend who watches the whole movie on their phone in ten second clips and says it's boring; a wine expert telling you the wine is great and you tasting it and hating it; a VR reconstruction of a cathedral that's supposedly pixel perfect; reading a plot summary of a novel versus reading the novel; someone who was in the room while the symphony played but was on their phone the whole time; and a computer that has read every review ever written of a book it can't read.

I also have my own made up position that I want on the map, because I think it's what I actually believe and I've never heard anyone else say it: that you can borrow someone's judgment the way you borrow a jacket. While you're standing next to them and they're telling you, you genuinely know the painting is good, but the second you're alone with it the knowledge evaporates and you're back to having no opinion. I want to know if that's a real spot on the map or if it just collapses into one of the normal positions.

So build me the quiz. I'm thinking 8 to 10 questions, each one a concrete case with two or three answer options, and no question should just ask whether you think you can know a thing is good without seeing it, they all have to be answerable by someone who has never heard of any of this. Then a scoring key that maps answer patterns onto positions. Underneath that, give me the full map: a tree where every branch is labelled with the question that splits it, and the leaves are the positions, including the ones nobody in my club will land on. One line per leaf on the most awkward thing it's committed to. And be honest about which positions the quiz can't tell apart, if two of them give the same answer to every single case, say so instead of pretending the quiz separates them. Same for any combination of answers that's a spot on the map but that no actual position occupies, tell me if that's because it's incoherent, or just unattractive, or because nobody's thought of it yet.

Oh, and one more thing, since I'm the one running this. Don't give me just the standard two sides version. I want to be able to tell the difference between someone who thinks testimony can't give you knowledge and someone who thinks it can give you knowledge but that the knowledge doesn't count as yours, and those two definately get lumped together all the time.
