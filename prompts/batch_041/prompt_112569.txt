I want a parody of a position I keep running into in the comment sections I read: that the interwar calculation debate was settled by technology — that Mises and Hayek were right about a planning board equipped with pencils, telegrams, and no computers, and wrong as a matter of principle, so a sufficiently good model of the economy dissolves the objection to central allocation. I'd like the piece to be a document rather than a rant, and I'd like it to be the sort of thing I could link in an argument and have quoted back at me.

So: a confidential Series B investment memo, roughly 800 words, written by the founder-CEO of a company that claims to have solved the problem, addressed to a partner at an invented fund. Deadpan throughout. No winking, no scare quotes, no sentence that admits it is a joke; the founder believes every word, and the reader supplies the verdict. It should read perfectly well as a startup memo to someone who has never heard of the debate, while the jokes only land for someone who has.

Make the company concrete enough to be damning. Its product ingests the customer's transaction data, supplier feeds, machine telemetry, and consumer preference signals — self-reports, dwell time, whatever biometrics the founder can get — and emits an hourly vector of shadow prices covering every intermediate good and capital asset, which the customer then uses to instruct its plants what to build. It is emphatically a B2B SaaS company, not a planning bureau, and the founder is at pains to say so. Use the standard memo furniture — Problem, Product, Traction, Unit Economics, Risks, Ask — and let the furniture do the work. The traction numbers should be genuine achievements that are also the objection: the engine beats the customer's own demand forecasts on stable, homogeneous SKUs; it cut stockouts in a pilot; it ships a local knowledge capture app where workers file tips the engine may or may not consult. The risks section should concede, in the founder's own confident voice, exactly what the critics said — that the training signal is other people's prices, that nobody can lose money on a wrong shadow price, that the move from consumer goods to capital goods is where the case gets hard, that the plant managers are being paid to want what the plan wants. At least one joke should require knowing that the incentive problem and the information problem are different problems, and at least one should require knowing that prices are a discovery procedure rather than an aggregation of facts already known.

Fair play matters to me more than the jokes do. The system should be genuinely excellent at the narrow thing such systems are actually good at, and its failure should be a single consistent overreach from there, so that someone who knows the literature can point to the exact sentence where it goes wrong. Don't have the founder argue with the critics; the objections should arrive as boasts. No citations, no reading lists, no real companies, no living people. End on the ask and the valuation, and let the size of the ask be the punchline.
