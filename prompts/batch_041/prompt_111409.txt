I run a 90-minute session for a reading group of about 12 (phil and econ grad students) on decision theory and the far future. They already know the simulation trilemma and the usual astronomical-value material, so don't spend words explaining either. I want an activity that makes them feel the pull of the claim that the simulation argument takes some of the wind out of far-future fanaticism -- the whole 'bet everything on a tiny chance of 10^30 value' move -- and then lets them find out that the obvious version of the dampening doesn't work. My hunch, which I'd rather the activity test on them than have me assert: a probability discount does nothing here (multiply 10^30 by 0.1, you're still fanatical), so if the simulation argument dampens anything it has to be by breaking something else -- our causal grip on the outcome, or the assumption that simulated welfare sits in the same currency as the real thing. And if sims do count, the argument should push the other way, since there'd be vastly more simulated beings than real ones.

Concretely: 100 credits to split between a sure near-term welfare project and a far-future project with an absurd payoff. Make them commit, then reveal the hidden world-card (whether we're simulated, and how that deck got built), let them reallocate, and see whether anything actually moves. Materials, the payoff table, and the reveal order are up to you; I'd rather the mechanics carry the point than a lecture afterwards.

Then a debrief that maps what happened onto the real moves -- probability discount vs. causal severance vs. whether sim-welfare counts -- and says which of those is doing the work. Tell me what will go wrong the first time, especially if they all just stay fanatical and it lands as a dud, and be straight about which part of the idea the game can't reproduce and I'll have to say out loud. Also: if the design doesn't land, which feature would you change first, and where is your reasoning about the participants most likely to be off?
