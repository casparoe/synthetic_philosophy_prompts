I've spent a lot of time in the sorts of forums where any public act of commitment gets diagnosed, sometimes fairly and sometimes not, as costly signalling, and in reading around the underlying theory — Zahavi's handicap principle, Grafen's formalisation of it, the cheap-talk models showing that honest signals needn't be costly at all — I keep running into a case I can't place. I'd like to know whether it has an established name, or whether I'm simply confused about where the cost is being booked.

Here is the shape of it. In every version I know, the cost that makes a signal credible is paid by the signaller: the peacock carries the tail, the graduate pays the tuition, the sect member forgoes the pork. The examples I keep noticing, though, have a three-party structure — a signaller, an audience whose beliefs are supposed to move, and a third party who bears the cost — and the signaller pays little or nothing. A charity stages an extravagant gala to persuade donors that it is well connected; the money comes out of the programme budget, so the beneficiaries pay. A department advertises its seriousness about some cause through a norm of unpaid weekend work, enforced against junior staff, while the people whose commitment is being advertised pay nothing. A faction demonstrates its willingness to fight through an operation whose victims are neither the faction nor the audience it wants to impress. In each case the audience does seem to update, and in each case the standard account looks as though it has nothing to say, because the party whose quality is being advertised is not the party paying.

Is there a concept for this, or is it the handicap principle with an accounting error? I would want to know how it stands to the usual taxonomy — index signals, handicaps, conventional signals — and, more to the point, how honesty could be maintained: if externalising the cost is free, a low-quality signaller can externalise just as readily, and the signal ought to be uninformative. My suspicion is that it can work only where the ability to impose the cost is itself correlated with the quality being signalled, but I can't tell whether that is a distinct phenomenon, a special case of something familiar, or nothing at all. Nor can I see where the edges lie: does a transfer that costs the payer and benefits the payee count as a cost, and what separates this from coercion, or from the loose talk of virtue signalling, where the cost may be purely notional? A name would be welcome, but only if it comes with a definition and some boundary cases — the carving is what I'm after, not the label.
