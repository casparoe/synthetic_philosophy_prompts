My brother and a friend of ours got into it at a dinner last week about whether actual infinities are possible, and I couldn't referee because I kept agreeing with whoever spoke last. It started from a kalam-style argument my brother had read somewhere and went on for an hour. Can you go through it move by move and say who's right where?

Call them Ravi (thinks an actual infinite is impossible) and Jo (thinks it's fine).

Ravi: Hilbert's hotel is a reductio. Every room full, and the manager moves the guest in room 1 to room 2, room 2 to room 3, and so on, then puts a new arrival in room 1. Same rooms, one more guest. Do that repeatedly and the hotel absorbs any number of extra guests. Empty the odd-numbered rooms and you've got infinitely many vacancies with nobody checking out. Anything that lets you add without getting bigger is incoherent.

Jo: None of that is a contradiction, it's just what infinite means. A collection is infinite exactly when it can be put in one-to-one correspondence with a proper part of itself. You're treating a rule about finite sizes — a whole is bigger than any of its proper parts — as if it were a law of logic. It's a definition of 'finite'.

Ravi: It's not just a definition, it's real guests and real rooms. The manager can't even say how many guests he has, and after the shuffle he's got more guests and the same number. That's nonsense.

Jo: He can say how many: countably many. And it's still countably many after the shuffle. Adding one to it leaves it unchanged. Weird, not contradictory.

Ravi: Then drop the hotel. Take successive addition. You can't finish counting through all the natural numbers, however long you go. If the past were an actual infinite it would have to have been completed by having events added to it one at a time, and that's precisely what can't happen. So the past is finite.

Jo: That assumes the past is being 'completed', which assumes events come into being one after another and pile up. If you take time the way most physicists do, as a four-dimensional extent, the past is just a stretch that's unbounded in one direction. Nothing is being finished, any more than the negative numbers had to be counted down to zero. So the argument only bites if you already accept the tensed picture.

Ravi: And you're assuming the tenseless picture, which is the controversial thing. If it only works against tensed time, fine — plenty of people think tensed time is true.

Jo: The argument also proves too much. A line segment is infinitely divisible, and you can't reach the end of infinitely many sub-intervals by dividing successively either, but nobody concludes that motion is impossible — they say Zeno made a mistake. So the form of the argument is suspect on its own.

Ravi: Last one. The finitist instinct, that a part can't be as big as the whole, is more certain to me than any of Cantor's theory. If a theory forces me to give that up, that's a reason to reject the theory, not the intuition.

Jo: Galileo noticed the squares thing centuries before Cantor — every number has a square, so there are as many squares as numbers, even though the squares are plainly only part of the numbers. He didn't conclude the world was contradictory; he concluded that bigger, smaller, and equal just don't behave for infinite collections the way they do for finite ones. You get a choice: keep the part-whole principle and say infinite collections have no sizes at all, or keep sizes and give up the principle. Cantor picked one. You're treating the other as obvious.

So: who's right on each of these? I especially want to know whether the hotel is actually a reductio of anything, whether the successive-addition argument just smuggles in tensed time, and whether 'my finitist intuition is more certain than your theory' is a real move or a dodge. And is there something both of them are missing?
