Working through Locke's *Some Thoughts Concerning Education* for a thing I'm writting and I keep getting stuck on the same knot. Here's the bit, from the middle sections on authority and habit:

"Fear and awe ought to give you the first power over their minds, and love and friendship in riper years to hold it: for the time must come, when they will be past the rod and correction."

And a couple sections on:

"A compliance and suppleness of their wills, being by a steady hand introduc'd by parents, before children have memories to retain the beginnings of it, will seem natural to them, and work afterwards in them as if it were so, preventing all occasions of struggling or repining."

What I can't sqaure is that in the same stretch he insists a good man "must be made so within," and that virtue has to be "habits woven into the very principles of his nature, and not a counterfeit carriage, and dissembled outside, put on by fear." So the will is being bent before the child has memory or reason to consent to it, and then he turns around and says real virtue can't be a fear-driven veneer. Is the early suppling supposed to be continuous with the later rational endorsement — the child grows into reasons that retroactively justify the habit — or is there a genuine gap, where he's just betting that a well-bent will will be a docile host for reason once reason shows up? And does it matter that the bending happens pre-memory, so the person can never quite locate the origin of his own disposition?

Second thing, tied to it: when he swaps beating for esteem and disgrace, is he grounding virtue in the child's appetite for credit rather than in reason? If so, what stops his virtuous man from being a creature of other people's opinions — and where in his account does the capacity come from to hold to virtue when the credit goes away? Don't hand me a reading list; argue it out with the text. And if the honest answer is that this is just Locke being a seventeenth-century man about children, say that instead of rescuing him.
