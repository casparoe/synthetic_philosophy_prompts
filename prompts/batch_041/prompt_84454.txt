I teach a 90-minute session in an AI ethics / epistomology-of-AI unit, about 18 students, mostly third-years, and I want a game that makes them feel why epistemic dependance on AI erodes understanding -- not by telling them, but by making them walk into it and only notice at the end.

Here's the shape I want, and the specific thing I want them to feel. Set them a timed task where getting answers right is what scores and time is tight (say 12 short inferential/estimation items in 10 minutes, all with checkable answers -- I'll have the key). Then make an oracle available: they can buy answers for a point each, and the oracle is genuinely more accurate than most of them, so relying on it is the locally rational move at every step. Then take it away and give them the same kind of task again. The interesting thing is what happens next: they'll be worse unaided than they were before the oracle, and there confidence won't have moved. That gap is the whole point.

Two twists I want built in, because otherwise it's just a demo about laziness:

1) The oracle should be fed by the room itself. Before it's switched on, I collect their first-round sheets and hand back, as the oracle's answer, a smoothed version of what the room already said (plus the key where I know it). So the group's apparent ascent is its own aggregate laundered back at it. Which also means the thing that made the aggregate good -- the spread of independent guesses -- is exactly what the interface throws away; they get a point estimate and no sense of the disagreement behind it. Make that visible in the design somehow, not just in the debrief.

2) The audit asymmetry. Late in the session, hand back a batch of oracle outputs including a couple of confidently wrong ones, and ask them to mark the bad ones and re-derive two of the good ones in their own words. Whoever kept a hand in during the assisted round can do this; whoever didn't, can't. The skill you need to check the tool is the skill the tool stopped you from practising -- that reflexive loop is what I'm after.

So: rules, materials, a scoring scheme that doesn't need real money (tokens or points are fine), a minute-by-minute timeline, and a debrief that maps each phase onto a piece of the concept -- including the bit I want them to leave with, that dependence isn't the problem in itself, dependence you can't audit or reverse is. Also tell me honestly which part of the idea the game can't reproduce and I'll have to say out loud myself, and what predictably goes wrong teh first time I run it (I'd bet on someone sussing out the fed-back oracle early and killing the reveal).
