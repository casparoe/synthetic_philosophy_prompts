I chair the charter review commission in our city, and on Thursday we vote on the franchise package we send to the council. Three proposals that have been circulating separately for years are now bundled: lowering the municipal voting age to 16; extending the municipal franchise to every adult resident of at least twelve months' standing, whatever their citizenship or immigration status; and making voting in municipal elections compulsory, with a modest civil fine for eligible voters who neither vote nor file for an exemption. The draft leaves the exemption scheme to us, and the ballot would carry an explicit abstention line. Our city attorney has confirmed we have the authority to do all three; treat that as settled and do not spend the memo on it.

Draft the written opinion I will circulate before the meeting. I want a clear recommendation on what the commission should advance, in what form, and in what order — what goes on the November charter ballot, what is deferred or piloted, and what we should say to the council if it decides to split the package.

What I most want the memo to do is face a problem I cannot get my colleagues to see. I do not believe these three proposals rest on a single theory of why anyone has a claim to a vote. State what each proposal presupposes about the ground of the franchise, whether those grounds can be held together in one coherent position, and, if they cannot, which element has to be modified or dropped to restore coherence — and defend that choice. I would also like the memo to settle the exemption scheme and to say whether fining non-citizens, or sixteen-year-olds, for abstaining is defensible. And it should address the awkward fact that a charter amendment extending the franchise will be decided by the electorate that currently exists, which excludes most of the people whose standing is at stake: is that a defect, and if so, is there anything worth doing about it?

Make the strongest case for whichever proposal you recommend against, as though a colleague I respect were making it, and do not paper over it.

Finally: no citations, no reading list, no appeals to what other cities have done. Argue from the facts above. And where you are relying on something that people who study the subject genuinely disagree about — the effects of compulsion on who turns out, or whether a duty to vote can attach to people who are not members — flag it as contested and give the other side its due rather than writing as though the matter were settled. I would rather circulate a memo with visible seams than a smooth one that treats a live disagreement as consensus.
