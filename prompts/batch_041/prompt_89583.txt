I am a retired experimental physicist who took up philosophy after retiring, and in three weeks I am presenting a short paper to an interdisciplinary reading group — mainly intellectual historians of empire and Frankfurt-School-trained critical theorists, plus one analytic philosopher of the Enlightenment and a Foucauldian historian of the human sciences. The room takes the entanglement of Enlightenment reason with colonial administration to be constitutive rather than contingent. My thesis is that the Enlightenment is best understood not as a body of doctrine but as a set of self-correcting epistemic practices (public criticism, fallibilism, the obligation to give reasons, the expectation of revision), and that the crimes committed in its name are failures to apply those practices rather than expressions of them. I expect this to be heard as an evasion of the no-true-Enlightenment kind. Prepare me for the question period: the half-dozen or so objections this particular audience will actually put to me, in their vocabulary rather than mine, ordered by how much damage they are likely to do, each with a reply I could deliver in two minutes and an honest note on where that reply is weakest. I am least sure how to answer the charge that the universal subject was constituted by its exclusions rather than merely accompanied by them, and the related charge that my narrative of self-correction quietly borrows the very stadial, progressive picture of history — the ranking of societies as so many stages of development — that supplied the vocabulary of civilisational hierarchy in the first place. Include the question I am dreading and hoping nobody asks. Do not give me citations, reading lists or suggestions for further reading; I cannot consult them and want the arguments themselves. Begin with the single most damaging objection in one sentence, then work through the rest, with no preamble and no restatement of this brief. Write in British English.
