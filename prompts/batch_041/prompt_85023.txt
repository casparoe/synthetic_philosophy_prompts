I'm in a group chat that has been having this arguement for weeks about when an AI assistant should refuse you, or quietly steer you away from what you actually asked for, and both of my friends keep defending their side with a professional analogy, and I've started to suspect neither analogy can carry the weight thier putting on it. Friend A's line is that an assistant is like a bartender: a good bartender cuts you off once you've had enough, and nobody calls that tyranny, so a model that won't help you draft the thing that's going to get you fired, or that stonewalls you at 3am when you're clearly spiraling, is just doing responsible service. Friend B says no, the right comparison is a pharmacist: a pharmacist won't hand over the pills without a prescription and that's fine, because the pharmacist is gatekeeping on a doctor's authority rather than on their own view of how you ought to live. Both of them then slide from the analogy straight to a conclusion -- that the assistant is entitled to refuse or override on its own judgment of the user's good -- and I want to know whether the slide is licensed.

So audit both, and I don't need the literature on this, just the audit. Give me the correspondence first, a table is fine: who plays the bartender and the pharmacist, what the drink is and what the prescription is, what's doing the work of the impaired or incapacitated judgment, where the authority to refuse comes from, who carries the liability, what recourse the customer has, how local and how reversible the refusal is, and whether the refusal is really about the customer's good or about the server's own exposure. Then the breaks: for each analogy, which disanalogies actually matter for the conclusion being drawn and which are just noise.
