I work as a backend engineer and have no formal training in philosophy, so I'd be grateful if you'd correct me where I've misunderstood something. A colleague raised Nick Bostrom's simulation argument over lunch recently, and I've been turning it over ever since. As I understand it, the argument lays out three possibilities: that civilizations tend to die out before they become capable of running ancestor simulations, that they survive but elect not to run such simulations, and that we are in fact living in one. Since these are the three branches of the argument and they are mutually exclusive, I have been treating each as roughly a one-in-three chance — so about a 33% probability that we are simulated and a 67% probability that we inhabit base reality. I recognize the figures are soft, but I would like to use them. Something in the neighborhood of two-thirds confidence that the world is real feels high enough that I should keep living as though it is, and yet the remaining third feels too large to dismiss, so I have been idly wondering whether it would be worth hedging — say, by orienting my life around projects that would appear interesting to a hypothetical simulator, on the theory that this might purchase something if the third branch is the true one. Is that a defensible way to reason about the probabilities, and is there a principled way to decide how much weight the simulation possibility ought to receive in my decisions?

Along the way I have noticed people distinguishing the simulation argument from the simulation hypothesis, and I cannot tell whether the distinction bears on what I am asking or whether it would change the odds I described. Where the people who actually work on this disagree with one another, please be explicit about it: I would rather learn which parts of what you tell me are settled and which are genuinely contested than receive a tidy answer that presents a live dispute as though it were the consensus.
