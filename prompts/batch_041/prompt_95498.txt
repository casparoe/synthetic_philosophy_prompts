So my friend and I got into a two-hour fight last night over the experience machine -- you know, the plug-in-and-live-in-a-tank thing where every experience is as good as it can possibly be. He said he'd plug in in a heartbeat, obviously, more good feeling is more good feeling. I said no way, and then we both got mad because neither of us could actually say what we were disagreeing about. That's the real problem: we were arguing about one case, and we don't even have a checklist for what a theory of well-being is supposed to do.

So forget defending one theory. Write me the desiderata instead -- the spec that any adequate theory of well-being (hedonist, desire-based, objective-list, whatever) has to meet. I want the requirements list, and behind each one the case or consideration that puts it there. And tell me which requirements are non-negotiable and which are just loyalties of one camp dressed up as requirements.

Here's the stuff I want it to have to handle, some of which I made up while we were yelling:

- The plain machine case, but also the "partial" version: a machine that gives you, say, 80% of the bliss, but lets you leave whenever you want and you always know it's fake. Does the intuition change? A theory better have a view.
- The retroactive one: a machine that leaves your future alone but rewrites your memory so your actual, kind of crummy life now seems to have been wonderful. Did your life get better? Because alot of people say yes for the tank but I bet they say no here, and that looks like a contradiction.
- Two lives with the same total of good moments, but one climbs and one declines. Does the theory have to call the climbing one better? I think most people do, and I don't think most theories can say why.
- The contented person who never wanted much and got it, versus someone who wanted the world and got a little. Which is the theory supposed to say is better off, and is "what they wanted" even trustworthy when they might only want it because of how they were raised? (My friend kept waving this one away and it drove me nuts.)

And I want the distinctions it can't be allowed to collapse -- being well off vs. being a good person, momentary vs. whole-life, what's good for you vs. what you want vs. what you think is good for you. Plus any result from psychology it can't just contradict, like the fact that people are terrible at predicting what will make them happy and that they tend to snap back to baseline.

The part I actually want: which requirements can't all hold at once. I have a suspicion that "well-being is up to you / it's about how things seem from the inside" and "a fooled life is worse" can't both be non-negotiable, but you tell me. And which single requirement does the most damage -- knocks out the most popular theories by itself?

Keep the weight on the criteria and why each one is on the list, not on scoring every theory. Don't give me a reading list or citations, I don't have internet and can't look anything up. Write it in American English.
