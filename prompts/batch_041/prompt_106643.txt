I am a high school senior working on an independent-study project in the philosophy of mathematics, and I have reached the point where I need someone to tell me what I actually believe. I have been keeping a list of proofs I have met, together with my honest reaction to each, and I want to know whether a single principle accounts for the reactions or whether some of them have to be given up. I know the difference between a proof being correct and a proof telling me where to find something; that distinction is exactly what I am trying to pin down.

Here are the cases, in the order I wrote them down.

1. The standard proof that for every n there is a prime larger than n. The argument hands me a recipe. Verdict: established, and I feel I have learned something.

2. A proof that there are at least 10^90 different 100×100 grids of zeros and ones with a certain property, obtained by counting all such grids and bounding how many can fail. Nobody has ever written one of these grids down. Verdict: established. The grids are finite objects, and an exhaustive search would eventually turn one up, so I do not feel cheated even though I will never see one.

3. A proof that every continuous function on the interval from 0 to 1 that is negative at 0 and positive at 1 has a zero in between, proved without locating the zero. Verdict: I accept the theorem, but the proof feels like sleight of hand, and I cannot use it to find anything.

4. The sentence "the decimal expansion of π either contains a run of a thousand consecutive 7s or it does not." Verdict: I believe the sentence, but I do not believe I have established either half of it, and if you asked me which half is true I would say I have no idea. This is the case I keep going back and forth on.

5. A proof that it is impossible for there to be no number with a certain property, concluding that such a number exists. Verdict: I accept the conclusion, but with the same discomfort as in case 4.

6. Two proofs of the same theorem about a finite game, namely that the first player has a winning strategy: the first proof builds the strategy move by move, the second shows that if the first player could lose, a contradiction follows. Verdict: I treat the first as having established the theorem and the second as having shown only that I cannot see how the first player loses, even though both conclude the same sentence. I notice this means my verdicts are about proofs rather than about the statements proved, and I am not sure that is coherent.

My tentative principle is this: a sentence is established for me only when the proof supplies a finite procedure that would settle it — for an existence claim, a procedure that would produce the object; for a disjunction, a procedure that would tell me which side holds — and a proof that merely shows the contrary to be impossible does not establish the sentence, because it tells me only about the failure of my imagination.

That principle handles cases 1, 4, 5, and 6, but it would make me give up case 2, which I am not willing to do, and case 3 is where I cannot tell whether I am being consistent. I suspect I should surrender case 3 rather than case 2, but I cannot say why the difference between them is anything more than the length of the search. A friend tells me there is a tradition in logic that agrees with me about some of this, but I do not want the label; I want the principle.

What I would like is one principle, or a short list of candidates, stated precisely, with each clause attached to the case that forces it. Please tell me which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate and which one I should abandon; say what it predicts for two cases I have not listed — a proof that a certain procedure must eventually halt but with no bound on how long it takes, and the claim that a statement about the year 3000 is already either true or false; and tell me honestly whether case 6 can be made coherent at all. I am not asking for a reading list. I want the principle.
