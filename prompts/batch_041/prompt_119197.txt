Im teaching a 300-level seminar on rationality and belief — 18 students, 90 minutes, no maths beyond a bit of probability — and I want them to feel the lottery paradox and the preface paradox from the inside before anyone says either name. I've got the bones of a thing and I want you to make it runnable.

The disguise is an insurance underwriting desk. Each student is an underwriter, and the licence they're issued says two things: initial any file you'd stake your own money on at 33:1, and sign the desk's standard humility line — somewhere in my book there's a file I got wrong — because that's what the regulator wants. The book is 60 files, one per policy, each reading "Policy #n will not pay out this quarter". Exactly one of the 60 will pay out, and that's a public fact of the setup, they all know it. Stakes: chocolate coins, one per wrong file.

Then the audit form arrives, and the line on it is "I stand by every file in my book". So they've already initialled the claim that some file is wrong, and now they're initialling its negation, and both lines were individually forced on them by rules rather than by me. Make refusing to sign either line cost them something real-ish, so they can't just decline and shrug. I don't want to be the person who points out the squeeze.

Second round, same desk, different paperwork: an analyst files a 60-finding report, each finding resting on an independent test that's been right 97 times in 100, and the desk has to sign off on the report. Same squeeze from the other direction, and this time nothing in the setup hands them the certain claim for free — I want to see if that makes it feel different.

Give me the whole thing: roles, materials, the exact wording on cards and forms, the timeline, where it lands, and a debrief that maps what happened onto acceptance vs credence, the threshold view, conjunctive closure, and the escape routes they'll actually reach for with what each one costs them — the real ones, not "people are just inconsistent". Be honest about the bit the game can't reproduce and that I'll have to supply myself, and about what goes wrong the first time — someone blurts the name, someone refuses to initial anything, someone says the licence is just badly written — and how I handle each.

Give it to me as exactly five numbered items and nothing else, no intro and no closing paragraph: setup and materials, the rules that do the forcing, the twist, the debrief, and what breaks. A sentence or two each is fine, dense beats padded.
