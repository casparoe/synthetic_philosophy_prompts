I'm a high school junior, and I'm applying to a summer philosophy workshop that asks for a two-page research proposal for a project I'd carry out over about six weeks (roughly 4,000 words of writing) with no lab, no archive, and no supervisor who knows decision theory — just me, a notebook, and whatever I can work out by hand. My half-formed idea comes out of an argument I had with two friends in my school's philosophy club, which started as a fight about whether it can ever be rational to settle a decision by flipping a coin and turned into a fight about a puzzle I'd read about called the absent-minded driver problem. The setup, as I understand it, is this: a driver is on a road with two intersections in a row. If he exits at the first, he gets 0; if he continues past the first and exits at the second, he gets 4; if he continues past both, he crashes and gets 1. The catch is that he is absent-minded, so when he is at an intersection he cannot tell whether it is the first or the second, and he cannot remember whether he has already passed one. What bothers me is that the usual answer seems to be that he should adopt a probability of continuing — I have seen the number two-thirds, which I think comes from maximizing 4p minus 3p squared, though I would want to re-derive it myself — and that this gives him a better expected payoff than any plan of the form always continue or always exit. My friends and I could not agree on whether that answer is genuinely an answer, because at the moment the driver is actually at an intersection, continuing does not look like the best thing to do with the information he has, unless he is at the first intersection, and he cannot know that. So the plan that looks best from the armchair seems to recommend acts that look wrong from the driver's seat, and I cannot tell whether that is a real paradox about rationality or an artifact of how the puzzle is set up.

What I would like help turning into a proposal is a sharper version of my hunch: that the interesting question is not what the driver should do, but what kind of thing the driver's plan is, and what makes one plan better than another when the agent cannot tell which part of the plan he is in. I suspect the puzzle is pulling apart three things that usually get bundled together — imperfect recall (he cannot remember), imperfect information (he cannot tell where he is), and the difference between choosing an act and choosing a policy — and that the standard discussion treats them as one problem when they come apart in variants. The variants I want to build and think through are: first, the driver gets a free memory aid, such as a mark he can leave at the first intersection and see later, and I want to know whether the puzzle disappears, and if so whether it was really about recall all along; second, three or four intersections instead of two, to see whether the recommendation's shape depends on the specific numbers or on something structural; third, a version where the crash payoff is much worse, or only mildly bad, to see whether the resolute plan survives when the stakes change; and fourth, a version with two drivers who share a single payoff and can each see the other's choice but not their own position, to see whether the puzzle is really about one agent's memory or about coordinating parts of a single agent over time. My working hypothesis, which I would want the proposal to flag as possibly wrong, is that the paradox is not really about imperfect recall, because it survives a memory aid, and that what it actually exposes is a gap between two ways of evaluating a plan: by its expected payoff from before the journey starts, and by whether each step of it would be endorsed by the agent at the moment he has to take it. If that is right, the payoff of the project is a clearer statement of when the two standards come apart and whether either one deserves to be called the standard; if it is wrong, I want the proposal to say which variant kills it and what the nearest surviving question would be.

So please write the proposal itself: state the question precisely enough that it has a determinate answer, explain why the obvious positions on the puzzle leave it open rather than settling it, lay out the method as a realistic sequence for six weeks (what I would do in weeks one and two, three and four, five and six), say what a result would look like and what would count as the project failing — for instance, if every variant collapses back into the standard answer, or if the resolute-plan idea turns out to be a relabeling of something already settled — and give an honest assessment of what could make this too big or too technical for a high school student in six weeks. I do not need a bibliography, a reading list, or citations; I will find and add those myself, and I would rather the proposal refer to positions and arguments in plain terms than lean on names and dates I might get wrong. If my half-formed idea is already a settled position in the literature, please say so directly and reframe the proposal around the nearest genuinely open question instead of pretending my version is new.
