For thirty years I worked as a field biologist, and I used the word function the way I used a lens or a net: constantly, and without ever stopping to ask what it meant. A trait had a function the way a bird had wings. Since retiring I have begun reading philosophy of biology, and I have discovered that the one concept my entire discipline leans on is precisely the one the philosophers cannot agree about. I have the etiological and the causal-role accounts in front of me, and before I go further I want the specification rather than another round of advocacy. Please write me the desiderata: what must any adequate theory of biological function deliver in order to count as a theory of that subject at all?

Be concrete about the requirements, and give the case that motivates each one. I have in mind at least the following, and I would like you to organize them, add what I have missed, and tell me where I have smuggled in a prejudice rather than a genuine demand:

The malfunctioning heart. A heart that cannot pump still has the function of pumping; that is what makes its failure a failure and not merely a different way of being. Whatever the theory says, it must underwrite this normativity, the fact that ascribing a function licenses judgments of malfunction. If a theory can only describe what a trait does or did, I want to be told that it cannot do the job, however elegant it otherwise is.

The vestigial trait. The human appendix, the pelvic bones of a whale. Does the theory assign these a function, a former function, or no function at all? I want the verdict forced.

The novel trait before selection has acted. A chance variant appears and happens to help its bearer survive one season. In ordinary biological speech we are often happy to say it has a function already, but the etiological story seems to require a history of selection that does not yet exist. Reconcile this or tell me that ordinary speech is wrong.

Exaptation. Feathers selected for warmth, later co-opted for flight. Can a trait acquire a function it was not selected for, and does the theory allow a trait to have more than one, or to change its function over time?

The byproduct. The classic spandrel critique: many traits are architectural consequences rather than adaptations. The theory must be able to return a verdict of no function, and must not colonize every effect with a purpose.

Drift and neutrality. A trait fixed by drift that nonetheless has a current effect. Function or not? And what becomes of functional ascription when selection has been weak or absent?

Parts, artifacts, and medicine. Physiology and clinical medicine speak of the function of an organ and of the parietal bone protecting the brain; a broken clock is still a clock that fails to keep time. Must the theory cover these alongside evolved traits, or may it quarantine them? If it must cover them, say what the cases share.

Distinctions the theory must not collapse. Among others: function versus mere effect; function versus adaptation; function versus current utility; function versus design or purpose; and having a function versus actually working. Which of these are load-bearing and which are terminological?

Constraints from biology it may not contradict. Natural selection is the only known source of design-like fit, but it is not omnipotent; drift, pleiotropy, developmental constraint, and exaptation are real; many traits are neutral; and function-talk is applied at every level from the organelle to the colony, and to cases such as selfish genetic elements and extended phenotypes. Tell me which of these results genuinely constrain a theory of function and which are merely background.

Finally, the part I most want: mark which requirements are non-negotiable and which are preferences of a particular camp; flag any subset you suspect cannot be satisfied together, in which case the list itself is the result and I want to see it stated plainly; and tell me which single requirement does the most to exclude candidate theories. I do not need a scorecard of the current contenders — I will do that myself once I trust the criteria — but I would like to know whether the requirements, as you finally state them, leave room for a third kind of account beyond the etiological and the causal-role, or whether they force a choice between them.
