I sit on the transportation committee of a small town, and in three weeks we vote on whether to keep our least-used bus route. The route costs the town about nineteen thousand dollars a year beyond what it collects in fares, which comes out of a general levy spread so thin that no household would notice it. On an average day it carries one regular passenger and about a dozen occasional ones. The occasional passengers own cars; they ride because it saves them a few minutes of walking and the price of parking, and if the route goes they will drive and think no more about it. The regular passenger works the early shift at the hospital, does not drive, and lives nearly two miles from the nearest stop on any other route; without the bus she cannot reach work at the hours she is scheduled, and she has told us, credibly, that she would lose the job — a little over twelve thousand dollars a year, which is most of what she lives on.

Here is what I cannot get straight. If I compare her with any single one of the other people who bear this cost, she loses vastly more, and it seems obvious that we should keep the route: it would be monstrous to take a woman's livelihood so that a few thousand households can each save a few dollars. But if I add those small losses together, the total plainly exceeds everything she stands to lose, and then it seems equally obvious that we should cut the route. The two ways of looking at the case give me opposite answers, and I cannot see which one I am entitled to use.

Our committee has a rough working rule, adopted during an earlier dispute, which I will state as plainly as I can: where the people on the losing side would each lose something of the same kind, the size of the largest single loss decides; where the losses are equal in size, the number of people decides. That rule has never had to handle a case like this one, in which one person's loss is larger than any single one of the others' but is of an entirely different kind, and in which the others' losses are not equal to one another either — for a few households the extra few dollars a year genuinely comes out of a tight budget, while for the rest it is invisible.

Two further things, which may or may not matter. First, we asked a sample of households on the levy whether they would rather keep the route and pay or cut it and save the money; most said keep it, though almost none of them rides the bus, and none of them has offered to pay the difference themselves. Second, I have noticed that my own judgment shifts with how I describe the case to myself: when I think of it as one woman against a crowd of strangers I am certain we must keep the route, and when I think of it as one household against several thousand I am much less certain.

What should we decide, and how should I argue for it at the meeting? I am less interested in a verdict than in knowing which comparisons I am entitled to make and which I am not: whether the sum of many small losses may ever be set against one large one, what difference it makes that the losses are of different kinds, whether the poll of levy-payers tells us anything about what is fair, and whether it matters that the route already exists, so that cancelling it takes something away from her rather than merely failing to give it. And if the answer is that the small losses may not be added together at all, I would like to know what that commits us to when the numbers run very high, because I do not think I could bring myself to say that any number of people must forgo a trivial convenience so that one person keeps her job, and I would rather learn now that I am accepting that than discover it at the meeting.
