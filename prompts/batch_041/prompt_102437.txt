A couple weeks ago I was at a science-and-ideas conference dinner and got wedged between a philosopher of mind and a novelist who spent the whole main course going at each other about the self as a center of narrative gravity -- the view that there's no little person in your head, just a story your brain tells about the body it's attached to, and the self is real the way an object's center of gravity is real: you can calculate with it, but if you cut the thing open you'll never find it.

The novelist went first: if the self is a story, then somebody's telling it, and that somebody can change it. So remaking yourself is just rewriting, and you're the author. The philosopher said no -- the brain tells the story, you're the product of the telling and not the teller. A character can't grab the pen. So "rewriting your narrative" is confused, because there's no you standing outside the story to do the rewriting.

She said fine, but then the story is doing all the causal work -- it's what makes you get up, quit the job, stay married -- and we call things real when they have that kind of grip on us, so it's real enough. He said that's a sleight of hand: money and borders are real because we all agree to them, but nobody agreed to your self-narrative, it just gets produced, and "real enough" is carrying the whole sentence.

She came back with the responsibility point: if there's no author, then when we blame somebody we're blaming a story, which is absurd, so there must be an author after all. He said that doesn't follow -- the whole system responds to reasons, so the whole system is the responsible thing, and fiction was never meant to mean not real in the first place.

Then she threw in that the analogy actually cuts his way in the wrong direction, because a center of gravity can sit outside the object entirely (think of a boomerang), so if the self is like that, the self isn't even located in the person -- which is her point, that it's something we place. He said that just shows the fiction is useful for certain calculations, not that it has a will of its own.

I couldn't referee it to save my life, and I'd definately like to write about it. Can you go through it round by round and say who's right at each step, what each of them is missing, and only then who's more right overall? I don't want a summary of the view, I want an adjudication of the actual moves.
