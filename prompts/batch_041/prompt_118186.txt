Okay so here's a thing about my city that's been eating at me and i can't let it go. If someone wants to build an apartment building on my block, it basically has to pass the neighbors first — the local council member won't back it if the block is against it, and that's pretty much that, the thing is dead. Nobody citywide votes on it, the people who'd actually end up living there obviously get no say, and a good chunk of the people doing the vetoing are renters who'll move out in two years anyway. But the same city will turn around and say you can't refuse to rent to someone because of their race, there's a whole fair housing office for it, and everyone nods along when someone says housing is a human right or that everybody's got a right to the city. So both of these are true at once and I can't square them.

What I actually want to know is what the block-veto thing presupposes about who has a claim to a place. That the people living there right now are like a little sovereign people with the right to decide who gets to join them — a friend of mine calls it "block sovereignty" and I genuinely can't tell if that's a fair description of the practice or just a cute phrase he made up. Or is it really a property right wearing a costume, or a right to protect the character of a community, or just the plain assumption that the people already there count for more than the people who'd come? Which of those does the practice strictly need, and which is it merely compatible with? Where is it underdetermined, so that a bunch of different theories would all spit out the same veto?

And then — is it actually consistent with the fair housing stuff, or is one of them doing the real work while the other's just decoration? If we swapped the underlying view for an actual right to the city that everyone holds, including people who haven't moved in yet, what would concretely have to change about how the veto works?
