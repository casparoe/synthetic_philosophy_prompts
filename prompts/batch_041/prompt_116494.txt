I'm in a reading group that has spent the last several weeks on evolutionary psychology, and we keep deadlocking the same way: someone proposes an adaptationist explanation for some feature of human psychology — a cheater-detection faculty, a disgust response tuned to pathogens, a taste for gossip — and someone else answers, "that's just a just-so story," and the conversation stops there. I don't think either move is doing real work, and I suspect the reason is that we never agreed on what an adaptationist hypothesis would actually have to deliver before we're entitled to take it seriously. Before we read anything else, I want the specification itself.

So write me the desiderata: what must a hypothesis in evolutionary psychology be able to do, or show, in order to count as a genuine explanation rather than a story told after the fact? Lay out the requirements — the phenomena it has to account for, the evidence it has to marshal, the distinctions it cannot collapse — with the consideration behind each. I'm thinking of things like these, though I don't want to restrict you to my list: whether it must demonstrate a history of selection, and how anyone could do that when the ancestral environment is unobservable; whether cross-species comparison is mandatory or merely nice to have; whether the trait has to be shown to be a specific adaptation rather than an exaptation or a byproduct of something else; what role, if any, evidence of functional design or optimality has to play; how the hypothesis must handle the obvious rival explanations — learning, culture, and general-purpose cognition; what makes it genuinely testable rather than merely compatible with the data; and whether it must specify the trait at the computational level or may stay at the level of behavior, and at what grain the trait is to be individuated in the first place.

For each requirement, say whether it is genuinely non-negotiable or merely a preference of one camp — the sociobiologists, the evolutionary psychologists proper, the human behavioral ecologists, the cultural-evolution people — and flag any subset you think cannot be jointly satisfied, because that is the part I most want: I suspect at least one pair is in tension, and that the deadlock in my group comes from each side demanding a requirement the other side cannot possibly meet. Then tell me which single requirement does the most work — which one, if enforced, would eliminate the largest number of hypotheses people actually advance. Skip the scorecard of particular theories; I'll do that with the group. And don't send me a reading list — I want the criteria, argued, not a bibliography.
