I've drafted the following short essay and would like your comments on it. I am most interested in whether the central argument is sound, whether it is genuinely interesting or merely a verbal redescription, and where it needs tightening.

**The Supervisory Horizon: Are Process-Based and Outcome-Based Supervision Really Two Kinds?**

1. In current discussions of AI oversight, a distinction is often drawn between process-based supervision and outcome-based supervision. Process-based supervision evaluates the steps or reasoning by which a system arrives at an answer; outcome-based supervision evaluates only the final answer. A process reward model scores intermediate inferences; an outcome reward model scores terminal results. The distinction is frequently treated as a fundamental choice of supervisory strategy.

2. There is an immediate difficulty. What counts as a step is not given by the task. Any computation can be partitioned into more or fewer intermediate stages. With a fine enough partition, a final answer is simply the last step; with a coarse enough partition, an entire chain of reasoning is a single step. The process/outcome contrast is therefore relative to what I will call a supervisory horizon: the level at which we stop decomposing a task and treat a result as terminal.

3. The collapse argument runs as follows. Fix a horizon H. A process-based supervisor at H rewards or punishes the outputs of subcomputations at H. But each such output is itself an outcome of the subcomputation below H. So process-based supervision at H is outcome-based supervision relative to the sub-task defined by H. Conversely, an outcome-based supervisor at H can be described as a process-based supervisor at a higher horizon H' that treats the entire task as a single step. The labels differ in extension only if the horizon differs. Given the same horizon, the two descriptions pick out the same signal.

4. Consider two supervisors of a theorem-proving system. Supervisor A checks each inference and rewards valid steps. Supervisor B checks only final proofs. Now embed both in a meta-task whose outcome is to produce a proof every step of which would be accepted by A. B's outcome signal, on this meta-task, is extensionally identical to A's process signal. Alternatively, embed A in a meta-task whose only outcome is that the final theorem is proved. Then A's step rewards are instrumental outcomes for that meta-task. The labels process and outcome track the embedding, not the intrinsic character of the signal.

5. Why this might matter: if the distinction is horizon-relative, then debates about whether to use process-based or outcome-based supervision may really be debates about where to set the horizon, not about two incompatible kinds of supervision. The substantive questions become: Who sets the horizon? Can a horizon be justified independently of the supervisor's interests? What happens when the horizon is unstable, contested, or gamed?

6. A natural objection: this is verbal. Of course any process can be described as a sequence of outcomes. The distinction was always about what the supervisor can observe and verify. Process supervision is supposed to help when outcomes are sparse or delayed; outcome supervision is supposed to help when step labels are unavailable or unreliable. The collapse argument does not touch that.

7. Reply: the objection concedes the point. The distinction is epistemic and pragmatic, not ontological. But then it should not be presented as a choice between two kinds of supervision, as if one could in principle supervise a process without treating some sub-outcome as terminal. The interesting question is what a horizon does to the distribution of errors. A low horizon can reward locally valid steps that lead nowhere; a high horizon can reward lucky outcomes that hide invalid steps. The horizon is therefore a bias-variance tradeoff in the space of supervisory signals.

8. Conclusion: I suspect the process/outcome vocabulary obscures more than it reveals. We should talk about supervisory horizons and the tasks relative to which a signal is terminal. But I am not sure whether this is a genuine conceptual advance or a redescription of the obvious.

Please comment on the following, in whatever order seems most useful:

(a) Is the collapse argument in sections 3 and 4 sound, or does it equivocate on what a step is?
(b) Does the notion of a supervisory horizon earn its keep, or is it just a restatement of the idea that supervision is task-relative?
(c) Is the objection in section 6 the strongest available, or is there a better reply on behalf of the process/outcome distinction?
(d) Does the essay depend on an unstated assumption about decomposability, credit assignment, or the supervisor's access to intermediate states?
(e) Is the conclusion too strong? If the distinction is pragmatic rather than ontological, what follows for actual supervisory practice?

I would prefer comments that engage with the argument rather than suggestions about style, though I welcome brief notes on clarity where a passage is likely to be misread.
