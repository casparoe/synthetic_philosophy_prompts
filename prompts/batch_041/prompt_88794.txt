I am a novelist, and I have reached the chapter in which my protagonist—a statistician who has begun to distrust the very idea of a population—must read a poem aloud at a memorial. I need you to write that poem. It should be about Simpson's paradox, but I do not want the poem to say the name or explain itself. The paradox should be the poem's load-bearing structure: the same evidence, differently grouped, must support opposite conclusions, and the reversal must come from the grouping rather than from any new fact.

Write it as a double sonnet: two fourteen-line columns sharing a rhyme scheme, with a volta in each column and a third volta when the columns are read across as a single poem. Read down the left column, the poem should find for one conclusion; read down the right, against it; read across, line by line, the pooled poem should reverse the verdict of both columns. No line may be altered between readings. The reversal should be carried by the form—by a pronoun that changes its antecedent depending on whether you read down or across, by rhymes that pair one subgroup with another only in the pooled reading, by line lengths that weight one column more heavily when the columns are read together. I want the reader to feel the ecological fallacy and the atomistic fallacy as temptations, not as mistakes to be named. Keep the diction concrete—counties, clinics, siblings, rain—so that the abstraction arrives through the image. A reader who knows the paradox should recognize it in the architecture; a reader who does not should still feel the ground shift under the final line.

After the poem, on a line by itself, write Summary: followed by one sentence stating which verdict the poem finally leaves standing, or whether it leaves both standing, and what the poem's form does to keep them both alive. Nothing after that line.
