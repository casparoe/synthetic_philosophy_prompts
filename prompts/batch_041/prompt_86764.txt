Some months ago I did something I would have mocked a year earlier: I signed up for one of those services that holds a deposit in escrow and forfeits it if I fail to keep a commitment. I have kept every commitment so far, which means that so far I have paid a few hundred dollars for nothing at all, and I have spent the same few months arguing with friends about whether that was stupid. The argument has pushed me into a question I now care about more than the app: whether there is a principle behind my verdicts about sequences of choices, and if there is, which of the verdicts I have to give up. Here is what I think, case by case. (1) A friend holds, at a single moment, cinema over ballet, ballet over opera, and opera over cinema. Offered the cinema, he pays ten dollars to trade for the ballet; offered the ballet, he pays ten to trade for the opera; offered the opera, he pays ten to trade back for the cinema. He ends with exactly what he began with, thirty dollars poorer. I say he has made a mistake, not merely had bad luck, and I say this even if no one ever actually runs the sequence on him, and even if the trades are never offered at all. (2) A different friend's tastes simply change: on Monday she prefers the opera, by Wednesday she prefers the cinema; she pays no one anything. No mistake, and nothing irrational in the change itself. (3) A third friend, foreseeing that she will want to spend her savings on Friday, pays a bank fifty dollars on Monday to lock the money up until Saturday. Not merely rational, but admirable. (4) Here I get stuck. A trader's preferences shift back and forth, week by week, for reasons I would call good ones, new information and honest reappraisal of her own risk tolerance. Each week she pays a broker five dollars to bring her holdings into line with the current preference; over a year she moves back and forth many times and ends very nearly where she began, minus the fees. I cannot call her irrational the way I call the first friend irrational, but I cannot say what the difference is: both end where they started, minus money, and in neither case does any single step strike the agent, at the time, as a mistake. (5) For contrast: a fourth friend takes a long series of bets, each of which she is right to think favours her, and through sheer bad luck ends up ruined. I call her rational and unlucky, and I would resist any suggestion that she was irrational, though I notice how awkwardly this sits beside my confidence about (1). (6) A colleague insists that what is wrong in (1) is not the loss but the mere possibility of the sequence: the three preferences are defective in themselves, he says, whether or not anyone ever trades with the man. I am inclined to agree, and I cannot say what the defect is, since nothing bad ever happens to him.

My own case is the one that made me write. (7) The escrow service costs me twenty dollars a month; suppose I would have kept the commitments anyway, so that the whole year turns out to be a net fee paid for a state of affairs identical to the one I started in, and I still say I was rational, because at the time I could not have known that and the fee bought a hedge against a real chance of failure. The best principle I have managed to formulate is this: an agent is criticisable when there is a sequence of steps, each acceptable to her at the moment she takes it, that returns her to exactly the state she began in and leaves her poorer, a round trip; and she is not criticisable when the sequence merely carries her somewhere new, however bad the destination, a one-way slide. That principle acquits (2), (3) and (5) and convicts (1), but it convicts (4) as well, since her year is a round trip, and it convicts (7) unless the hedge is somehow allowed to count; and it says nothing at all about (6), where no sequence is ever run.

What I would like from you is the principle, or the two or three competing candidates, stated precisely enough to apply: which clause is motivated by which case, which of my seven verdicts each candidate cannot accommodate and therefore forces me to abandon, and what the surviving candidate predicts about a case I have deliberately not listed, namely an agent whose preferences are stable and transitive and who nevertheless pays a dollar, every time she considers them, for a pill that makes her prefer whatever she already prefers more strongly; she pays, gains nothing she can name, and reports afterwards that she is glad she did it.
