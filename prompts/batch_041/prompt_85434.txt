I'm building a set of discussion materials for a social ontology unit, and I need you to write me a dialogue. Take on the role of a philosopher of the social sciences who genuinely also knows demography and statistics — not just the philosophy side. Before you write a single line, think it through and brainstorm the strongest possible version of each position, because the fastest way to ruin this is to make one of them a strawman.

Here's the setup. Two people, both sharp, killing time at an airport bar after a conference, arguing about whether "generations" — Boomers, Millennials, Gen Z, whatever — are real kinds. One of them is a cultural sociologist who thinks generations are real social kinds. Not natural kinds like gold, obviously, but real in the sense that sharing a birth window hands you a shared formative environment (same media, same wars, same recessions, same technology landing at the same age), that people born in the same window interact with each other more than with other cohorts, and so cohort membership has actual causal power over attitudes and life outcomes. The other is a quantitative demographer who thinks it's mostly a folk taxonomy with a marketing budget: cohort is just period minus age, so age, period and cohort are linearly dependent, which means you literally can't identify a separate cohort effect from the data without smuggling in extra assumptions — and the specific cutoffs people use (roughly 1981–96 for Millennials, 1997–2012 for Gen Z, depending who you ask) are arbitrary anyway.

I want them to actually fight about these, don't skip them:

- The identification problem. Let the demographer press it hard, and let the sociologist give a real reply — something like: failing to identify an effect in a linear model isn't the same as the effect not existing, you can bring in theory or external constraints or natural experiments, and some age effects are known to be smooth, so the leftover variance has to go somewhere. Don't let either one win by fiat.

- The difference between a plain birth cohort (everyone born in a window, full stop) and a generation in a thicker sense — a cohort that shares a defining experience and actually knows itself as a unit, with a name it uses about itself. One of them should push on whether that thicker thing ever really exists or whether it's retrofitted onto people after the fact by journalists and ad agencies.

- Whether fuzzy boundaries disqualify generations from being kinds at all. Species have fuzzy boundaries. Mountains do too. Let the other one respond — maybe the fuzziness is fine but these particular labels are gerrymandered around what sells.

- The looping effect: once a generation label is out in the world, people self-identify with it, and that self-identification does real causal work. Does that make the kind real, or does it just make the label self-fulfilling and tell us nothing about a pre-existing kind?

Both characters equally competent, and they should be responding to each other's actual points rather than trading monologues. Aim for maybe 1200–1800 words. American English, and keep it conversational — they're at a bar, not writing a paper.

After the dialogue, give me a short structured summary as a JSON object with exactly these fields:

- "shared_ground": array of strings, the claims both characters accept
- "crux": string, the single point the disagreement actually turns on
- "realist_best_move": string, the strongest argument the sociologist makes
- "skeptic_best_move": string, the strongest argument the demographer makes
- "concessions": object with keys "sociologist" and "demographer", each an array of strings listing what that character grants the other
- "unresolved": array of strings, questions the dialogue leaves open
- "verdict": string, one or two sentences on who has the better of it and why
- "verdict_confidence": number from 0 to 1

No need for citations or a reading list, I just want the two of them going at it and the breakdown after.
