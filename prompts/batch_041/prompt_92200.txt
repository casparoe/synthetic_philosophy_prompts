I teach a 200-level ethics class, 18 students, one 75-minute block, and I want to do something on moral luck that doesn't just rehash the trolley stuff they've all heard. Here's the shape I'm after, though change the mechanics if you see something better: I want them making moral judgments first, then discovering that their judgments were tracking luck, then trying to correct for that and finding out the correction has a price.

Setting: they're all shift supervisors at a (made up) municipal water plant. Each supervisor privately picks a maintenance regime -- cheap, standard, or gold-plated -- and each option costs them points from a personal budget of 10. Choices get written down and sealed before anything else happens. Then we roll a die per supervisor: cheap fails on 1-4, standard on 1-2, gold on 1 only. A failure is a contamination incident and a named townsperson card gets hurt. Only outcomes go on the public board, never the regimes. Round one: everyone writes a verdict on every supervisor -- blame out of 5, restitution out of 5 -- using only the board. Round two: unseal the choices and let them re-judge. Round three is the part I actually care about: I hand back the incident reports with the regime names blacked out and ask them to rank the supervisors by how reckless they were, from the reports alone. They can't do it without reading the harm back into the choice, and that's the moment I'm after. I also want them to stumble onto the fact that the restitution number is the one that can't be scrubbed of luck even after the blame number can.

So I need the full thing: rules, materials, timeline, the debrief. The debrief should map the rounds onto the different kinds of luck -- outcome luck in round one, and the luck of which post you got as circumstantial luck (I'll secretly hand some people the risky post). Be honest that constitutive luck is the one this can't stage at all, since nobody in the room chose their own risk tolerance and we can't show them that from the outside; that part I'll have to just say. Nagel's four-way split and Williams's blameless lorry driver, the one who feels something worse than regret -- those should be nameable at the end without me lecturing for twenty minutes, so tell me how to get there in a few sentences. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time. I already expect someone to blurt their choice out loud and blow up round one, but what else? And what do I do about the students almost definately feeling manipulated by round three and over-correcting into outcomes never matter, which I don't want to leave as the takeaway.
