I'm a third-year PhD student in philosophy of biology and next month I have to give a talk at a two-day workshop on explanation in the behavioural sciences that my department co-hosts with the bio side, and I'm nervous in a very specific way. My thesis is that the distinction everyone reaches for in this area — the one from Dennett, cranes versus skyhooks, where a crane is a mechanism that does its lifting out of ordinary lower-level parts and a skyhook is a free lunch, a lift with nothing underneath it — is not the distinction that's actually doing the work, and that we should cut the space differently. What I want to argue is that "greedy reductionism" is best cashed out neither as a sin of going too low nor as a sin of being too ambitious, but as question-substitution: a reductive explanation is greedy when it silently swaps the explanandum for a neighbouring question it happens to be able to answer (typically a question about mechanism or about variance, offered in place of a question about cause, function, or history). On this view the crane/skyhook axis and the greedy/ungreedy axis are orthogonal, which is why Dennett can praise sociobiological cranes in one place and denounce greedy reductionism in another without contradicting himself, and it also lets me name the case I care about most, which I've been calling the cuckoo crane: a perfectly respectable lower-level mechanism — a heritability estimate, an inclusive-fitness calculation, a neural correlate — that gets laid in a nest where it can't do the work, and is then defended by pointing at its credentials as a mechanism. My flatmate, who's doing a stats MSc, told me last week that this is just me not liking that his field answers questions, and honestly it stung more than I let on.

The room is what worries me. It's mostly empirical people: two behavioural geneticists who run twin and polygenic-score studies on educational attainment and personality and who think "levels of explanation" talk is mush; a molecular person who does gene expression in social insects and is a hard-nosed adaptationist; an evo-devo postdoc who is deep in the genes-as-followers / causal-parity literature and thinks gene-centric causal talk is a category error; a systems biologist who will say there are no levels, only scales, and that I'm smuggling in a hierarchy; and an emeritus field ecologist who has spent forty years being told his behavioural observations are anecdotes. Then three philosophers of biology: one is a card-carrying Dennett fan who will tell me I've misread the metaphor and that a skyhook is just a crane that hasn't been naturalised yet; one is a causal-parity person who will want to know why I need "levels" at all if I've got parity; and one works on explanation proper and will press me on how I individuate questions and explananda. Plus a historian of science who wrote a book about the sociobiology wars and is allergic to anything that smells like reviving them in a new vocabulary.

What I want from you is my Q&A prep sheet. Give me the eight or nine objections I will actually get from this room, ordered by how much damage they'd do, each phrased the way these particular people would phrase it — the geneticists are not going to say "explanandum", they're going to say that's not what h-squared means, and the systems person is going to say something about scales not levels — with a two-minute reply for each and a candid note on where my reply is soft or hand-wavy. Tell me what to concede up front so I don't spend the first twenty minutes losing the room, which objections I should treat as free hits and which as genuinely dangerous, and finish with the question I'm dreading and hoping nobody asks. If you think the whole thesis is a non-starter, don't hedge: say which single objection kills it and what I'd have to give up to survive it.
