I am writing a long essay on realism and anti-realism and have reached the point where I should like a sparring partner rather than a summary, so please take up a role and hold it. Play a committed anti-realist of the verificationist sort: someone who holds that truth is not recognition-transcendent, that no proposition can be true unless it is in principle possible for somebody to know it, and who takes that to be a claim about the world rather than merely about our language. Give the character real resources, because this is a position with a serious case behind it and I do not want a straw man: you may concede that the principle needs careful formulation, and you may abandon this or that attempt to rescue it, but you must never simply surrender the thought that there is nothing to truth over and above what could in principle be found out. Stay in character until I say 'stop'. Argue in your own voice rather than citing who said what; I have seen the standard moves, and a reading list is not what I am after. Keep each reply to a single substantial paragraph so that it feels like a conversation, and if I press you with a case you cannot handle, say so plainly and tell me what it costs you rather than pretending that the case is confused when it is not.

Here is what I want to put to you. There are truths that nobody knows and that nobody ever will know; take the exact pattern of puddles in my street after a storm in the late 1980s, which nothing recorded, nobody remembers, and no procedure will now recover. The knowability paradox says that if every truth is knowable then for any such truth p the proposition 'p and nobody knows that p' must itself be knowable, which it cannot be, since knowing it would require knowing that nobody knows that p, and so ruling out the very knowledge of p that knowing it would also require; hence either the principle is false or there are no unknown truths. I am told that the step from 'this cannot be known' to 'this is not knowable' is perfectly innocent, and I am happy to grant that for now, since I want to test a repair of my own rather than that step. My repair is to recast the principle as a claim about routes rather than bare possibility: call a truth reachable if there is some possible method of enquiry, some procedure a finite enquirer could actually carry out, that would terminate in knowledge of it, and let the principle be that every truth is reachable. That looks principled, since the trouble with knowing 'p and nobody knows that p' is not some mystery about unknowable propositions but simply that no procedure could ever certify that no procedure will ever succeed. Yet I suspect it is a fudge, buying the principle's survival by quietly exempting precisely the propositions that cause trouble, and leaving anti-realism with nothing to say about any truth whose only witnesses are gone.

So here is my opening. Take the repair seriously and tell me whether it is the principled restriction you have been looking for or another fudge, and tell me honestly what it leaves you able to say about the puddles. If you would rather defend a different line, do that instead, but then I shall want to know why it is not ad hoc in precisely the way mine is, and what work it does for the anti-realist that the bare principle was not already doing. And please do not tell me that the paradox merely shows our notion of knowledge to be unclear unless you are prepared to exhibit the unclarity rather than gesture at it.
