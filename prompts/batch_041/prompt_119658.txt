I'm leading a session at our local philosophy reading group next month and we're pairing Shakespeare's Othello with the current arguments about manipulation versus legitimate persuasion, and I've got myself thoroughly stuck — it's definately ruining my evenings. The thing that bothers me is that most of the accounts define manipulation negatively, as influence that isn't coercion and isn't straight persuasion, and then say the distinctive mark is that it bypasses the target's rational capacities: it routes around someone's deliberation instead of engaging it. But Iago doesn't route around anything, he supplies reasons. He opens with 'I like not that' and then refuses to say what he means; he answers Othello's question about Cassio with a denial that does the work of an accusation ('Cassio, my lord! No, sure, I cannot think it, / That he would steal away so guilty-like, / Seeing you coming'); he lets Othello demand proof and then hands him the handkerchief, which is a genuine fact arranged into a false story; and he spends the whole middle of the play posing as the calm voice of reason warning against the very jealousy he's nursing. When Othello says 'Men should be what they seem' it lands like a punch precisely because Iago has got him reasoning his own way to a conclusion Iago planted — and the play has already handed us the counter-motto in Iago's own boast that he is 'not what I am'. So: does the play dramatise a sort of manipulation the bypass account can't capture, because Iago works through Othello's evidential standards rather than around them? And if so, is subversion a different thing from bypassing, and does the difference do any moral work? Or am I just redescribing ordinary persuasion in a sinister tone?

I'm also less sure than I'd like about where the play draws the line between manipulation and legitimate influence, because nobody in it is clean. Desdemona lobbies nonstop for Cassio, promises him his place back before she's asked her husband, and pretty obviously means to wear Othello down — is that persuasion, or is nagging someone who loves you a soft form of manipulation? Emilia steals the handkerchief for her husband's favour and never asks what it's for, which looks like manipulation working through loyalty rather than through deception. And Brabantio, who can't believe his daughter chose Othello of her own accord and insists he must have used spells or potions on her, makes me wonder whether accusing someone of manipulation isn't itself a way of stripping them of agency, and whether the play is conscious of that. I'd also like something on the truth-telling point: Iago tells plenty of lies, but the handkerchief is real and Cassio's possession of it is real, so I'm inclined to think manipulation can't just be a species of deception.

Where I'd like the reading to go: which scenes the theory actually illuminates (the handkerchief, the drinking that ruins Cassio, the invented story about Cassio talking in his sleep, the scene where Othello insists on seeing it proved); where the play resists the theory and I have to admit the categories are too tidy — I'm sceptical that any of these accounts survives the play intact, and I suspect Iago's motives being so plainly incoherent is a problem for anything that treats manipulation as a tidy intentional act aimed at a target; and what the play adds to the debate that the arguements on their own don't. My hunch is that it's about the relationship rather than the target's individual rationality — that Iago's power comes from having been installed as the trusted confidant, so the wrong looks more like a betrayal of trust than a failure to respect autonomy — but I'd rather you told me than I told you. I don't need a reading list or a who-said-what survey; I know roughly who said what, I want the reading itself. And please don't just tell me Iago is a Machiavel, my flatmate's already done that one.
