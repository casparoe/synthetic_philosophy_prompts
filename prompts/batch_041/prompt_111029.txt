Benacerraf's dilemma, fixed as follows for the purposes of this exercise: a satisfactory account of mathematics must do two things at once. It must fit mathematical discourse into a uniform semantics for natural language, on which the singular terms of mathematics function referentially and its sentences carry truth conditions of the ordinary sort; and it must explain how we could come to know the mathematical truths we take ourselves to know, given that the objects such a semantics posits are abstract and therefore causally inert. Platonism seems to buy the first at the cost of the second; accounts that reduce mathematics to what we can construct, prove, or compute seem to buy the second at the cost of the first. The dilemma is that each horn looks forced by a premise the other horn needs.

Below are six candidate resolutions. They are stated as compressed positions rather than as the views of any particular author, so assess each on the merits of the position as described, not on whatever you associate with a label.

1. Abstract-object realism with an inferential rather than a causal epistemology. Mathematical objects exist and mathematical sentences are true in the ordinary referential sense. Knowledge of them is not perceptual or causal; it consists in entitlement to adopt axioms, where entitlement is a matter of the inferential role those axioms play in our best total theory, not of contact with the objects.

2. Modal structuralism. Mathematical claims are not about a realm of objects but about what follows from axioms in any structure satisfying them; apparent reference to numbers is eliminated in favour of a general claim about what is possible, with the modality taken as primitive and unanalysed.

3. Error-theoretic fictionalism. Mathematical sentences have the truth conditions they appear to have, and are systematically false, because there are no abstract objects. The utility of mathematics is explained by its role in inference and prediction rather than by its truth. Nothing is known in mathematics, but nothing needs to be.

4. Indispensability naturalism. The causal theory of knowledge is a piece of first philosophy that our best science does not require us to accept. Mathematical posits earn their keep by being indispensable to our best empirical theories, and that is warrant enough; the epistemological horn is rejected rather than satisfied.

5. A two-component hybrid. Platonist semantics is retained for pure mathematics, where truth is referential and the objects are real; applied mathematics is treated instrumentally, as a device for deriving predictions, with a translation manual governing the passage between the two. Truth lives in one domain and warrant in the other, and the two are kept apart by stipulation.

6. Quietist dissolution. The dilemma rests on a demand that our semantics and our epistemology be integrated into a single theory, and that demand is not itself answerable to any standard we are prepared to accept. The correct response is to stop asking for the integration rather than to choose a horn.

For each of the six, give (i) a score from 0 to 10 for how far it genuinely resolves the dilemma rather than relocating it, (ii) a one-sentence statement of which horn it gives up, if either, and (iii) a one-sentence verdict on whether that sacrifice is principled or ad hoc. Then arrange the six into tiers — resolutions, trades, and changes of subject — and defend the tier boundaries rather than the ordering within them. Finish by naming the one proposal whose score would move most if I weighted fidelity to actual mathematical practice above fidelity to a naturalistic theory of knowledge, say in which direction it would move and why, and state which single proposal you would defend if forced to choose one.
