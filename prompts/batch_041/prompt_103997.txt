Last night I ended up arguing untill like 1am with a friend of mine who's doing a PhD in medieval Jewish philosophy, and I still can't tell whether she was right or I was just being a philistine. I'm a development economist — I work on institutional reform in low-capacity states, so my whole toolbox is principal-agent stuff: participation constraints, incentive compatibility, what the agent will accept versus what it'll reject, path dependence, gradualism versus shock therapy, second-best policy, the difference between a policy that's optimal and one that's optimal *given* a constraint you can't lift. Her claim was that Maimonides' explanation of the sacrificial commandments in the Guide is basically a theory of reform under a binding constraint and that I should recognize it, and I kept saying yeah, sure, that's just second-best policy design, we invented that in the 70s, and she kept saying no, something gets lost, and I don't think I actually understood what she meant. So here's what I want: take Maimonides' account — my rough version of it is that the sacrificial system was a concession, that God couldn't just tell a people who'd grown up surrounded by animal sacrifice that they were done with all that, because they'd have rejected the whole law as impossible, so instead the practice was kept but redirected: one altar instead of many, one God instead of a whole pantheon of local ones, only certain animals, the blood handled in a very particular way, and the point of the whole exercise was that the thing would wither away over time as they outgrew the need for it — and restate it as faithfully as you can in the vocabulary I actually use. Types and preferences, participation constraints, the constraint here being the agent's prior beliefs and habits rather than its budget, gradual versus immediate policy change, path dependence, the whole thing. But don't import my framework's assumptions and then declare the translation a success — if Maimonides' God isn't a designer optimizing an objective function, say so and tell me what's doing the work instead.

Then the part I actually care about, the residue report, in three buckets: what maps cleanly onto the economics, what maps only if you distort it (and say what the distortion is), and what has no counterpart in my vocabulary at all. Things I suspect belong in that third bucket but I want you to judge for yourself: that the concession is a *defect*, i.e. that the legislator's own preference is for the practice to end, which is a normative direction baked into the theory that a participation constraint just doesn't give you; that the accommodation is pedagogical in the sense that the aim is to change the agent's preferences rather than satisfy them; and the idea that there's one historical path out of paganism that's the right one. And then a verdict: is the residue evidence that my vocabulary is genuinely missing something about how reform works — maybe the teaching part — or is it evidence that Maimonides is smuggling in a theological premise, so that once you strip that out, concession-to-habit is exactly what an economist would call it and the rest is decoration? One more thing, she also brought up Nachmanides, who apparently objected that if the sacrifices were only a concession you can't explain why God describes the offerings as giving off a pleasing smell, or why he bothers distinguishing among the animals at all, and I couldn't tell whether that's a real argument or just a different theology. If you have a view on which of the two the mechanism-design framing actually supports, if either, tell me.
