I have spent the past few months defending something close to a Burkean position in a seminar on political epistemology, and I have now been asked to write up, honestly, what would actually count against it. The version I want to defend is deliberately stronger than the familiar platitude that change should be gradual. It says that for any inherited institution or practice -- legal, moral, ceremonial, it does not matter which -- the fact that it has been transmitted across many generations constitutes a defeasible but genuinely weighty reason to retain it, and that this reason does not depend on our being able to identify what the institution does. Call it a function-independent presumption: the warrant is supposed to come from the thought that a long-lived arrangement has survived repeated, distributed trial and error, and so encodes information that no single generation, and certainly no single reformer, is in a position to reconstruct. Chesterton's fence is the popular image for the idea, and Burke's presumption in favour of settled arrangements over untried projects is usually cited as its canonical statement, but I want to know how much of the doctrine is load-bearing rather than rhetorical.

So: what would count against it? Sort the defeaters by how much of the view they actually threaten, and include the ones a defender would concede rather than only the ones critics press. Specifically I want to know (1) what would have to be true about the mechanism by which institutions survive for the presumption to carry any epistemic force at all -- if what survives is selected for usefulness to the powerful rather than for benefit to those subject to it, does the presumption survive in weakened form or simply collapse? (2) Whether the position can avoid licensing the retention of institutions whose functions we do know and know to be harmful -- that is, what becomes of the presumption once the fence's purpose is fully transparent and indefensible? (3) Whether it predicts anything that a plain consequentialism or a Millian liberalism does not equally predict, and if not, what the differentiating case would look like and how likely such cases are to arise. (4) What a defender should say about the asymmetry of the burden of proof: why the cost of wrongly removing a beneficial institution should be weighted more heavily than the cost of wrongly retaining a harmful one, and whether that asymmetry can be defended without assuming the very thing it is meant to establish. And (5) whether the view is falsifiable in any useful sense, given that its central claim concerns information we by hypothesis do not possess; if the honest answer is that the doctrine has been insulated by successive qualifications, say so, and say whether that is a defect of the doctrine or of the demand for falsifiability.

I would also like you to take up the self-application case, since it seems to me the most awkward one for a committed Burkean: the presumption is itself a piece of intellectual machinery with a short pedigree, so does consistency require that it be granted less deference than the institutions it is invoked to protect? For what it is worth, the defeaters I would personally find hardest to shrug off are the ones that turn on the mechanism in (1); I am comparatively unmoved by the bare accusation that the view is conservative, and I do not think an argument's political valence counts against it. I would rather you tell me where the position is genuinely exposed than reassure me that it can be patched.
