So my game theory reading group has spent three sessions now on Hofstadter's superrationality stuff — the Scientific American piece that ends with him actually running the Luring Lottery on his own readers, and the Platonia dialogue where everybody reasons alike — and I've noticed I've stopped being able to say what would change my mind about it. I basically accept the position now: that in a symmetric problem where the other players are the kind of thinkers who'd reach the same conclusion I do, the rational move is the one that's best when we all make it together, which is why one-boxing, cooperating in a one-shot prisoner's dilemma, and not mailing in the postcard come out right even though causal decision theory says otherwise. So here's what I want from you: tell me what should count against this. Not the cheap version — I know the usual complaint that it's magical thinking, that I'm pretending my choice causes theirs, and I've never found it persuasive, because the whole point is that there's no causal arrow, just a shared answer to a shared question.

What I want is the defeaters, ranked, from fatal to merely awkward. Some are already on my list and I want to know how much they actually hurt: the reference class problem, that the class of people who reason like me can be gerrymandered until the view spits out whatever answer you wanted, so it's less a theory of rationality than a machine for laundering intuitions; the suspicion that it isn't a rival to expected utility maximization at all but a quiet switch to maximizing the group's payoff, which makes it not false but a different question; the fact that it has nothing to say about asymmetric games, which is most games; and that it seems to need evidential decision theory, so whatever kills EDT kills it — and I want your honest read on whether the smoking lesion case or the tickle defense actually kills EDT or just embarrasses it. Then the ones I haven't thought of. I'd also like you to be straight about whether the view has been qualified into unfalsifiability — Hofstadter's own hedging, the later probabilistic treatment where the superrational strategy in some payoffs turns out to be a mixed strategy, the retreat to the renormalized-rationality label — and whether that's a fatal problem for a normative view of this kind or just the normal cost of doing business. And if any of it would be settled by data (say someone ran the lottery on real people with real money, or ran the symmetric PD with the framing that everyone is getting the identical problem), tell me what pattern of results should move me, in which direction, and what I should predict if the view is right. I'd rather you tell me it's well insulated and that this is a problem than pad the list with defeators that don't bite.
