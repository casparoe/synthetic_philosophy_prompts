**Conditionality as a signal rather than a price**

1. There are two standard stories about why work-related conditionality survives its own evidence.

The incentive story: conditionality is a price placed on not-working, set so that benefits are less attractive than wages. It predicts that loosening conditionality raises claiming and tightening it raises employment. The desert story: conditionality expresses the judgement that claimants have to earn their share; it predicts that conditionality tracks moralised judgements about who deserves what, and is fairly insensitive to evidence.

Both have a problem with the same fact. The evaluations I've read on conditionality's employment effects are at best mixed and quite often null, this has been known for a while, and nothing has been rolled back. The incentive story should have died of this. The desert story survives but only by being unfalsifiable, which makes it more of an admission than an explanation — and it can't explain why the rhetoric moved from desert to activation, from the scrounger to the jobseeker, while the machinery stayed roughly the same.

2. Third story: conditionality is a costly signal, and its addressee is not the claimant but the contributor.

Treat the transfer as a gift that carries a standing risk: if it's unconditional, the contributor can't tell whether the recipient is a partner in a cooperative scheme or someone free-riding on it. A signal only works if it's costly to send and hard to fake. Conditionality is exactly that — paperwork, appointments, sanction risk, often the loss of the most flexible jobs — a cost a pure free-rider would rather not pay. On this reading the policy is aimed not at the claimant's behaviour but at the contributor's willingness to keep paying. Null employment effects are then not a refutation: moving people into work was never the point. And it predicts something the other two stories don't: the more generous the benefit, the more conditionality you need, because the signal has to be proportionate to the gift. Harsh conditionality and generous benefits are complements, not opposites. I'd expect the strongest conditionality regimes to sit in the more generous welfare states, which I think is roughly what you see, though I haven't nailed that down.

3. Fraud as counterfeit.

If conditionality is a signal, fraud isn't primarily a fiscal problem, it's a counterfeiting problem: it demonstrates that the signal can be sent by people who aren't paying the cost. That, I think, explains why the salience of fraud is so wildly out of proportion to its measured size and why enforcement is so symbolically charged. The money is beside the point; the credibility of the sorting device is the point.

4. What this doesn't do.

It doesn't tell you whether conditionality is justified. It says the standard evaluation — does it move people into work? — is aimed at the wrong target, and that the normative debate about reciprocity has been run as though the empirical question were the interesting one.
