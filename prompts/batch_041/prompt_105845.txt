I teach a 90-minute session in an upper-level seminar on philosophy of mind, sixteen students, most of them comfortable with functionalism and the Chinese-room debate but with no background in the specific thought experiment where a colony of ants is imagined to be a minded acquaintance of the sort one might chat with over tea. I want to run an activity that makes them feel the pull of the claim that the colony has a mind of its own, and then makes them feel how hard it is to say what would settle it — before I ever say the words "intentional stance" or raise the question of whether the colony is genuinely a subject of experience or merely something we find it convenient to talk about as if it were. My worry is that the standard discussion is too easy: everyone agrees the colony is "just ants" doing simple things, and the conversation dies there. So I want them to actually be the ants first, each with a rule that is genuinely local and stupid, and then to be confronted with the thing their collective produced, which none of them intended and none of them could have described from inside their own rule. Please design the whole session: the setup, the materials, the local rules (I want a real, runnable set — something like deposit-and-follow rules that make the group sort, bridge, or forage without any ant knowing the goal), the number of rounds, and the moment where I reveal to them what they collectively did and ask them to attribute a goal to it. I would like the attribution step to be forced rather than optional: arrange it so that answering a simple question about the group's behavior — what it was trying to do, whether it made a mistake, whether it wanted the thing it got — is much easier in agent language than in ant language, so that the convenience of the mental vocabulary is something they feel rather than something I assert.

Then build in a second phase that puts pressure on the first: after the colony has been treated as an agent, run a variation in which the same output is produced by a script, a lookup table, or by a shill who has been told the answer, so the group has to decide whether the mentality was in the behavior or in the producing of it; and, if you can, a variation in which the colony behaves identically but each ant is secretly following a different rule than the one on its card, so they must ask whether there is any fact of the matter about what the colony is doing independent of our description. I want the debrief to map each moment of the activity onto the philosophical joints — the difference between a system whose parts have their own aims and a system that has an aim, the difference between something we can predict and something that means or wants, the recurrence of the same question one level up when we ask whether a single human is more than her neurons — and to be honest about which feature of the idea the activity simply cannot reproduce, namely the question of whether there is anything it is like to be the colony, since every participant is a human with a mind already, so the appearance of a collective mentality may be borrowed from them and the activity cannot rule that out. Finally, tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this — where the rules will break down, where a clever student will short-circuit the emergence by shouting the global pattern out loud, and how to keep the group from collapsing into the "just ants" verdict too early — and give me a contingency for a group that is too small or too uncoordinated to produce anything emergent at all. Assume I can supply index cards, a large floor space, and some tokens or sticky notes, but no technology and no budget.
