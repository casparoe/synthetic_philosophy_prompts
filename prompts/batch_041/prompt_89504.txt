I take lying to be wrong in the way that betrayal is wrong rather than in the way that injury is wrong: a perversion of the will and a misuse of the faculty of speech, not merely a source of harm. I take that to yield an absolutism of the Augustinian kind — a false signification is never permissible, however grave what hangs on it, and where the truth cannot safely be told the honest options are silence or concealment rather than a false word. What I want is a map of the position's exposure: what would count against it, ranked by how decisive each consideration would be. I would rather have the structural threats than another recital of the murderer at the door. (I am asking about the position, not about the accuracy of the attribution; if the historical Augustine held something importantly weaker or stronger, flag it, but do not let the exegetical question absorb the philosophical one.)

First, which of the distinctions the position leans on are load-bearing, and what becomes of the position as each gives way? Consider the line between lying and concealing; the line between a falsehood meant to deceive and a falsehood no one will believe (a joke, a transparent courtesy, an obvious fiction); and the asymmetry by which a true statement made in order to deceive counts as a lie while a false statement made in order to be disbelieved does not. If the prohibition attaches to a category fixed by intent to deceive, and that category turns out not to track the moral fault — if the most effective deceptions are often the ones that use only true statements — does the position survive that discovery, or does it show that the prohibition was aimed at the wrong thing all along?

Second, does the appeal to concealment genuinely escape the difficulty, or only relocate it? In many concrete situations, refusing to answer, or falling conspicuously silent, conveys precisely the information one meant to withhold: the hearer is deceived by one's truthfulness rather than by a falsehood. If the position must bless that outcome, is the remaining difference between lying and silence anything more than the familiar difference between doing and allowing — and is that difference any more defensible here than it is elsewhere?

Third, what does the grounding of the prohibition over-generate? If lying is wrong because it perverts the will or misuses speech, it is not obvious why the same verdict does not fall on the deliberate cultivation of a false impression by true means — on selective disclosure, on misleading emphasis, on the well-timed omission. Either the grounding condemns more than the position wants, or it is narrowed until it condemns only the false word, and the narrowing looks arbitrary. Which horn does the position take, and can it take either without loss?

Fourth, what does the position predict that a permissive rival does not, and are those predictions ones we should accept? The rival that allows the helpful lie buys the hard cases at the price of the clean rule. What does absolutism deliver beyond the clean rule itself — and does the deliverance depend on premises (that speech is owed to a truth that is divine, say) which a defender unwilling to invoke them cannot keep?

Finally, where has the position been insulated by qualification? If the answer to every difficult case is that the act in question would not be a lie on the correct definition, the position may have become unfalsifiable. Tell me whether that is what has happened, and whether it matters for a view of this kind.

Then the honest part. Say what the position's own defenders would concede counts against it, not only what its critics allege: which considerations would a committed Augustinian themselves find awkward, and which would they dismiss as failing to engage the view rather than refuting it? Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely embarrassing, and be willing to conclude that the position is well insulated rather than well defended. Tell me, too, where your own reasoning is likeliest to fail, and what would change your verdict on whether the position is well defended or merely well insulated.
