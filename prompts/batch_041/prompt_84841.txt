I'm a philosophy PhD student who has drifted into a seminar on the foundations of statistics, and I keep having the same vertiginous experience: the reference class problem is presented there as a tidy puzzle about the single case, but it also looks like the daily working life of an insurance company, and I can't tell how much philosophy the underwriters are implicitly signed up to. Here is the practice as I understand it. An insurer must quote a premium for one particular applicant about whom it knows nearly nothing, so it begins from a manual rate for a broad class — age band, sex (where lawful), region, occupation, smoking status, prior claims — and then blends that group rate with whatever individual experience the applicant brings, weighting the two by a credibility factor between zero and one: the thinner the personal record, the more the group rate dominates, and the weight slides toward the individual as data accumulate. The class definitions themselves are not fixed. Actuaries split and merge classes, add and drop rating variables, whenever the finer partition predicts better, so the boundaries look chosen rather than discovered. Layered over all of this, the law forbids some classifications that are demonstrably predictive — race in most places, sex in European insurance, genetic test results in American health insurance — while permitting others that are, at best, correlates of risk.

I'd like you to reconstruct the philosophical commitments this practice presupposes, as opposed to the ones its practitioners would avow if you asked them. Specifically: what view of probability for the single case does the credibility weighting presuppose — a frequentism on which the applicant's chance simply is the frequency in the class, with his own history supplying a better sample of the same quantity, or an epistemic or evidential view on which the class rate is only an estimate and the blend is a calibrated guess about something else? Is there anything the practice does that distinguishes these two, or does it run on either? Second, does the practice presuppose that there is a fact of the matter about the correct reference class — that nature has a privileged partition of risk and the actuary's job is to find it — or does it treat the partition as an artifact chosen for decision-theoretic payoff, so that the "right" class is the one that prices well and holds up in a rate filing? Third, what conception of fairness does it carry, given the standing tension between actuarial fairness (like risks, like premiums) and the pooling of risk that is the institution's entire raison d'être — and if the law's forbidden classes cannot be distinguished from the permitted ones by predictive power, is there a principled line implicit in the practice or only a political one? Finally, what would have to change in the practice's self-understanding if we adopted a thoroughgoing propensity view of chance, or a fully individualized n-of-1 approach of the sort telematics, wearables, and personal genomics now make feasible?

Please be careful to separate what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits comfortably with, and to say plainly where it is underdetermined between rival theories rather than forcing a single reconstruction.
