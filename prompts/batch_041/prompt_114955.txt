I run a small interpretability team (six people, one shared node of GPUs, which will matter below) and we've got a thing we call the mechanism registry. Every time someone finds a circuit that seems to explain a behaviour — an induction head, a thing that does modular addition, whatever — we add an entry: the task, the parts, the causal story, the interventions we ran (ablations, activation patching, i.e. swapping activations between two runs and seeing whether the effect travels with them), and a name. The registry is load-bearing, because our anomaly detector sits on top of it: we take our best explanation of what the model is doing on task X, we watch for behaviour that explanation can't account for, and when something looks off, the question we're supposed to answer is wether the model is still using the same mechanism or has quietly switched to a different one.

Which is fine until you ask what 'same mechanism' means. Three of us have been re-litigating the same two entries for a month, each holding up a different test:

- structural: same kind of parts, same wiring, same layers
- counterfactual: run the same interventions and get the same pattern of effects
- explanatory role: same job in the bigger story we tell about the model — it's the thing that produces the pattern we care about

And our theory person keeps saying all three are relative to what you're trying to explain, and that sameness probably comes in degrees rather than being yes or no, which is not what I want to hear when the detector fires at 2am.

What I want from you is a procedure my team can actually run on a flagged case — something a rotation student can apply with me out of the room. Not a lit review. In particular:

- the questions to ask in order, and what counts as a sufficient answer to each
- a small set of interventions to run, in order, and when to stop early: our budget is about one afternoon of GPU time per case, so it has to fail fast
- where the output is allowed to be 'same', where 'different', and where it has to be 'this is a registry merge or split, take it to the team'
- which of my three tests above are doing real work and which are decorative, and where a human has to decide something that isn't a fact
- what the procedure can't do, and how it will misfire

Then run it on three cases:

1. A circuit in a 7B model that looks like a match for an entry we registered from a 160M model: same task, same apparent role, but the relevant heads sit at different relative depths and the one we'd point to is partly redundant with two others.
2. An entry we registered as 'does modular addition with a rotation-like algorithm', which on closer inspection seems to be a memorised lookup for the small moduli and something else entirely for the large ones. Our detector's threshold was calibrated on the rotation story.
3. A case where the procedure gives an answer we won't like, and I want you to pick that case yourself: some flagged behaviour where the honest verdict forces us to weaken something we've already said publicly about how well we'd notice a mechanism switch.

Be honest about which steps are judgment calls dressed up as procedure, and about how badly this will go the first few times we use it.
