I run a fortnightly reading group on classical Indian epistemology — six of us, translations and long arguments, no exams or grades — and for our last session I would like to set a written question rather than have another discussion, so that people have to commit to a position on paper. The puzzle I want the question to turn on is one we kept circling back to: a whole village believes the water upstream is safe to drink, everyone has it from someone else, the person who actually checked is long dead and nobody can name him, and the water is in fact safe — so nobody in the village has reasoned badly or been deceived, and yet I cannot bring myself to say that the village knows. I would like the question to make them decide whether Nyāya's requirement that testimony count as a knowledge source only when it is the utterance of a trustworthy speaker (āptopadeśa) can survive a case in which the speaker has dropped out of the picture, or whether the case shows that what really does the work in Nyāya is the external confirmation of a cognition — its coherence with our other cognitions and its leading to successful action — while it is the Mīmāṃsā insistence that a cognition's validity is intrinsic that allows the Veda to be treated as valid despite having no author at all. It should not be answerable by reciting the list of the pramāṇas or the usual school-by-school summary, and it should be doable with no sources at hand. So: one essay question, answerable in forty-five minutes without notes, worth twenty marks, together with a model answer of about six hundred words and a marking scheme built on five must-hit points, the marks each is worth, and a one-line note on how a member of the group typically misses each one.
