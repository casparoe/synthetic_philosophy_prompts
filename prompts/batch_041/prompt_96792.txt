I teach aesthetics and I need a favor for next week's seminar. One of my colleagues — Marta, the department's resident Tolstoy person, she's spent the better part of a decade rebuilding the theory from scratch and now runs it over everything like a tax code — has been giving me rulings on individual works for years. I want to use her as a live case study, because I'm fairly sure she isn't running the theory she says she's running.

What she says she's running: art is when a maker calls up in himself a feeling he's actually had, and transmits it to other people by outward signs. Three things make the feeling better or worse — how individual it is (a stock emotion anyone could have had counts for less than a feeling only this one person could have had), how clearly it comes across, and how sincere the maker is. And then, seperately, the moral quality of the feeling — whether it pulls people together or splits them apart — decides how good the art is, not whether it's art. She's adamant about that last part; she thinks you can call something art and still think it's poison.

Her rulings that I've actually written down, accumulated over the years:

- A hauling song a road crew sang, no author anyone could name, everyone on the crew carried it and it plainly carried the feeling. Art, and first rate.
- A piano showpiece commissioned to show off a touring virtuoso's hands, which reliably makes audiences weep. She says the composer felt nothing while writing it and that the tears are a reflex, not an infection. Not art. She has a word for this kind of thing — counterfeit — and it's clearly the worst thing she can say.
- A hymn from a faith she thinks is false, sung by a congregation that means every word of it, and she can feel it land in her own chest. Art, and she'll defend it against anyone.
- A novel that celebrates cruelty, which she grants is sincere and powerful. Art. Wicked art, and she'd burn it, but art.
- A little thing a friend of hers wrote about one specific afternoon, in a private vocabulary the two of them shared, which does absolutely nothing for me. She ranks it above a famous symphony everyone loves. Highest praise she has.

Now the case I want to use in class. A woman recorded a set of songs for her brother while he was dying. They'd invented a way of talking to each other as kids — pet names, a half-made grammar, references to things nobody else was ever present for — and the songs are in that. She meant every second of them, and they are as individual as a feeling can get. But I've heard the recordings and I get nothing, and Marta, who's heard them too, says she gets nothing either. Nobody who didn't grow up in that house will ever be infected by them. They're addressed to one person, and he's dead.

So what does Marta say? Art or not art — and if it's some third thing, like art but the lowest kind, say that and say why. And here's what I actually want out of this: what rule is she running, what are the rival rules that fit everything above just as well, what does each of them predict here, which do you think is really operating, and which further case would tell them apart? Also tell me whether there's a reading of her own stated theory on which the individuality requirement and the clarity requirement both come out satisfied here, or whether the theory is just internally at war with itself at this exact point. She doesn't do half measures, so I need a definite verdict from her, not a shrug.
