Next Thursday our health board votes on how to spend the last two million of this year's budget, and since I'm the lay member I've been asked to write the recommendation that goes out with the papers. (I ran a chip shop for thirty years. No medical training, no philosophy, I just read a lot.)

The choice is between two things. Option A is a drug for four local patients with a rare nerve disease: it won't cure them, but it stops the decline, so they keep the use of their hands and stay out of full-time care. Option B is a visiting scheme for about six thousand housebound elderly people; each of them gets a bit less lonely and a bit less likely to end up in hospital. A is four people getting something enormous. B is thousands of people each getting something small but real.

Our chair used to teach ethics, and he says the way to decide is to stop adding things up. For each option, find the single person with the strongest grounds to object to it, and ask whether that objection would be reasonable. He calls this contractualism (I'd always assumed that was about signing things) and says he got it from a philosopher called Scanlon. On his test B loses: nobody among the six thousand could reasonably object to missing out on a bit of company, whereas any of the four could reasonably object to losing their hands. I've read his summary twice and I still cant see how it can be right that it makes no difference whether it's six thousand people or six. But I can also see that if you just count heads, a million people with a stubbed toe beat one person being paralysed, which seems worse. My daughter says this problem has a name; she couldn't remember what the answer is meant to be.

Write the memo I'll read out at the meeting. Pick A or B, and give us a rule we can apply to the next case too, not just this one. Take the chair seriously, because I don't want to be the lay member who just says the numbers are big, and tell me where your own reasoning is weakest and what would make you come out the other way.
