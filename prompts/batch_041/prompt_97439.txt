I've been arguing with a friend about moral worth, and we keep sliding between different questions, so I want to fix a single proposition and get a credence with the reasoning exposed rather than another tour of the literature. Here is the proposition: for an action to have moral worth — the property in virtue of which it is creditable to the agent, as opposed to merely permissible, useful, or fortunate — the agent must have acted for the very considerations that made the action right, and not merely from some standing disposition that reliably issues in right action. On this narrow view, the guilt-averse person who does the honest thing because dishonesty would make her feel awful — who reliably does the honest thing, and would do it again in the same circumstances — earns no credit for it; only the person moved by the dishonesty's being wrong does. I put my credence at about 0.7 and my friend puts hers at about 0.3, and I suspect we are not disagreeing where we think we are. Please give me your own number — I would rather have a specific figure with an honest uncertainty range than a hedge — and then decompose it into the sub-questions it actually rests on: whether there is a determinate fact about an agent's operative motive, and whether motives can be individuated finely enough to bear this much weight; whether "moral worth" names a single property or a cluster (credit, admirability, being a good person, being the sort of person one can rely on) whose members could come apart; whether motive-sensitivity is necessary for worth at all, as against objective or externalist accounts on which a right action credited to no one is still worth something; and, conditional on the narrow view being roughly on track, whether the relevant motive must be the moral considerations themselves or whether any reliably pro-moral disposition will do.

Assign each sub-question a rough probability with one line of justification, show how they combine into your final figure, and then tell me which single sub-question your number is most sensitive to and what evidence or case would move it most. Finally, since the real point is to locate a disagreement: if you had to bet, is the gap between my 0.7 and my friend's 0.3 living mostly in the individuation-of-motives question, in the cluster-versus-single-property question, or in whether reliability without the right motive can ever earn credit — and is there a variant of the guilt-averse case, or a case in which the agent's motive and the action's right-making feature come apart in some more awkward way, on which one of us would be forced to revise? I would like the decomposition to do the work here, so please don't collapse it into a verdict about which view is more intuitive; I want to see the joints.
