I keep running into a pattern and I don't have a word for it, and it's bugging me enough that I'm writing it out.

The shape: somebody predicts something about how *you* are going to experience something, and the prediction turns out true exactly when you don't believe it, and false when you do. The prediction is about your own surprise or your own ignorance, so your belief in it isn't just sitting off to the side being accurate or inaccurate — it's part of what decides the outcome.

Instance one, and yes I know this is the old surprise-quiz thing, but stay with me: teacher says there'll be a surprise quiz next week. The students run the familiar argument, conclude no surprise is possible, and stop expecting one. Quiz comes Wednesday, they're genuinely caught out. The part I care about isn't whether their proof is any good. It's that the announcement came out true, and it came out true becuase they didn't believe it — believing it was the thing that would have killed it.

Instance two, no exam involved: a magician announces up front exactly how the trick works, then says you still won't see it coming. Take him at his word and you stare at his hands and catch the move, so he's wrong. Write it off as bluffing and you relax and get fooled, so he's right. Notice he'd want to be disbelieved. The most reliable way to make his sentence true is to get you to stop taking it seriously.

Instance three, the one that actually bothers me: the teacher could hand the students the impossibility proof herself, on purpose, before quiz week. Not as a gag — as the mechanism. Making them certain there's no quiz is the surest way to make the quiz a surprise. So the proof isn't a bug in the setup, it's a tool for making the announcement true.

What I want: is there a name for this class — predictions about the audience's own epistemic state where believing them flips their truth value — and separately for the sub-case where the predictor's best play is to engineer disbelief? I know it's in the neighborhood of self-fulfilling prophecy and of Moore's paradox, but neither seems to fit. A self-fulfilling prophecy is belief → true, a self-defeating one is belief → false, and this looks like both at once, in a loop. Moore sentences are awkward to assert but their truth doesn't hinge on what you believe. So please don't just say it's the surprise exam paradox. That's one instance. I want the general phenomenon, what it excludes, and where its edges fall.

Cases I honestly can't classify: being told not to think about a white bear, or to just be spontaneous — those are impossible-by-trying, not belief-sensitive predictions, so presumably a different animal, but I'd like to know exactly why. And a doctor saying a pill only works if you believe it works: belief-dependent for sure, but the sentence isn't about your belief, and believing it doesn't make the sentence false. So where's the line?
