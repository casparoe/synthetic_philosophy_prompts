I want a credence rather than a survey of positions, and I want the credence built from its parts rather than asserted.

The proposition is this: the demandingness of act-consequentialism — the fact that, taken at face value, it would require most of us to surrender the greater part of our income, time, and attention, and to treat our own projects and attachments as no weightier than anyone else's — is a genuine objection to the theory's truth, as opposed to a reason only to think the theory impractical, self-effacing, or unfit to serve as a public moral code. My own credence is around 0.3; I suspect the objection is doing less work than its popularity suggests.

Give me your number, and then decompose it. I want a rough probability and a one-line justification for at least each of the following, together with any further term you think belongs:

(a) that common-sense intuitions about the permissibility of favoring one's own projects and loved ones are reliable evidence about the moral facts at all;

(b) that the objection proves too much — that any morality which took the claims of distant strangers seriously would be comparably demanding, so that a sound objection here refutes more than act-consequentialism;

(c) that the integrity and alienation complaints — that the committed consequentialist is estranged from her own life, or that the theory must be self-effacing if it is to be lived — add force beyond the bare magnitude of the demands;

(d) that the objection rests on an empirical premise about how much sacrifice human beings can sustain, and how firm that premise is;

(e) whether demandingness is even a defect a moral theory can have, or whether it is a feature of the world that the theory reports.

State how the terms combine into your final number, and identify the term the number is most sensitive to. Then say what would move it most: a distinction, a thought experiment, or a piece of evidence.

There is one argument I want you to weigh explicitly, because I think it is under-discussed. Call it the evidential-symmetry argument. The objection asks us to trust the intuitions that generate it — that I may keep my money for my child rather than give it to a stranger who needs it more. But act-consequentialism's own core claim, that only outcomes matter, is likewise supported chiefly by intuition. So the consequentialist may reply that we cannot discount the intuitions that embarrass the theory while keeping the ones that support it: either common-sense moral intuition is evidence about the moral facts or it is not, and it cannot be evidence only when convenient. Tell me whether this argument moves your number, in which direction, and by roughly how much — and whether the asymmetry you may want to invoke, that the two sets of intuitions differ in kind or in quality, is doing legitimate work or is itself the thing in dispute.
