Last month a friend in my decision-theory reading group, who has become a thoroughgoing convert to the idea that expected value should govern every choice, tried to convince me that the maximization principle taken literally collapses into absurdity, and that the collapse is the theory's honest content rather than a bug. We had been arguing about whether he should keep spending his evenings on what he calls the Ritual — a small, private practice he thinks raises, by a genuinely positive though minute probability, the chance of an outcome he values without any finite bound. He would not call it infinite, but he insists no finite number captures it, and that nothing else in his life competes with it on that scale. He wrote the argument out for me; here it is as he gave it. (1) A rational agent ranks available actions by expected value, the probability-weighted sum of the values of their possible outcomes. (2) For any two actions, if one raises the probability of some outcome O by a positive amount, however small, and O's value is large enough, then that action outranks the other. (3) The Ritual raises the probability of O, and O's value outruns anything the alternatives could produce. (4) So the Ritual's expected value exceeds that of every ordinary alternative — the career, the friendships, the sleep. (5) The action that outranks every alternative is the one rationality requires. (6) So rationality requires the Ritual. (7) But the same premises require a rival Ritual aimed at a different unbounded good, and a third, and a fourth; these cannot all be performed, and each is said to be required. (8) A principle that requires mutually incompatible actions is not correct. (9) Therefore (1) is false.

I would rather keep (1) — I do think expected value is the right guide — so I am looking for a bad step rather than a reason to abandon the principle, though I want to know what that costs me. His own view is that the cheap move is (7)–(8), the reductio, because it merely shows that you cannot serve two masters and not that the principle is false; I am not so sure, and I suspect the trouble lies earlier, in the way (2) lets any positive amount, however small, do work that a probability is not entitled to do, or in the slide in (4) from something valued beyond any finite bound to a quantity that can be compared and outweighed. Tell me where the argument goes wrong, if it does; if it is in fact valid, tell me which premise I should surrender and why that one rather than another. And settle a prior question for me: does any of this depend on the unbounded value, or would the same structure go through with an outcome that is merely very valuable and a probability that is merely very small — because if it would, I think the pressure falls on (1) itself, and I want to understand whether the repair (a ceiling on value, a floor on probability, a lexical ordering) rescues expected value maximization or merely disguises it.
