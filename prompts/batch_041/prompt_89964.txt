I am writing a novel in which a scholar of nineteenth-century German philosophy is called to testify before a fictional international tribunal, and I need a rigorous prediction rather than a summary: what position would Hegel have been driven to, on his own premises, on a question that could not have arisen in his lifetime — the criminal liability of individual leaders for initiating an aggressive war? The scene turns on whether she can give a straight answer, and the drama depends on the answer being both determinate and uncomfortable.

The modern architecture is this: since the middle of the twentieth century, a body of international law has treated the launching of an aggressive war not as a prerogative of sovereign states but as a crime, and has pinned responsibility on the individuals who ordered it — heads of government, ministers, commanders — rather than on the state as such. The same body of law insists that a soldier who fights honorably in an unjust war commits no crime, and that the order to fight does not excuse the leader who gave it. The court that exercises jurisdiction over aggression is not a world state; it operates within a system of state consent, and its reach over aggression is entangled with the political body in which the great powers hold vetoes. I am not interested in whether Hegel would have approved of this or that institution. I am interested in what his own commitments — about the state as the actuality of ethical life, about war as a moment in which the state's individuality is asserted and tested, about world history as the tribunal that judges states, about the absence of any arbiter above states — force him to say when the accused is a person rather than a polity.

I need a determinate verdict on three questions, not a survey of possibilities. First: is the proper subject of responsibility for an unjust war the individual leader or the state? Second: can a court above states be reconciled with Hegel's account of international relations, or does it contradict the very nature of the state? Third: if such a court is legitimate, what would Hegel say about punishing a leader whose war was, by his own lights, the rational expression of his state's historical mission — and would the punishment be justice, or merely the victor's judgment?

Please structure the response in four named sections. In "The Premises" (roughly 150 words), state the Hegelian commitments your prediction rests on, in your own words, without quotation or citation. In "The Predicted Position" (roughly 600 words), give your verdict on each of the three questions and the reasoning that drives it, distinguishing where Hegel's texts clearly point from where you are reconstructing. In "The Counter-Case" (roughly 300 words), present the strongest objection to your own prediction from within Hegel's thought, and identify the point at which the reconstruction is genuinely underdetermined — where no honest reading can decide. In "The Testimony" (roughly 150 words), write the paragraph my scholar would actually deliver in the witness box: first person, compressed, in the register of a person who knows the position is uncomfortable.
