Mill died in 1873. "Wild animal suffering" as a named cause area, and the associated hypothesis that the net welfare of ecosystems might be negative, arrived some hundred and fifty years later. I would like you to reason this out from his commitments rather than gesture at his general reputation, and to commit to an answer.

Here is the substance of the debate I am writing about. A growing body of work argues that the lives of most wild animals are, on average, not worth living: r-strategists produce enormous broods, the overwhelming majority of offspring die young and often painfully, and the sheer quantity of such lives may make the total suffering in a hectare of rainforest or a square kilometre of ocean staggeringly large. If that premise holds, an uncomfortable corollary follows. Because the number of wild animals in an area is roughly proportional to the habitat and resources available, destroying habitat — draining a wetland, clear-cutting a forest, grading a grassland for a solar farm — could reduce the total quantity of suffering, at least in the near term, by reducing the number of animals that exist to suffer. One researcher I interviewed proposes calling the resulting shortfall in suffering the "welfare dividend" of habitat loss, and distinguishes it from a second effect she calls the "displacement penalty," in which the animals that are not simply killed are crowded into marginal habitat where their welfare falls further. Set against all this are several objections: that habitat loss is plainly bad for the animals that do survive and for ecosystem function; that comparing the welfare of a mayfly with that of a macaque may be incoherent; that destroyed ecosystems are recolonised within a few seasons, so any dividend is temporary; and that a totalist calculus which welcomes the non-existence of suffering beings, if generalised, would license horrors.

I want your best reconstruction of Mill's position on four specific points. First, would he accept the net-negative-lives premise — that the average wild animal's life contains more suffering than enjoyment — or would some feature of his hedonism push him to deny it, for instance a reluctance to count short lives as worthless? Second, conditional on that premise, would he endorse habitat destruction as a net improvement in total welfare, or would something in his utilitarianism block the inference? Third, how would he handle the total-versus-average question and the replaceability of animal generations — would the prospect of future animals that never come to exist count in the ledger, and in which direction? Fourth, what weight would he give to the fact that he prized wild nature both aesthetically and as a source of human improvement, when that consideration runs against an impersonal welfare calculus that might favour razing it? Where you see genuine tension between his stated commitments, say so and adjudicate rather than papering it over; and where the historical Mill simply did not have the conceptual resources to take a view, say that too and tell me which way the nearest reconstruction goes.
