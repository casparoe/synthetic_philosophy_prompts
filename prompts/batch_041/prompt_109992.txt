For a graduate decision-theory reading group I am assembling a handout on expected-value fanaticism, and I want the classification built out of the logical space rather than out of a roster of authors. Please produce a decision tree: first the branching questions, then the positions that occupy the resulting leaves. Every internal node should be a question a respondent can answer without already knowing which leaf it leads to; every leaf should carry the name of the position, or be marked unnamed if nobody has defended it, plus one sentence naming the commitment its defenders are least happy to state plainly.

The branch points must include at least these: whether the trouble is located in the decision rule, in the axiology, or in our intuitions; whether infinite value and merely astronomically large finite value raise one problem or two; whether the proposed fix applies only where the tiny probability is given directly, as opposed to arising as the product of many independent small chances; whether the axiology admits lexical priority between goods or insists on continuity; and whether the view is restricted to cases in which a determinate probability exists at all.

I want these five to come out as leaves: full expected-value maximization with the fanaticism accepted; rejection of expected-value maximization in favour of a risk-sensitive or ex ante prioritarian rule; axiological finitism, on which there are no infinite values or no comparisons across scales; a probability-threshold rule that disregards sufficiently small chances; and the incommensurability view, on which the comparison the fanaticism argument needs simply cannot be made. Where an answer combination is unoccupied, say so and diagnose whether the position is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored, and state explicitly which pairs of leaves your tree cannot separate.

Two cases have to be usable at the nodes: a fair-coin St Petersburg lottery with unbounded payoffs, and a choice between a certain modest gain and an astronomically small chance of a lexically better outcome for an enormous population. At the relevant nodes, indicate where the problem for a finite but very large population collapses into the population-axiology problem of the repugnant conclusion, and where it instead turns on whether probability is treated as an object of rational preference at all.
