I teach a seminar on norms and cultural variation, and I keep running into the same pedagogical problem. My students can recite the descriptive claim that societies differ in how strictly they enforce norms, but the moment we turn to the normative question — how much rule-following should a society actually demand of its members? — the discussion collapses into a two-way shouting match between 'let people live as they like' and 'a society needs shared rules.' I want a classification instrument that exposes the real structure of the disagreement instead. Please act as a political philosopher and social theorist with a strong background in the theory of norms, conventions, and social coordination, and take your time with this: begin by brainstorming, in your own working, the genuinely distinct claims one could hold about (a) the grounds on which a society may demand conformity, (b) the scope over which that demand should range, and (c) the means by which it should be upheld, and only then assemble the instrument, so that its branches are generated by the logical space rather than by a list of familiar political labels. A few candidate distinctions I find myself unsure how to fit in, and which you should consider and either use or explicitly set aside: the difference between regulative rules that constrain a pre-existing activity and constitutive rules that create the activity in the first place; the idea that some norms are pure coordination equilibria whose value to each person rises as more people adopt them, while others carry moral content independent of uptake; the principle that decisions should be made at the lowest level competent to make them; the thought that a norm's real force depends on whether members can credibly exit the community rather than merely voice objection; the observation that informal norms rush in to fill whatever vacuum formal rules leave, so that loosening can increase rather than decrease social pressure; and the worry that a highly uniform practice is fragile, in the way a monoculture is, because a single bad rule then propagates everywhere at once.

The deliverable is a decision tree or taxonomy — a sorting instrument, not an essay. Every branch node must be a question that someone could answer without already knowing which position it leads to, phrased as a concrete claim or case rather than as a label ('do you think society should be tighter or looser?' is exactly the kind of question I want to avoid). Each leaf should carry the name of a position, or the word 'unnamed' if nobody plausibly defends it, plus one sentence stating that position's most awkward commitment. Please make sure the following appear somewhere among the leaves: a view that fixes a single society-wide level of strictness on intrinsic grounds, holding that a shared and demanding form of life is good in itself; a view that fixes a single society-wide level on instrumental grounds, treating strictness as a means to coordination, trust, or order; a permissive view that permits the society to demand only what prevents direct harm to others; a domain-relative view that sets different levels for different spheres of life; a view on which a robust right of exit is the linchpin of whatever strictness is legitimate; and a view on which the correct answer turns on whether the norm in question is a coordination equilibrium or a norm with moral content. Please build the tree around these concrete cases, and use them at the branch nodes: a city weighing a civility code that would require residents to keep their front lawns tidy and to greet their neighbours; a small religious community that enforces strict internal norms while living inside a much looser surrounding society; a public-health emergency that requires a normally permissive society to tighten its rules temporarily; an arbitrary convention such as which side of the road to drive on, contrasted with a norm against lying; and a norm that is loose enough to let a few visible free-riders impose diffuse costs on everyone else.

Aim for roughly ten to twelve branch nodes and seven to nine leaves, and give me the instrument in a form I can photocopy: the questions in order, with the routing made explicit, and then the leaves with their one-line diagnoses. Where two positions that I would think of as distinct end up at the same leaf, say so and flag them as positions the tree cannot separate rather than pretending the questions distinguish them; where a combination of answers corresponds to no defended view, mark the empty cell and briefly say whether it is empty because the view is incoherent, because it is unattractive, or merely because nobody has explored it. Finally, please add a short note on which of the branch questions you think are doing the most diagnostic work and which are nearly redundant, so that I can trim the instrument for a fifty-minute class.
