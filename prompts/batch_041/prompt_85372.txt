I'm a novelist, about two-thirds through a book set in a govenment press office in the late seventies — not quite Yes Minister, not quite le Carré, more a bunch of decent-ish people slowly learning to say nothing at length. The whole thing has got me arguing with Orwell in my head, so I want you to take the position I've talked myself into and defend it properly, because at the moment I can only do it in a ranting way.

Here's the position. Orwell's famous line in Politics and the English Language is roughly that slovenly language makes foolish thought easier, that if you get rid of the bad habits you can think more clearly, and that thinking clearly is a necessary first step towards political regeneration. I've come to think the causal story is backwards in the interesting cases, and that the real work of political euphemism isn't to blur the speaker's thought but to license action without thought — the phrase isn't a fog the speaker gets lost in, it's a permission slip. Pacification, enhanced interrogation, collateral damage: nobody using those is confused about what happened. The phrase is doing something else. It's pre-emptive self-exculpation, a way of having already written the memo you'll need later. The fog is on the outside, for the reader and the historian, not on the inside, for the man who gave the order.

Argue for this against Orwell — don't just agree with me. Make the strongest case that the interesting political damage is done by language that doesn't require the speaker to be muddled, that the muddle is manufactured for an audience, and that Orwell's remedy (concrete words, short sentences, no dead metaphors) either misses the point or is useless against it, because you can be perfectly concrete and still be pulling the exculpation trick. We shot him in the back of the head is concrete. If that's right, what does the remedy even look like, and what of Orwell survives? I don't want a survey of what critics have said — I want the argument made properly, with the mechanism spelled out: how does the phrase do its work, who is it for, why wouldn't clarity touch it.

And the bit I actually care about for the book: if the damage is done by language everyone using it understands perfectly well, then my press office people aren't tragic figures corrupted by jargon. They're something more interesting — competent people who've found a way to do things they couldn't otherwise do. Argue me into that, or argue me out of it. And while you're at it, tell me where the novelist's instinct goes wrong here, if it does: I keep wanting the moment of corruption to be a moment, and you're about to tell me it dissapears into ordinary professional competence instead.
