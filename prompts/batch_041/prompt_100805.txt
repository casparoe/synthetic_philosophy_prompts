Ok so I'm a second-year PhD student and I've been lumbered with running the discussion section for our logic-and-reasoning unit next Thursday — 16 undergrads, 90 minutes flat, and the professor wants me to "make the tortoise land" before she lectures on it the week after. Last time I taught this I just wrote the regress on the board, everyone nodded, and nothing happened. So what I want is an activity, not a lecture: something they do with their hands that makes them feel the wall themselves before anyone names it.

Here's the setup I've got in mind, but please redesign it as you see fit. Pairs, one plays the Logician and one plays the Tortoise. Give the Tortoise a hidden card with his character on it: he will happily accept any statement anyone hands him, no argument, no resistance — but he will only ever draw a conclusion if a rule already written in his rulebook licenses that exact move, and any rule you want in the rulebook has to be handed to him as a statement he accepts. That's the whole character. The Logician gets a stack of blank cards and can write whatever he wants.

Score it with tokens or points, no money. Tortoise scores one for every statement he's handed and accepts. Logician scores one only if the Tortoise actually comes out and asserts the conclusion on his own initiative. So both of them are incentivised to keep the thing running forever, and the Logician's only way to score is to do something that isn't a move in the game. Watch that happen.

Round 2, new pairs, and here I want a twist so they can't just pattern-match the famous version: let the Logician write an inference rule instead of a premise, and have the Tortoise, per his card, accept it as a statement and then ask for a rule licensing the application of *that* one. Then hand out a rule card that says it applies to itself, and make the room argue about whether they've escaped the regress or just smuggled the conclusion in through the back.

Round 3, and this is the part I care most about: swap the target inference for an inductive one — from "every X we've looked at has been Y" to "the next X will be Y" — and run it again. Get them to notice whether the self-applying-rule trick that felt like cheating in the deductive case feels like cheating here too, and whether anyone can articulate what the difference is, if there is one.

Then the debrief, and it should map back to the actual machinery: the regress of premises versus the regress of rules, the difference between a rule being *used* and a rule being *justified*, why treating a rule as a rule rather than another premise looks like a move to a practice rather than a proof, and why rule-circularity sounds harmless in the deductive case and question-begging in the inductive one. I'd like the debrief to leave them with at least three live escape routes — take the rule as a practice, say the Tortoise's demand is incoherent because understanding "if" just *is* being willing to infer, or refuse his frame altogether — and a sense of what each costs.

Also tell me honestly: what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this (I'm bracing for pairs where the Logician gives up and just explains, and for someone insisting the Tortoise is merely confused about conditionals), and which feature of the real problem the activity simply cannot reproduce and that I'll have to supply myself in the last ten minutes. One request: don't let the words tortoise or Carroll anywhere near them until the debrief — I want them to arrive at the shape of it on their own. Materials should be stuff I can prep the night before with index cards and a printer.
