right, so I'm trying to work out what a principle of credal reflection actually has to deliver before I'm willing to use it to settle cases, and I want the spec written out first.

Background so we're on the same page: the reflection principle is the one that says your credence in A right now has to match what you currently expect your credence in A to be at some later time — if you're certain that in an hour you'll be 0.7 in A, then you have to be 0.7 in A now. I get that it falls out of some Dutch-book-ish argument about diachronic coherence, and that it gets used to police things like Sleeping Beauty. What I want from you is the desiderata list: what any adequate principle of this shape — one linking my present credences to the credences I expect my future self to have — has to get right before I can call it adequate. And I want it aimed at a specific purpose: a principle good enough to tell me whether some particular case is a violation, not a taxonomy of every position anyone's ever taken.

For each item on the list give me the case or consideration behind it, not just the label. Things I'd expect to be in there, which you should work out properly rather than take from me:

- the cases it has to handle. Sleeping Beauty and other self-locating setups; the case where you know your future self will be drunk, concussed or simply knackered; the case where you know they'll have strictly better evidence; the case where you know they'll have worse evidence; the case where they'll have simply forgotten something you know now; and the case where you're certain you're about to be talked into something by an argument you can't currently see through.
- the case I actually care about, which I dont think is standard: you're not certain whether your future self is more or less reliable than you are now — you think they'll have better evidence, but you also think their reasoning may get worse under time pressure. So you can neither just defer to them nor just ignore them. What does the principle say here, and is there a version that weights your future self's reliability instead of treating them as authoritative? If there is, is that still reflection or is it a different principle that needs its own justification?
- the distinctions it can't collapse: better informed versus better at reasoning; a constraint on coherence versus a claim about accuracy; deferring to a future you versus deferring to an expert or another person, and why those get treated differently if they do; being certain what you'll believe versus merely expecting it; and whether the principle is only about degrees of belief or needs a counterpart for outright belief.
- the background results it has to stay consistent with: the probability calculus, conditionalisation, and whatever the Dutch-book argument actually delivers — my suspicion is that it delivers less than people assume, and in particular that I don't know whether it forces reflection on its own or only in combination with something else. Also the awkward fact that any diachronic constraint of this kind will either bind agents who can't compute the relevant quantities, or be quietly restricted to idealised ones.

Then the part I most want. Which items on that list can't be satisfied together? My suspicion is that 'has to apply whenever you're certain what your future credence will be' and 'must not require deference to a future self you've got reason to think is epistemically worse off' can't both hold, and that a lot of writing on this quietly drops one of them without saying so. If that's right, say so, say which one you'd drop and what you'd put in its place, and say what the principle stops being able to do once you drop it. Also tell me which single requirement does the most work in ruling candidates out — I'd guess the certainty-about-your-future-credence one, but I want to be told rather than confirmed.

Mark which requirements are non-negotiable and which are just commitments of one camp. And finish with a short verdict: is this a norm of rationality at all, or only a norm of internal consistency for people who've already bought into a particular picture of what credences are for? If it's only the latter, tell me what's lost.
