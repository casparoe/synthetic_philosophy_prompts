I'm a novelist, about 60k words into a manuscript where two brothers, Ewan and Tam, get into a bidding war in a pub over a tenner that Tam puts on the table one slow Tuesday. The rules of the game: bids in 50p steps, highest bidder wins the tenner, and the second-highest bidder still has to pay what they bid. I've got alot of the scene itself written, but I want the chapter to open with a short poem — something Ewan might have scrawled years later — and I want the poem's form to do to the reader what the auction does to the two of them, rather than just describing it from outside.

So here's what I'm after. A poem of maybe 16–20 short lines, built as a cascade: each line picks up the last word or phrase of the line before and carries it forward while adding something new, so nothing ever comes to rest, the way no bid can ever be the last one. Let the actual numbers surface every couple of lines (50p, a pound, two, four, eight...) so you feel the doubling, and give me a refrain along the lines of "one more" that keeps coming back but shifts meaning each time it does. The thing I really care about getting right, and the reason I don't want a poem about two blokes who are just definately irrational: sunk costs are sunk, and the real trap here is structural. Whatever his brother's standing bid is, one more 50p wins the tenner for barely over that bid, whereas quitting means paying his own last bid and getting nothing for it — and those two numbers always fall on the same side of zero, no matter how high they've climbed. At some point bidding again genuinely is the better move, and that's what makes it a trap instead of a mistake. I want the poem to hold both halves at once: the speaker can explain the structure perfectly and bids anyway, and Tam should be audible in there as being in exactly the same position, not as a fool. If there's a line to spare for a third punter who wanders in and joins at six quid, I'd take it — fresh entry should feel like it deepens the hole rather than breaks it. And let the last line be a bid, or else the moment he sees that the one move costlier than bidding again is stopping.

It doesn't need to rhyme throughout; if a rhyme keeps the cascade's momentum, use it, don't force it. It should be accurate enough that someone who's read the dollar-auction literature wouldn't wince, and it should still land as a poem for someone who's never heard of any of this. After the poem, as the last line, give me one sentence on what the form is doing, starting with "Summary:".
