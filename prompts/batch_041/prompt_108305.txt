I'm giving a paper at a departmental seminar next month. It's nominally an economics department, but the paper is on Keynes's conception of the good life, so the room will be mixed: mostly economists who know the General Theory and the Treatise inside out and think of Keynes as a policy theorist with a colourful biography, plus two intellectual historians who have actually read the Memoir Club material, and one philosopher who works on early analytic ethics and has read Moore properly. My thesis is that Keynes held a substantive and fairly stable conception of the good life rather than a set of undergraduate enthusiasms he later outgrew. Roughly: economic activity is instrumental; the point of it is to make possible intrinsically valuable states of mind, friendship, love, aesthetic enjoyment, contemplation; and treating the accumulation of wealth as an end in itself is a corruption rather than a neutral preference. I argue this runs from the memoir he read to the Memoir Club in the late thirties (published posthumously) through to the 1930 essay on economic possibilities for our grandchildren, where the fifteen-hour week and the problem of how to use the resulting leisure are the payoff, and that his later practical work isn't inconsistent with it.
