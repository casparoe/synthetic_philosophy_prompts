I'm a third-year student in an epistemology course, and I've hit a snag with the textbook's treatment of logical omniscience that my study partner and I cannot resolve. The book introduces the problem by noting that formal models of knowledge seem to imply that any knower knows all the logical consequences of what it knows, and it presents this as obviously absurd for humans. Fine. But earlier in the term, in the same book's opening chapters on knowledge, we were taught a principle I took to be beyond dispute: if you know that P, and you know that P implies Q, then you know that Q. The book used it constantly, and it felt less like a substantive thesis than like a rule of the game. Now it looks like exactly the kind of thing the later chapter calls absurd.

To make matters worse, my study partner used the principle to argue that a computer running a proof search "knows" every theorem it can derive from its axioms. The machine knows the axioms, and it knows that the axioms imply the theorem, so by the principle it knows the theorem — and there is nothing mysterious about that, just a machine doing what knowledge always does. I think this is wrong, but every way I try to say why keeps sounding like I am denying something I was taught to accept. Either the computer does not really know the axioms, or it does not really know that they imply the theorem, or the principle is false. None of these feels right, and the fact that the textbook seems to be both the source of the principle and the source of the complaint about omniscience makes me suspect I am missing a distinction rather than a premise.

What I want is a careful sorting of the precise claims that principle could be making, arranged from the near-trivial to the ambitious, with a verdict on each: which readings are true but say almost nothing, which are substantive and defensible, which are actually false, and which ones I would have to accept in order to keep applying the principle in the way my course does. I would like to know, for each reading, what it says about the computer, and in particular whether any reading makes the computer case a genuine counterexample to the principle rather than merely a limit on the machine. I am also unsure whether "implies" in the principle is supposed to mean material implication, some stronger notion of entailment, or something else, and whether that choice changes which reading is available. Finally, tell me which single reading the textbook is most likely running when it moves from the principle to the omniscience objection — because if the two chapters are using the same sentence in different senses, that would explain why the objection feels like a betrayal of something I was told to rely on.
