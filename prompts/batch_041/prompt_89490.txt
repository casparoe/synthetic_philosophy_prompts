Essay: Anchor-Relative Transitivity and the Spectrum Argument

The spectrum argument begins with a series of small improvements. World A is better than World B because A has slightly more of some good or slightly less of some bad. B is better than C for the same reason. After enough steps, we reach World Z, which seems clearly worse than A, yet the chain of pairwise comparisons suggests that Z is better than A if betterness is transitive. The standard options are to deny the small improvements, deny transitivity, or accept some form of incommensurability or incomparability.

I want to defend a fourth option: transitivity is not the culprit; the culprit is the assumption that betterness has a single, context-independent extension across the whole spectrum. On my view, each pairwise comparison is made relative to an anchor, a local baseline that fixes which dimension of value is salient and how much of it counts as a small improvement. A is better than B relative to anchor a1; B is better than C relative to anchor a2; and so on. The anchors shift as the spectrum progresses. The argument's conclusion only follows if all the comparisons share the same anchor, which they do not. So the spectrum does not show that betterness is intransitive; it shows that betterness is anchor-relative. Within a fixed anchor, betterness is transitive. Across anchors, the predicate behaves like a context-sensitive comparative such as tall: a tall mouse is not tall for a building, and transitivity fails across contexts, but not within a fixed comparison class.

The hard question is whether this is a real solution or a verbal trick. If every apparent intransitivity can be explained by positing a new anchor, the account risks being unfalsifiable. I try to constrain anchors by tying them to the dimensions of value that are actually at issue in a comparison, and by requiring that anchor shifts be licensed by changes in the salient dimension. But I am unsure whether this constraint is strong enough, and whether the analogy to tall is misleading because betterness is supposed to be an all-things-considered relation, whereas tallness is not.

(3) Is the analogy to context-sensitive adjectives like tall apt, or does disanalogy undermine the account? (4) What is the weakest premise in the essay, and what would you revise?
