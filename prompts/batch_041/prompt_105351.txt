OK so I'm a journalist — I cover science and ideas for a general interest magazine, which means half my job is finding the spot where a movie turns out to be doing philosophy without anybody noticing. I've got a piece brewing and I want you to do the heavy lifting on the reading, so to speak.

The movie is Amadeus. The idea is that whole cluster about who gets to be great and what we do about it: on one end the American Dream, where the winner is the hero, the self-made man is the saint, and the rest of us are supposed to celebrate rather than seethe; on the other end the Law of Jante, that scandanavian code from a 1930s novel — ten rules, every one of them some version of don't think you're anything special, don't think you're better than us, don't think anyone cares about you — plus tall poppy syndrome, the Australian and New Zealand version, where the group just cuts the successful person down to size. I don't want a survey of the three. I definately want you to read Salieri as a case and tell me which one he is, because I honestly can't decide and I think the whole piece turns on it.

Here's my problem in a nutshell. Salieri isn't the herd. He's the court composer, he's got the job, the Emporer's ear, the respectable career — he's an insider, and the man he wants destroyed is the outsider with no manners who keeps getting the better reviews. That looks like the opposite of a leveling norm. Unless you read his boyhood vow as a contract: God, I'll give you my chastity and my industry and my humility, and you make me famous. That's the American Dream with a deity on the other side of the deal, where talent is a wage you earn by working for it. And the movie's engine is that the deal is void, because Mozart never earned anything — he just has it, and he's a giggling little vulgarian who hasn't held up his end of anything. So what I keep circling is whether Amadeus is quietly an argument that the American Dream, when it fails, manufactures exactly the person who cuts the tall poppy down.

Then I want you to bring in some machinery. Flagging up front that I may be misremembering names:

1. There's an anthropologist — Boehm, I think, the reverse dominance hierarchy guy — leveling mechanisms in egalitarian forager bands, where the group uses ridicule and gossip and ostracism to keep any upstart from climbing over them. Is tall poppy syndrome that? And if it is, does Amadeus dramatize it or refute it? Because Salieri doesn't want Mozart brought down to the level of the pack, he wants to be Mozart. That disanalogy is the honest core of the piece, so please don't paper over it.

2. Envy versus resentment. Rawls, or somebody, draws the line: resentment is a moral feeling, a response to an actual wrong, while envy is just wanting what the other guy has and isn't a moral complaint at all. Which one is Salieri? My hunch is neither, and that's why the movie lasts. He doesn't want Mozart's talent, he wants a world in which his love of music counts for something. Which sounds like Scheler on ressentiment — you can't have the good, so you quietly rewrite the value system until it turns out the good wasn't good — except Salieri never rewrites anything. He keeps the values fully intact and hates himself inside them. So is Amadeus a refutation of the ressentiment story? Tell me.

3. Leveling down — the objection that making the tall poppy shorter doesn't help anyone and is still a loss. Is the Jante norm ever defensible as more than leveling down? I'd like a serious attempt at the strongest case for it (social insurance, protection against hubris, cohesion, whatever you've got), because the piece will be smug if it just assumes the Nordic norm is spite.

Scenes I want you to actually use: the boy's vow in the church; the welcome march sequence, where Mozart takes Salieri's little tune and improves it in front of him and is delighted about it; the Emperor's verdict that the Abduction has too many notes, which is the actual cut in this movie and comes from the top rather than the herd; the masked man and the Requiem commission, where Salieri decides to steal the work and pass it off as his own; and the framing device, the whole thing being a confession told to a priest by a man in an asylum, ending with him absolving all the mediocrities of the world. That last beat is doing something I can't quite name and I'd like you to name it.

What I want at the end is the part argument alone can't give me: what does the film add to this debate that a philosophy paper wouldn't? Where does the reading break down — where is Salieri just a jealous man and my Jante/American Dream frame is a stretch I'm imposing on him? And be honest about the play versus the movie if the difference matters.

Ground rules. No reading lists, no citations, no see-such-and-such-page-numbers — I can't look anything up and neither can you, so just make the argument. Don't recap the plot, I've seen it maybe fifteen times. American English please, I'm filing to a US desk. And if I've got a name or an attribution wrong, correct it inline and move on, don't make me feel bad about it.
