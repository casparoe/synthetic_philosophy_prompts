I am trying to judge accounts of large historical changes -- demographic collapse, the abandonment of settlements, the fall of states -- that give a central role to non-human causes: pathogens, volcanic forcing, shifts in climate. Before I weigh any particular account, I want the specification in full: what must a theory of non-human historical causation deliver to count as adequate for the purpose of writing causal narrative?

Lay out the desiderata, each with the case or consideration that motivates it. I want the cases it has to get right (the same eruption or the same pathogen producing opposite outcomes in neighbouring societies; a non-human cause that is necessary but not sufficient; a so-called natural disaster that is partly a product of human decisions); the distinctions it cannot collapse (cause versus agent; proximal versus distal; explaining the timing of an event versus its magnitude or direction; an exogenous nature versus one already shaped by human action); and the results it must stay consistent with, both from the natural sciences and from what we know about how institutions and choices mediate shocks. Mark which requirements are non-negotiable and which are the prejudices of a particular camp, and tell me if any pair cannot be jointly satisfied -- that is the part I most want to see.

One proposed criterion I would like you to rule on: that a non-human cause counts as a historical actor only if it alters the set of options open to the people involved. Say whether that belongs on the list as a genuine requirement or is doing illegitimate work.

Then take a definite position: which single requirement does the most to rule out accounts that treat a volcano or a germ as a self-sufficient historical actor, and where does that leave the strongest version of the case for non-human agency?
