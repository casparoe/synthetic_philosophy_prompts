Ok so I'm a third-year PhD student working on decision theory and the axiology of the far future, and I keep running into the same move in reading group. Whenever someone raises expected value fanaticism — the thing where, if you maximize EV, you'll hand over one unit of value for a 1-in-10^20 shot at 10^40 units, and this seems to eat your entire life, every purchase, every hour — someone says "yeah but that's just Pascal's wager, and we all know how that one goes." Same structure, they say: a sure small loss traded for a tiny chance at an astronomically huge payoff, and since we all find the wager absurd we should find the fanatic absurd too. One guy in the group is convinced the wager and the mugging are the same animal and that the whole fanaticism literature is people rediscovering that betting on God is silly.

I want you to audit that analogy properly, because I half-suspect it's doing more work than it's entitled to. Start with the correspondence table: in the wager, what plays the role of the mugger, the tiny probability, the enormous payoff, the sure cost, the unverifiability, the one-shotness. Then the places the mapping breaks. Two things I'm especially hung up on:

(a) The wager's payoff is supposed to be infinite (that's the version everyone has in mind), and its whole punch is that any positive probability times infinity beats any finite cost — so the probability drops out of the calculation entirely and you get domination, not a tradeoff. In the fanaticism cases the numbers are finite, the expectation is well-defined, and the magnitudes still do real work. So does that mean the wager's failure, if it fails, is a failure of *infinity* rather than a failure of fanaticism? And if so, is the analogy transferring a verdict from a case where the weird thing is the infinity to a case that doesn't have it?

(b) There's one God-proposition but there are lots of tiny-probability opportunities in the world, and a fanatic who takes every one of them can be bled dry. The wager has no analogue of that, so the analogy might break exactly where fanaticism bites hardest. I want to know whether that matters or whether I'm making too much of it.

Also tell me what the wager picture quietly imports that the fanaticism case doesn't have. My guess is: a forced choice rather than a local one, a bet on a whole life rather than on a single action, and a probability that's unassignable rather than a number — but check me.

Then give me a verdict. Does "it's just Pascals wager" actually support the conclusion people draw from it, or is the analogy carrying the conclusion the whole time? And if the analogy is bad, is there a better one that does the same work — something that captures the real discomfort without borrowing the infinity?

Be committal about this, don't just lay out the ways it could go — tell me where you land and why. If you think the analogy survives and I'm overthinking it, just say so.
