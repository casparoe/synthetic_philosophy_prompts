I am assembling materials for a graduate seminar on the metaphysics of mind, and I need one case built to order. The point of the case is to test whether the dispute over whether dreams are experiences is a genuine disagreement about a fact or a clash between two standards for settling the question. For the exercise, take the two positions as follows, treating the gloss as stipulative rather than exegetical: I am not asking whether either writer would endorse every clause, and no textual support is needed or wanted.

Malcolm's position: dreaming is not a species of experiencing. What a person dreamed is fixed by what they are, on waking, prepared to report or to recall having been through; there is no other route to the fact of the matter, first-person or third-person. So a dream has no location or duration in physical time, a dreamer cannot be mistaken about what they dreamed, and no one else can check up on a dream.

Dennett's position: whether dreams are experiences is an empirical question about what occupies the causal role of an experience in the sleeper's life, to be settled by evidence about what happens in sleep rather than by the logic of the concept; evidence independent of the waking report is admissible and may be decisive; and the possibility that the remembered narrative is assembled at the moment of waking is one of the empirical possibilities to be weighed, not a refutation.

Construct a single case meeting all five of the following specifications.

1. Ordinary and non-technological: an ordinary bedroom or a sleep laboratory, with no fictional device, no drug, no disorder or neurological condition, no deception by any party, no telepathy or shared dreaming, and a normal adult sleeper.
2. It must supply two independent items of evidence about that sleeper on that night: one available only through the sleeper's own waking testimony, the other available to a third party without it.
3. The two items must bear on the same question in incompatible ways. The incompatibility must not be dissolved by the reply that the sleeper had two dreams in succession, unless that reply costs whoever makes it something they are otherwise committed to; nor may it be dissolved by appeal to poor recall, since the waking report is given immediately on waking and confidently.
4. The verdicts must be generated by the two positions rather than built into the scenario: the case may not assert that an experience did or did not occur during sleep, may not assert anything about the sleeper's brain beyond what an ordinary observer could record, and may not settle the question by fiat in either direction.
5. It must be tellable in a single paragraph, without naming any study, experiment, or author other than Malcolm and Dennett, and about a subject an ordinary person could have been.

Give the result as a numbered list of exactly five items, with no introduction, no conclusion, and no headings: (1) the case; (2) the verdict Malcolm's position yields, with the steps that yield it; (3) the verdict Dennett's position yields, with the steps that yield it; (4) the single feature of the case that does the work, and what becomes of the case if that feature is removed or reversed; (5) how a defender of whichever verdict fares worse would attack the case. Keep each item as brief as its content allows, and none longer than a short paragraph.
