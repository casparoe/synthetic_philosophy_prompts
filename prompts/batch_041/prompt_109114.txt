I'm workshopping a short piece on internal vs. external reasons for a reading group and I'd like someone to tear into it before I hand it over. Here's the draft — it's rough.

*Internalism about reasons says that whether you have a reason to do something depends on what's already in your motivational set — your desires, projects, commitments, whatever actually moves you. You have a reason to do a thing only if you could arrive at the conclusion that you should do it by a sound deliberative route starting from where you already are. Call that the source condition.*

*The standard objection is that this makes everything hostage to what people happen to care about. The person who genuinely doesn't care whether anyone else suffers comes out as having no reason to stop. That seems backwards; we want to say the cruel person has a reason whether or not they're moved by it.*

*My attempt at a reply. Internalism comes in two versions that get run together. One is a claim about which reasons there are: only considerations reachable from your motivational set are reasons at all. The other is a claim about what a reason ascription is doing: when we say someone has a reason, we're saying something about the light in which their own commitments cast a given action. Only the first version has the counterintuitive consequence. The second is perfectly compatible with the cruel person having a reason, so long as we're not asking that reason to do explanatory work — to explain why they act, or why they'd be irrational if they didn't.*

*So my verdict: internalism is true as a thesis about the reasons that can figure in the explanation of action, and false as a thesis about all the normative reasons there are. The interesting question, then, isn't internalism vs. externalism but which job you're hiring the word 'reason' to do.*
