I am assembling a set of counterfactual intellectual-history exercises, and I would like you to work through one. The subject is Jeremy Bentham; the development I want him to confront is contemporary suffering-focused ethics — the family of positions, largely a twentieth- and twenty-first-century growth, that treats the reduction of suffering as the central, or even the sole, aim of ethics. Bentham is a useful anchor here because several of his commitments cut in different directions when the questions get hard. He was a hedonist who treated pleasure and pain as the only intrinsic good and evil; he proposed an additive calculus that weighs pleasures and pains by intensity, duration, certainty, and the like; he insisted that each person's interest counts equally, no one's happiness weighted above another's; and he held that the capacity to suffer, rather than the capacity to reason or speak, is what makes a being's interests morally relevant — the point he pressed on behalf of animals. Each of these is familiar on its own. What I want to know is what happens when they are pulled apart by questions he never faced.

Please give me your best reconstruction of Bentham's considered position on the following, treating it as an exercise in disciplined inference from his actual commitments rather than an occasion for biography.

(a) Negative versus classical utilitarianism. If the disvalue of suffering were lexically prior to the value of positive well-being — if no quantity of pleasure could outweigh even one instance of extreme suffering — would Bentham accept that as a refinement of his principle, or reject it as a betrayal of it? His calculus is additive and therefore seems unable to represent a lexicographic priority of one kind of experience over another. Does that arithmetic commit him to rejecting the priority, or does the asymmetry already present in his psychology — his sense that pain, not the mere absence of pleasure, is what drives and ought to drive action — leave room for it?

(b) A puzzle I would like you to resolve on his behalf. Imagine a dial that, if turned, would abolish all intense suffering in the world but, as a side effect, also abolish all intense pleasure, leaving every creature in a state of mild, uninterrupted contentment: no agony, but also no rapture, no great love, no ecstasy. Turning it raises no one's suffering and lowers no one's pleasure below the mild baseline. Would Bentham turn it? Now suppose a second dial traded in the opposite direction: it would leave all existing suffering untouched but multiply by a large factor the number of intensely happy lives. Would he regard the two dials as commensurable at all? I care less about which way he would flip a switch than about what the answer reveals concerning whether his hedonism is symmetric between pleasure and pain.

(c) The population dimension. A recurring move in contemporary suffering-focused ethics is to argue that bringing a happy person into existence is not obviously a benefit, since there is no one who is made better off by it, whereas sparing someone from suffering clearly benefits a person who exists. Would Bentham's equal-weighting rule and his hedonism push him toward this asymmetry or away from it? On his own terms, would he treat the creation of additional happy lives as itself a good?

(d) Wild animal suffering. Bentham extended moral concern to animals precisely because they can suffer. If he took the scale of suffering in wild nature seriously, would his calculus drive him toward intervening in nature, or would other parts of his outlook — his gradualism, his legalistic cast of mind, his respect for settled arrangements — hold him back?

I would like the answer to reason from his stated principles wherever it can, and to flag clearly where it is extrapolating beyond them. Do not give me a bibliography or a reading list; I am after the argument, not the sources. For each part, tell me what would change your mind — which consideration about Bentham's texts, or about the structure of the problem, would lead you to a different verdict — and where your reconstruction is most likely to be wrong.
