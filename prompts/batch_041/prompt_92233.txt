I am a retired physiologist who has lately taken up philosophy, and I am trying to settle what I actually believe about how many subjects there are in a split-brain case. I would rather begin from my verdicts on concrete cases and look for the principle behind them than adopt a theory first.

Here is what I find myself saying.

1. After the operation, a word is flashed to the left visual field. The patient's left hand reaches for the matching object, but he tells me, sincerely, that he saw nothing. One subject: a single person, part of whose processing he cannot report.

2. The same patient, shown one picture to the left field and a different one to the right, has both hands reach at once for the two different pictures, with no sign of conflict or surprise. Here I am strongly drawn to saying two subjects — two streams of preference, each working a hand, neither owning the other.

3. When his left hand does something he did not will, and the speaking hemisphere promptly invents a reason for it, I return to one subject: a single narrator making the best of a divided body.

4. A patient who volunteers that he feels "as if there are two of us in here" still strikes me as one subject offering a report about a division, not as two subjects reporting.

5. Two ordinary people across my desk are, plainly, two subjects.

My tentative principle is that there are as many subjects as there are streams of consciousness, each able to support its own experiences; on that principle case 2 is right and cases 1, 3, and 4 are wrong. I cannot bring myself to give up 1, 3, and 4. I also notice that cases 1 and 2 both involve two streams of processing, yet I answer them differently.

Is there a principle, or a small set of them, that delivers all five verdicts? If none does, which verdict should I abandon, and why that one rather than another? Please state your principle precisely, name the case that forces each part of it, say what it implies for cases I have not considered — a patient whose hemispheres stay joined by a thin band of fibres that lets a little information through, so that the division is partial; and a case I can only imagine, in which the connection could be switched on and off at will, so that the number of subjects might change with the switch — and tell me honestly which of my five verdicts it cannot accommodate.
