Me and my mate Dan have just spent most of a Sunday arguing about the bit at the end of Turing's 1950 Mind paper — the ninth objection, the one about extra-sensory perception — and I've come out of it wanting to build a whole seminar around it, because it's the part everyone skips and I think it's actually where the interesting trouble is. Dan reckons it's an embarassing footnote that Turing should have had the nerve to cut. I reckon it's the one place in the paper where Turing admits his test rests on a presupposition he can't defend, and then sort of shrugs and carries on anyway. I want my students to feel that, not just be told it.

So design me the activity.

Setting: upper-level undergrad seminar, "Minds and Machines", 16 students, one 90-minute session, ordinary seminar room, no lab, no budget, phones allowed, chairs and tables movable, one whiteboard. I can recruit two or three colleagues or postgrads as confederates if needed, but I'd rather not depend on more than that.

What I want them to walk out feeling: that the imitation game is not a test of a machine. It's a test of a machine *in a world*, and its verdict depends on facts about that world that the test itself never mentions. Turing sees this because of telepathy, and his worry runs in both directions. If telepathy is real, an interrogator might be able to pick out the human by a route the machine simply doesn't have — so the machine fails for a reason that has nothing to do with thinking. Or the machine might get its answers telepathically from the interrogator, in which case it passes, but the thing it's doing isn't the thing we wanted to test. Either way the test's authority is conditional on a contingent empirical fact about what channels exist in the universe. And Turing can't just wave that fact away, because he seems to have taken the parapsychology research of the 1930s more seriously than a modern reader expects — he says in the paper that the statistical evidence, at least for telepathy, is hard to shrug off, and that it's difficult to rearrange one's ideas to fit it. His proposed fix is to put the interrogator somewhere telepathy can't reach. Which is a stipulation, not a solution, because you can only build that room if you already know how telepathy works.

Here's my rough sketch — improve it, fix it, tell me where it'll break.

**Materials.** Four sets of about 24 pre-printed reply cards (things like "that's an interesting question", "I'd rather not say", "what makes you ask that?", "probably", "I'm not sure I follow", "can you rephrase that?"), plus three blank cards per set. Four clipboards. Pens. A stack of blank answer slips in two subtly different sizes. Four runners (students will do). A whiteboard tally.

**Round 1 (15 min): the clean game.** Four groups of four: one interrogator, two contestants, one runner/observer. Contestants sit in a corner behind a screen or just facing away, answers go back and forth on paper via the runner. One contestant answers as themselves. The other answers only by playing cards from the deck, with the three blanks as their only free text. Nobody knows which is which. Eight minutes of questions, then the interrogator writes down a guess and a confidence rating. Tally on the board.

**Round 2 (15 min): the same game, quietly compromised.** New roles, same structure. This time the runners have been briefed — ostensibly about "pacing so it feels natural" — to always deliver the machine contestant's answer slip first, by two to four seconds, and to hand the two slips over separately rather than together. The machine's slips also come from the slightly smaller pad. Nobody is told this is a channel. Nobody has broken a rule, because there is no rule about it. Expect accuracy to jump. Collect the confidence ratings again and put them next to round 1's.

Then stop and ask the room what happened. Let them explain it. They'll say the interrogators got better, or that the card-deck contestant got worse, or that they learned the game. Then reveal the delivery order and the paper size. The number on the board was never a fact about the machine.

**Round 3 (10 min): flip the direction.** Run one group in front of everyone. Before the game, the interrogator gets a sixty-second typed exchange with each contestant on a neutral topic — favourite room, that sort of thing — "just to settle nerves". Unknown to the room, the human contestant is the interrogator's flatmate, arranged beforehand, and the machine contestant is someone the interrogator has never met. The interrogator will identify the human instantly and confidently. Everyone will conclude the test works. Reveal: they didn't spot the human, they spotted their flatmate. Now ask them to imagine the channel is telepathy, running between two human minds but not between a human mind and a silicon one. The machine has just failed a test of intelligence for the crime of not belonging to the species that has the channel. That's Turing's worry, and it's why he couldn't just laugh the parapsychologists off.

**The room-building bit (10 min).** Hand everyone a slip: write one sentence describing what you'd have to do to this activity to close the channel you just found. Collect them and read a few out. Every answer will either name the specific channel they've just been shown (useless against a channel nobody has found) or say "control for everything" (empty). Point out that Turing's telepathy-proof room is exactly this, and that he knows it.

**Debrief (25 min).** Map round 1 to the test as designed; round 2 to a result produced by a channel in the world that the test never controlled for, with no cheating anywhere; round 3 to the direction-reversal where the channel favours the human and the machine loses for reasons unrelated to thought; the slips to the emptiness of the stipulation. Close by asking whether a criterion that arrives with an unsecured conditional attached is still a criterion. Don't answer it for them. And do say plainly that Turing doesn't think ESP sinks the test — he thinks the test is fine conditional on a picture of the world, and he's honest that he can't fully secure the condition. That ambivalence is the thing.

**What will predictably go wrong.** Someone will spot the delivery order in round 2 and blurt it out. Don't deny it — ask them to hold it for ten minutes and then hand them the reveal. If it blows early, use that group as the case study and keep going. Some groups will guess right by luck and take credit for insight; make sure at least two groups get it wrong so the board stays ambiguous, because the ambiguity is the point. The runners will forget the order at least once, which gives you a null result you can debrief as "you'd have concluded the test was valid" — that's a gift, not a disaster. Someone will moralise: so the test is rubbish then. Resist it, the lesson is conditional, not dismissive. Someone else will say this is just about cheating, and you have to hold the line hard: nobody cheated, nobody knew there was anything to cheat about. And someone will want to talk about chatbots; park it for the last five minutes if there's time.

**Be honest with me about what this can't do.** It can't produce genuine ignorance. My round 2 channel is concealed by a person; in Turing's world the channel, if it's there, is concealed by nobody, because nobody knows about it. Students will reach for "the experimenter was lying", and that's the wrong lesson entirely — Turing's position is worse, because no one is lying and the doubt still won't go away. The facilitator has to say that out loud. It also can't make the empirical question live: in the activity the channel is certain, whereas for Turing it's an open question he can't close, and the philosophical interest is in the unresolved doubt rather than the reveal. And it can't reproduce the oddity of the actual hypothesis — telepathy as his contemporaries imagined it wasn't a reliable mechanical leak, it was patchy and poorly understood, so the analogy is structural rather than literal, and I should flag that in the debrief rather than let them think I've modelled parapsychology. Tell me which of those limits you think I can partly work around and which I just have to own.
