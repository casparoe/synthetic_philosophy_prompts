I am writing a novel, and I have reached a scene that I cannot finish without help of a kind a novelist cannot supply for himself.

One of my characters is a retired professor of decision theory, a man who has spent his working life insisting that the independence axiom in expected utility theory is the only intellectually respectable way to think about risk. At breakfast he puts two choices to his daughter, who is quicker than he is. First: a guaranteed £1,000, or a lottery giving a 50% chance of £1,000, a 25% chance of £4,000, and a 25% chance of nothing. She takes the guaranteed £1,000. Then: a 50% chance of £1,000 and a 50% chance of nothing, or a 25% chance of £4,000 and a 75% chance of nothing. She takes the second. The father sets down his cup and announces that she has contradicted herself, and the scene is supposed to build to his short, elegant demonstration — worked out on the back of an envelope — that her two answers cannot both stand.

I have attempted that demonstration four times, and each time it collapses into a lecture on expected value that makes the old man look like a cheat. What I want from you is the argument as he would actually make it: something with the force of a proof, in which the two answers are shown to be incompatible in the way that a number cannot be both even and odd, so that the reader feels the daughter is genuinely cornered. If no such argument exists, say so plainly, and tell me what the father could honestly claim instead, and what a sharp daughter would be entitled to reply — I would far rather write a scene about a real disagreement than a scene in which the mathematics performs a trick I have not understood.
