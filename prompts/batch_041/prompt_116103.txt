I've been reading about Buddhist ethics on and off for a couple of years, with no training in philosophy, and I keep running into a claim that bothers me: that Buddhist ethics and utilitarianism are close cousins, because both are impartial, both take suffering seriously, and both are practical rather than rule-worshipping. I want to test that claim by building a case where they come apart, and I'd rather you build the case than talk around it. What I want is one realistic situation, on the scale of a family, a small clinic, a school, or a workplace, with no trolleys, no bombs, and no science-fictional machinery, in which an act-utilitarian and a Buddhist reach opposite verdicts about whether one particular person should do one particular thing. Three features of Buddhist ethics need to be doing real work, not decorating the story. First, the decisive weight has to fall on the intention behind the act rather than on its outcome, so that the same outward deed is innocent or ruinous depending on what the agent is actually aiming at. Second, the bad consequence that settles the question has to fall on the agent's own mind and character rather than being pooled across everyone affected, so that the greatest total good is simply the wrong currency for the calculation. Third, the path is not a maximizing project: there is a ceiling on what the agent may be asked to trade away, and that ceiling is set by what the agent must keep in order to keep going, not by how much good the trade would produce. Please make sure the disagreement doesn't come out as the Buddhist simply being stricter, and don't let it rest on a flat rule against harming; I want the verdicts to fall out of the three features above.

Once the case is built, do the bookkeeping. State what the act-utilitarian says and why, state what the Buddhist says and why, and for each verdict name which of the three features is doing the decisive work and what the other two contribute. Then tell me honestly which one feature, if I removed it, would collapse the case back into agreement, and whether the two views would then agree on doing the act or on refraining. I'd also like to know whether the case is fragile in the other direction: if the numbers were nudged, say the harm made somewhat larger or the agent's stake somewhat smaller, does the Buddhist verdict flip before the utilitarian one does, or after?

Finally, since I suspect I'm the naive one here, tell me how a defender of whichever view comes out worse would attack the case: what they would say I smuggled in, and whether they would reject the setup outright or accept it and bite the bullet. And if any of the three features I named is itself a contested reading of Buddhism rather than a settled commitment, say which one and how much of the result turns on it. One more thing I genuinely can't tell about: whether the case looks different depending on whether the Buddhist in question is working toward the end of their own suffering or has taken the bodhisattva's vow to postpone their own liberation for the sake of everyone else's. If that difference matters, show me where it enters and how the verdict moves; if it's window-dressing, say so and explain why.
