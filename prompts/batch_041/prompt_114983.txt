The claim that civilizations are like organisms — born, growing, maturing, and dying — is everywhere in arguments that a civilization's decline is a natural process beyond remedy, so that reform is little more than medicine for old age. I want an audit of that analogy, not a survey of theories of historical cycles.

Do four things. First, give the correspondence table: what plays the role of the organism, its birth, growth, maturation, senescence, death, its organs, its metabolism, its inheritance, its environment, its kin. Second, mark where the mapping breaks, then sort the breaks into those that matter for the conclusion the analogy is used to license and those that are incidental. Third, say what the analogy imports that the target lacks. My own suspicion, which you should check rather than assume: an organism's life is one-way and irreversible, whereas the older cyclical accounts — Polybius on the recurrence of constitutions, Ibn Khaldun on the rise and decay of dynasties — describe recurrence rather than a single lifespan, so the metaphor may be quietly converting a cycle into a one-way trip, and the organic reading sits more naturally with the morphology-of-civilizations tradition (Spengler, Toynbee) than with the cyclical historians. If I have that wrong, say so. Fourth, test a repair I have seen proposed: swap the organism for the species, so that what looks like a civilization's death is really its fission into successor cultures and nothing strictly dies. Does that repair rescue the analogy or change the subject? If some other analogy carries the argument better, name it and say why.

Then give the bottom line on the conclusion itself, and be blunt about the premise of my question: if the inevitability claim is doing all the work and the analogy is mere decoration, or if an audit of this kind simply cannot settle the verdict, tell me so plainly instead of producing a tidy answer.
