I write about science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I am working on an essay about the philosophical roots of contemporary debates over algorithmic governance. I would like your comments on the draft below.

Rudolf Carnap is usually remembered as the great formalizer of the Vienna Circle: the philosopher who wanted to replace metaphysics with the logic of science, who argued for tolerance in the choice of linguistic frameworks, and who treated many philosophical disputes as confusions about language. What is less often recalled is that Carnap worked alongside Otto Neurath, whose proposals for a planned economy were a central reference point in interwar socialist debates, and that Carnap moved in left-wing circles. This suggests a thought experiment. Suppose Carnap were alive today and asked to assess what I call algorithmic price coordination: a public agency runs a computational system that sets shadow prices for essential goods and services, updating them in real time from production and consumption data, while a democratically elected body specifies the objective function and the constraints. The system is not a command economy; it is a market-like mechanism with administered prices and a publicly deliberated welfare function.

My essay argues that Carnap would have been cautiously sympathetic but would have insisted on a sharp distinction between two questions. The first is technical: can the system be designed so that its categories are explicit, its inferences transparent, and its failures corrigible? Here Carnap's demand for explication would lead him to press the designers on how they operationalize need, well-being, and fairness. The second is political: who gets to fix the objective function, and through what procedures can it be revised? Carnap's principle of tolerance, I claim, does not mean that anything goes politically; it means that the choice of framework is a practical decision, not a theoretical truth. He would therefore support experimentation with algorithmic coordination while warning against treating the algorithm's output as a discovery about the good rather than a decision about it.

I also suggest that Carnap would have been more worried about the opacity of machine learning than about central planning as such. His objection to metaphysics was not that it was ambitious but that it was unverifiable; his objection to political mystification would be similar. A system that could not explain its price signals, or that concealed normative choices inside technical jargon, would strike him as a new form of the very obscurantism he spent his career opposing.

I would like comments on this draft. Specifically: Is the reconstruction of Carnap's politics fair, or am I overreading the political implications of his work? Does the analogy between his principle of tolerance and democratic control of the objective function hold up? Where does the essay need more concrete examples? And what would be the strongest Carnapian objection to algorithmic price coordination that I have missed?
