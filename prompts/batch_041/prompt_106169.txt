I am a graduate student in political theory, and I am working through Federalist 78 for a seminar on constitutionalism. I follow Hamilton's general argument that courts must treat the Constitution as fundamental law and void statutes that contradict it, but I am stuck on a specific move he makes when he tries to defuse the objection that judicial review makes the judiciary superior to the legislature. He writes: "It may truly be said to have neither FORCE nor WILL, but merely judgment; and must ultimately depend upon the aid of the executive arm even for the efficacy of its judgments." I understand that he means the judiciary cannot raise armies or spend money, but I do not see how that answers the objection. When a court strikes down a statute, it is not merely interpreting a text; it is overriding the considered will of a legislative majority. How can that be described as an exercise of judgment rather than will? Is Hamilton drawing a distinction between the will to initiate policy and the will to invalidate it, and if so, does that distinction survive scrutiny? I would like an explanation of what Hamilton means by "judgment" here, why he thinks it excludes "will," and whether that exclusion is plausible.
