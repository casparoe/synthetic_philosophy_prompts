I spent 35 years as a physiologist before I retired, and I've spent a chunk of the last few reading philosophy of medicine — mostly because a thing I always took totally for granted at the bench, that when I called something a disease I was just describing a fact about the body, has started to look a lot less obvious to me.

Here's what I'd like you to do. Take the decision by the American Psychiatric Association in the early 1970s to stop classifying homosexuality as a mental disorder — the board vote, the referendum of the membership that backed it up, the whole fight over whether what happened there was science or politics or some mix of the two. I know the basic story, so don't reherse it for me. What I want is for you to run that episode through the disagreement between the naturalists and the normativists about health and disease: the camp that says a disease is an objective biological dysfunction you can read off the organism (Boorse and his biostatistical theory, species-typical functioning, reference classes, all of that), against the camp that says there's no way to draw the line without bringing in values about what's bad or unwanted or disabling — and then the hybrid views, like the idea that a disorder needs both a genuine dysfunction and a harm.

Give me an actual mapping, not a summary. On the naturalist story, what were the psychiatrists really doing when they reclassified it — correcting a mistake, or just relabeling and dressing the change up as a correction? On the normativist story, was it values all the way down, and if so, what made this particular value shift happen when it did and not twenty years earlier? Which of the two views explains the episode better, and where does each one have to fudge something to make it fit? Then flip it around: does this case count as evidence for one side, or is it the kind of thing neither side ever really anticipated and both just absorb after the fact?

One more thing, and I mean this sincerely: if the naturalism/normativism split is too crude to do any honest work on a case like this — if I'm leaning on a distinction that falls apart the second you press on it — then say so and explain why, instead of going ahead and doing the analysis anyway to be polite. I'd much rather find out my framing is confused than get a tidy answer to a bad question. I can take it.
