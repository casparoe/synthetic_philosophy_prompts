Next month I am presenting a paper to the philosophy-of-education seminar of a large teacher-training faculty, and I need a preparation sheet for the discussion that will follow.

The thesis I am defending is this: Locke's Some Thoughts Concerning Education is best read as the applied half of the Essay Concerning Human Understanding — a practical manual for engineering the child's early associations of ideas so that virtue becomes habitual — and, read that way, it offers a more defensible account of moral education than the character-education literature that has unknowingly revived it, at the price of a paternalism about the child's inner life that the modern literature will not name.

My argument runs roughly so. Locke denies innate practical principles, so virtue cannot be drawn out of the child; it has to be built, by custom and repetition, which is why he holds that habits rather than precepts do the work, and why the earliest impressions count for most. The mechanism is the association of ideas, which he treats in the Essay — the chapter was added in the fourth edition, in 1700 — as a source of error: associations laid down in infancy, often by accident and often by servants, which harden into settled temper. Education therefore becomes the deliberate control of what gets associated with what. Some Thoughts itself grew out of letters Locke wrote in 1684 to Edward and Mary Clarke about the upbringing of their son, and was published anonymously in 1693; its recommendations — the hardening of the body, plain diet, learning to read through play rather than compulsion, reasoning with children as rational creatures, and above all a private tutor rather than a school — amount to a technology for producing a particular kind of person.

The audience is not a philosophy department, and I am not worried about the objections a Locke specialist would raise. It is a seminar of about a dozen teacher educators: two early-years specialists trained in Montessori and Reggio-inspired practice, for whom the child's self-directed activity is the first principle; a developmental psychologist who works on attachment and self-regulation and distrusts anything that sounds like training a small child's habits; an early-modern historian who knows Locke's texts and their occasions; a critical pedagogue in the Freire tradition who will ask whose interests a pedagogy of habit serves; and a primary headteacher who will ask what any of this means on Monday morning with thirty children and no tutor.

Write me the Q&A prep sheet. I want the objections I will actually get from this room, ranked by how much damage they would do to the paper rather than by how likely they are, with the ones that are both likely and damaging marked as such; each objection phrased the way that particular person would put it, in their vocabulary and with their concerns showing, not translated back into mine; and for each, a reply I can give in about two minutes without notes plus a candid note on where that reply is weakest or where I am bluffing. Then tell me the two or three things I should concede at the outset rather than let them be dragged out of me; the objection that has genuine force and that I will be tempted to brush aside, with what conceding it would actually cost me; the single reply that has to land for the paper to survive the session; and the one question I am silently hoping will not come up.

Keep it to what this audience could raise and what I could answer from the texts and from my own head. No reading lists, no citations, no references to scholarship I would have to go and check.
