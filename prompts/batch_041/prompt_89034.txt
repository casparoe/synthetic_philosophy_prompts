I am a fourth-year PhD student in cognitive science, and my dissertation is organized around heterophenomenology in roughly the form Dennett gives it. I have come to think the method is not just useful but correct, and I want to know how much of that position is actually exposed rather than merely defended. Let me state the version I hold as sharply as I can.

The method takes as its data the utterances and behavior of subjects, treats them as texts to be interpreted rather than as windows onto anything, and constructs the heterophenomenological world — the subject's own account of what their experience is like — as a theoretical object to be set alongside the rest of the science. I hold three further commitments. First, no investigator has first-person evidential privilege: the difference between my subjects' reports and my own introspective reports is a difference in who is speaking, not in the kind of evidence, and my own reports enter the record as data of exactly the same sort as theirs. Second, once we have a satisfactory account of why subjects say and do what they do, there is no further fact about their experience left over to explain; the apparent remainder is an artifact of sliding from explaining a report to identifying with it. Third, and consequently, the hard problem is not a problem but the price of refusing the method.

What should count against this? I do not want the familiar catalogue recited back to me; I want the considerations that ought to move someone who already accepts the method, ordered by how much they should move me. I am especially interested in three families of defeater. The first is cases in which the reports and the rest of the science pull apart in a way the method cannot adjudicate — including cases where the report is elicited after the fact and is demonstrably shaped by what the subject learns in the interval, so that it is unclear whether we are learning about the experience or about the elicitation. The second is findings suggesting that the heterophenomenological world is partly an artifact of the interpreter's own commitments rather than a discovery about the subject. The third is scope: systems that plausibly have experiences but produce no interpretable texts, and whether the method's silence about them is principled or merely a limitation of its reach.

For each family, say what the heterophenomenologist would have to concede and what they could reasonably deny, and mark where a concession would cost them more than a denial would. Then tell me whether the position as I have stated it risks anything at all, or whether its qualifications have already insulated it from disconfirmation — and if the latter, whether that is a defect of the view or simply what a methodological stance, as opposed to an empirical claim, is like.

For my own part, I suspect I would be most moved by a case in which we have independent, non-report-based grounds for attributing an experience that the subject's reports deny. My worry is that no such case can be described without already assuming what the method denies, and I would like you to tell me whether that worry is right.
