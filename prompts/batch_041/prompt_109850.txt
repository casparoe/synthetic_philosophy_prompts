After thirty-nine years in a chemistry department, I retired and, at the invitation of a former colleague, joined a fortnightly seminar on Hellenistic ethics at our local university's continuing-education programme. I had read one of the popular books on Stoicism the winter before, which is how the subject came to interest me at all. What I want to put to you is an argument that broke out at the fourth meeting and that I have been unable to settle in my own mind since. I should say at once that my sympathies moved during the course of it, and that I shall not tell you where they came to rest, for fear of colouring your reasoning.

The two participants were Helena, a retired teacher of Latin with a strong interest in the ancient sources, and Peter, a practising psychotherapist who has been recommending Stoic exercises to his patients for some years. Neither is a fool, and neither, so far as I could tell, was being unfair to the other. The exchange went roughly as follows.

(1) Helena opened by saying that the phrase in which the whole modern discussion is conducted — the dichotomy of control — is a modern label rather than an ancient one, and that it misdescribes the doctrine it names. Epictetus begins from a division between what is up to us and what is not; but the Stoics held that everything that happens is fated. If so, up to us cannot mean causally within our power. What it marks is the boundary of our agency: our judgements, our impulses, our desires and aversions, the use we make of our impressions. The modern revival, she said, converts a claim about what belongs to us into a claim about what we can affect, and those are not the same claim.

(2) Peter replied that he was happy to grant the philology and that it made no difference to him. If up to us means only attributable to us as agents, then in the causal sense nothing whatever is up to us, and the doctrine is either empty or a version of compatibilism in ancient dress. He said he preferred the three-way division some of the contemporary writers use: things over which we have complete control, things over which we have none, and things over which we have some but not complete control. That, he said, is simply more honest, since he plainly has some influence over whether his patients improve and none whatever over the weather; the two-box version has to cheat, because health, reputation and the like are at least partly responsive to effort.

(3) Helena answered that the middle box is the problem and not the remedy. Everything the training is designed to cure lives in that box: one's standing with one's colleagues, one's adult child's opinion of one, the fate of a book one has written. If a person is told that he has partial control, then keep working at it is licensed, and the attachment is preserved under a new name rather than dissolved. The three-way division does not repair the dichotomy; it quietly restores the very thing the exercises exist to remove.

(4) Peter offered two replies. The first was that her objection is an empirical claim about what the middle category does to people, and that it is not obviously true: a person told he has no control over something he demonstrably influences will either disbelieve the doctrine or wear himself out trying to decide which box a given matter falls into. The second was that her own reading has a difficulty in it. If the Stoics were determinists, and our judgements are themselves caused, then up to us cannot mean uncaused from outside; it must mean something like proceeding through us. He said the ancients appear to have seen this — he recalled the old reply to the lazy argument, that a recovery and the calling of the physician are fated together, so that what is in our power is itself included in fate — and that this is compatibilism by another name. And if compatibilism is good enough to save the ancient doctrine, he did not see why it should not be good enough to save the modern one.

(5) Helena said she was not making a point about psychology. On the Stoic account the sage's judgements are true and the rest of ours are false, and truth is not settled by therapeutic utility. What the modern revival characteristically discards is the whole framework that made those judgements true in the first place — the providential order of the world, and the thesis that virtue alone is good — and what is left is a set of techniques with no theory behind them. That, she said, is not Stoicism continued but Stoicism with its spine removed.

(6) Peter said the cosmology is dead and nobody is obliged to resurrect it, and that if a practice outlives the theory that once supported it, that is a point in the practice's favour rather than against it. He added that true was doing a great deal of work in her last remark, and that he would want to know true by whose lights: what is available to us now is a set of exercises answerable to whether they make a life go better, not to a physics we have abandoned.

They were still at it when the session ended. It has since struck me that they may be arguing about two different things — Helena about what a doctrine means, Peter about what a practice does — and I cannot decide whether that observation dissolves the dispute or merely postpones it.

What I would like from you is a point-by-point adjudication rather than a verdict for one of them overall. For each of the six moves, say whether it is sound, partly sound, or mistaken, and why. Tell me where each of them is right about something the other denies, and what both of them are leaving out. I would also be grateful if you would say whether the two questions they are pursuing — what the doctrine meant and what the practice accomplishes — can be answered independently of one another, or whether one must be settled first. And since I spent my working life in a discipline where a disagreement of this shape would eventually have been settled by measurement, I should like to know whether this one is even the sort of disagreement that admits of being settled, and if so, by what. I am not asking for a bibliography; a sorting-out of the arguments will do.
