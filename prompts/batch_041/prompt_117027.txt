I'm a cognitive science graduate student, and I'm supposed to lead the discussion in a seminar on concepts and category learning. The reading I volunteered for pairs Borges's Funes the Memorious with Bongard problems, and I want help working out a reading that takes both sides seriously. For anyone who hasn't met them: a Bongard problem is a puzzle with twelve small drawings of simple geometric figures, split into a left set of six and a right set of six, where all the figures on one side share some property that the other side lacks, and the solver has to find it. The interesting part is not the perception but the indeterminacy: any finite collection of pictures is compatible with indefinitely many rules, so what you are really doing is guessing which rule the puzzle-maker meant, which makes the task look less like extracting a generalization from data and more like guessing at an intention. The people who study these puzzles disagree about whether the intended answer is the uniquely natural one, the one a cooperative designer would have had in mind, or simply the one that strikes a solver as elegant, and I'd like the reading to keep that disagreement in view rather than settling it by fiat.

Here is why Funes seems to me the perfect counter-case, and I'd like you to test whether that's right. Ireneo Funes, after his fall from a horse, remembers everything he has ever perceived and perceives everything in intolerable detail; he is said to be incapable of generalities or abstractions; he is tormented that the single word dog covers the animal he saw at one moment and the animal he saw a minute later; he asks for Latin books and a dictionary and within days is reciting Pliny from memory; and he passes his bedridden years devising a scheme of enumeration in which every number receives its own arbitrary proper name, a project the narrator tells him is the opposite of a system. He is, it seems to me, a man given every example and no rule — the limiting case of a solver with perfect data and no inductive bias. So: would Funes fail Bongard problems, and if so, is his failure a defect in him or a discovery about what a concept is? Or might he solve them in a way we would not recognize as solving, noticing relations among the panels that the rest of us suppress in order to see them as instances at all? I'd like the reading to say whether concept induction requires forgetting and loss, as the narrator's final reflections suggest, or whether it requires something else that Funes lacks and that has nothing to do with storage — a sense of relevance, or an assumption that the world is addressed to us.

Please be honest in both directions. Where the story resists the reading, say so: the narrator's own memory of Funes is selective and reconstructive, Funes does after all invent a system, and the story is itself a generalization about a singular case. Where the Bongard framing distorts the story, say that too, and tell me what the story adds to the philosophical debate that the puzzles alone cannot — I don't need a survey of the literature or citations, and please don't quote the story, since I'll be working from memory along with everyone else in the room.
