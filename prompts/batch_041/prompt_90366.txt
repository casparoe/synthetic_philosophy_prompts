My opening. I think your case is two arguements wearing one coat, and the famous one is doing none of the work. There's the no-privileged-now arguement: a real passage of time would need a global now, a single world-wide slicing of events into past and future, and relativity hands every observer their own slicing, so the passage was never in the world to begin with. Then there's the time-travel arguement: in your rotating universe a worldline can loop back, you can in principle revisit an event you've already lived through, and if the past is still there to be visited it never passed away. Everyone remembers the loops because they're vivid. My suspicion is that the loops are either redundant or circular — redundant if the missing now already does the job, circular if "coming back" quietly presupposes the very becoming you're denying. So take my test case seriously and don't reach for the loops. Imagine a universe with no closed timelike curves at all, every worldline open, nothing ever meets itself — but still no privileged simultaneity: observers in relative motion disagree about which events are happening now, and the world itself prefers no foliation. Is time unreal there too? If it is, then the rotating universe is a distraction, your whole case rests on the no-global-now point, and I want to know why the absence of a preferred slicing is a fact about time rather than a fact about our bookkeeping. If time is real there and only dies when you can loop, then tell me exactly what the loops buy you that the missing now didn't already give you.
