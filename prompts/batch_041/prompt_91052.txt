I was covering a criticism panel at a literary festival last month and ended up at the bar with two of the panelists while they argued about whether you can get an aesthetic judgment from somebody else. I write about art and ideas, I've been in this fight before, but I couldnt decide who was winning, so I stayed out of it and took notes instead. I want a ruling.

Dara, a freelance critic: she'd hated one particular album for years, and now she thinks it's great, and the only thing that changed is that a friend kept telling her it was great. Mal, who writes about philosophy of art, said that doesn't count. A verdict you take on someone's say-so isn't yours. If the only thing she can say for it is that her friend said so, she doesn't know it's good, she just believes it.

Dara: the friend never argued for it. He said, put your ear on what the drummer's doing under the second verse, that's the whole record. So what she got from him was a pointer about where to look, and the judgment was hers once she could actually hear it.

Mal: real distinction, doesn't help. If she trusted him about which feature is the one that matters, she's trusting him about what's valuable, and that's the aesthetic part. And she had no reason to think that bass line was worth attending to except that he said so. If he'd pointed her at something worthless she'd have been just as convinced.

Dara: the trust wasn't blind, she knows this guy's track record with her taste, and once she heard it she had a reason of her own, because the thing was right there in the music. Testimony got her to the door, the listening did the justifying.

Mal: then why is the testimony doing anything at all? If the listening does the work, she could have got there on her own just by paying attention, and the friend is an alarm clock, and an alarm clock doesn't testify. But if the listening only works because she'd already been told to hear that bass line as the point rather than as a mistake or as nothing, then part of her justification is his say-so after all.

Dara: maybe the line is between hearing a feature and hearing it as valuable, and you can't draw that line before you've heard it. And everybody's attention was shaped by somebody. If that's disqualifying then nobody's taste is their own.

Go move by move and tell me who's right at each step, not just overall — rate each one from clearly mistaken to clearly right and say why. Two specific things I want: is Mal's alarm-clock-or-priming fork exhaustive, or is there a third option where the friend's remark did something to Dara's perceptual abilities that's neither a reason she inherited nor a nudge she could've done without? And what are the two of them both missing? Skip the history of the debate, I don't need to know who said what when, I need the argument sorted out on its own terms.
