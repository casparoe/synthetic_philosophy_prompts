I would like to spend a few turns arguing with Gödel about time, and I want you to argue his side of it. Speak in the first person as Gödel, defending the conclusion he drew from his rotating-universe solution: the cosmos of rotating dust in which a traveler who journeys far enough out from the center of rotation can return to his own past, and in which the events of the world admit no consistent global division into earlier and later. Hold the position that this solution, together with the fact that the presence or absence of an objective lapse of time can never be settled by any observation, shows that the passage of time belongs to the observer rather than to the world, and that what is real is a four-dimensional order of events with no privileged present moving along it. Stay in character until I say stop; if I ask a factual question about the physics, answer it as Gödel would, but do not step outside the role to tell me what the literature says about the problem. Be formidable rather than accommodating: concede a point only when it is genuinely forced on you, and never give up the main conclusion. Write in continuous prose, a few paragraphs at most, the way one would write a letter, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables. Do not mention any titles, authors, dates, or page numbers, and do not quote from anything; I want the argument, not the bibliography. Two things I would rather you avoid. Do not rest the case on the relativity of simultaneity by itself, since I know that move and I want to see what the rotating solution is supposed to add to it. And do not reach for quantum mechanics, quantum gravity, or any other speculative physics. Here is my opening. You say that if the passage of time were something real it could not depend on how the matter of the universe happens to be arranged, since then it would be a mere contingency of our particular cosmos; but by that standard rotation is unreal too, because your cosmos turns and ours does not, and nobody concludes that rotation is a projection of the observer. Consider, then, one of your own observers, who lives out his whole life near the axis and never makes the long outward journey that would close a loop. Everything he can learn without making that journey tells him that the difference between what has already happened and what has not is as definite as it is for anyone else. What is missing from his life that you claim is missing from the universe, and how can the absence of a global lapse make his own past and future unreal when nothing he could ever do would reveal to him that anything was wrong?
