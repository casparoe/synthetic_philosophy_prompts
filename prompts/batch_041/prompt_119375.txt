Ok so I'm a novelist, and I've had this one sitting in a drawer for two years and I can't get it to work, so I want you to just write the whole thing for me so I can watch it fail in some interesting way. Write me the short story. Here's the set-up, and then there's a thing it has to get right, because if it gets that wrong the whole thing is dead on arrival.

The set-up. A woman in her forties. Her father is dying, six weeks maybe, and he's still lucid, and he's still himself, which is the problem. Years back he did something to her. You pick it, but keep it real and not lurid — a betrayal with money in it, or a promise, the kind of thing where he never once said sorry and never will, and where he genuinely does not believe he did anything wrong. Her younger brother needs money for a medical thing, and needs it soon. The father arranged all this while he was still well enough to arrange things, and the arrangement is this: she gets everything, all of it, if on a set date she comes to the house, sits down with him, and reads him out loud a letter she has written herself. The letter has to say what she actually feels. And it has to be a letter of forgiveness. No lawyer in the room, no witness, nothing to cheat. Just him, her, the letter, and the fact that if it isn't true he will know, because he's known her her whole life.

Here's the thing I keep circling, and it's why I can't write it. There's this problem in philosophy, I forget whose, called the wrong kind of reasons. Roughly: some reasons to have an attitude bear on whether the attitude is deserved, and some don't. A gun to your head is a reason to admire a man, and it's a real reason in some sense, but it's the wrong kind — it has nothing to do with him being admirable. And forgiving seems to be one of those attitudes you can only have for the right kind of reason. You can't forgive somebody in order to get something, because the second that's your reason it isn't forgiveness anymore. Same way you can't be ordered to be spontaneous. Wich means this arrangement is either impossible or it's the only thing that could ever work, and I honestly dont know which.

What I want the story to do. She tries. On the page, over about a week, she tries to get herself onto the right kind of reason. She rereads old cards and school reports, she goes back through the house looking for a version of him she can use, she tries to remember him before, she tries arguing herself into it the way you'd argue yourself into a maths problem. She tries the trick of doing it for her own sake — for her peace, so she can sleep — and I think that one's also the wrong kind, but she doesn't know that. And she revises the letter. I want the letter in the story more than once, in versions, so we can watch what changes and what she's finally willing to put in her own handwriting. The revisions are the plot. The story is mostly her drafting, and the house, and her brother on the phone.

The rules. Close third on her, past tense, no flashback longer than a paragraph, keep it in that house and those few days. No philosophy vocabulary anywhere in the story, no character states the thesis, nobody explains the problem — the brother can gesture at it at the kitchen table in his own words and get it about half wrong, and that should be the closest the story ever comes to naming it. And do not resolve whether she means it. The last beat has to leave it genuinely open, and not by going vague — by being specific and double. Two true things at once.

Format, and I'm fussy about this because it's how I'll read it:

The story. Roughly 1,400 words, somewhere between 1,200 and 1,600.

Note on the choices. Then a second section under exactly that heading, 250 to 300 words, telling me the choices you made — where you decided the line between the right kind of reason and the wrong kind actually falls in her case, what you did to keep the ending undecided, and what you deliberately left off the page.
