I sit on the grantmaking committee of a small foundation that funds conceptual work in suffering-focused ethics, and we are trying to decide what to commission over the next three years. We can support roughly eight researcher-years in total; we have ruled out empirical work (animal-welfare measurement, AI welfare, survey research) and outreach; and we want to fund questions where a philosopher's careful thinking is the bottleneck rather than data. The board divides in a now-predictable way. One faction wants us to shore up the axiological foundations — whether suffering is the only intrinsic disvalue or merely the thing that gets lexical priority, whether there is an asymmetry between bringing a happy being into existence and bringing a suffering one into existence, how to compare intensities of suffering across radically different kinds of minds. The other faction insists we fund work that will be action-guiding soon: whether the view licenses anti-natalism, whether it requires reducing the risk of astronomically large amounts of future suffering (what some of us call s-risks), and how a suffering-focused agent should act under deep uncertainty about others' inner lives.

I would like a prioritized agenda, not a survey and not a literature review. Which open questions in this area most deserve the next three years, in what order, and why — where "why" means how upstream the question is of other work, how tractable it is for a handful of philosophers rather than an entire research program, and how much downstream would actually change depending on how it came out. I also want to know which questions look important but are, in your judgment, either effectively settled or unlikely to repay further work.

Three things from our internal discussions should shape your answer, because they are where we keep stalling. First, one trustee draws a distinction between "pure" negative utilitarianism, on which suffering is the only thing that is intrinsically bad, and "suffering-prioritizing" views, on which suffering is lexically prior to other goods but not the only bad. She claims the two carry different open-question lists and that we have been conflating them; I would like you to say whether that distinction does any real work here, and if so, which questions belong on which list.

Second, a colleague raises what he calls the "aggregation floor" worry: if suffering is what matters, and enough mild suffering can always outweigh a smaller quantity of intense suffering, then the view may collapse into ordinary utilitarianism with a minus sign, in which case its distinctive commitments evaporate. He wants to know whether avoiding this requires a threshold or lexical structure, whether any such structure can be principled rather than arbitrary, and whether this is a live question or a dead end we can stop funding.

Third, a trustee asks how the view can even state its central claim without a common currency of suffering — whether the position needs a unit of suffering to be a position at all, and whether the absence of one is a fatal defect, a tractable technical problem, or a confusion.

Please structure your response as follows: "Summary" (roughly 150 words, your top three in order); "Criteria" (roughly 200 words, the criteria you are using and how you trade them off against each other); "The ranking" (roughly 700–900 words, each question with its defense, its place relative to the others, and its dependency relations); "Overrated or closed" (roughly 200 words, questions that look important but should not be funded, with your reasons); and "What would change the ranking" (roughly 200 words, the arguments or developments over the next couple of years that would move a question up or down).
