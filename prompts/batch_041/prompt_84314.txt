I broke my wrist in a bike crash last month and they put me on IV morphine in the ER, and the strange part is I could still feel the pain. I could point right at where it was, tell you it was sharp and hot and about a 7 out of 10, and then just... not care. It hurt and I shrugged. Since then I've been reading about pain asymbolia, which is apparently the same thing — people have pain without the unpleasantness, and apparently morphine does a version of it too.

What I want is a thought experiment that pulls apart three things I keep smushing together: the sensation, the "ow" — the pull to make it stop — and whether the thing actually makes your life go worse. Let's call them the signal, the pull, and the toll. Build me one ordinary scenario where the signal and the pull are both fully there but the toll is zero, and where the zero toll falls out of the story instead of me just declaring it — and no brain lesions, no lobotomies, no sci-fi drugs, just something a normal adult could actually go through. The person has to sincerely call it pain, and they can't be mistaken about their own state. Then build me the mirror case: something that takes a real toll on someone's life but has no pain anywhere in it. For each, tell me what hedonism says, what a desire-satisfaction view says, and what the view that pains are perceptions of bodily damage says — on that last one, does my first case even count as a pain? Then the part I actually care about: which single feature is doing the work, the missing pull or the missing toll? Take that feature out and does the case collapse back into ordinary pain? And how would someone who thinks pain is bad by its very nature fight the case — do they say it wasn't really pain, or that it was pain plus a separate indifference sitting next to it?
