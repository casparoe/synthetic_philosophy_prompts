I'm trying to get a better grip on Lakatos's methodology of scientific research programmes, and the case I keep coming back to is the ulcer story — Marshall and Warren, the whole Helicobacter pylori thing. You probably know the outline: for most of the twentieth century the ruling idea was that peptic ulcers came from excess acid, stress, spicy food, smoking, that kind of thing, and the treatments were antacids, then H2 blockers like cimetidine, then proton pump inhibitors, plus surgery in the bad cases (vagotomy and so on). Then in the early eighties these two people in Perth, a pathologist and a trainee physician, found spiral bacteria in stomach biopsies, managed to culture them, and proposed that the bacteria cause gastritis and ulcers. They got hammered for it — their 1983 abstract to the Australian gastroenterology society was rejected, ranked near the bottom of what came in. Marshall couldn't infect piglets, so in 1984 he drank a broth of the cultured bug himself, got gastritis within days, a biopsy showed it had colonised him, and he took antibiotics. They published in 1985. It then took about a decade for the field to really turn: a NIH consensus conference in 1994 said ulcers associated with H. pylori should be treated with antibiotics, and Marshall and Warren got the Nobel in 2005. There's also a Greek physician, Lykoudis, who was treating ulcers with antibiotics back in the late fifties and early sixties, got fined by a disciplinary committee and prosecuted, and never got published in the journals that mattered — a ghost in the story.

What I want is a Lakatosian reading of this, but a disciplined one, not just "old paradigm, new paradigm, Kuhn wins."

1. What exactly was the hard core of the acid/stress programme, and what sat in the protective belt? Be specific about which auxiliary hypotheses were doing the work — about acid secretion, about the stomach being effectively sterile because of acid, about stress and personality, about why plenty of people with high acid never get ulcers. And what was the negative heuristic (what people in that programme treated as not-to-be-questioned) versus the positive heuristic (what they were told to go work on)?

2. Map the counterexamples. I want monster-barring, exception-barring, and lemma-incorporation actually at work here, not just name-dropped. Where does the establishment monster-bar an ulcer — redefining what counts, calling the bacteria cases atypical, or saying the bug is opportunistic, a consequence rather than a cause? Where does it exception-bar — fine, bacteria in some patients, but not in the classic stress-ulcer patient? And then where does the bacterial programme itself have to do lemma-incorporation: what auxiliary claims did it have to add or revise as it went, about who gets infected, about asymptomatic carriers, about why most infected people never get an ulcer?

3. The hard question. By Lakatos's lights, was the acid programme degenerating and the bacterial programme progressive from the start, or is that only obvious in retrospect? Lakatos says you shouldn't drop a research programme just because of anomalies; anomalies are expected, and a lone anomaly with no rival programme is basically nothing. So was the establishment rationally entitled to keep going? And what makes the bacterial programme progressive — the novel prediction that antibiotics cure ulcers and prevent recurrence, or something else? Where does the self-experiment fit? It's a striking gesture, but is it evidence in Lakatos's sense, or rhetoric that happened to be epistemically useful?

4. Lykoudis. He had the treatment working on tens of thousands of patients, decades early, and it didn't count. He wasn't in a research programme, he wasn't publishing where the field would read it, and he got prosecuted for it. Is that a case Lakatos's methodology can't even see — an anomaly with no carrier programme, so no rational reason for the field to attend to it — or does the methodology have something to say about why a research community should still track it? And does the fact that patients were bleeding and dying while the old programme ran its course create a problem for the be-patient-with-degenerating-programmes line?

5. The twist, and this is the part I care most about. H. pylori turned out not to be straightforwardly a pathogen for everyone. A huge fraction of the world carries it and never gets sick; and there's now evidence it's inversely associated with things like asthma and esophageal cancer, and may even be part of normal stomach ecology. So the triumphant new programme had to start building its own protective belt — why only some strains cause disease, why populations differ, what the cost of eradicating it is. Is that a degeneration of the bacterial programme, or just normal belt-work? And does it retroactively change the verdict on the old programme, which maybe was right that acid and the stomach environment matter, just wrong about the causal direction?

I'd like the answer to run in both directions — Lakatos illuminating the episode, and the episode testing Lakatos. Where does the framework earn its keep, and where does it go silent or force the history into a shape it doesn't fit? In particular, Lakatos's rational reconstruction has no real place for funding, prestige, journals, patient harm, or the fact that a self-experiment by a junior researcher moved things in a way a better-controlled study by an outsider might not have. Say honestly what it can't explain rather than patching it over with ad hoc sociology. And try to be absolutly explicit about which parts of this are Lakatos's own machinery and which parts are you improvising on his behalf.
