I am a software engineer. I have spent several years building consent and permissions flows for a data platform, and I have now been asked to contribute the written examination question for a graduate course on data ethics that is cross-listed between our computer science department and the philosophy department. My training is entirely technical. I can recognize the standard account of valid consent — adequate disclosure, voluntariness, competence — but I cannot adjudicate the hard cases, and I certainly cannot tell a good answer from a fluent one without help. I need you to produce the examination question, a model answer, and a marking scheme that I can defend when the philosopher who co-teaches the course reviews my grading.

Build the question around a case rather than around the standard conditions, because I have watched students recite those conditions and then say nothing useful about an actual situation. Use the following case, or a tightened version of it, as the spine of the question.

A company sells a workplace wellness app. An employer requires its employees to use the app to log their hours; hours that are not logged are not paid. The app requests continuous location and heart-rate data. Its disclosure screen states that the company shares data with analytics partners in order to improve the service — a statement that is literally true and that omits that those partners build health-risk profiles which are sold to insurers. The user is a competent adult. The company offers a paid tier, at eight dollars a month, that collects no continuous data; the user has not chosen it. No threat comes from the company: the pressure to accept comes from the employer's requirement, and the employer is not the party asking for consent.

I want the question to force students to take positions on two puzzles that the standard conditions do not resolve on their own. Introduce both explicitly in the question, so that a student who has never encountered them can still engage with them.

The first is the baseline problem. Voluntariness is measured against the alternative the person would otherwise have had, and in this case the choice of baseline decides the verdict. Is the baseline not using the app at all, which for this user means not being paid? Is it the paid tier, which means paying to avoid surveillance? Or is it the situation the user would have occupied had the employer never adopted the app? The question should require students to state which baseline they are using and what turns on the choice.

The second is the act/attitude problem. Consent might be an act — the click, the signature, the spoken words — or it might be a state of mind, such as understanding and endorsing what is happening. On the act reading, the click can settle the matter even where understanding is thin; on the attitude reading, the click is only evidence of consent and can be defeated by what the user did or did not understand. The question should require students to say which reading their verdict depends on.

The question should also require students to decide two case-specific matters: whether a disclosure that is literally true but materially incomplete can deceive, and whether competence is a general capacity or a capacity with respect to this particular transaction.

The question should be answerable in seventy-five minutes and be worth twenty-five marks, and the model answer should run to roughly seven hundred words. The marking scheme should be organized around the specific moves a strong answer must make, rather than around generic virtues such as clarity, and it should name the specific errors that ought to cost marks — including at least one error that is tempting precisely because it sounds sophisticated. Finally, tell me which one or two moves separate a first-class answer from a solid second-class one, since that is the distinction I will have to defend when grades are challenged.

One constraint: do not build the question or the model answer around names, dates, or citations. The students are not expected to have read the literature, and I could not verify a reference if I tried.
