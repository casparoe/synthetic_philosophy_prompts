I keep getting into arguements about whether everyday etiquette stuff — queue order, tipping, jaywalking — is actually morally binding. My friend says these are just conventions: break them and you're being odd or rude at most, not doing anything wrong. I think that's way too quick, but I also definitely don't want to say every local habit is a moral duty, since then a tourist who doesn't tip in the US is a moral criminal and so is someone who stands on the "wrong" side of the escalator.

So here's the distinction I want you to run with. Some conventions are load-bearing: the practice they're attached to would collapse or dump a cost on someone else if enough people stopped (US tipping, where servers' pay depends on it, looks like this). Others are basically decorative: pure coordination where nobody's worse off if you defect (which side of the escalator you stand on, which way you face in an elevator).

Write an essay that lands on a verdict about two things: can decorative conventions ever be real moral obligations, and does a visitor have to follow load-bearing local conventions even when she thinks the arrangement underneath is unjust — say she thinks the restaurant should just pay its staff properly instead of leaning on customers?

Format it as exactly five numbered points, a sentence or two each, no intro and no wrap-up, and make the fifth one the verdict.
