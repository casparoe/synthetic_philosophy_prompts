I'm a PhD student in early modern philosophy, and I'm trying to get a clearer picture of Hobbes's relationship to geometry. A colleague in the history of science, whose judgment I generally trust, recently gave me the following summary of Hobbes's position, and I'd like to know how much of it is fair. His summary runs roughly as follows.

Hobbes took Euclid's geometry as the model of demonstrative science because it showed that certainty is available when we reason from definitions that we ourselves lay down. Geometry is thus a science of our own constructions: we know the causes of figures because we make them, and this is why its conclusions are necessary. Hobbes then transferred this method wholesale to civil philosophy, since we also make the commonwealth. But because the definitions are arbitrary, geometrical truths are in the end verbal; they tell us only about the consequences of the names we choose, not about anything in nature. Hobbes accordingly dismissed the experimental natural philosophy and algebraic mathematics of his contemporaries—Wallis in particular—as a fallible and confused enterprise, and his late attempts at squaring the circle and duplicating the cube were not eccentric departures from his philosophy but the natural expression of a conventionalist view of mathematics. The upshot is that Hobbes's political theory is best read as a piece of applied geometry: the sovereign is constructed from definitions the way a circle is constructed from a definition, and the whole system stands or falls with the deductive rigor of its first definitions.

I'm inclined to think the conventionalism charge is too strong, but I don't trust my grasp of the texts. How much of this is right? Please go claim by claim and tell me what a historian of early modern philosophy would accept, what they would wince at, and what is simply wrong. I'm particularly interested in whether the alleged conventionalism about geometry is really Hobbes's view, or whether it conflates commitments—nominalism, constructivism, and the arbitrariness of definitions—that Hobbes kept apart. I'd also like to know whether the inference to his political theory being applied geometry follows from anything true in the summary, or whether it overstates a methodological analogy. And if the summary's picture of his disputes with Wallis and his late mathematical work is off, please say how. I'm not asking for a reading list; I want the conceptual corrections.
