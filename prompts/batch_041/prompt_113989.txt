I'm a third-year PhD student in philosophy of social science, writing a chapter on what cross-national comparisons can and cannot license, and I keep running into an argument in seminars that I've now reconstructed in a form I can't fault line by line. It goes like this. (1) In cross-national data, the more equal men and women are within a country — by the standard indices of economic and political participation — the larger the average difference between men's and women's occupational choices tends to be; the share of women among graduates of mathematically intensive fields, for instance, tends to fall rather than rise as equality rises. (2) Where a group difference persists or grows as the constraints on that group's choices are removed, the difference cannot be explained by those constraints. (3) So the difference in occupational choice is not explained by constraints on women's choices. (4) What survives the removal of constraints must come from the preferences the choosers themselves bring to the situation. (5) Preferences that persist once constraints are gone are, to that extent, features of the agents rather than products of their social environment. (6) So the difference in occupational choice reflects intrinsic, sex-typed preferences rather than socialization. (7) So policies aimed at changing the composition of fields — quotas, role models, early encouragement — can change who is admitted against the grain of preference, but not the preferences themselves.

What bothers me is not the conclusion as such. I'm not asking you to rescue me from an uncomfortable result, and I'd rather you didn't try. What bothers me is that the same inferential shape would license conclusions I'm confident are false: it would tell me that because the children of wealthy families still dominate selective universities in countries with formally open access, their advantage reflects intrinsic preference. So either (2) is too strong, or (2) is fine and something else in the chain is doing illicit work. If it helps you see where I'm stuck, the steps I would most like to keep are (1), because it is the only one I can check against data, and (5), because I do think there is a real distinction between a preference someone would have had under any upbringing and one they would have had only under this one. I know the measures behind (1) are contested, but I don't know whether that is a problem for the argument or only for its rhetoric.

Please tell me which step or steps to give up, and say for each why it looked plausible on a first pass. Whether the culprit is a whole step, a single term being asked to do two jobs, or a slide in what the argument is about, I want the offending item named and the two readings spelled out. Then price the repairs against each other: if I drop (2) I seem to lose a general principle I use elsewhere, and if I drop (5) I am no longer sure I can distinguish intrinsic from socially produced preferences at all. Finally, tell me whether any reading of the argument survives intact — if the thing is valid and I simply have to pay for one of the premises, I would rather know that than have it quietly patched.
