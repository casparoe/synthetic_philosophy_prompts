I am a high school student writing an extended essay on Thomas Hobbes's conception of science, and I have run into a difficulty in the fifth chapter of Leviathan, the chapter on reason and science. Toward the end of it Hobbes makes a claim that I can paraphrase but not quite accept: that geometry is the only science it has pleased God so far to bestow upon mankind, and that what sets geometry apart is that its practitioners do not dispute the truth of the propositions themselves, whatever they may dispute about the right to teach them. I am paraphrasing rather than quoting, since I am working from a modernized edition and do not want to garble the wording, but I am confident about the sense of the claim, and it is the sense that I need help with.

My difficulty is that the remark seems to pull in two directions. Hobbes plainly treats geometry as the model of demonstration and wants to imitate its method, yet if geometry is the only science God has granted us, then by his own account there is no demonstrative science of ethics or politics, and the conclusions of reason that he calls the laws of nature must be something else. A friend of mine concludes from the remark that Hobbes's political philosophy cannot be a science at all and that his geometrical manner of writing is decorative, a rhetorical costume rather than a method. I suspect this is too quick, but I cannot say precisely why.

I would like you to explain the passage and to adjudicate between my friend's reading and a more charitable one. What does Hobbes mean by science in this chapter, and why should geometry satisfy his definition better than any other inquiry does? Is geometry's freedom from controversy, on his account, a consequence of its subject matter, or of the fact that our interests and passions are not engaged by it, and what difference does that make for whether moral and political inquiry could ever be demonstrative? If geometry rests on definitions that we ourselves stipulate, does that make it a human construction rather than a discovery, and how then should I take the claim that God has bestowed it on us? And since Hobbes holds that reasoning is a kind of computation upon names, does the privileged position he gives geometry follow from that theory of reasoning, or does it sit awkwardly with it?

Finally, please tell me where I am most likely to be misreading the passage, and which earlier or later parts of Leviathan would be most useful for settling the question. I have a vague impression that Hobbes was notorious for insisting on his own geometrical results and being rebuffed by the mathematicians of his day; if that is right, I would be glad to know whether it should affect how I read this remark.
