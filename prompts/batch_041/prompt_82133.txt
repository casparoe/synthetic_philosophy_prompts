Bit of a strange request, I think, but here goes. I retired from geology a few years back (mineralogy for a long stretch, then basin modelling — nothing philosophical) and I've been sitting in on a philosophy reading group at the library, which is how I've ended up spending my Tuesdays arguing about when somebody counts as having done a thing on purpose. I've gotten alot out of it, honestly. One of the regulars is a retired philosophy professor, Hal, and he's the most confident person in the room. Lately we've been working through a stack of little stories that all have the same skeleton: somebody does a thing for a reason, and then there's a second thing that happens that they knew about but didn't care about one way or the other. Hal's verdicts so far, as best I can write them down:

- A company boss pushes through a new programme because it'll raise profits. He knows it will also pollute the river. He doesn't care about the river at all, he just wants the profits. Hal: yes, he polluted the river on purpose.
- Same story, except the programme will clean the river up. He knows it, doesn't care either way. Hal: no, he didn't clean up the river on purpose.
- An engineer whose project will wreck a wetland. She's miserable about it, she's told her boss she thinks it's wrong, but she builds it anyway, and it's against the regulations to boot. Hal: yes, she wrecked it on purpose. (So it isn't that the person has to want the thing — that was my first guess and it's dead.)
- A department head who changes how the work is allocated, knowing his assistants will end up doing unpaid overtime, which the university's own rules don't allow. He doesn't care. Hal: yes, on purpose.

Hal says this whole thing is famous, something about a boss and profits and the environment, and that there's a name for it. The Knobe effect, maybe? Anyway, here's the case I'd like your help with, because I keep wanting a control condition and this is the closest thing I can think of:

A surgeon does an operation that is plainly the right call. Standard procedure, properly indicated, no error, no negligence, nothing she could be sued over, nothing anyone in the room would even frown at, no rule anywhere against it. She knows the operation will leave the patient with permanent numbness in one shoulder — a known and accepted cost of a procedure that's genuinely needed. She doesn't care about the numbness one way or the other; she cares about the thing the operation is for. Does Hal say she numbed the patient's shoulder on purpose?

I'm not really after a yes or a no. I'm after the rule Hal is running. I can see at least two that fit every verdict above, and I think they come apart on my surgeon. Tell me which one he's probly running, what each of the candidates predicts about her, which one you'd bet on and why, and what case I should bring to the group next week to tell them apart for good. And if it turns out the stories themselves can't decide it, say so — I'd like to know whether Hal and I are disagreeing about a fact or just about which word to use.
