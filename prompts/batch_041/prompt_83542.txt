I am marking a second-year undergraduate answer in a module on historical theory. The examination question was:

Ranke's ideal of recounting the past as it essentially was is either naive or untenable. Discuss with reference to the sceptical challenges posed by E.H. Carr and Hayden White, and say whether either leaves room for objective historical knowledge.

The student wrote:

Ranke is usually taken as the father of scientific history. He insisted that historians should go back to the sources, criticize them, and avoid imposing their own moral or political judgments on the past. His aim was to let the past speak for itself, to show it as it essentially was, rather than to use it for present purposes. This is why he is often called a positivist, though he also believed that the historian could discern a divine order in history.

Carr's What Is History? attacks this picture. Carr argues that facts do not speak for themselves; they become historical facts only when the historian selects them and gives them significance. The historian is not a passive recorder but an active interpreter. History is therefore a dialogue between the present and the past. However, Carr concludes that because selection is governed by the historian's present-day concerns, historical interpretation is ultimately arbitrary. This makes him a relativist about historical truth.

Hayden White radicalizes Carr's point. In Metahistory and later work, White argues that historians do not simply find a story in the evidence; they impose a story on it by emploting events into genres such as romance, tragedy, comedy, and satire. The same set of events can be narrated in different modes, and the choice of mode is not dictated by the evidence. White therefore calls historical narratives verbal fictions. This means that historical events themselves are invented by the historian, and that historical accounts cannot be true or false in any ordinary sense; they can only be more or less persuasive.

Taken together, Carr and White show that Ranke's ideal is impossible. If facts are selected and narratives are constructed, then there is no neutral standpoint from which the past can be reported as it actually was. The most we can hope for is a self-conscious acknowledgment that all history is written from a particular perspective. Neither Carr nor White leaves room for objective historical knowledge.
