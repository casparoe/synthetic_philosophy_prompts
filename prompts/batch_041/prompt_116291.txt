For the past few months I've been leaning on a coding assistant every day at work, and something I used to treat as a seminar game has turned into a question I actually have to have a view about: my research group has asked me to draft the position statement on whether the systems we build can understand anything, and before I circulate it I want to make sure the views I'm putting my name to actually hang together. The framing I want to use is the one that runs through Darwin and Turing — the thought that reliable competence can be produced by processes with no comprehension anywhere inside them, selection yielding the appearance of design with no designer, a machine yielding correct sums with no grasp of arithmetic, so that comprehension, if it exists at all, arrives late and from below rather than being the precondition of competence. Take that as the background; it isn't one of the commitments I'm worried about.

Here is the position as I'd state it, in six claims. (1) That inversion is right and universal: whatever the mind does is at bottom the product of mindless processes getting organized, and nothing is added at the top. (2) Human beings are not an exception to the competence-without-comprehension story; most of what I do — my grammar, my vision, my social fluency — is competence with no comprehension behind it, and I don't believe there is a little person inside who understands on my behalf. (3) Comprehension is nonetheless real and not merely a compliment we pay to systems we like: there is a genuine difference between a system that reliably gets things right and one that grasps what it is doing, and the mark of the latter is that the correct explanation of its transitions cites what it takes to be reasons — it is moved by considerations as considerations — rather than citing features that merely happen to correlate with the right answer. I also think this comes in degrees, with no sharp threshold anywhere. (4) There is no fact of the matter about what an internal state represents; talk of what a system believes or represents is an instrument for predicting and explaining it, and what it "really means" varies with the stance that pays off. (5) I am confident that nothing built the way current systems are built understands anything, and that no amount of scaling or refinement will change this, however fluent it becomes and however well it argues for itself; I'd say the same of any system whose outputs are generated the way theirs are, even one that behaved impeccably forever. (6) This is not a verbal dispute: whether a system understands is precisely what licenses trusting it, blaming it, and treating its outputs as testimony rather than as evidence about something else.

I suspect at least one of these has to give, but I genuinely can't see which, and I don't want to be the person who announces that they all fit together nicely. Please audit the set. Tell me which pairs are genuinely in tension and rank them by severity rather than treating them as equally bad; tell me which pairs only look like they conflict and why the appearance dissolves on inspection; and for each real tension say what the cheapest repair would be and what it costs me. Finish by telling me which single revision would clean up the most. I would much rather you be specific about which commitment is doing the damage than generous to the package as a whole.
