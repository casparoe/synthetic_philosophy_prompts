You have been asked to give the commencement address at a small liberal arts college where you taught philosophy for decades. The graduating class is heading into a world of instantaneous answers, and the provost's one instruction was to say something about what an education is actually for. You want to build the whole address around an old and genuinely unsettling puzzle: why should knowledge be prized over mere true belief, when someone who only happens to believe the truth about how to get where they are going will navigate just as well as someone who really knows the way? The address cannot simply assert that knowledge is better; it has to let the audience feel the force of the puzzle before earning whatever resolution it offers, and it must leave the students with something usable without implying that ungrounded true beliefs are worthless. You may use the image of true beliefs as statues of Daedalus that run away unless they are tied down, but do not let that image carry the whole argument. Write the address at roughly eight hundred words, pitched so that parents and trustees with no philosophy background can follow it, while keeping it precise enough that the philosophers on the faculty will not wince.
