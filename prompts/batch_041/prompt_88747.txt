I sit on a small committee that has to decide how to spend about three years of a postdoc's time plus a modest workshop budget, and we have settled on the problem of the wrong kind of reasons — the cluster of puzzles you get when an attitude can be justified by considerations that do not bear on whether the attitude is fitting, so that a threat or a bribe can give me a reason to admire someone, or to believe my flight will land safely, without making the admiration merited or the belief true. The topic came up over dinner at a workshop last spring and none of us could agree on what the central question even is. A colleague has been pushing a line I find both attractive and slippery: that the label covers a family of distinctions rather than one, that what unifies the family is that every attitude comes with a correctness condition of its own, and that the real work is to say which attitudes have conditions fixed by their objects — belief, fear, admiration — and which are answerable to something else entirely, such as convention, prudence, or the point of having the attitude at all. She thinks the literature has been warped by treating fitting-attitude accounts of value as the central case, and that we would learn more from the fringe: reasons to intend, to suspend judgment, to feel grateful, to regret, even to be amused.

What I want from you is an agenda, not a survey. Which open questions here actually deserve a postdoc's three years, ranked, and why that order? I care much less about the list than about the reasoning: for each, whether it is upstream of the others, in the sense that settling it would dissolve or render tractable some of the rest; how much of it is conceptual spadework a philosopher can do at a desk versus something that needs empirical input about how attitudes actually work in people; how neglected it is relative to the threat-and-bribe cases everyone reaches for first; and whether settling it would change anything downstream — how we build fitting-attitude accounts of value, how we think about evidentialism and pragmatic encroachment in epistemology, whether emotions can be reasons-responsive at all. Be explicit about the criteria you are trading off, since importance is relative to what we are funding and for how long.

Tell me where my colleague's framing genuinely helps and where it begs the question, which two or three questions you would actually put the money behind, and what argument or finding over the next couple of years should move a question up or down the ranking. If you think the honest answer is that the whole area is less fruitful than something adjacent to it, say so and defend that. Write in American English.
