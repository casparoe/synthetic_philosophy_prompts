I'm in a seminar on ethics and techonology and I've been trying to pin down what I actually think about privacy, because the nothing-to-hide line everyone repeats seems obviously wrong to me but I can never say why without sounding like I'm just anti-corporate. So here's my gut on a pile of cases, and I want to know what principle is underneath them, if there is one. My roommate reads my unlocked diary while I'm out and learns nothing she didn't already know about me: I think that's a serious violation, and only a little less serious than if she'd learned something new -- the wrong doesn't seem to be about what she found out. A stranger recognizes me on the street from my public instagram: completely fine. Someone reads over my shoulder on the train: rude, invasive, but way less bad than the diary. My friend tells his partner a secret I told him: wrong, but it feels like breaking a promise more than violating my privacy, and I dont think the partner did anything wrong by hearing it. My friend tells me a secret about her sister: fine, I've done nothing wrong, even though I now hold something about the sister that she never chose to give me. A store infers from my purchase history that I'm pregnant and mails me coupons before I've told anyone: this one makes my skin crawl, even though no human being ever looked at anything about me and I'd have told my friends within the month.

My doctor asks about my sex life: fine, she needs it. My insurer asks the same question: not fine, even though it's the same fact and they'd use it to price me. My neighbor's porch camera catches the sidewalk in front of his house: fine, anyone walking by can see that. A drone hovering over my fenced backyard: not fine, even though the drone is also only seeing what's technically outside my walls. And my employer asks for my Facebook login as a condition of a promotion and I hand it over: I still think that was a violation, even though I agreed to it.

My tentative theory is that it's all about control -- a violation is information about me going somewhere I didn't authorize it to go. That gets the diary, the instagram post, the doctor versus the insurer, and the drone. But it seems to get the store case wrong (nobody took anything, and I did authorize the shopping), it seems to get the sidewalk camera wrong (I didn't authorize that either), and it gets the employer case wrong in the other direction (I said yes, so on the control view there's nothing to complain about). My fallback was that it's about what I can reasonably expect to be seen or inferred in a given context -- but then what fixes the expectation, and if the answer is just the norms of the context, has the principle explained anything, or is it a fancy way of restating my verdicts back to me? Take these seriously and tell me what principle, or small set of principles, actually generates them; whether it has to be one thing or a bundle of different interests that happen to share the word privacy; which of my verdicts it forces me to give up; and what it predicts for a case I haven't listed -- my landlord installing a camera in the shared hallway that logs when each tenant comes and goes, which I think is wrong even though the hallway is semi-public and my neighbors see me all the time. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence and then give the reasoning, no preamble, and don't restate my cases back to me.
