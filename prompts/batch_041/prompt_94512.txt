Here's what's bugging me. Citizens assemblies. Members are picked by lot, which is supposed to be the whole point: equal chance for everyone, no campaigning, no self-selection, so the body can't be captured by the usual suspects. But in practice basically every real one stratifies the draw — so many women, so many under-30s, so many from each region — and some go further and deliberately recruit people who'd never normally turn up (phone calls, door knocking, a per diem for showing up). Then the members are sat down for several weekends with balanced expert briefings, and by the end they've usually shifted their views on something, which is kind of the point of deliberating. Then the recommendation goes to a parliament or a referendum, where it can be quietly shelved or voted down.

So what does that package presuppose? I want the implicit theory of legitimacy underneath it — what a body has to be, or do, for its output to count. Which rival views does it rule out: consent or authorization, electoral accountability, plain epistemic quality, or something about mirroring the population? Which parts of the practice actually need that theory, and which would sit fine with several theories at once? And where's the internal tension — if legitimacy comes from being a microcosm, why brief and pay people until they stop being one; if it comes from deliberating well, what are the demographic quotas for; if the recommendation can be outvoted or shelved, was the lottery doing any work at all? Finish by saying what would have to change in the practice if you swapped the implicit view for a purely epistemic one.

Give it to me as exactly five numbered points, a sentence or two each, no intro, no conclusion, no citations or reading list — I'll add the references myself. And dont hedge everything into "it depends": say what the practice most likely presupposes.
