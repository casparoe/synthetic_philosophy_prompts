Three weeks ago a colleague whose own expense reports are, to put it mildly, creative informed me that I had no business padding mine. Two weeks before that I had delivered a nearly identical rebuke to someone else, with a record no cleaner than hers. I have been chewing on both episodes since, and I would like you to help me turn the following into a principle.

My verdicts. When that colleague rebukes me for padding, I do not think she has standing to do it; the rebuke is not one she gets to make, though I would not say it is false and would not say I may simply ignore it. When the same colleague, after a year of straight reports, says the same thing, I think she does have standing, and that the change in her record changes something important. When a colleague who has never padded anything says it, her standing is not in question and I feel no impulse to audit her past. When a judge who was himself convicted of the offence sentences a defendant for it, I think he has full standing: the bench confers what the man lacks. Likewise a parent who lies to her children may tell them not to lie, and I do not think her standing is thereby diminished. When my brother, whom I lent money knowing he would spend it on the drugs that then wrecked his family, tells me I have no standing to blame him for wrecking it, I think I do have standing, though the blame lands softer for my part in it. When a man who cheerfully informs me that he is about to do the very thing he is blaming me for rebukes me for it, I think he has standing — oddly more than my unrepentant colleague, though his record is no better and his future worse. And when I ask a friend to carry a rebuke to someone I cannot rebuke myself, I think the friend's delivery stands or falls on the friend, and that my authorship behind it does not poison it.

The last two verdicts will not sit with the first, and I suspect the first and the fourth cannot both be right as I have stated them. My tentative principle is that a person has standing to blame just in case addressing the blame to the person would not itself wrong that person — the rebuke must not be a vehicle for wronging the rebuked. That seems to save the first, second, sixth, and eighth verdicts; I cannot see whether it saves the fourth or the fifth, and it threatens to collapse into the claim that only those who are themselves wronging the rebuked lack standing, which is surely too strong.

Please give me the principle, or the smallest set of competing principles, that best fits this pattern, saying for each clause which case forces it. Tell me what the principle predicts for cases I have not mentioned — blaming the dead, blaming a corporation, blaming oneself, blaming someone for a fault one shares but has never once been tempted by. And tell me honestly which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate. If the truer story is that I have been running two questions together — whether the rebuke is deserved, and whether the rebuker is in a position to deliver it — then say so, and say which of my verdicts belong to which question.
