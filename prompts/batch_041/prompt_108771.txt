Last night a friend of mine — a historian who works on interwar Germany — and I argued for something like four hours about who was to blame for the First World War, and we ended exactly where we began. He takes the position associated with Fritz Fischer's work in the early 1960s: that the German leadership deliberately risked a European war and in important respects wanted one, and that the evidence of German war aims supports reading 1914 as a calculated bid for continental hegemony, which is why he thinks the question of German responsibility was never merely a matter of postwar resentment. I take the position of the critics who answered Fischer at the time: that what the documents actually show is a gamble taken inside a crisis by men who did not understand what they were setting in motion, and that once you weight the alliance system, the arms race, and the mobilization timetables that nobody could stop once started, singling out one government's intentions looks like a choice of focus rather than a finding. I had assumed this was a dispute about evidence, and that some future archive would settle it. What struck me is that we agreed about almost every particular document either of us could name, including the awkward fact that the most-cited statement of German aims was drafted after the war had already begun, so that it cannot by itself show the war was planned.

So I have started to suspect the argument is not empirical at all, and I want you to tell me whether that is right and, if so, what kind of non-empirical disagreement it is. My friend's view is that we are really disagreeing about what an explanation of an event like a war has to consist in — whether you explain it by recovering the intentions of agents or by describing a structure that made the outcome likely — and he adds that the moral question is separate and largely unanswerable, since a nation is not the sort of thing that can be guilty, only individuals are. I have two competing hypotheses. The first is that we are answering different questions under a single heading: he is asking who was answerable, in the sense that a court or a treaty might hold someone answerable, while I am asking what caused the thing, and the word responsibility is quietly doing both jobs. The second is that the impasse is really about counterfactuals — that any claim of the form Germany caused the war requires a comparison with a world in which Germany acted differently, and that we have no principled way of specifying that world, so the dispute is stalled because the question is under-specified rather than because either of us is wrong. There is also a smaller thing we could not resolve and that may be a symptom: I said responsibility can be apportioned in degrees, historians assign shares all the time, and he said that is a category mistake, because blame is not a divisible quantity and talk of percentages is a metaphor that has been mistaken for a measure.

I want a diagnosis rather than a verdict on 1914. Is this a verbal dispute about the word responsibility, a substantive disagreement about explanatory standards, a hidden empirical question that better history could close, or two sets of bedrock intuitions about agency and structure that neither side can argue for? Say which of my hypotheses is closest to the truth and where it goes wrong, using what each side actually maintains rather than a survey of the historiography, and then tell me what would count as progress given your diagnosis — a distinction we could both accept, a change in the question, a formal constraint on the counterfactuals, or an honest argument that nothing will move and why.
