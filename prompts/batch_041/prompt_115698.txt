I teach a graduate seminar on explanation in history, and I keep returning to an institutional practice that I would like to have reconstructed rather than merely criticised. In the wake of serious failures — a hospital's run of avoidable deaths, the collapse of a bank, a fatal fire in a tower block — the standard artefact is a review report whose two halves pull in opposite directions. One half is a chronological narrative: this was the state of affairs, then this, then this, and the outcome is rendered intelligible as the upshot, with the earlier moments retrospectively marked as warning signs. The other half, together with the methodological preamble that usually accompanies it, insists that the reviewers must not judge by hindsight — that the actors are to be assessed only against what was knowable and which options were live at the time. My colleagues and I have been arguing about whether those halves can be held together, and about what each of them presupposes. My suspicion is that the narrative half presupposes something quite strong: that the outcome is a legitimate principle of selection for deciding which of the then-available facts deserve mention, and hence that there was, before the event, a story only awaiting its ending — that the salient antecedents were salient all along, and that a suitably placed observer could have run the film forward. If that is right, the prohibition in the other half is not a free-standing methodological scruple but a restriction on a way of thinking that the narrative has already licensed.

There is a complication I would like you to take seriously, because it is the part I find genuinely hard. One of our research centres has responded to this tension with a procedure rather than a precept: for each major review they commission a second, replayed account, in which a fresh panel is given the documentary record as it stood at each successive decision point — no later minutes, no outcome — and asked to say, at each point, what a reasonable practitioner should have done and how the possible outcomes then appeared. The two reports are compared, and the divergence between them is treated as a measurement of hindsight. I am not persuaded that the procedure measures what it claims to. It seems to presuppose that the record as it stood is a determinate object and not itself constituted by what later turned out to matter, and that the forward-looking and backward-looking accounts are answers to the same question, so that their disagreement is an error rather than a difference of category. What I want from you is an account of what each of these three practices — the narrative review, the prohibition on hindsight, and the blind replay — presupposes: about the structure of historical explanation, about what could have been known before the event, about whether an outcome can be evidence of the significance of its own antecedents, and about what blame and responsibility require. Distinguish what a practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits comfortably with, and say where it is philosophically underdetermined, so that several incompatible theories of explanation would license it. Where the practices presuppose inconsistent things, say which part would have to go.

I should be explicit about what I want from the answer. I have heard the deflationary line — that hindsight bias is a robust and well-documented tendency and that the remedy is simply to correct for it — and I do not want it asserted as though it settled the matter. Separate what is actually settled, among people who work on historical explanation and on the psychology of judgement, from what remains contested, and mark clearly which of your claims fall on the contested side rather than presenting them as consensus. Where you attribute a commitment to a practice, tell me how confident you are that the practice could not be described equally well without it.
