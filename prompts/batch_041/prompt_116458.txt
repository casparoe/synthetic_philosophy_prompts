I'm a first-year PhD student sitting in on a foundations-of-physics reading group, and there is a move that keeps stopping conversation and that I have never managed to pin down. The other week we had spent an hour on whether a measurement outcome is definite before anyone records it — decoherence, branching, whether there is any fact of the matter at all — and one of the older students ended the discussion by saying that the wavefunction obviously isn't a real thing, it is just a description of what we know, so there was nothing left to argue about. I have heard the same sentence do at least four different jobs since then: to defend a bland shut-up-and-calculate attitude toward interpretation; to suggest that quantum probabilities are no more mysterious than ignorance about a coin that has already landed; to insist that anyone who asks about the quantum state of the entire universe has made a category mistake; and, once, to wave away a question about whether two different agents can assign different states to one and the same system. I have the strong suspicion that 'the wavefunction is just information' is several quite different claims wearing a single coat, that the one my classmate needs in order to end the argument is the strongest of them, and that he would be unhappy to see it spelled out.

What I want from you is a menu rather than a verdict. Separate out the distinct precise theses that this one sentence could be expressing, ordered roughly from the nearly empty to the genuinely radical, and for each one tell me exactly what it commits you to about the quantum state, what it leaves open, and whether it is a platitude, a substantive and defensible position, or something the physics has already closed off. I am particularly interested in three neighbours of the slogan, since I can never keep them apart: the claim that the state is a state of knowledge in the technical sense that distinct states may correspond to one and the same underlying physical situation; the no-go results that constrain that claim — a colleague cited the Pusey–Barrett–Rudolph theorem, which I gather rules such models out unless you abandon the independence of separately prepared systems — and what exactly those results do and do not establish; and the older idea that quantum probabilities are just ignorance probabilities over pre-existing definite values, which I take to be in some tension with Bell-type results. Finally, tell me which of the readings my reading group actually needs in order to shut down the question about definite outcomes, and whether that reading is available to someone who also wants to deny that the theory is a mere predictive device; if some pair of the readings cannot be held together, say so explicitly. Lay the whole thing out as a list I can bring to the next meeting, and end with a final line beginning "Verdict:" that names, in one short sentence, the reading the slogan has to be taking for the dismissal to work and whether that reading survives.
