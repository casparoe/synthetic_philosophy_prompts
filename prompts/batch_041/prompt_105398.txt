I've been writing novels for twenty years and privacy keeps coming up as a real moral problem in my own life, so here's where my gut lands on a pile of cases and I want to know if one principle is behind them.

Snooping my 15-year-old's phone when she's been acting strange: fine. Snooping my husband's phone because I've got a hunch: not fine. A reporter publishing a senator's undisclosed heart condition that explains a wierd voting record: fine. A biographer revealing that a beloved dead novelist secretly supported a second family: not fine, and I can't fully say why, since he's dead and it's true. A memoirist outing her own brother as gay while he's alive and doesn't want it known: not fine. A neighbor's doorbell cam pointed straight at my front steps: not fine. Google Street View catching me in my bathrobe out in the yard: fine. A friend passing along something I told her in confidence to our mutual friend: not fine. Uploading my own DNA to a genealogy site and thereby blowing up a cousin's life by revealing he isn't his father's son: fine on my part, though it definately wrecked him.

My tentative theory is that a privacy violation is really about using someone's information to get power over them -- that's why the phone cases split (I'm trying to help my kid, I'm trying to catch my husband) and why the senator is fair game. But it doesn't obviously handle the dead novelist or the doorbell camera, and the bathrobe thing bugs me, because a camera is a camera. Is there a better single principle, or do I have to give up one of these verdicts? And tell me what it says about the case I can't settle: writing a novel that's basically my ex-wife with the serial numbers filed off.
