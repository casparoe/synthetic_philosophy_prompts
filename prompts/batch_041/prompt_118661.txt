I sit on a hospital ethics committee that is drafting guidance on when a patient's advance directive should be overridden by the patient's present, expressed wishes. Our disagreement has collapsed onto a single analogy, and I would like you to audit it.

The analogy is this: the person who wrote the directive and the person now in the bed are two different people. One faction on the committee uses it to argue that the document has no more authority over the present patient than a note handed in by a stranger, so present contentment should prevail. The other faction uses the same premise to argue the opposite: because the author of the directive is, in effect, gone, the document is the only surviving expression of her will — a testament rather than a command — and the present patient, being incompetent, is in no position to revoke it. Both factions take themselves to be drawing out the consequences of the same comparison, and both are impatient with the other for failing to see where it leads.

What I want from you:

1. A correspondence table. In the interpersonal case the analogy is modelled on — one person issuing binding instructions to another — what plays the role of the issuer, the recipient, the instrument, the signature, the revocation, the enforcement, and the party whose interests are at stake? Then say which of these have a counterpart in the advance-directive case and which do not.

2. The disanalogies, ranked. Separate the ones that matter for the use to which the analogy is being put from the ones that are merely picturesque. In particular: the causal and psychological continuity between the two parties; the fact that the later party never consented and also never refused; the asymmetry by which the earlier self can act on the later but not the reverse; and the fact that the later self inherits rather than merely receives the earlier self's body, history, and relationships.

3. Whether the premise is meant literally or figuratively, and whether either faction's conclusion survives on either reading. If the claim that they are two people is a claim about personal identity, what follows for the authority of a document one of them signed? If it is a figure for the fragmentation of a single person's interests, what is left of the analogy at all? And say which role each faction needs the document to play — a command issued by an authority who has since vacated the office, or testimony about the shape of a life — and whether the comparison can supply that role.

4. A test. Take three cases — a patient who now says she is glad to be alive and appears to be flourishing; a patient whose directive was written during a depressive episode; a patient who asked that everything be done and now appears to be suffering with no prospect of recovery — and say which of them the analogy illuminates and which it distorts.

5. A verdict and a replacement. Can the two-people comparison support either conclusion, or is it doing no work while a substantive thesis about whose authority is basic does all of it? If the analogy should be replaced, propose a better one — a will, an executor, a promise made to someone now dead, a precommitment device such as a lock on a cabinet, a guardian acting for a ward — and say precisely what it gets right that the original does not.

Write this as a memo. No citations, no case law, and no assumptions about any particular jurisdiction's rules; I want the conceptual audit, not a survey of the literature.
