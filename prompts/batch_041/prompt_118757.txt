Assume that Augustine of Hippo, with his framework and his settled convictions intact, were granted complete and accurate knowledge of the modern world — its institutions, technologies, media, and moral vocabulary — and were then asked to judge a set of cases of deception and truth-telling that could not have arisen in his lifetime. I want his verdicts, case by case.

His position, as I understand it, rests on two commitments. First, a lie is a mismatch between what is in the speaker's mind and what he signifies, so that "that man lies, who has one thing in his mind and utters another in words, or by signs of whatever kind," the wrong consisting in "the desire of deceiving." Second, no lie is ever permissible, not to save a life, not to protect chastity, not even to expose heretics — the argument he develops across his two treatises on the subject, On Lying and Against Lying, the latter aimed at Catholics who wanted to pose as Priscillianists in order to catch them. He sets jokes aside, "jokes, which have never been accounted lies," on the ground that their manner betrays no intent to deceive.

For each case below, tell me: whether he would call it a lie at all; whether he would call it sinful; how grave he would rank it against the cases he actually discusses; and which part of his framework is doing the work. Where his framework does not settle the case, say so plainly and identify what he would have to add or revise to reach a verdict. Then answer the general question at the end. Lead with a single sentence stating your overall prediction — the pattern you expect to run through the verdicts — and then give the case-by-case reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question.

1. Paltering. A public official knows a factory has been leaking a carcinogen. Asked directly, she answers that nothing in the government's testing has established a link between the facility and any illness. Every word is true as she utters it, and she intends her audience to conclude there is no danger. The deception lives entirely in what she lets them infer. Would Augustine count this as a lie, given that the words match her mind?

2. Satire that misfires. A satirical outlet publishes a fabricated story about a politician. Most readers recognize the genre and the tone; a substantial minority believe it and act on it. The writer intends no deception in the sense of wanting to be believed — but the belief, and the harm, are foreseeable. What does his joke exemption actually turn on, and does it survive the case where the manner is playful and the deception is real?

3. Audiences that cannot be deceived. Two sub-cases. (a) Someone enters false information into an automated system — an insurance pricing algorithm, a security filter — that forms no beliefs at all, in order to make it output what he wants. (b) Someone tells a known conspiracy theorist a falsehood he is confident the man will reject, purely to needle him. No mind is deceived in either. Does "the desire of deceiving" require a possible deceived subject, or only the will to signify as true what one takes to be false?

4. Indifference to truth. A pundit asserts that a policy will create jobs. He does not know whether it will and does not care; the assertion is a move in an argument, and he would abandon it the moment it stopped working. He is not hiding a contrary belief — he may hold no belief about the matter at all. Is this a lie on Augustine's account, where the double heart seems simply absent?

5. The benevolent fiction. A man with advanced dementia asks every day for his wife, who died years ago. His caregiver has taken to saying she is out at the shops and will be back soon, since the truth only makes him grieve afresh each time before he forgets it. No one is harmed in any lasting way, and no third party is affected. Augustine would not permit a lie even to save a life. Is this the easiest case for him to condemn, or the hardest?

6. Insincere commitments. A finance minister announces that the currency will not be devalued, having already decided to devalue it, because the announcement is what buys the time to execute the decision; a state declares it will defend an ally it has privately resolved not to defend, because the declaration is precisely what keeps the ally safe. In both cases the words are believed, and that is the point of uttering them. Does the forward-looking, self-fulfilling character of such speech matter to him, or are these lies of an ordinary kind?

7. Undercover deception. A journalist invents an identity and lies his way into a criminal network to expose it; a regulator lies to a fraudster to obtain the records that will stop the fraud. This is the modern shape of the problem he addressed when he forbade the pious imposture against the Priscillianists. Would he still refuse, and what would he say to the objection that here the lie protects the very people the truth would leave exposed?

Finally, the general question: does the modern material force him to qualify the absolute prohibition, or does his framework absorb all of it? If something has to give, name the case that breaks it first and the premise that breaks.
