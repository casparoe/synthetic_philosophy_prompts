I am a student in a course on bargaining and social choice, and I have been trying to understand what a familiar arbitration practice commits us to. In final-offer arbitration, as used in baseball salary disputes and in some public-sector labor disputes, the arbitrator must choose one of the two final offers submitted by the parties; splitting the difference is not permitted. Yet the chosen figure often lands near the midpoint of the two offers, and parties seem to anticipate this by moderating their offers. I want you to reconstruct the philosophical commitments this practice presupposes. Does it presuppose a substantive theory of fair division, or only a procedural view on which the arbitrator's job is to pick one of two already-legitimate claims? Does it presuppose that the fair outcome is the point at which each side's gain relative to the status quo is the same proportion of its maximum feasible gain, as in the Kalai-Smorodinsky solution, or is that merely one interpretation among several that the rule could bear? What does the no-split rule assume about the arbitrator's role, the parties' incentives, and the difference between bargaining power and desert? Does the practice require interpersonal comparisons of utility, or can it be reconstructed with ordinal preferences alone? In particular, does it implicitly demand a solution that is monotonic in each player's maximum feasible gain, as Kalai-Smorodinsky is, and therefore willing to violate independence of irrelevant alternatives, or does it demand the reverse? Finally, tell me which parts of the practice strictly presuppose a contested philosophical view, which parts are underdetermined and could be licensed by rival theories such as Nash bargaining or a simple egalitarian rule, and what would have to change in the practice if the Kalai-Smorodinsky commitment were replaced by one of those rivals.
