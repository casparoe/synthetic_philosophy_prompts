I retired a few years back from a career in biology (social insects, mostly — ants and termites) and I've been filling my new free time reading philosophy, which has been way more fun than I expected. Anyway, I keep running into this African idea about persons — that you're only really a person through other people, that personhood is something the community gives you and that you can gain or lose, rather than just a fact about what's going on inside your skull. Having spent thirty years arguing that an ant colony is in some ways a better unit of analysis than the ant, I'm probably way too sympathetic to this, so I want a case that puts it under real pressure.

Here's what I'd like you to build for me. A single realistic scenario — present day, no science fiction, no brains in vats, nothing that needs exotic technology — in which two views disagree about whether someone counts as a person (or has full moral standing, or fully counts in the moral math; you tell me which of those is the cleaner thing to actually test). View A: personhood is relational and comes in degrees, it's conferred and sustained by the community someone is embedded in and can be withdrawn by it. View B: personhood is intrinsic, you have it in full as long as you've got the relevant capacities — rationality, self-awareness, the capacity to suffer, whatever the standard list is.

Some constraints, because I've noticed I can rig these things without meaning to:

- Everything about the person's intrinsic capacities stays fixed. The only thing that varies — across the case, or across two versions of it — is their standing in the community. If the person is damaged in the head, or the case only works because they're a saint or a monster, you've cheated.
- No leaning on a fetus, an animal, or someone already dead. I want a full-grown human being, so both views are being asked about the same object and can't wriggle out by pointing at a different one.
- The disagreement has to fall out of the theories themselves, not out of you announcing that the relationalist says such-and-such. If the relational verdict only looks defensible because you made the community obviously right or obviously bigoted, that's not the case I'm after.
- Bonus points if you can get it to come out in the direction that's supposed to be awkward — the relational view granting full standing where the intrinsic view denies it, rather than the other way round. If that turns out to be impossible under my constraints, say so and tell me why, that's a useful answer too.

Then walk me through it. For each view: what's the verdict, and what in the theory forces that verdict. Tell me which single feature of the case is doing the real work, and what happens to the disagreement if I tweak or remove that feature. Then, for whichever view comes off worse, give me the strongest version of its defender saying you've rigged the case. And at the end, tell me what you take the case to show — honestly I'm not sure yet whether I'm after a counterexample to the relational view, a defense of it, or just a clearer picture of what the two sides are actually disagreeing about, so help me work out what the case is even for. Don't cite any literature at me, just argue from the two views as I've described them.
