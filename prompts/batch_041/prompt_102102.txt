I'm writing a feature on the unity of consciousness and I need a worked thought experiment I can put in front of researchers without its being one they have seen a hundred times before. The brief follows.

Build a case in which three distinct tests for how many minds are present come apart in a specified pattern. Let the three tests be: (1) co-consciousness — the events in question are experienced together, as belonging to a single phenomenal field, so that there is something it is like to be the whole system over and above the two candidate streams; (2) co-availability — information present in one stream is, in principle, retrievable by the other, so the streams are not informationally encapsulated from each other; and (3) co-authorship — the streams issue in a single course of action, owned and reported as one agent's. I want the case to fall out in the hard direction: co-availability at its maximum, with each stream able, on demand, to report what is going on in the other; co-authorship intact, one body, one continuous autobiography, one agent whom both streams serve; and co-consciousness absent, the two streams each constituting a separate field, neither experienced by the other, with nothing that it is like to be the pair.

The constraints are strict. No callosotomy or any other surgery, no implants, no hypothetical neurotechnology — a neurologist or psychologist working today could plausibly encounter this case. Do not construct it out of the canonical split-brain findings; the work must be done by the structure of the case, not by resemblance to the famous patients. The scenario itself must fit in one paragraph. And you may not simply stipulate that there are two fields: the separation of the fields has to be generated by the described mechanism, in a way a skeptical reader can inspect.

Then do the analysis. State the case in the terms of each test and give the verdict each test returns, with the reasoning. Identify the single feature of the case that keeps co-consciousness out while letting co-availability and co-authorship in. Tell me what happens if that feature is removed — does the case collapse to one mind, to two, or to something none of the tests can classify? Finally, take the side that fares worst, the view that a system with maximal mutual information and a single agent is thereby one mind, and give me the strongest attack its defender would mount, in that defender's own voice and in a few sentences. I want to know whether the case survives it. The purpose of the exercise is an argument that "one mind" is not a single question but three, so a case that stipulates its verdicts rather than deriving them from the mechanism is of no use to me.
