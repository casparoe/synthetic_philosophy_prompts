I am a third-year undergraduate currently taking a course on the philosophy of the infinite, and I am trying to write a paper on when a supertask — a task with infinitely many steps completed in a finite time — has a determinate outcome. My difficulty is that my own verdicts on the familiar cases will not settle into any pattern I can state, and I would like help finding the principle, if there is one, that they express. I will set out the cases and my verdicts as honestly as I can, including the ones I find embarrassing.

Case 1. A lamp begins off. At time 1/2 it is switched on, at 1/4 off, at 1/8 on, and so on, so that the switches accumulate at t = 1. Verdict: there is no fact of the matter about whether the lamp is on or off at t = 1.

Case 2. A lamp begins at full brightness, and at each of those same times its brightness is halved: 1, then 1/2, then 1/4, and so on. Verdict: again, no fact of the matter. The sequence of numbers 1, 1/2, 1/4, ... converges to 0, but that is a fact about the numbers and not about the lamp; nothing in the setup requires the lamp's brightness at t = 1 to be the limit of its brightnesses before t = 1.

Case 3. A walker covers half the remaining distance to a wall, then half of what remains, and so on. Verdict: there is a fact — the walker is at the wall at t = 1.

Case 4. At step n a machine puts balls numbered 10n − 9 through 10n into an urn and removes ball n. The number of balls present grows without bound at every finite stage. Verdict: the urn is empty at t = 1, and I take this to be a fact rather than a paradox, because every ball is out for good from some stage onward.

Case 5. At step n a machine writes the n-th digit of π in cell n of a tape. Verdict: the tape at t = 1 carries the whole decimal expansion of π, and this is a fact, even though no finite stage carries it.

Case 6. At step n a machine puts ball n into an urn and removes ball n + 1, which has not yet been put in. Verdict: at t = 1 every ball is in the urn. The rule settles each ball's fate individually — each ball is put in at its own step, and any attempt to remove it comes earlier — so I accept the conclusion, absurd as it is that the urn should hold a single ball at every finite stage and infinitely many at t = 1.

Case 7. The machine performs a step at every instant strictly before t = 1, with the steps arranged in a well-ordering in which each step has only finitely many predecessors, so that there is no last step and no step immediately before t = 1. Verdict: no fact of the matter about the final state; there is no stage from which the outcome can be read off.

My tentative principle is this: an outcome is determinate just when the rule settles the state of each individual thing it acts on — each ball, each cell — by some stage before t = 1; where the only candidate for the outcome is a limit that no stage settles, there is no fact of the matter. This delivers cases 1, 2, 4, 5, 6, and 7, and I like it, but it costs me case 3: the walker's position never settles either, so the principle says the walker's arrival is not a fact, and I am not willing to say that. I have tried repairing the principle with a clause to the effect that where the motion is continuous in time the limit is forced, but that clause seems to make case 2 determinate as well, at least if the dimming is continuous, and I do not want to give up case 2.

So: is there a principle, or a small set of competing principles, that captures most of my verdicts, and which verdict does each of them force me to abandon? I would also like to know what the surviving principles predict for cases I have not listed, in particular a supertask that ends with a genuine final step at t = 1, and one in which the fates of some items settle while those of others do not.
