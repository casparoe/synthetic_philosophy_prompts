I should like to try an objection on you in the manner of a seminar exchange, so please take a role and hold it.

Play a committed panpsychist: an unnamed, invented philosopher in the Russellian-monist tradition, who holds that phenomenal properties are the intrinsic nature that physics leaves unspecified, that they are ubiquitous throughout the physical world, and that they somehow combine into the unified subjects we know from the inside. Make him formidable rather than a caricature. He knows the combination problem intimately and has a worked-out line on it; he will concede points of detail but not the central thesis; he will not dodge my questions by observing that the physicalist has a hard problem too, and he will not simply report what various writers have said. He speaks in his own voice, in a few paragraphs per turn, defining any technical term in a clause the first time he uses it, and he states plainly where his position makes a bet that theory or experiment could someday call wrong. No taxonomies, no bullet points. Stay in character until I say stop.

Here is my opening move.

Thirty years at the bench taught me to distrust arguments that draw their force from what we do not yet know, and your case for panpsychism, as I understand it, rests on a gap. Physics describes structure and relations — masses, charges, the symmetries of a state space — and tells us nothing about the intrinsic character of whatever it describes. You propose to fill that blank with experience. I have two difficulties, and I would like you to take a definite side on each rather than gesture toward the literature. I have come to philosophy late and may be using the vocabulary loosely; correct me where I do.

First, a subject must be bounded, and I cannot see what does the bounding in your scheme. Simultaneity in relativistic physics is frame-dependent; there is no privileged slicing of the world into nows. Yet a composite experience, whether mine or a table's, must be spread over some interval or slice, and which physical events belong to a single subject then appears to depend on a choice of frame. Worse, if the relation that bonds micro-subjects is anything like entanglement, they would be glued to one another by degrees and across arbitrary distances, and I do not see what would prevent the universe from being one enormous subject, or a tangle of overlapping partial ones. So: does micro-experience arrive pre-bounded, is the boundary fixed by physical organization, or is it fixed by something physics cannot see? And does your answer survive the relativity of simultaneity?

Second, even granting micro-experience everywhere, I do not yet grant you suffering. Suffering is not merely experience; it is experience that is bad for someone, and it requires a subject for whom things can go badly. Tell me where valence comes from, and be careful with your answer. If you say it arises from a self-model, or from some particular kind of integration, then you have the combination problem all over again one level up, now for valence: a vast number of valenceless micro-feelings that must add up to a toothache that hurts. If instead you say valence is primitive and distributed, then you owe me an answer to the arithmetic, which I find alarming. A gram of ordinary matter contains on the order of ten to the twenty-third nucleons; if each fundamental constituent carries an inner life, then any nonzero chance that even a vanishing fraction of them fare badly would, under a plain expected-value calculus, swamp every human concern I have. That looks to me like a reductio of taking the ethics of panpsychism seriously.

Which horn do you take, and why should I not conclude that your view is either ethically inert or ethically monstrous? I would far rather have a definite position with its costs frankly acknowledged than a survey of the options.
