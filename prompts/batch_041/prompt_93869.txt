I have been reading in the philosophy of mathematics for some years now, and I am increasingly struck by how little the platonist and the nominalist actually manage to say to one another. The platonist, pressed on the familiar Benacerrafian worry that abstract objects cannot stand in causal relations with us and so cannot be known in the ordinary way, tends to reach for one of two moves: either he insists that mathematical knowledge is a priori in a manner that simply does not require a causal story, or he leans on the indispensability of mathematics to our best physical theories and argues that we ought to believe in whatever those theories cannot be rewritten without. The nominalist, for his part, either offers a reconstrual of scientific practice that dispenses with reference to mathematical objects altogether, or retreats to the thought that mathematical sentences, read literally, are simply false and that we should treat them as useful fictions. These exchanges have been rehearsed for decades; each side knows the other's arguments by heart, and yet nobody moves. My own suspicion — which I would like you to test rather than merely endorse — is that the impasse is not really about abstract objects at all, but about the word 'exists'. The platonist hears 'there are numbers' as a claim of the same kind as 'there are electrons', so that the nominalist's denial strikes him as plainly false; the nominalist hears the very same sentence as a claim about what must be added to the inventory of the world, so that the platonist's affirmation strikes him as a piece of metaphysical extravagance. If that is right, then much of the heat may be generated by a word that the two sides are using to different ends.

I want a diagnosis, not a verdict. Is this a substantive disagreement about the facts — a genuine dispute about what there is — or is it a verbal one about the meaning of 'exists', or perhaps two groups answering different questions under a single heading, one about truth-makers and one about ontology? Or is some hidden formal or empirical question doing the work, such that a theorem or a reconstruction would settle it? I am especially interested in whether the sides differ about which cases count as data (do we take mathematical practice at face value, or our physical theories, or our causal scruples?), or about standards of explanation, or about starting intuitions that neither side can argue for. And I want an honest account of what would count as progress given your diagnosis: a distinction, a formal result, a change of question — or an argument that no progress should be expected here and why. Please write your answer in British English.
