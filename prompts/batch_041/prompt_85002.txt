I'm building a unit for my ethics seminar on wether everyday conventions are moral obligations or just manners we police, and I want to pin down what I actually think before I teach it. My gut verdicts, case by case: cutting to the front of a long queue to ask a one-word question, fine; cutting to buy a ticket, not fine; jaywalking across an empty road at midnight, fine, illegality and all; standing on the left of an escalator when everyone else stands right, not fine, mildly, and I'd move if asked; taking a call on speaker in a quiet train carriage, not fine; leaving my trolley loose in the car park instead of the corral, not fine, mildly; as a tourist somewhere tipping is expected, stiffing the waiter, I don't think it's wrong, though it feels bad; walking into someone's house with my shoes on when the household takes shoes off at the door, not fine.

The principle I'd most like to defend is that a convention binds me morally just when breaking it would leave someone who was counting on it worse off. But that runs jaywalking and the escalator together -- nobody's counting on the empty road, plenty are counting on the left lane. The rival I keep drifting to is that a convention binds when what it protects isn't arbitrary: any side of the escalator would do, but not any wage for the waiter or any floor for the host. Which oddly lets the escalator off. Is there a best version of either, stated precisely, with the case that forces each clause? Then run it on cases I haven't given, especially the visitor ones: if I didn't know the local convention, am I off the hook, and does it matter whether I could have known? Finally, say which of my verdicts the principle can't save, and what would change your mind about the crux.
