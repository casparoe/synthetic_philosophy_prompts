I'm a third-year PhD student (philosophy, though the chapter I'm stuck on has dragged me deep into decision theory), and I've got a pattern I can't find a name for.

Two instances. My flatmate will cheerfully take a bet whose odds she doesn't know, as long as the menu of outcomes is fixed and she's seen the whole menu — "fifty down or two hundred up, no idea which, fine". Give her the same bet with the loss uncapped, where what she owes depends on something the other party decides later, and it's a completely different kind of no, even though in both cases she doesn't know the probabilities. Pressed on it, she doesn't say she doesn't know the probability; she says she doesn't know what she'd be ruling out. Second instance: she'll happily bet on a sealed urn as long as you tell her it contains exactly 50 black and 50 white and just refuse to say which ball is which — counts known, arrangement hidden. Reverse it (counts hidden, but a closed and known set of outcomes) and she's still fine. So the discomfort isn't tracking ignorance as such; it flips on whether the hidden thing is the weights or the menu.

I know the Ellsberg setup, and the risk-vs-uncertainty distinction, and the known-unknowns vocabulary, but those all seem to hold the set of outcomes fixed and vary only the numbers. Does my pattern have an established name? Is it just Knightian uncertainty taken seriously, as opposed to how it gets oprationalised in the lab, or is it a seperate thing? Is "I don't know if there's a tiny chance of something catastrophic" the same phenomenon or a different one? And if there's no name, coin one and tell me its edges — what it covers that plain ambiguity aversion doesn't, and what it wrongly sweeps in. A bare label won't help me; I need the carving.
