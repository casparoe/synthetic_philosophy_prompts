I don't have any academic background in philosophy — I studied accounting and now run a small business — but I've been thinking a great deal on my own about the worry that people are handing their thinking over to chatbots, and there's one comparison that keeps being used against me, and that I've caught myself using too. The comparison is this: asking a language model to explain or work out something is like using a calculator for arithmetic. We stopped doing long division by hand and nobody believes our grasp of numbers was damaged; we moved the mechanical part of the work onto a machine and kept the understanding, and the machine's output is something we can always check for ourselves. A friend of mine who teaches secondary-school mathematics leans on this hard: his students can no longer do long division, and he regards that as progress rather than decline, since the skill they lost was never the point. The conclusion everyone draws is that the anxiety about depending on AI — about outsourcing judgment rather than merely computation — is nostalgia or snobbery dressed up as epistemology, and that there is no new problem here, only a new tool.

I'd like you to audit the comparison rather than accept or reject it wholesale. Begin with the correspondence: set out explicitly what in the calculator case is meant to line up with what in the chatbot case — the machine, the user, the skill that gets displaced, the check on the output, the competence that is supposed to survive untouched — and a table is welcome if it makes the mapping clearer. Then tell me where the mapping breaks, and be discriminating about it: I don't want the general observation that AI is more opaque, I want the specific joints at which the parallel fails, sorted into those that genuinely matter for the conclusion people are using the analogy to reach and those that are merely colourful differences. I'm especially interested in what the calculator picture quietly assumes — that the displaced competence was separable from the competence needed to verify the output, that the tool was acquired after the skill rather than instead of it, that its deliverances remain checkable by the very person who no longer has the skill, that it reports nothing and so makes no claim on our trust. If you think some of those assumptions hold for chatbots as well, say so; if the analogy can be repaired, show me how; and if there is a better comparison that carries the same anti-panic conclusion, give me that instead. Include at least one respect in which the calculator comparison makes AI look worse than it really is, so that I can see the audit isn't rigged in one direction.

Be decisive about the upshot: I want a verdict on whether the conclusion — no new problem, only a new tool — survives the disanalogies once they are laid out, not a survey of what various people think. Tell me which single consideration, if it turned out to be true, would flip your verdict, and where in your own reasoning you would place the least weight. Finish with a final line beginning with the word Verdict, a colon, and then one sentence of no more than about twenty-five words stating whether the analogy holds and whether the conclusion it is being used to support survives.
