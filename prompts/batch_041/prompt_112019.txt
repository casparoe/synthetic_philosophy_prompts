Okay so I've been lurking on rationalist blogs and forums since roughly the glory days, and I run a monthly meetup in my city — about 14 people, mixed backgrounds, a few grad students, a couple of engineers, one guy who will argue about literally anything. 90 minutes, one room, no budget. I want to do a session on Goodhart's law, because everyone nods at the slogan and almost nobody I've met can actually tell the different failure modes apart, including me on a bad day.

Here's the thing I keep running into. People treat Goodhart as one phenomenon, but there's a four-way split that keeps getting re-derived and it's the actuallly interesting part:

1. Regressional. You picked the measure because it correlated with what you cared about. But the correlation is imperfect — think of the measure as goal-plus-noise. When you then select on high measure values, you're partly selecting on high noise, so the goal doesn't rise as fast as the measure does. Nobody has to be scheming; it happens to a scatter plot.

2. Extremal. The measure tracks the goal fine across the range you've actually seen. You're now pushing to values you've never seen, out in the tails, where the relationship may just stop holding.

3. Causal. The measure is a downstream effect of the goal, or a proxy that sits on the causal path between your action and the goal. Intervene directly on the measure and you've cut the path, so the number moves and the goal doesn't.

4. Adversarial. There's an agent on the other side optimizing against you, and it deliberately finds and exploits the gap between measure and goal.

The point I want people to *feel*, rather than hear me announce, is that these are seperate failures with different fixes, and that conflating them makes you apply the wrong fix — like trying to "align the incentives" of a scatter plot, or treating a regression artifact as sabotage.

All four modes should show up in what *happens*, ideally as escalating rounds, so that by the end they've lived through all four and can tell them apart on their own. I'd especially like the regressional and extremal ones to hit them by surprise — they think they've solved the previous round's problem and then walk straight into the next one.
