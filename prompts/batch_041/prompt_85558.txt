I am a third-year PhD student working in formal epistemology, and I want to write a short story that does for the Dutch book argument what the best philosophical fiction does for a philosophical position: makes a reader feel the mechanism working rather than nod at a label. Here is the apparatus I would like you to treat as fixed, so that the story is exact rather than impressionistic.

A person's degrees of belief can be read off the prices at which she will take either side of a ticket that pays one unit if some proposition turns out true: a ticket priced at her credence is, by her own lights, a fair ticket, and she will buy or sell it at that price. Given a set of such prices that fails to satisfy the probability calculus, a counterparty can assemble bets, each one individually acceptable to her, whose sum leaves her down whatever happens. That is the synchronic case, and it needs only her present credences. What interests me more is the slow case, in which the exploitation runs across time: she sells a ticket cheaply today and buys the same ticket back expensively tomorrow, or the reverse, because her price for it shifts in a way she already expects. Each transaction is fair at the moment she makes it; the sequence is not. The profit in that second case is real only because she keeps trading at her own prices on both occasions, and only because the sums at stake are small enough that money means to her what it means to her counterparty.

Please write a short story, roughly 1,200 to 2,000 words, built on the diachronic case, subject to the following constraints.

- No lectures and no essayistic asides. The mechanics must be carried by scenes, transactions, and dialogue. If a character explains the argument, the explanation must be motivated by who she is and what she wants; the narrator must never turn teacher.
- The exploitation has to be fair play. Every bet must be one the protagonist accepts on her own terms at the moment she accepts it: no concealed information, no bets she would refuse, no coercion, no exploiting a mistake she could not have avoided. The story fails if a reader can say she was cheated.
- Make the exit routes visible. Someone in the story should notice that the whole thing depends on her willingness to trade at her own price in both directions and at the offered size, and should try to use that: by refusing to trade at all, by quoting one price at which she will buy and a different price at which she will sell, or by insisting on stakes large enough that the money stops being linear in what she actually cares about. Whether this resistance rescues her, or merely leaves her incoherent at greater expense, must be left genuinely open.
- The philosophical stake is whether being exploitable and being irrational are the same thing. Do not resolve it. End at a point where a reader feels the pull of both answers, and where it is clear that the two answers would license different verdicts about her life and not merely about her betting book.
- Pick one concrete world and commit to it: a family firm, a ship, a betting shop, the kitchen of a research group, a wedding. No seminar-room philosophers and no anonymous graduate students trading on a chalkboard.
- Do not name real philosophers or cite published work; where the story needs a position to have authority, let a character hold it rather than quoting it.

For the shape of the case, something like an agent who knows she will be terrified after tomorrow's diagnosis, or swept up by tomorrow's pitch, and who prices her tickets accordingly, is exactly what I have in mind — though you should invent your own if you prefer.

Prose fiction only: no epigraph, no section headings, and no closing paragraph explaining what the story was about.
