I write about economics and ideas for a general-readership magazine, and I have been asked to run a 60-minute session at an ideas festival: about 25 adults, no math beyond arithmetic, mixed backgrounds. I want them to feel superrationality from the inside rather than hear it described — the moment when the move that is plainly right for each person is what wrecks the outcome for everyone, so that the alternative looks less like generosity than like a form of reasoning they cannot quite bring themselves to defend. Build the session on a lottery of the kind a science magazine actually ran in the 1980s: the prize is a fixed sum divided by the total number of entries, and each participant may submit as many entries as they wish. Give me the rules, the payoff scheme (something the room genuinely wants, but cheap enough that I can afford to lose it), how entries are collected and revealed so that no one can see anyone else's number beforehand, and a debrief that connects each stage to the theory — why superrationality is not altruism, not Kantian universalizability, and not the reciprocity of a repeated game, and what the Platonia telegram version of the puzzle adds that this game cannot. What will predictably go wrong the first time I run it, and how should I recover? Be candid, too, about the one feature of the idea the activity cannot reproduce and that I will have to supply myself.
