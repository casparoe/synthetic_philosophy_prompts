You are an expert in the philosophy of probability who also works as a hands-on quantitative risk analyst — someone who knows the technical literature on what probability statements actually mean and has spent years defending real numbers in front of skeptical review committees. Work carefully and deliberately. Before you write the procedure itself, spend some effort brainstorming: enumerate the distinct things a probability claim could be asserting (a long-run frequency in an actual reference class; a propensity or disposition of a physical setup; a degree of belief rational for an agent with given evidence; a logical or evidential degree of support), and note where these come apart in practice — cases with no frequency data, single-event predictions, reference classes that can be gerrymandered, model-based estimates that are not frequencies of anything observed.

I am the lead quantitative risk assessor at a national regulatory agency. My team produces probability estimates that go into safety decisions — whether to approve a substance, whether to order an inspection, whether to mandate a retrofit — and each estimate has to survive a public hearing and a sign-off by our chief scientist. We are not philosophers, we have roughly a week per estimate, and we keep getting stuck in the same fight: one colleague insists a number is meaningless unless it is a frequency, another treats it as whatever our best-informed expert believes, and a third talks about the inherent chance built into the physical system. I want this fight turned into something my team can actually run.

Please write a decision procedure we can follow for any proposed probability estimate. Give me the questions to ask, in order; for each question, what counts as a sufficient answer and what counts as a failure to answer; where the procedure terminates with a refusal to state a number at all; and, crucially, where the procedure must hand the matter to a person because the remaining question is one of values rather than facts — for instance choosing a reference class, choosing a prior, weighing mechanistic against statistical evidence, or setting an acceptable risk. The procedure should keep what the competing interpretations of probability actually claim while being usable by an engineer or statistician with no background in the philosophy, and it should be explicit about which distinctions have to survive that simplification and which can be dropped. Include a short set of tests my team can apply to a candidate number to see whether it is being used consistently with the interpretation it is supposed to be an instance of.

Then run the procedure on two estimates and show the work:

1. The probability that a novel food additive causes a specific adverse health effect in humans, where the evidence is almost entirely mechanistic — in vitro assays, an animal study, structural analogy to a known toxicant — and there is essentially no human frequency data.
2. The probability that a particular dam will fail within fifty years, where there is a sparse historical record of failures of similar structures and extensive engineering models with quantified uncertainties.

At least one of these should come out in a way I will not like — say so plainly and explain why the procedure forces that result rather than softening it. End by telling me where this procedure will misfire, what it cannot decide, and the honest limits of translating these interpretations into a checklist.
