For my advanced ethics seminar I am writing a paper on the difference between a moral preference and a moral bias, and before I defend any particular account of the distinction I want to be clear about what such an account would have to accomplish. Would you write me the desiderata — the specification that any adequate theory of the preference/bias distinction must meet?

I would like each requirement stated, followed by the case or consideration that motivates it, so that I can see why it is a requirement rather than an assumption. The cases I keep returning to are these: a classmate who was raised in a household where a certain practice was ordinary and who now finds it morally unobjectionable but cannot give a reason for that attitude; a person who feels visceral disgust at a harmless practice and reports the disgust as a moral verdict; a student who sincerely believes that grading should weight whatever he happens to be good at; someone who, having been denied a range of options all her life, reports that she prefers the narrow life she has; two communities each of which regards the other's settled moral outlook as a distortion; and someone who sincerely disavows a bias while his behavior keeps betraying it. I would also like the account to say something about the asymmetry I notice in myself — that I am quick to call other people's views biased and slow to call my own — and about what happens when the criterion I use to call your judgment biased, applied honestly to mine, calls mine biased too.

The requirements I have already guessed at, which you should feel free to correct, add to, or reject, are: that the account not define bias by the content of the judgment, since the content is precisely what I am trying to explain; that it allow bias to be present without the agent's awareness and even against his explicit endorsement; that it still leave room for legitimate moral diversity, so that not every preference becomes a bias; that it not reduce to majority opinion or to whatever a fully informed impartial observer would say without independent argument for why that observer settles the matter; that it distinguish, or explain why it cannot distinguish, bias in a moral judgment from error about non-moral facts; that it handle the case of preferences shaped by unjust or restricted circumstances; and that it be usable, at least sometimes, by an ordinary person applying it to her own case.

Two things matter to me most. First, tell me which of the requirements are genuinely constitutive of an adequate account and which are merely commitments of one camp, and flag any subset that cannot be jointly satisfied — if the list is internally inconsistent, that is itself the most interesting result, and I would rather find it now than halfway through the paper. Second, tell me which single requirement does the most to exclude otherwise popular accounts.

Finally, score the broad families I have encountered against the list: the view that treats bias as a defect in the psychological process that produced the judgment, the view that treats it as divergence from an ideal or corrected standpoint, and the deflationary view that the preference/bias line is itself only a rhetorical weapon one side uses against the other. Keep this part brief; the criteria are what I actually need. I am not asking for readings or citations — I want the specification itself, worked out on its own terms.
