I am setting the final essay for an upper-level philosophy of science course, and I need one question on Popper's definition of verisimilitude, worth 20 marks and answerable in about sixty minutes, that cannot be passed by reciting the familiar story that the definition was refuted in 1974 because it makes no false theory closer to the truth than any other. The question should make candidates explain why the collapse is structural rather than a technical slip — why any account that identifies a theory's content with the class of its logical consequences and ranks theories by containment relations among their true and false consequences cannot, without further restriction, order two false theories. Candidates must say whether either repair still answers the question Popper was asking or quietly changes the subject, and must address the objection that the first family merely relabels the problem it was meant to solve.

Please supply the question, a model answer of roughly 700 words, and a marking scheme built around five must-hit points totalling 20 marks, each with a one-line note on the error that typically costs candidates that point. The model answer should commit to a definite verdict on whether the consequence-based approach can be salvaged and defend it, rather than surveying the options and leaving the choice open.
