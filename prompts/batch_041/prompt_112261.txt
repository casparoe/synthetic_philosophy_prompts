I am putting together a one-page handout for an advanced undergraduate seminar on twentieth-century analytic philosophy of religion, and I want a decision tree that sorts the positions one can take on the meaningfulness and possible truth of the claim that a person survives the death of their body, with A. J. Ayer's own shifting views occupying several of the leaves. The tree should be built from concrete cases rather than from labels. Start with a case in which a medium reports a message that only the deceased could have known: does the claim that the deceased survives have observable consequences at all, and if so, are they the sort a finite observer could in principle check? Then a case in which a person's memories and character are gradually transferred to a new body: does survival require the continuation of the same person, or is a causal trace or an impersonal influence enough? Then a case in which a subject undergoes a near-death experience and reports seeing the room from above: does that count as evidence for a disembodied survivor, or is it a brain event? Then a case in which a disembodied voice speaks with the deceased's memories: is the surviving consciousness continuous with the ante-mortem person or a fresh entity? From those branch questions, the leaves should include at least: Ayer's early strict-verificationist verdict that survival claims are literally meaningless unless they are given an empirical interpretation; a weak-verificationist or interpretationist position on which the claim is meaningful but evidentially unsupported; a logical-construction view of the person on which survival is conceivable but not verifiable; a naturalistic reading of near-death experiences as brain events rather than evidence of survival, the position Ayer gravitated toward after his own experience in the late 1980s; a Lockean memory-continuity view; a bodily-resurrection view; a reincarnation view; a mystical-absorption view; and an eliminative-materialist view on which the claim is meaningful but false. For each leaf, give the name of the position (or mark it unnamed if no one defends it), one sentence on its most awkward commitment, and a note on which branch answers would have to change to move it to a neighboring leaf.

The tree should be built from the logical space first — the combinations of answers the branch questions allow — and only then populated with the named positions, so that we can see which combinations are unoccupied. For every unoccupied combination, say briefly whether it is empty because the view is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. Where two leaves cannot be separated by any of the branch questions as phrased, flag them as indistinguishable by this instrument rather than forcing a distinction. Keep the whole thing to a single page: at most ten leaves, no more than six branch nodes, and no reading list or citations. The point is not to survey the literature but to give students a diagnostic map they can use to locate their own pre-theoretical commitments and see where Ayer's various positions fall on it.
