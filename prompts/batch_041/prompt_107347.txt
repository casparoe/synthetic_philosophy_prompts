I've got a pattern I keep noticing and I don't know what it's called, if anything.

I write software for a living, and here's what started it. Say you build a little gadget that takes inputs and gives outputs, and you're only ever allowed to see the outputs. Now two totally different insides — one that's secretly a giant lookup table, one that's secretly a handful of if-then rules — happen to produce the exact same output for every input you could ever give it. Not every input you've tried; every input that's even possible. So no matter how long you poke at it, you'll never be able to tell them apart, not even in principle.

Here's the part I can't name. When I read about this kind of setup (I've seen it come up with whether other people really have experiences or just act exactly like they do — if the acting matches down to the last detail, is there a fact of the matter?), people react in three different ways and I can't tell which one is supposed to be the serious position:

- there's no fact of the matter at all, the question is malformed, like asking whether 7 is purple;
- there is a fact of the matter, one of the insides is really the case, we just can never know which;
- the simpler inside is the real one, or probably is, so you can actually get it right by guessing well.

Those can't all be right. Is there a name for the situation itself — two candidate "real" insides of a system split apart by information that's inaccessible even in principle, not just info nobody's bothered to collect yet? And is there a name for each of those three moves? I've heard "underdetermination" but my sense is that's about evidence we could get if we tried, and it doesn't say anything about the simplicity move. Also, and this is the bit that actually bugs me: is there a real difference between "we can't know" and "there's nothing to know" here, or are those the same claim dressed up differently? People swap them like they're interchangeable and I dont buy it.

Not after a reading list — I just want to know if the thing has a name, what the nearest things are that aren't quite it, and how you'd carve it.
