I'm a PhD student in epistemology, and I'm putting together the handout for one week of a graduate seminar I'm co-teaching on self-knowledge. The week is devoted to the anti-luminosity argument, and in office hours I keep finding that students run three quite different things together: the claim that a mental state is luminous, in the sense that whenever you are in it you are in a position to know that you are in it; the claim that introspection is self-intimating, in the sense that whenever you are in it you actually know, or at least believe, that you are; and the claim that introspection is infallible, in the sense that whenever you take yourself to be in it you really are. Layered on top of those is the KK principle — if you know something, you know that you know it — which concerns knowledge rather than mental states generally, and which I suspect comes apart from all three of the above, though I want the handout to show exactly where the separations fall rather than just assert that they exist. (My co-teacher thinks the handout is a waste of time and wants to just assign the reading; I think a one-page map is the only way anyone learns the shape of this debate, so I'm hoping the tree will settle the argument.) What I want from you is a decision tree rather than an essay: please take the role of an epistemologist who has taught this material for years and who is better at drawing logical spaces than at reciting positions, think carefully about the structure before committing to it, and begin by brainstorming the axes along which these views can differ.

Those axes should include at least the following: whether the privileged access is doxastic or merely a matter of a discriminatory capacity; whether being in a position to know is to be read as safety from error, as the absence of defeaters, or as the bare availability of a reliable route; whether the condition in question is a state such as a pain or a mood rather than a propositional attitude, and whether the guarantee runs from state to cognition, from cognition back to state, or in both directions; whether the access is restricted to occurrent or attended states; and whether it iterates, and if so over knowledge, over belief, or over degrees of confidence. I'd also like one axis my reading group has been chewing on and that I don't recall seeing listed anywhere: whether what is transparent is the content of the state or its epistemic standing — that is, whether what you can always tell is what you are thinking about, or how well placed you are with respect to it. Only once the axes are on the table should you draw the tree, and I'd like you to work out which combinations of answers the axes allow before you name any position.

Each branch node should be phrased as a question a student could answer without already knowing which position it leads to, and anchored in a concrete case wherever possible. Please use at least these three cases as nodes: a slow-drift case, in which a sensation or a degree of confidence changes by imperceptible increments so that at no stage can the subject tell that anything has changed; an expertise-blind case, in which a subject knows something through trained perceptual skill but cannot tell whether she knows it or merely believes it confidently; and a confabulator case, in which a subject's reports about her own state are produced by a process that tracks the state perfectly but that she cannot herself vouch for — the point being to force infallibility, self-intimation and luminosity apart inside the tree rather than in a footnote. Each leaf should carry a short descriptive label and one sentence naming its most awkward commitment; where a combination of answers is one that nobody, as far as you know, has defended, mark it unnamed and say in a sentence whether you take it to be incoherent, merely unattractive, or simply unexplored. I need the leaves to cover at a minimum unrestricted luminosity about all mental states, a restricted version covering only occurrent or attended states, infallibilism without self-intimation, self-intimation without infallibility, the KK principle without luminosity, luminosity about knowledge without KK, negative introspection on its own (knowing that you do not know), a view on which content but never epistemic standing is transparent, and a deflationary leaf on which there is no privileged access at all. Finish with a compact table mapping answer patterns onto leaves, and a short list of the leaf pairs your tree cannot separate, with a note on whether that is because they give the same answers throughout or because they are genuinely different views your axes do not reach. Aim for six to nine branch nodes and roughly a dozen leaves, keep the whole thing short enough to photocopy onto two sides of a page, and don't bother with citations or with attaching philosophers' names — descriptive labels are enough, and I would far rather have a leaf marked unnamed than one carrying a guessed attribution.
