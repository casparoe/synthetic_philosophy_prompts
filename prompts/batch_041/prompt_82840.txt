I have been sitting in on a seminar on Hofstadter's Gödel, Escher, Bach, and we have reached the chapter where he distinguishes the frame message, the outer message, and the inner message: the wrapper that announces that something is a message at all, the instructions that tell a recipient how to decode it, and the content that only appears once the decoding has been done. I want to test that distinction against a real case rather than another puzzle about self-destroying record players, and the case I keep returning to is the Voyager Golden Record. The salient facts, as I understand them: two identical phonograph records were launched aboard the Voyager probes in 1977, each carrying 116 encoded images, natural sounds, musical selections drawn from several cultures, spoken greetings in 55 languages, and printed messages from Jimmy Carter and Kurt Waldheim. The record's cover carries a diagram of the hydrogen hyperfine transition, the 21-centimeter line, as a putatively universal unit of length and time; a radial map of fourteen pulsars keyed to that unit, which fixes both the Sun's location and the epoch of launch; and instructions for playing the record and reconstructing the images from the audio signal. The Pioneer plaques of 1972 and 1973 had already tried something similar with a hydrogen diagram, a pulsar map, and line drawings of two human figures.

What I want from you is a case analysis that runs in both directions. Map the record onto Hofstadter's three levels: say what is doing the work of the frame message, what is doing the work of the outer message, and what counts as the inner message, and be careful, because I suspect the levels do not line up the way the designers hoped. The hydrogen diagram and the pulsar map are meant to be maximally presuppositionless, yet they seem to carry almost no content of their own; the greetings, music, laughter, and images are the content, yet they seem recoverable only by a recipient who already shares an enormous amount of context with us. Is that inversion a defect in the design, or is it exactly what Hofstadter's account predicts? Does the frame message here have to be inferred rather than perceived, in the sense that the object must first be recognized as a message before any of the cover diagrams are read as instructions at all? And what happens to the analysis if the recipient is not a decoder in our sense but simply an intelligence with different perceptual and conceptual equipment, so that the outer message succeeds only by selecting for a receiver already disposed to read physics as a key?

Then turn the lens around and tell me what the case shows about the theory. On one natural reading of Hofstadter, meaning is not located in the message but in the interaction between message and decoder: a mark becomes meaningful only relative to a system capable of reconstructing the right isomorphism with the sender's state. If that is right, the Golden Record looks less like a message sent into the void and more like a projective test for whatever intelligence finds it, and the designers' careful choice of universal constants becomes an attempt to constrain in advance what kind of decoder could be on the other end. Where does that leave the theory: confirmed by the case, or exposed as unable to distinguish a genuine message from a suggestive artifact that merely invites interpretation? If you conclude that the record's most universal elements are also its least meaningful, say whether that is an anomaly for Hofstadter or something his framework already anticipates, and give me a verdict on which of the three levels is doing the real epistemic work here.
