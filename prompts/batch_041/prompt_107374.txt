I'm a novelist, maybe two-thirds through a book set in a made-up country — call it Karistan — that won its independence about a century ago. The thing I keep circling is this: the founding story everybody tells, the folk epic, the national costume, the harvest festival that's supposedly been going since forever — my own invented archive says all of it got stitched together in the 1890s by a schoolteacher and two newspapermen who wanted to hand the new nation a past. So the characters I love are walking around sincerely believing in something that was basically manufactured within their grandparents' lifetime, and I can't decide whether that makes them dupes or just people.

I've read enough Hobsbawm and Anderson to know the obvious moves, and I dont want the obvious moves. What are the most important implications of the whole invented-traditions / imagined-communities way of thinking for a book like mine? I mean concrete ones. Whether my characters can be sincere. Whether "it was invented" is even the right accusation to level at them, or whether the debunking cuts against Karistan and not, say, against every nation equally (in which case what's left of the charge?). Someone also told me Hobsbawm is careful to separate traditions that were genuinely fabricated from old customs that just drift and adapt, and that only some of what he's talking about is the first kind — if that's right, tell me what difference it makes for my story, because I suspect I've been treating the two as one thing. And the seams: when the seams show in the book, should the reader feel the ground drop, or should it feel like nothing much, just Tuesday?

Also, be straight with me about which parts of this are settled among the people who actually work on it and which parts are still genuinely contested — I don't want a confident, tidy summary that quietly takes one side of a live argument and hands it to me as the consensus.
