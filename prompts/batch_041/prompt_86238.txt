I serve on the clinical priorities committee of a regional health authority, and I spent most of last night arguing with a friend — a philosopher who sits on the same committee — about the case we vote on Thursday. The budget covers one of two programmes: a treatment that would arrest a degeneration in forty patients, each of whom will otherwise lose the use of their hands, or a treatment that would prevent quadriplegia in three patients; our analysts report that the forty-patient programme yields more total health gain, and our draft policy allocates by aggregate benefit per unit of cost. My friend says this is precisely the reasoning contractualism forbids: that each person's complaint must be answerable on its own terms, and that no one's complaint may be multiplied or pooled with anyone else's, so the bare fact that there are forty of them cannot, as such, tell against the three. I need a written opinion to circulate before the meeting. Give me a concrete recommendation on the case and, more usefully, a standing rule the committee can apply to cases of this shape, including what to do when the individual complaints are incommensurable and the procedure yields no determinate answer. Engage two things rather than waving them away. First, my friend's argument that the ban on pooling complaints collapses the moment you permit aggregation within a single life, since the line between the two is drawn by nothing more than where one body ends. Second, a proposal of mine that he thinks is incoherent: that we should also weigh what I have been calling the precedent complaint, the objection of someone whose kind of case will reliably lose under the rule we adopt, even though that class is indefinite and includes people not yet born. If the precedent complaint is admissible, has aggregation simply come back in through the back door? End with the bottom line I can defend to non-philosophers, the strongest case for the aggregative policy we already have, and what would have to be true for me to change my mind.
