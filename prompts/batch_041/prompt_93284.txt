For some years now I have been reading popular accounts of animal behaviour, and I keep meeting an argumentative pattern that I have no name for. The examples are the usual ones: a ground squirrel that gives an alarm call and thereby draws a hawk's attention to itself; a honeybee worker that dies when it stings; a vampire bat that regurgitates blood into the mouth of a roost-mate that has failed to feed. One writer explains each case by saying that the behaviour persists because it benefits the colony, or the herd, or the species. Another writer explains the very same case by saying that it persists because it benefits the individual's genes, and that the beneficiaries — the warned squirrels, the queen's other daughters, the fed bat — are relatives in whom copies of those genes happen to sit. My puzzle is this: once the two writers have finished disagreeing, they go on to make identical predictions about every case I can put to them — whether the behaviour should be present, in which species, at what cost, and how it would change if the ecology changed. I have spent a good deal of time trying to construct a case in which the two accounts yield different predictions, and I have not succeeded.

I am not asking for the name of the debate about whether selection acts on genes or on groups; I know that debate exists, and I know roughly what its positions are called. What I want is a name, if there is one, for the shape of the disagreement itself: two parties who accept the same evidence, the same mechanisms, and the same predictions, yet each insists that only one of the two descriptions is the real explanation and that the other is a manner of speaking. When I have put this to people, I have been offered two diagnoses. One is that the dispute is merely verbal. The other is that the two descriptions stand to a single underlying process as two coordinate systems stand to one geometry. Neither satisfies me. The parties are not confused about words: each would accept the other's description as a correct account of the mechanism and still deny that it explains anything. And a change of coordinates is precisely the sort of thing one is supposed to be able to make without remainder, whereas here the choice of description appears to do work — it decides what the two writers notice, what they go looking for next, and which facts strike them as standing in need of explanation at all.

So: is there an established concept for a dispute that no possible observation could settle and that is nevertheless not a quarrel over words? If there is, what is it, and how does it stand to the nearest ideas in the philosophy of science — to the thought that a body of evidence may leave several incompatible theories equally well supported, or to the thought that an explanation is always an answer to some particular question rather than to the world in general? If no single concept fits, I would rather be told so, and given a term of art with a definition and its boundary cases: what distinguishes a disagreement of this kind from an ordinary disagreement about the facts, and from a plain equivocation, and where the edges of the category lie.
