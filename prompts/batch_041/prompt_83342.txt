My closest friend and I have been arguing for the better part of a year about whether compromise between people with very different moral views is ever anything better than a truce, and I would like to stop trading adjectives with her and get a number out of someone. The case we keep returning to is our own. She is a strict animal-welfare person: she thinks the suffering of farmed animals is one of the great wrongs of the age and that anyone who can avoid paying for it should. I am not moved by that at all, but I am moved, more than by anything else, by the thought that humanity might waste its one shot — that we could fumble the coming century and lose the whole future, and that this dwarfs everything else. Somewhere in the middle of one of these arguments we noticed that we could probably make a deal: I would give up meat, which costs me something but rather less than she assumes, and she would put the time and money she now gives to animal charities into the causes I care about, which would cost her a great deal. My claim is that this is not merely two people doing two unrelated things, but that by each of our own standards we would both be better off than we are now — she gets a world with less suffering, I get a future less likely to be thrown away, and neither of us has to concede that the other was right about anything. Her claim is that this is a conjuring trick: that a moral view is not a bundle of preferences you can sell off in pieces, that the moment she trades her animals for my future she stops holding a view and starts holding a portfolio, and that whatever she booked as a gain she would have to write off again the moment she noticed what she had become. What I want from you is your actual credence — a number, not a survey — that for two value systems as far apart as ours there exist joint arrangements that each side would rank higher by its own lights than the present state of mutual disapproval. Count the claim as true if at least one such arrangement exists for at least one pair of systems this far apart, and false if every apparent arrangement of this kind dissolves once you look at it closely.

Break the number up for me, with a rough probability and a line of justification for each part, and then combine them: how much rides on whether a value can be satisfied by somebody else's action at all, so that my going vegetarian is a real gain in her ledger and not merely a change in mine; how much on whether the two of us could actually bind ourselves and tell whether the other had kept the bargain, given that moral commitments are supposed to be unconditional and are therefore the last thing anyone builds an enforcement mechanism around; how much on whether our values would hold still under the trade or would shift the moment we began pricing them, so that the gain is genuine when it is booked and gone by the time it is collected; how much on the people and animals who are not at the table, since an arrangement that suits the two of us by our own lights might still leave the world worse in ways that at least one of us ought to care about; and how much on the baseline I am measuring against, since the alternative to a bargain is not really the status quo but another few years of each of us trying to talk the other out of her views. Tell me which of those terms the final number is most sensitive to, and what would move it — a finding about moral psychology, a worked-out case, or nothing short of a decision about what a value is. For what it is worth, I put myself at about 0.7 that such arrangements exist and deserve to be called gains, and she puts herself at about 0.15; if you can, tell me which of those terms we are most likely actually disagreeing about, as opposed to merely describing differently.
