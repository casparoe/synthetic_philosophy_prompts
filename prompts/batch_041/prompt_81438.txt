**The argument from illusion is not an argument from illusion**

The argument is usually set out like this. A straight stick half-submerged in water looks bent; a white wall lit by a red lamp looks red; a round penny seen at an angle looks elliptical. In each case the object is not as it appears. But the experience is exactly as it would be if the object really were that way. So the experience cannot consist in the object's being that way: what the subject is aware of must be something bent, or red, or elliptical — hence, if not a material object, then a sense-datum, or at any rate some element common to the veridical and the illusory case.

I want to argue that the label is a misnomer, and that the mislabelling has distorted the debate. Notice first that the defectiveness of the particular case does no work in the argument. We do not generally reason from a defective token to a defective type: from a clock that runs slow I do not conclude that clocks are not really instruments for telling the time. Where such an inference is tempting, something else licenses it — normally the thought that the defective token is indistinguishable from the good one, so that I cannot be treating the good case as the paradigm. That is precisely what happens with the stick. The point is not merely that a perceptual claim fails; it is that the subject cannot tell, from the inside, that it has failed. Remove the indistinguishability and the argument collapses. Keep it and the talk of illusion is decoration. The real engine of the argument is therefore a premise about first-person indiscriminability: if two experiences cannot be told apart from the inside, they must be experiences of the same kind.

Once the argument is restated in that form, the standard disjunctivist reply looks misdirected. Disjunctivists typically deny the common element, insisting that the veridical and the non-veridical case are not two species of a single experiential genus. But if the force of the argument comes from indiscriminability rather than from illusion, denying the common element is not enough; what has to be denied is the inference from indiscriminability to sameness of kind. That is a far harder thing to deny, because the indiscriminability itself is not in dispute. A refinement is worth entering here: some disjunctivists distinguish the epistemic question, what the subject is in a position to know, from the metaphysical question about the nature of the experience, and deny a common element only in the first register. If that is the intended view, then what I say next should be read as a request for the metaphysical story rather than as a refutation of the epistemic one.

Here is where I get stuck, and this is the part I most want help with. Suppose the disjunctivist does deny the inference. Then two experiences that the subject cannot distinguish are said to differ in kind. The difference cannot be something the subject is in a position to detect, or the cases would not be indiscriminable. So what makes it a difference in the *experience* rather than a difference in the *world* — the presence or absence of an object on the far side of the subject? The natural answer is that the presence of the object is not an addition to the experience but part of what the experience is. I cannot see how to state that answer so that it is neither trivial, in that everything about a relational state depends on what it relates to, nor question-begging, in that it assumes the very relational view of experience that the argument from illusion was introduced to refute. If there is a third option, I have not found it.
