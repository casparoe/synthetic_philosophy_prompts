You are a philosopher of decision theory with a strong background in probability, expected utility, and the long-running dispute between expected-value maximization and long-run growth as rival accounts of practical rationality. Before you answer, please think carefully and at length, and begin with a short brainstorming section in which you set out the candidate readings of the practice below before you commit to one; then give your settled verdicts.

I am an undergraduate in a decision-theory course, and we have been reading about the Kelly criterion and what is sometimes called the geometric view of rationality. What I keep meeting outside the seminar is a cluster of practices among people who bet or invest for a living, and I would like to know what those practices presuppose philosophically. Here is the cluster, as concretely as I can state it.

(a) Bankroll management. Almost everyone who bets seriously for a living refuses to stake a large fraction of their capital on a single favorable bet, even when the edge is very large. A bet paying even money on a 60/40 proposition is, by expected value, one they should want to take as often and as large as possible; the practice instead is to stake a fixed small fraction of capital.

(b) Fractional Kelly. The standard sizing rule in the professional literature is not full Kelly but a fraction of it, commonly half and sometimes less, and practitioners describe the reduction as prudence rather than as a correction for any specific error.

(c) Screening by expected value, sizing by growth. The same people decide whether a bet is worth taking by asking whether it has positive expected value, and then decide how much to stake by a rule that is not expected value at all. Nobody seems troubled by the combination.

(d) Ruin treated as special. They treat the loss of the entire bankroll as categorically worse than any sequence of losses summing to the same amount. They will give up expected profit to make ruin impossible, and they speak of ruin not as a bad outcome among others but as the end of the game.

I want three things.

First, reconstruct the implicit theory. What conception of practical rationality and of value do these practices only make sense under? Specifically: do they presuppose that the rational objective is the growth rate of capital rather than expected value, and if so, is that a claim about what is valuable, namely that wealth has logarithmic utility, or a claim about what is rational over repeated multiplicative bets, namely that the growth rate dominates in the long run? Are those two claims distinct, and does the practice have to choose between them, or can it run on either?

Second, the tension. Is the combination in (c) coherent, or does it show that the practitioners are really expected-utility maximizers with a concave utility function, in which case the geometric apparatus is doing no independent work and could be dropped? And how should we read (b)? Is half-Kelly a concession to risk aversion, which would contradict the growth-optimal story, or a rational response to the fact that the edge is estimated rather than known, which would keep it inside the geometric framework? Tell me which reading the practice strictly presupposes and which it merely fits well with, and say plainly if the practice is underdetermined between them.

Third, the case I cannot place. A one-shot bet, never to be repeated, offering a 51 percent chance to double the bankroll and a 49 percent chance to lose it: the practitioners in (a) through (d) refuse it, but the geometric story seems to license the refusal only by smuggling in repetition. Does the practice presuppose that all real betting is a repeated game, or does it need an independent, non-instrumental constraint against ruin, a lexicographic priority of survival over growth? If the latter, what is that constraint a commitment to, and is it a commitment about value or about rationality?

Finally, tell me what would have to change in the practice if the presupposed view were replaced by straightforward expected-value maximization, and where the practice would look different if we adopted instead a fully subjectivist expected-utility view with an ordinary risk-averse utility function. State your verdicts plainly, flag every point at which the practice turns out to be philosophically underdetermined, and do not give me citations or a reading list: I want the reconstruction and the argument, not references.
