I am a fourth-year PhD student in philosophy of science, working on imprecise probability and its applications, and I am six months into a placement as the decision analyst for a national regulator that handles chemical approvals, fishery closures, and emissions permits. I sit with a panel of five or six scientists from different disciplines, decisions arrive roughly weekly, each has to be signed off by a director, and I get about two hours per case, which is nowhere near enough to run a proper elicitation. The current practice is to pick one expert's number as the central estimate and, when the case is politically hot, to add a conservative number that nobody can defend except by calling it conservative; the lawyers then want a single number they can write into the decision document, and the economists want it to be an expected value. An approval can cost several hundred jobs or produce a handful of excess cancers, so none of this is hypothetical, and I would like to replace it with a written procedure I can actually run and defend, one that a competent analyst who is not a decision theorist could execute and that I could hand to whoever takes this desk after me.

The hard part is not the arithmetic but the translation, so the procedure has to be explicit about the modelling choices that fix the answer before any decision rule is applied. What I usually receive is: two or three competing model classes, each with parameter estimates from the experts who favour it; a spread of point estimates for the parameter that matters; and occasionally an expert who refuses to give a number at all on the grounds that every model class on the table is wrong. I do not know what to do with that refusal, and I suspect it is not a probability and should not be pressed into service as one. I also have to decide how the set of probabilities gets built: a list of expert point estimates is not yet a set of distributions, treating two parameters as independent is a substantive assumption that will quietly shrink the answer, and the difference between possessing a set of probabilities and possessing a second-order distribution over them matters a great deal, because in the second case I am back inside an ordinary expected-utility calculation and the whole exercise has lost its point. Once the set exists, the rules on offer behave very differently. A cheap filter will often settle the case with no rule at all: if one act beats the other under every distribution in the set, the imprecision is irrelevant and the elaborate machinery is unnecessary. Taking the best worst case is easy to compute but gives veto power to whichever member of the set is most extreme, so an act can be chosen that is worse than its rival under all but one distribution. The more permissive criteria, which only ask that an act not be beaten under every distribution, do not deliver a complete ranking, so two honest analysts can reach different recommendations from identical inputs. And I have read enough to worry that some of these rules make information worthless or even harmful, and that whichever rule I fix in advance will constrain what I can do with the next piece of evidence.

Please write the procedure. Use these headings in this order. (1) "Inputs and preconditions": what I must have in hand before the procedure can run, what to do with the expert who refuses to give a probability, and when to stop and fetch a statistician instead — roughly 250 words. (2) "The procedure, step by step": an ordered set of steps, each naming the question it answers, what counts as a sufficient answer, and where it exits; begin with the cheap filter that determines whether the imprecision matters at all, then say which rule to adopt under which features of the case (irreversibility, who bears the risk, whether the disagreement is about the world or about how much a bad outcome counts), then include a step for producing the single number the lawyers require together with an account of what that number does and does not mean — roughly 700 words. (3) "Case one": apply it to whether to set a tolerable daily intake at a level that would effectively remove a widely used crop-protectant from the market, where the panel's cancer-potency estimates span two orders of magnitude and two of the five members believe the dose-response model class is wrong — roughly 400 words. (4) "Case two": apply it to whether to close a fishery, where the interval around the collapse threshold is wide but the comparison between closing and staying open may come out the same way under every distribution in the set; one of these two cases should land on an answer I will not like — roughly 400 words. (5) "Where this procedure fails": what it cannot settle, how it will misfire, including any phenomenon in which acquiring information leaves my posterior interval wider rather than narrower, any step whose apparent neutrality conceals a value judgment, and what I should tell the director I cannot give her — roughly 300 words. (6) "Bottom line": no more than 150 words, the procedure in miniature, for the version of me who has ten minutes and a hostile audience. I need no citations or reading lists; I need the procedure to be usable, and I need you to be candid about which steps are judgment dressed up as method.
