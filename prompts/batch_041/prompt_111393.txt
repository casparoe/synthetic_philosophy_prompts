Bit of an odd one, but here goes. I've been reading LessWrong since the sequences and ACX since it was SSC, and I ended up running a tiny seminar on probability and self-locating belief for six taught-masters students, so now I have to set there take-home paper. I want one long question on the reference class problem, and I want it genuinely hard rather than "name the problem and shrug".

What bugs me about how the topic is normally taught is that it gets presented as a practical nuisance: you've got a case, several classes contain it, they give you different frequencies, so use the narrowest class you have decent statistics for and move on. I want the students to see that this rule is being asked to do something it can't, because narrowing is a choice rather than a discovery, and every frequency is itself relative to some class, so the rule just relocates the problem one level up. I also want them to face the thought that this may be the same thing as the coarse-graining problem: a chance or a frequency only attaches to an event-type once somebody has decided how finely to slice the types, and nothing in the world does the slicing for you. If that's right, the reference class problem isn't a gap in our data, it's a gap in the idea that chances are simply out there in the world.

Here's the case I want at the centre of the question — please check it's coherent and quietly fix it if it isn't. A woman in her forties has been tested for a gene variant. The variant was picked up by a study of people who had already been referred for testing because of family history, and that study puts carriers at roughly a thirty per cent ten-year risk of a particular cancer. Her clinic's own records for carriers enrolled in its screening programme give a figure nearer five per cent. And the class she would fall into if you let the selection process define it — carriers who only found out because a relative got ill — is precisely the class the thirty per cent came from, so that number is partly a fact about who gets tested rather than about what the gene does. Three nested classes, three numbers, and the narrowest is the most circular.

Two parts to the question. The main part should be about whether any of the going rules (narrowest class, causally relevant class, the class defined by the process that will actually produce her outcome) can be made to work without collapsing into one of the others or into a regress, and whether the failure is in our knowledge or in the thing itself. The short second part should ask whether this is the same problem as self-locating belief — doomsday-style — or only a family resemblance, with the students made to say what would make the difference. I expect the weaker answers to start reciting the doomsday argument in part two instead of answering, so mark accordingly.

Give me the exam question, a model answer of about 700 words for the main part plus a short one for part two, and a marking scheme out of 20 built round five or six must-hit points, each with its marks and a one-line note on how candidates typically miss it, then a sentence on what seperates a first from a 2:1. All of it in British English please, since I'm the one marking it and I can't cope with "analyze". And no invented literature — no reading lists, no "see X 1997". They can't get online in the exam and neither can I. Ta.
