Two of the most celebrated moves in modern thought — Darwin's explanation of design without a designer, and Turing's explanation of computation without a comprehender — are almost always presented as instances of a single pattern: the strange inversion in which competence comes first and comprehension, if it appears at all, is a late and optional by-product. I want a short research proposal that treats the unity of that pattern as an open question rather than a premise. The proposal is for a departmental workshop series on explanation in the cognitive and biological sciences, it should run to about 1,500 words, and it should grow out of the half-formed idea set out below.

The idea. The two inversions may not be one inversion. In the Darwinian case the competence is real and owned by something: the organism is genuinely good at surviving and reproducing, and the explanation tells us where that goodness comes from. In the Turingian case it is far less clear that the competence is owned by anything at all — the machine's being good at arithmetic, or chess, or translation may be a fact about us and the descriptions we bring rather than a fact about the machine. If so, the second inversion does not relocate comprehension, it dissolves the question of where comprehension lives; and that is a much stronger and stranger claim than the one usually advertised.

What I want the proposal to do.

First, state the question so that it has a determinate answer. Something like: do the Darwinian and the Turingian inversions belong to a single explanatory kind, or do they differ in whether the competence they explain is a property of the system or an artifact of the description under which we view it — and is there a criterion that tells us which kind of inversion we are confronting in a new case?

Second, explain why the existing positions leave this open. The standard defenses of the schema treat the two cases as mutually reinforcing: if Darwin showed that design needs no designer, Turing showed that intelligence needs no mind, and the analogy is meant to carry conviction by symmetry. I suspect the symmetry is rhetorical — it holds only as long as no one asks what comprehension is doing in each case. The proposal should argue that the discussion oscillates between treating comprehension as a genuinely explanatory posit that the inversions successfully eliminate, and treating it as a piece of folk psychology with no explanatory work to do, and that neither reading can be applied uniformly across both cases. Argue this by reference to the positions themselves; I will supply the references, so do not construct a bibliography or a literature review by citation.

Third, set out the method, and say why this approach rather than the obvious alternatives. I would like a taxonomy built by working through a small set of cases chosen so that the two candidate readings pull apart: a simple feedback device such as a thermostat; a chess engine; a biological mechanism that is competent without representing its goal, such as an immune response or the proofreading of a polymerase; and a market or a bureaucracy, in which comprehension is genuinely present but distributed across people, none of whom grasps the whole. The proposal should say what distinguishes these cases along the axis that matters, and what would show the distinction to be a real one rather than a verbal one. It should also say how to avoid the trap of defining comprehension in a way that makes the answer come out by stipulation.

Fourth, give an honest treatment of the most serious alternative, namely that my distinction collapses: that owned-by-the-system and artifact-of-description are just the intrinsic and observer-relative ways of describing one and the same competence, that every competence is observer-relative, and therefore that the Darwinian case is no different from the Turingian one. The proposal should say what would have to be true for that collapse to be correct and what would resist it.

Fifth, say what a result looks like and what would count as failure. Success is not a verdict on whether machines understand; it is a usable criterion — a way of saying, for a given case, whether a competence-without-comprehension explanation has discharged the demand for understanding or merely postponed it. Failure would be a criterion that classifies cases only by how we choose to describe them, or one that cannot be applied without first settling contested questions in the philosophy of mind. The proposal should be willing to name those failure conditions.

Finally, be realistic about scope. This is a workshop-length project, so it should stake out the distinction, work it through the four cases, and end with the criterion and its known weaknesses, rather than attempt a survey. Close by making the case for why this framing is worth pursuing rather than the more familiar question of whether machines can think.
