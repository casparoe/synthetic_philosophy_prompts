I'm a third-year philosophy student in a graduate seminar on formal epistemology, and I'm writing a term paper on whether Dutch book arguments can do the normative work they are usually asked to do — whether they can justify the probability axioms and conditionalization as constraints on rational credence, rather than merely describing a consistency property that happens to be mathematically convenient. Before I commit to a thesis, I would like to get my bearings, and I would rather have your reasoning than a reading list, so please do not give me citations or a bibliography.

Please rank the following objections to the Dutch book argument from the one that most threatens its normative force to the one that least threatens it, and assign each a score out of 10 for how much it should worry someone broadly sympathetic to the argument (10 = fatal, 0 = a mere technicality). For each objection, say in a few sentences what it actually turns on and what a defender's best reply would be.

A. The no-betting objection. The argument moves from a claim about an agent's betting dispositions, or about hypothetical bets she would accept, to a claim about her degrees of belief. But an agent can hold a perfectly coherent credence in a proposition while refusing to bet on it at any odds, so operationalizing credence as a betting price may not pick out her credences at all.

B. The sure-loss versus expected-loss objection. The argument typically needs to show that incoherent credences are irrational. But the synchronic results deliver a sure loss only under additional assumptions, and the diachronic results for principles like conditionalization guarantee only a loss in expectation relative to the agent's own credences — which is precisely what an incoherent agent may refuse to concede is bad.

C. The bad-company objection. Many constraints that are not norms of rationality, or that are intuitively irrational, also admit a Dutch book justification. If both good and bad principles can be book-justified, then vulnerability to a Dutch book cannot by itself be the mark of a genuine requirement of rationality.

D. The diachronic-identity objection (this is the one I intend to press). Diachronic Dutch book arguments require that the agent who accepts the first bet be bound by the bets accepted by her later self, and that the bookie be able to hold her to a plan across time. The argument's force therefore seems to depend on a prior assumption that the agent is a single, temporally unified locus of commitment — the very thing at issue when we ask whether a later self must honor an earlier self's constraints. If that unity is assumed rather than argued for, the argument may be circular for its intended purpose.

E. The idealization objection. Dutch books are built by a bookie who knows the agent's credences exactly, is free to offer arbitrarily many and arbitrarily complex bets, and faces no transaction costs or limits on the agent's willingness to bet. Real agents are never exposed to such books, so the argument may establish a fact about an idealized agent rather than a norm for us.

I also have a sixth candidate objection of my own, and I cannot tell whether it is genuinely distinct from the others or merely a relabeling. Please place it in the ranking as well.

F. The representation objection. The argument takes for granted that the agent's credences are already defined over a Boolean algebra of propositions with determinate truth-values, and that compound bets are evaluated as the appropriate sums of her attitudes toward their parts. But if her credences are only partially defined, or if the propositions at issue are ones whose truth is partly settled by what she goes on to do, then the algebra over which the book is built is not her actual representational state, and the incoherence the book reveals may be an artifact of the extension rather than a defect in her.

Then tell me whether F is really a distinct objection or collapses into one of the others, and if it collapses, into which one. Finally, give me a one-paragraph verdict on whether any of A through F, singly or together, should stop me from using Dutch book arguments in the paper as a partial justification of probabilism — and if they should not, which objection I most need to neutralize, and how.
