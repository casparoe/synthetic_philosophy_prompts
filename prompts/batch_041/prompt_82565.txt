I work as a backend engineer at a logistics company, and for the past two years I have run a small monthly interview series that a few colleagues and I put on in a borrowed conference room — thirty minutes of conversation with a visiting researcher, then questions from the room. I have no formal training in philosophy, so I prepare by reading as much of the guest's work as I can and drafting my questions well in advance, which is the part I am asking you to help with. Next month's guest is an applied animal-welfare researcher who works on farmed fish and, more recently, on farmed decapod crustaceans; I would rather not name him, because the position I am about to describe is a composite of claims he has made across several talks rather than the thesis of any single paper. His central claim is that the aquatic animals we farm are a moral blind spot: that fish and shrimp are killed in numbers and by methods that would cause an outcry if they were used on a pig, and that the reason nobody objects is that we cannot easily read their faces. His more provocative proposal, and the thing I would like the interview to circle around, is that farms should be required to publish something like a welfare budget — a single accounting figure per site, in units of adverse-affect-hours per tonne of protein, capped and tradeable, in roughly the way carbon is. I find this genuinely interesting and also suspicious, and I would like the conversation to test it rather than promote it.

Please draft nine or ten interview questions, each with a follow-up keyed to the answer he is most likely to give, so that I am not left improvising when he says the obvious thing and waits for me to move on. The questions should turn on the content of his position and not on his biography, and I would rather have no question at all than a soft one about what drew him to this work. In particular: I would like at least one question that forces him to defend the premise that a fish's experience is the kind of thing that can be added up in the first place, because I keep getting stuck on whether comparing a shrimp's suffering to a salmon's is a measurement or a metaphor, and at least one on whether his budget turns individual animals into interchangeable units, since a cap-and-trade scheme would in principle let a farm buy the right to inflict severe suffering on a few animals by sparing a great many others. I would also like something on the evidence — my understanding is that the case for fish pain rests partly on their having nociceptors and a pallium rather than a mammalian-style layered cortex, and partly on experiments in which they trade off avoiding a noxious stimulus against a reward, and I want him to say how much weight he puts on each strand and what would actually change his mind. Two further topics need covering: the practical one, namely what a serious slaughter standard would even look like if the standard methods are still chilling and asphyxiation, and the strategic one, namely whether a movement that argues for the moral status of shrimp risks looking absurd to exactly the public it needs to persuade. If you can work in a question about what he thinks we should do while the evidence remains genuinely uncertain, and one about whether welfare is exhausted by the absence of suffering or also includes positive states, I would be grateful.

Keep each question self-contained enough that I can read it aloud without having read the papers it responds to, and phrase them in language a competent outsider can follow without being talked down to. I do not need citations or suggested reading; I will handle that part myself. Finally, please order the sequence so that it builds, and let the last question be the one he would least like to be asked.
