Recently I have been reading about relational conceptions of personhood, of the kind associated with Ubuntu. On this picture, a person is a person through other persons; the community is prior to the individual in the order of explanation as well as in time; and personhood is not something one simply possesses from birth, but something one becomes — an achievement, realized through the quality of one's relationships and conduct, and possessed more or less fully by different people. Part of the attraction, as I understand it, is that this avoids the picture of the isolated individual who possesses humanity intrinsically and in full, independently of anyone else. What I cannot see is how the view escapes an implication its defenders would surely reject. If being a person admits of degrees, and if those degrees are earned through one's standing in a community, then an infant, an adult with advanced dementia, or someone who has been exiled or shunned is less of a person than the respected elder next door; and it seems to follow that such people have correspondingly weaker claims on the moral consideration of others. How do defenders of the relational conception handle this? Do they simply accept that moral standing tracks social achievement, or is there something in the view that blocks the inference?
