I have recently moved to Singapore for a two-year posting, and the dinner-table arguments I keep having have made me want to get this straight. A friend of mine — a political scientist by training, not someone who gets things obviously wrong — summarised the performance-legitimacy position for me more or less as follows, and I have since seen versions of it in a magazine essay and in a minister's remarks:

'Legitimacy just is a matter of whether a government delivers growth, security and order. By that test Singapore and China are more legitimate than most democracies, whose governments cannot deliver anything; so the Western habit of lecturing them about rights is cultural imperialism dressed up as philosophy. Nor is there anything special about democratic legitimacy, since no citizen anywhere meaningfully consents — what every state actually runs on is performance. The catch is that performance legitimacy is fragile: it is a running account that must be paid in growth every year, which is why a slowdown is an existential threat, as 1997 showed.'

I suspect that a good deal of this is defensible and that a good deal of it is doing something sneaky, and I cannot tell which parts are which. Please audit it claim by claim: for each of the six or so distinct assertions packed in there, say what a serious defender of the performance-legitimacy thesis would accept as their own view, what is a defensible simplification, what is a misrepresentation, and what is simply false. I am especially interested in whether the summary has quietly swapped a descriptive claim about what makes people comply for a normative claim about who has the right to rule, and whether the practical conclusion — that the criticism is cultural imperialism — survives once the descriptive claim is stated carefully.

One further thing to hold in view. A seminar handout I kept from my undergraduate days distinguished three things that I suspect are being run together here: performance legitimacy as a sociological thesis about the beliefs and acquiescence of subjects; 'output legitimacy' in the sense familiar from EU-governance debates, where results are offered as a substitute for democratic input; and plain state capacity, an empirical property of bureaucracies that has nothing to do with anyone's beliefs. Use that three-way distinction explicitly, saying where the summary's claims fall and where the thesis's defenders actually stand.

I would like a committed answer: do not lay out the options and leave the choice to me. Where the scholarship is genuinely divided, say which side you think is right and why; where correcting the summary leaves its conclusion untenable, say so plainly rather than hedging. No reading list, please — I want the argument, not the bibliography. Write in British English.
