I'm in a seminar on deception and assertion — we spent most of the term on the modern material (what is said versus what is implicated, whether the lying/misleading line is doing any real moral work) and the instructor has now pushed us back to the older sources. I'm stuck on a passage from Augustine's early treatise On Lying, which his own Retractations dates to about 395; the English translation I have is Victorian, and the sentences loop back on themselves. Here is the bit I keep re-reading:

"Wherefore, that man lies, who has one thing in his mind and utters another in words, or by signs of whatever kind. Whence also the heart of him who lies is said to be double; that is, there is a double thought: the one, of that thing which he either knows or thinks to be true and does not produce; the other, of that thing which he produces instead thereof, knowing or thinking it to be false. Whence it comes to pass, that he may say a false thing and yet not lie, if he thinks it to be so as he says although it be not so; and, that he may say a true thing, and yet lie, if he thinks it to be false and utters it for true, although in reality it be so as he utters it. For from the sense of his own mind, not from the verity or falsity of the things themselves, is he to be judged to lie or not to lie. […] But the fault of him who lies, is, the desire of deceiving in the uttering of his mind."

Our discussion got hung up on a case the passage will not settle for me: a spokesperson who says something he believes true, and says it precisely because he expects the audience to draw a false conclusion from it — a true statement made for its misleading effect. He plainly has the desire to deceive, but he has no "double heart" in the sense of uttering what he thinks false, so I cannot tell whether Augustine's definition catches him, and if it does not, whether that is a defect in the definition or exactly the point of it. I would like your help working through the passage on its own terms rather than a tour of the secondary literature: first, what a lie is on this passage and which clause is actually doing the defining; second, what the "double heart" metaphor amounts to and why it lets a true utterance be a lie while excusing a false one; third, whether the clause about the desire of deceiving belongs to the definition or is a separate remark about fault, and whether the two criteria can come apart; fourth, what Augustine would have to say about my spokesperson; and fifth, what all of this implies for the question whether lying and misleading differ morally, given that the same words can be a lie for one speaker and not for another. (The ellipsis in the quotation is mine.) Please answer with exactly five numbered points, one to two sentences each, following that order, with no introduction and no concluding summary.
