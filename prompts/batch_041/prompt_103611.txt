A rational agent must sometimes revise her credences in response to learning a conditional, rather than learning a proposition. Consider the canonical case: her credence is split evenly between being in a red region and a blue region, and conditional on being in the red region she takes two sub-regions to be equally likely. She then receives a message that tells her only that, conditional on being in the red region, the chance of the second sub-region is one quarter; the message says nothing about whether she is in the red region or the blue one. Everyone grants that her new conditional credence in the second sub-region given red should be one quarter; nobody agrees on what her new unconditional credences should be, or on whether there is a single rationally required answer. (This is the so-called Judy Benjamin problem.)

Write the desiderata that any adequate account of updating on conditional information must meet. These should include the cases it must get right: the case just described; learning a conditional with certainty rather than with some intermediate probability; learning that the antecedent holds; learning that it fails; and revising a conditional credence and then discovering that the antecedent is false. They should include the distinctions it must not collapse: between coming to accept a conditional as true and coming to assign it a conditional probability, and between revising a conditional credence and revising an unconditional one. And they should include the structural constraints it ought to satisfy: invariance under equivalent descriptions of the partition, agreement with ordinary conditionalization in the limiting cases, and some conservativeness or minimal-change condition. For each requirement, give the consideration that motivates it. Then state which subset of the requirements you believe cannot be jointly satisfied and explain why — that is the part I most want — and say what this implies about whether the case has a determinate rational solution.
