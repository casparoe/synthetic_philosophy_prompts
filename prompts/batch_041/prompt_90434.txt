Last night a friend and I spent close to three hours arguing about the paradox that gets called the doctrinal paradox (or the discursive dilemma, depending on who is doing the calling), and I came away holding a position I did not expect to hold. The case is familiar enough: three judges, or voters, or members of a committee, must settle a conclusion that follows by modus ponens from two premises. Each of them individually holds a logically consistent set of judgments, but the majority on the first premise and the majority on the second premise both run one way, while the majority on the conclusion runs the other, so that majority voting proposition by proposition yields a collectively inconsistent set, whereas voting only on the conclusion, or only on the premises, yields a consistent set that a majority of the individuals reject. I used to regard this as a curiosity about one peculiar agenda. I now think the standard lesson drawn from it is the wrong one. The standard lesson is that we face a forced choice between procedural fairness and collective rationality, after which the interesting work consists in finding some way to have both — restricting the domain of admissible profiles, adopting a premise-based procedure, or imposing a priority ordering on the propositions so that consistency is purchased at the cost of responsiveness. My own view is that the ideal of the group's having a single consistent, reasoned will is itself what should be surrendered. A democratic body is not a person; requiring its judgments to be closed under logical consequence is a demand we have imported from individual epistemology without ever justifying the import, and once the demand is dropped the dilemma simply dissolves — majorities on each issue, no closure, no problem. My friend, who is writing a dissertation on social choice, thinks this is a dodge dressed up as a dissolution, and we ended the evening without settling it.

What I want from you is not a survey but a case against my position. Which formal results, which concrete cases, and which conceptual objections should move me — ranked from the ones that would be fatal to the ones that would be merely awkward — and which of them would the people who draw the standard lesson themselves concede as counting against their own reading rather than mine? I am especially concerned about three things. First, whether my view is a substantive position at all or merely a redescription of the problem that quietly presupposes what is in dispute. Second, whether it collapses into the deflationary claim that a group's judgments are nothing over and above the output of whatever procedure it happens to use, in which case I have abandoned collective agency rather than reconceived it, and abandoned it to a rival I do not want. Third, whether it can survive the fact that groups must act: a body that has to decide one way or the other cannot act on an inconsistent set, so the inconsistency must be resolved somewhere, and if it is resolved by the procedure, I have conceded the standard lesson after all. I would also like an honest verdict on whether my position has been insulated against refutation — if I am permitted to say that any apparent collective irrationality is simply what a group is like, is there anything left that could count as evidence against me, and if there is not, is that a defect of my view or an acceptable feature of a view at this level of abstraction? I do not need citations or a reading list; describe the results and the cases well enough that I could track them down myself if I chose. Do not lay out the options and leave the choice to me — tell me where you come out on whether this reading is defensible, and give your reasons.
