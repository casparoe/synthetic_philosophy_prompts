I just got drafted onto my hospital's ethics committee and, long story short, I'm the one helping rewrite our policy for when there aren't enough ventilators or ECMO circuits to go around. I'm a nurse, this is all new to me, I said yes because nobody else would. Reading around, I keep noticing that everyone jumps straight to the answer without saying what an answer even has to do, and I have a suspcion the whole fight lives in that gap. So before we draft anything I want the spec, not the verdict.

Tell me what any decent theory of when the numbers count has to deliver. The case I mean is: you can save these people or those people, not both -- five vs. one, or one very sick patient vs. three less sick ones, that kind of thing. I want the list of requirements, each with the case or consideration behind it: the cases it has to get right, the distinctions it can't just collapse, the stuff it can't be allowed to say.

Things I specifically want you to take a position on, as requirements or not:

- The plain five-vs-one case, and the coin-flip line I keep running into (Taurek?) that says if every life counts the same then five don't outweigh one, so you're just as entitled to save the one. Does the theory have to get five-vs-one "right," and right according to who?
- The one-vs-one case where nothing distinguishes them. Does the theory owe me a verdict, a permission, or a coin?
- Adding someone who can't be helped. If numbers count because of how many there are, what happens when you add a sixth patient who will die no matter what, or a sixth whose claim is trivial (sprained ankle)? Does the number change? Should it? It feels like any theory that lets numbers count has to say something here, and the something tends to be ugly.
- One person's life vs. five people's fingers. If five small claims can gang up and outweigh one big one, say so and own it; if not, tell me where the cutoff is and why.
- "The numbers as a tiebreaker" vs. "the numbers as a weight." Those aren't the same thing and I think people slide between them without noticing.
- The separateness thing: I can't trade one person's life for the convenience of the many, people aren't buckets in a total. But that seems to sit right on top of "five beats one." If those two can't both be had, thats the single most useful thing you can tell me -- flag it hard, tell me which one you'd give up, and why.
- Framing. Same situation, same answer, whether I describe it as saving five or as letting one die. And the same policy whether the person applying it is a bystander, one of the patients, or the mother of one.
- The boring constraints: a night-shift nurse has to apply this in ten minutes with only what's on the chart, and we have to be able to explain the reason to a family, and later to a reporter, without it sounding like we priced their mother.

Then, briefly: which single requirement you think knocks out the most theories people actually hold. And where you come down. Please commit -- give me a definite answer with your reasons, not a menu of options and a shrug. If you think the list can't be satisfied all at once, say which requirement you'd drop.
