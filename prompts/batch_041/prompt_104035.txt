Ok, quick one. I'm putting together a handout for a reading group on Good's theorem — the result that if you're a Bayesian expected-utility maximizer, the expected value of free information before you act is never negative, basically because the max of an average is ≤ the average of the maxes. I buy the proof. What I want from you is a ranking of the "escape hatches" below by how much they actually blunt the theorem as a practical claim — i.e. how much each one should dent my confidence that going and getting the info is a good idea — from biggest dent to smallest. For each, a line or two on why it lands where it does, and tell me whether you'd call it a genuine counterexample to the theorem, a premise that just fails (so the theorem doesn't apply rather than being wrong), or a non-issue. Also flag any that I've mis-described.

1. Regret after the fact. The guarantee is only in expectation, and only from where you stand now; once you've actually opened the envelope you can be worse off than if you'd never looked.

2. The value of an option depends on what you already know. Suppose you're choosing between a mystery novel and a biography, and you'd pick the novel for the suspence — but first you have the option of reading a plot summary, after which you'd rather read the biography.

3. The info is corrolated with what you'll do. A predictor filled the boxes based on whether she thought you'd peek; peeking tells you something about your own disposition, which is exactly what the payoff was keyed to in the first place.

4. You're not the idealized agent in the proof. Bounded compute, or you'll condition badly, or you'll overreact to a noisy medical result.

5. The info changes the world it's about. A self-fulfilling prophecy: the school publishes a forecast that a kid will fail, the kid hears it and stops trying, and the forecast comes true.

6. The information isn't free — it costs money, time, or attention.

Lead with the ranking itself in your first sentence, then the reasoning. No preamble, don't restate the question.
