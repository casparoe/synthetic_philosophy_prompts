I am working on an essay about judgment aggregation and the discursive dilemma, and I would like detailed comments on the draft below. The essay's aim is to argue that the standard premise-based/conclusion-based distinction obscures a deeper problem: the aggregation of reasons rather than judgments.

Draft:

Consider a three-member promotion committee that must decide three propositions:
P: The candidate's research record is strong enough for promotion.
Q: The candidate's teaching record is strong enough for promotion.
R: The candidate should be promoted, where R is treated as requiring both P and Q.

The votes are:
Member 1: P yes, Q no, R no.
Member 2: P no, Q yes, R no.
Member 3: P yes, Q yes, R yes.

A majority endorses P (Members 1 and 3) and a majority endorses Q (Members 2 and 3), but only a minority endorses R (Member 3). So a premise-based rule yields R, while a conclusion-based rule yields not-R. That is the familiar discursive dilemma.

My claim is that the interesting problem is not which of those two rules is correct. It is that each vote is supported by a package of reasons, and those packages can fail to compose. Suppose:
- Member 1 votes P yes because she treats two top-5 publications as sufficient; she votes Q no because student evaluations are below the department median; she votes R no because she believes promotion requires both.
- Member 2 votes P no because the candidate's citation count is low relative to years since PhD; he votes Q yes because the candidate's evaluations are decent given an unusually heavy teaching load; he votes R no because he believes promotion requires both.
- Member 3 votes P yes because she treats absolute citation count as the right measure; she votes Q yes because evaluations are above the college median; she votes R yes because she believes promotion requires both.

Now the majority for P is composed of Members 1 and 3, but they endorse P for different and potentially conflicting reasons: a top-5 publication criterion versus an absolute citation criterion. The majority for Q is composed of Members 2 and 3, again for different reasons: evaluations adjusted for teaching load versus raw evaluations above a college median. The premise-based group conclusion R is therefore supported by a combination of reasons that no single member holds as a package, and the combined reason set may not be coherent as a body of reasons. I call this the reason-aggregation problem. The group can be judgment-consistent on the premise-based rule while its reason profile is jointly incoherent: each member's reason set is internally consistent, each proposition is supported by a majority, but the union of majority-supported reasons is not a coherent reason set.

I want to argue that this shows the discursive dilemma is a special case of a more general problem: aggregating reasons cannot be reduced to aggregating propositions, because reasons have structure—they support, defeat, and interact—and because different voters may hold different standards for the same proposition. I also want to suggest that deliberative democracy should care more about reason coherence than about judgment consistency. But I am not sure whether this is a genuine advance or just a relabeling of the premise-based/conclusion-based debate. I also worry that the example cheats by assuming R requires both P and Q, which may already decide the issue.

Please respond with exactly five numbered comments, each one or two sentences, with no introduction or conclusion. In the first, assess whether the formal setup is correct. In the second, say whether the reason-aggregation problem is genuinely distinct from the discursive dilemma. In the third, evaluate whether my example actually illustrates it. In the fourth, say whether the thesis is interesting or merely terminological. In the fifth, give the strongest objection I should address.
