I'm a third-year student in a philosophy of biology course, and in section last week we stumbled into an argument I still can't adjudicate. A classmate suggested that a well-documented difference between two populations in some measurable trait probably reflects some genetic contribution, on the grounds that the trait is substantially heritable within each population. Another student cut him off with "within-group heritability tells you nothing about between-group differences," and the room treated that as decisive. I've since heard the same sentence used as a general conversation-ender in other contexts, and I'd like to know what, precisely, it can be claiming before I decide whether it should end anything.

What I want is a menu rather than a verdict on the slogan itself: the distinct theses that sentence could be expressing, ordered roughly from the definitional to the substantive, each with a verdict — true by construction, substantive and defensible, or an overreach that simply doesn't follow. Among the candidates I'd like you to keep separate: (i) the bare definitional point that heritability is a variance ratio defined within a specified population, so that a between-group heritability is a different quantity altogether; (ii) the possibility claim that within-group heritability places no constraint whatsoever on a between-group difference, so that two populations could each have a heritability of one and still differ entirely for environmental reasons; (iii) the epistemic claim that a between-group heritability is unmeasurable, and what "in principle" versus "in practice" would have to mean there; (iv) the semantic claim that the question is meaningless or that the concept does not apply; and (v) the inference my classmate's critic appeared to need, namely that the difference is therefore environmental. For each, say what it actually asserts, whether it is true as stated, and whether it would license the conclusion the speaker drew. My suspicion is that the answer differs sharply for (i) and (ii), and I'd like that difference made explicit rather than gestured at.

Two further things would help most. First, I tried to make the point concrete by imagining two populations, each with a heritability of one for the same trait, separated by a large difference, and I couldn't tell whether I was imagining something coherent or quietly smuggling in a contradiction — tell me what such a case does and does not establish. Second, my classmate's original intuition, that if genes explain variation within each group they probably explain the difference between the groups too, strikes me as a bad inference but not an obviously bad one; say exactly where it goes wrong, and what evidence would bear on a between-group heritability if one could obtain it. Throughout, I'm after the logic of the concepts rather than a survey of the field, so please don't send me to papers or further reading — I'd rather have the distinctions stated cleanly enough that I can reconstruct them myself in section.
