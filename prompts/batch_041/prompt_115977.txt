My brother and his wife are holding a secular naming ceremony for their first child, and they have asked me to give the short speech. The boy is to be given two names: Jan, which is what my brother's Polish-speaking parents have called him since he was born, and John, which is what his mother's English family have used. Neither family has met the other; both will be in the room, along with two of my colleagues from the philosophy department, and I have five minutes.

I want the speech built on Frege's puzzle about identity statements. 'Jan is John' is perfectly true, and yet for most of the room it will be news in a way that 'Jan is Jan' could never be; the two names present one child in two different ways, and what the grandparents acquire on hearing the identity is not a new fact about the baby but a new way of reaching the one they already have. The speech therefore has to work as a welcome to a child and not as a lecture, and it must not commit any of the errors my colleagues would catch. It must not say that the boy is really Jan and merely called John, or the reverse; it must not treat the two names as interchangeable labels for the same thing, as though either would do equally well; and it must not fall back on the thought that a name is only a sound, so that no name could matter more than any other. It should also register that the same truth is old news to the parents and news to both sets of grandparents.

Please write the speech.
