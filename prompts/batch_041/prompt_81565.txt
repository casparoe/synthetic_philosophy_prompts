I am the interpretation lead at a small heritage trust which runs four sites, and I am commissioning the text for a permanent gallery whose working title is How Societies End. Our writers are capable generalists with no archaeological training; a panel runs to about ninety words, the whole gallery is roughly forty panels, and every one of them has to be signed off by a three-person curatorial group, one member of which is a geologist who objects to the word 'collapse' on principle and another of whom is a local historian who objects to anything that sounds like an excuse for the powerful. We produce a gallery of this size every two years or so and I will be running this argument each time. We keep having it in different costumes. On the Bronze Age eastern Mediterranean everyone is content to say that the palace economies were gone within a couple of generations, and nobody minds. The Classic Maya southern lowlands we cannot write about at all without a row, because half the team thinks 'collapse' is straightforwardly false of a region where millions of people still speak the languages of the people we are describing. The Rapa Nui panel is on its fourth rewrite: the self-inflicted ecocide story is what visitors arrive expecting, and so far as I can tell from the recent work on how much of the island was ever under cultivation it is probably wrong in its strongest form, but the correction reads to visitors like an academic quibble. I don't need a reading list or references — nothing in the gallery carries citations and our writers can't get into journals from the office anyway. What I need is a procedure.

Please write me a decision procedure for when a writer may use 'collapse' and its relatives — fell, ended, destroyed itself, was abandoned — and when the copy must use neutral vocabulary instead. Give it as an ordered set of questions a writer can run in twenty minutes: what counts as a sufficient answer to each, which question stops the process with a no, and which questions must be handed to the curatorial group because they are judgements of value rather than of fact. The distinctions I care about surviving the simplification are these. First, the subject problem: 'X collapsed' is only usable when X is named at the right grain, since a polity, a settlement, a dynasty, a language community and a farming system can all end at different times and only one of them is usually meant. Second, abruptness is scale-relative — a century is abrupt in ninety words and slow in the literature — so the procedure needs an explicit rule for how a rate gets translated into a sentence. Third, our evidence that something ended is very often just the evidence that elite monumental and inscriptional production stopped, which is a fact about our sample and not about the past, and I want the procedure to force a writer to ask what the quiet is made of. Fourth, the word does moral work as well as descriptive work: it is usually being used to teach the reader a lesson about us, and I want a step that makes the writer say what lesson and decide whether the gallery is in the business of teaching it.

Then run the procedure, in full, on four cases: the Bronze Age eastern Mediterranean, the Classic Maya southern lowlands, Rapa Nui, and one case of your own choosing in which the honest answer is that the word 'collapse' is simply correct, so that I can see the procedure is not just a ban on a word. At least one of your four must come out in a way I will not like, and you should tell me which of my writers will hate it most and why they will be wrong. Finish by telling me where the procedure will misfire, the two or three places a clever writer could game it, and which residual judgements no procedure can remove. Take a definite position throughout: if you think the word should be banned outright, say so and defend it; where you are genuinely torn, say which way you would rule and what would change your mind. Don't lay out the options and leave the choice to me — I have three curators for that.
