I'm a second-year PhD student in philosophy of science, loosely attached to an AI ethics group, and I'm putting together a two-page proposal for a small interdisciplinary workshop — the kind where the money is modest, the audience is mixed, and the whole point is to force you to say what you would actually do. I've been chewing on the topic with two friends from a reading group on AI governance and none of us can tell whether what I've got is a real question or just a terminological itch. The topic is the "debate" proposal for scalable oversight of AI systems: two models argue opposing sides of a question, a human judge rules on who made the better case, and the winning side's reasoning is treated as the training signal for the system being supervised. As I understand the state of play, the standard worry is a kind of verification asymmetry — the dishonest side can win by burying the honest case inside a chain of steps or a computation the judge cannot realistically follow, so the verdict stops tracking the truth. My half-formed suspicion is that this entire framing smuggles in two assumptions that deserve to be dragged into the open. The first is that there is a determinate answer which the judge could in principle be brought to see. The second, and for me the more interesting one, is that it treats the judge's verdict as a *measurement* of that answer rather than as something that partly *constitutes* it — as though the protocol were a thermometer rather than a vote that is then fed back into the system as ground truth. Both assumptions look shaky once the questions being overseen are themselves evaluative, contested, or open-ended ("is this plan safe?", "is this advice any good?"), and once the judge is also the person who will later be told that the verdict was correct. I suspect that if you take those two points seriously, the honest target for debate is not truth-recovery at all but something more like making the shape of the disagreement legible to the judge — surfacing where the two sides actually diverge and what would have to be true for either to win — and that this shift changes both what counts as the protocol succeeding and what counts as it being gamed.
