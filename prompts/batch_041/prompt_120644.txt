I am a retired experimental physicist. For forty years my working life consisted of disagreements that were settled by going back to the calibration and asking what quantity the instrument was supposed to be measuring, and I have spent the last few years reading philosophy with the aim of finding out what happens to that habit when the quantity in question is contested. I have been invited to contribute a single essay question to the final examination of an upper-level undergraduate course in the philosophy of the social sciences. The examination is ninety minutes, closed-book, and the essay question carries 25 marks. The course has covered measurement, construct validity, and the use of comparative indices.

The topic assigned to me is: which neighbour is more liberal, Sweden or Norway, and what would settle it?

What I want from the question is that it be unanswerable by reciting which of the two countries tops the standard cross-national rankings. A student who has memorised the standings in V-Dem, Freedom House, or the Economist Intelligence Unit's democracy index should not thereby be able to answer it, and the question must not depend on anyone recalling particular scores or particular editions of anything — those change from year to year, and I have no way to check them. I want the question to force three things. First, that "liberal" splits into at least two senses which, for this particular pair of countries, do not merely differ in degree but can point in opposite directions: the classical sense, in which the relevant comparison concerns the reach of the state, the marketisation of public provision, and the scope of personal economic discretion; and the social sense, in which it concerns permissiveness, the protection of minorities, and the absence of moralistic regulation. Second, that any verdict has to rest on a construct, a unit of analysis, and an aggregation rule, and that none of these choices is innocent — whether one compares national averages or distributions, whether one counts the whole resident population or only citizens, whether an advantage on one dimension may be traded against a deficit on another, and if so at what rate. Third, and this is the part I care about most, whether the question has a determinate answer waiting to be discovered, or whether "what would settle it" can be answered only by stipulation — and if only by stipulation, whether the stipulation answers the question or changes the subject.

So that the question is not a fishing expedition, please build the following material into the question itself. Both countries have among the most extensive welfare states in the world and both sit near the top of most comparative rankings of democracy and freedom, so that the coarse rankings cannot discriminate between them. On some measures Sweden has gone further in marketising public provision — the independent "free schools" financed by public vouchers are the standard example — while Norway has retained some conspicuously state-directed arrangements, such as the state alcohol monopoly and membership of the European Economic Area rather than the European Union. A defensible answer may therefore be that each country is more liberal than the other in different respects, which immediately raises the question of whether the respects can be weighed against one another at all. Students are not to be asked for citations or reading lists, and nothing should turn on a particular figure being remembered correctly.

Please write, in this order: (1) the examination question itself, as it would appear on the paper, including whatever brief instructions to candidates are needed to make the task well defined within ninety minutes; (2) a model answer of about 700 words; and (3) a marking scheme consisting of five must-hit points, whose marks sum to 25, with a one-line note against each point describing how candidates typically miss it. The marking scheme must separate an A answer from a B answer by naming the specific moves each must contain — the difference should not be "more depth" or "better writing" but a definite claim or distinction that one answer has and the other lacks.

Begin your response with the examination question itself: no preface, no restatement of my request, no account of what you are about to do. The model answer should open with its bottom line in the first sentence and then give the reasoning, without restating the question or warming up before committing itself.
