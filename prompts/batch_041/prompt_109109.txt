I am assembling a small anthology of poems that take philosophical positions seriously enough to let the form carry some of the argument, and I would like a sestina on the special composition question — the question of when, if ever, some things add up to a further thing. Please write it as a strict sestina: six six-line stanzas and a three-line envoi, with the end-words permuted by the usual retrogradatio rule, so that the same six words keep returning in shifting orders. The form is meant to do the work, since the reader is left holding six recurring words and has to decide whether the poem is merely those six over again or a seventh thing that they compose.

For the six end-words, take six ordinary objects from a single scene — say a windowsill holding a stone, a key, a cup, a spent candle, a photograph, and a dead moth — or substitute freely, provided the six are the sort of things no one would antecedently think compose anything. Let the poem speak in the voice of someone who keeps revising their verdict on what the arrangement amounts to.

Let the first two stanzas take the permissive line: any things whatever, however scattered, compose some further thing, and the whole is just the parts taken together. Let the middle two take the austere line: strictly speaking there are no composite objects at all, only simples arranged stonewise, keywise, cupwise, and so on, with the apparent wholes being nothing over and above the arrangement. Let the last two stanzas try to hold a middle position, that composition occurs only under some special condition — and make that condition concrete rather than gesturing at one. Use this: a further thing exists just when its candidate parts are jointly caught up in a single ongoing activity, so that the question of what the thing is doing has one answer rather than six. Then have the speaker notice the standard difficulty, that any such condition looks arbitrary beside the permissive and austere options and that we seem to have no way to tell a principled line from a gerrymandered one, without letting the poem resolve it.

The envoi should be the point where the poem turns on itself and asks whether the poem, like the windowsill, is one thing or six. Accuracy matters more than ornament: someone who has thought about these positions should find the stanzas fair to each, and someone who has not should still come away with a good poem. No need for names, dates, or references to the literature.
