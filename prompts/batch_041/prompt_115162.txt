I need to hand in a short statement of my own position before a session on material constitution, and I'd like it audited for consistency first. This is roughly where I've ended up.

(1) The statue and the lump of clay are numerically distinct objects. The lump would survive being squashed flat and the statue would not, and two things that differ in what they could and could not survive are not the same thing.

(2) Existence is causal through and through: nothing is real unless it makes some distinctive difference to what happens. Where one object does all the causal work, positing a second is a mistake.

(3) I'm wary of taking modal properties at face value. Claims about what a thing could or could not have survived seem to me to reflect the sortals and concepts we bring to a case, rather than deep features of the thing itself.

(4) I don't believe in ontological vagueness. For any two candidate objects it is determinately true that they are identical, or determinately true that they are distinct; the indeterminacy we feel in cases like clouds or repaired ships is in our words, not in the world.

(5) My pointing works fine: "that is a fine statue" and "that is a lump of clay" both succeed when I gesture at the pedestal, so the presence of two coinciding objects doesn't make my reference ambiguous.

(6) I refuse to posit a primitive constitution relation that is little more than identity's poor cousin; whatever marks the statue off from the lump should follow from their properties, not from a special tie between them.

Which of these genuinely conflict, which only appear to, and for each genuine conflict what is the cheapest commitment to surrender and what would surrendering it cost? Order the genuine conflicts by severity rather than treating them as equally bad.
