I am a second-year undergraduate enrolled in a metaphysics course, and I have been asked to lead one of our weekly tutorial sections. The assigned topic is Bradley's regress and the problem of the unity of the proposition, and I would like to teach it through an activity rather than a walkthrough at the board: I want the group to arrive at the regress under their own steam, so that it lands as a discovery rather than a puzzle I hand them already labelled.

Please act as an expert in metaphysics with a strong background in the philosophy of language and logic, and, at the same time, as an experienced tutor who designs active-learning sessions. Think carefully before you answer. Begin by brainstorming two or three genuinely different mechanical ideas for staging the regress physically or socially, say briefly what each would and would not capture, and then develop the strongest of them into a complete, runnable plan.

Parameters:

- Setting: one 50-minute tutorial, roughly fifteen students, a seminar table and a whiteboard, ordinary index cards and sticky notes, no budget and no real money.
- Aim: by the end, participants should have felt, in the doing, that you cannot turn a heap of constituents into a single unified content by adding one more item to the heap, because that item is itself a constituent and so invites the very same demand; and they should see that this is a point about unity as such, not merely about relations. They should not hear the phrases Bradley's regress or unity of the proposition until the debrief.

I would like the mechanics to include a required second round: after the group has run into the regress, one team is handed a special card that binds the constituents together without itself requiring any further binder. The group then has to argue about whether that move genuinely escapes the regress or merely relabels it. I want the debrief to use the moment where they either accept or refuse that card to open up the real options — treating unity as primitive, treating the binder as unsaturated rather than as one more item, or dissolving the problem outright — and to say what each option costs.

Your plan should give me: the rules and the win or satisfaction conditions; the materials and how to lay them out; any roles and how to assign them; a minute-by-minute timeline; and the debrief, which must map specific moments in the activity onto specific moves in the regress and in the unity problem. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how I should handle each failure, and be frank about which feature of the idea the activity cannot reproduce and that I will have to supply myself.
