A colleague of mine, a software engineer with a serious amateur interest in ethics, recently summarized Bernard Williams's integrity objection to utilitarianism as follows. Williams argues that utilitarianism alienates agents from their own moral lives. The classic case is a man who can save either his wife or a stranger from drowning. According to Williams, a utilitarian would have to deliberate about which rescue would produce the greatest overall happiness; but the man should not need to deliberate in that way. He should save his wife simply because she is his wife. The requirement to consider impartial utility first adds an unnecessary additional thought, and this shows that utilitarianism cannot accommodate the special commitments and projects that give a person's life meaning. More strongly, Williams thinks utilitarianism is internally unstable: it demands that agents be impartial at the moment of action, yet it also demands that they have deep personal commitments, which are necessarily partial. The theory therefore either destroys the commitments or fails to guide action, and this refutes utilitarianism as a moral theory.

Is this a fair summary? Please go claim by claim. Which parts would Williams himself accept, which are standard simplifications, and which are subtle misrepresentations? I am especially interested in whether the objection is best understood as a point about deliberation at the moment of action, about the value of a person's deepest projects, or about the moral psychology that utilitarianism presupposes; whether a utilitarian can account for special obligations without the problematic extra thought; and whether the conclusion that utilitarianism is refuted follows from any of the accurate parts. Please separate what is accurate, what is a common but loose gloss, and what a defender of Williams or of utilitarianism would reject.
