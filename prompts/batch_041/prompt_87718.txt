In a graduate seminar I ran last term on idealization in normative theory, two participants — I'll call them A and B — spent most of the session locked in an argument about whether idealized deliberation can ground normativity at all, and I let it run precisely because neither side was obviously wrong. I would like a careful adjudication of what they actually said.

A opened as follows. Any version of the view that locates the correct normative theory in what fully informed, fully coherent, suitably impartial agents would converge on is either circular or empty. If you characterize the idealizing conditions substantively — full information, global coherence, impartiality, and the like — you have already assumed a substantive normative theory, since each of those conditions is itself a normative commitment, and the ideal deliberation then does no work beyond serving as a middleman. If instead you characterize the ideal conditions by their output — the ideal theory is whatever ideal deliberation selects — the account is vacuous, telling us only that the correct theory is the one the correct procedure would yield.

B replied that A was treating the view as a derivation when it is an explication. On B's account, the idealizing conditions are not premises smuggled in from outside; they are constitutive of the standpoint of practical reason we already occupy when we ask what we have reason to do. The view is not circular, B said, because it never tries to derive normativity from something non-normative; it tries to explain the authority of norms — their independence from any actual agent's contingent desires — and to adjudicate between conflicting intuitions. A theory of reasons just is the theory of what survives ideal scrutiny, so the middleman is the point.

A then pressed two objections. First, if the idealization is constrained to be ours — our concepts, our considered judgments as starting points — it cannot deliver the independence from our contingent commitments that B promised; it merely launders them. If it is not so constrained, we get the alien-advisor problem: idealized agents might converge on a theory indifferent or hostile to everything we care about, and an ideal alien to us has no authority over us. Second, and more pointedly, A argued that the limit of ideal deliberation may not denote anything. Deliberation is path-dependent: what a deliberative process concludes depends on the order in which considerations are introduced, on how conflicts are resolved en route, and on which questions get asked. Unless one stipulates a canonical procedure, there is no unique ideal outcome; the phrase what ideal deliberation would conclude then functions as an empty definite description, and the account cannot even be stated, let alone used.

B answered that the path-dependence objection assumes an arbitrary procedure and forgets what idealization to the limit is for: at the limit all considerations are simultaneously on the table and coherence is global, so no order remains, and the outcome is robust across procedures. The alien-advisor worry, B said, is self-undermining, because to identify the alien outcome as one we have no reason to accept is already to reason about what is genuinely valuable, which shows the ideal standpoint is not alien to reason as such. As for parochialism, B insisted the view is reflective rather than foundationalist: considered judgments enter as data to be systematized under ideal scrutiny, not as fixed constraints.

A's last move was that global coherence erases order begs the question, since the coherence thesis is itself one of the contested outputs of the procedure rather than a neutral feature of it; a value pluralist who denies that all genuine values admit of a single coherent ranking is not obviously irrational, and if idealization presupposes coherence, it has already chosen a side. A also claimed B's reflective reply concedes the original point: if the outputs are a function of the inputs, then ideal adds consistency but not authority, and two agents with divergent considered judgments will each reach an ideal theory by their own lights, so the account cannot adjudicate the very disagreement it was introduced to settle. B's rejoinder was that every foundational account faces the same fork, and that the idealization account at least locates normativity where it must live — in the practice of holding one another to reasons — and that A conflates depends on the inputs with arbitrary, as if a proof's dependence on its axioms made it arbitrary.

Please adjudicate this move by move rather than simply declaring a winner. For each of A's and B's main contentions, say whether it is clearly right, clearly mistaken, or defensible but incomplete, and justify the rating in a few sentences. Then say what each party is missing that the other has hold of, and finally give your overall verdict on the narrow question that divided them — whether idealized deliberation can ground the authority of norms without collapsing into either circularity or vacuity — making explicit which of A's or B's points most constrains the answer.
