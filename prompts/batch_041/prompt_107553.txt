I am a third-year PhD student in the history and philosophy of science, and as part of my studentship I run a fortnightly methodology clinic for doctoral researchers on our cross-faculty training programme, most of them building simulation models in ecology, epidemiology and environmental economics. Each student gets one thirty-minute session and leaves with a one-page note that they and their supervisor both sign, setting out whether the line they are on looks worth continuing, what would have to happen for that judgement to change, and what they should do next. There are about a dozen of these a year, the stakes are a year or more of somebody's funded time, and I have neither expertise in their subject matter nor authority over them, so whatever I write has to be defensible to a supervisor in another discipline. At present I improvise, and I have noticed that I am running a crude implicit test: does this framework keep generating claims that turn out to be true, or does it keep being repaired after the fact?

Please turn Lakatos's methodology of scientific research programmes into a procedure I can actually run in half an hour. I want it as an ordered sequence of questions rather than a rubric of virtues; for each question, what would count as an answer sufficient to move on; the point at which the procedure returns a negative verdict; and the point at which it must stop and hand the matter back to the student as a question of ambition rather than of method. Make use of the machinery properly — the hard core and the protective belt, the positive and negative heuristics, the difference between a progressive and a degenerating problem-shift, the role of novel prediction, and Lakatos's refusal to count a programme as refuted while it is still the best one going. The hard part is the translation, and I want you to confront it rather than paper over it: Lakatos's unit is a programme assessed retrospectively by historians over decades, against rivals, whereas mine is a single doctoral project assessed prospectively in thirty minutes by someone who does not understand the science. Say plainly which parts of the original survive that move, which have to be weakened, and which cannot be carried across at all.

Then work the procedure through three cases. First, a student whose invasive-species model has acquired four auxiliary assumptions over eighteen months, each added after a failed calibration, each fitting the present data set, none yet tested against anything new. Second, a student whose transmission framework committed itself early to a counterintuitive claim about asymptomatic spread, which an independent cohort study later bore out, and which has since been extended to a second host species. Third, a case where the procedure returns a verdict I will not like: a student in a fashionable, well-funded modelling tradition whose recent work consists largely of defensive re-parameterisations, but whose entire professional network sits inside that tradition. Tell me what I should do with a verdict like that when the student has no realistic alternative and I am the only person in the room saying it.

Finally, tell me where the procedure will misfire. I am thinking especially of projects that are descriptive or methodological rather than predictive, of fields with no rival programme to compare against, of cases where the hard core is a modelling formalism rather than a substantive theory, and of the fact that a student can rarely change direction for funding reasons even when the method says they should. I do not need a reading list or references; assume I know the texts.
