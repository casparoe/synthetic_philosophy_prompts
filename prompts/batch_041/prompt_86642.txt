So I'm in this little reading group on perception — we've been working through the philosophy of perception stuff, naive realism, sense data, the usual — and two of the regulars have basically stopped speaking to each other over it. One's a hardline disjunctivist: veridical perception and hallucination are fundamentally different kinds of thing, no shared ingredient, end of story. The other's a sense-datum person (technically a representationalist now, but the same instinct): if you can't tell the two apart from the inside, there's got to be something shared, and that shared thing is what the argument from illusion was always really getting at.

What I want from you is the document these two should've written instead of bickering for six months. A joint adversarial-collaboration statement — and I want it aimed squarely at the argument from illusion, not the argument from hallucination, which is where everyone runs and which I think lets the disjunctivist off too easy. The illusion case being: a straight stick half in water looks bent, you see it as bent, but the stick is straight and you're looking right at it. No missing object, no hallucination, just the world appearing other than it is. My suspicion is that the disjunctivist's standard move — denying a common factor between the good case and the bad case — doesn't obviously apply here, since in the illusion case there isn't a 'bad case' with no object at all.

Lay it out as: first, a numbered list of claims both of them can sign — about the argument, about what the illusion case does and doesn't establish, about what any theory of perception owes us. Then a disagreements section, a table if that works better, each remaining disagreement stated in wording that whoever holds that side would actually accept, not a strawman of it. For each disagreement, name the crux — the sub-question such that if it got settled, one of them would have to move — and for each crux give me a case or argument both would agree bears on it, with each side's prediction about how it comes out written down in advance. And if any chunk of their fight turns out to be about words rather than substance — say whether 'experience' is being used to mean the phenomenology or the epistemic state — flag it and say which chunk.

One more thing to build in: a friend of mine keeps insisting on a distinction between what she calls the 'looks-the-same' sense of commonality and the 'is-the-same' sense, and I'd like you to use it if it earns its keep — i.e. separate the question of whether two experiences feel identical from the question of whether they're the same kind of mental state. My hunch is the whole debate definately hinges on whether those two questions can come apart, and I want the document to make that explicit rather than leave it buried.
