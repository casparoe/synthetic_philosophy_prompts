Until recently I would have said that the question of how to read a statute is a matter of craft rather than conviction. Six months on the bench has cured me of that. I have written enough opinions by now to know that I am applying a theory whether I admit it or not, and I would rather apply one I can defend. So I have settled on the following, and what I want from you is an account of its exposure.

The position, stated as precisely as I can manage it:

1. The meaning of a statutory provision is a fact about the linguistic conventions of the community of speakers at the time of enactment — what a competent speaker of the language, in the relevant context, would have taken the words to convey. It is not a fact about what any legislator, sponsor, or committee hoped the provision would accomplish.

2. The enacting legislature's purposes, its expectations about particular applications, and the various statements in the record about what a provision was meant to do are therefore not independent sources of meaning. They bear on meaning only as weak evidence of the conventions, and they are systematically worse evidence than the text itself, both because they are produced by a subset of the enactors for their own audiences and because a statute is typically a compromise among people with incompatible purposes, so that the purpose of the enactment may be a fiction.

3. Consequently, where the conventional meaning is determinate, the fact that the determinate reading frustrates the evident purpose of the enactment is not a reason to depart from it. The remedy is legislative correction; if the legislature does not correct it, that is the law.

I am not interested in being told that the position is undemocratic, or that it produces unpopular results, or that it has been adopted by people with political commitments. I am interested in what would count against it. Specifically:

First, what does the position predict that its rivals do not? I take it that claims 2 and 3 have consequences for the world: drafting practices should respond to the interpretive regime, legislatures should correct courts at some rate and in some pattern, the case law under such a regime should be more stable in some respects and less in others. Which of those predictions are actually entailed by the position, which would be decisive if they failed, and have any of them arguably already failed?

Second, which cases does the position handle badly? I am worried about three families in particular, and I would like to be told which is a genuine problem and which is a pseudo-problem.

(a) The scrivener's error and the bad cross-reference: a definitional provision pointing to the wrong section, or a numeral that plainly should have been another numeral, so that the conventional meaning is determinate and absurd. If I permit correction here, have I not admitted purpose through the back door and turned my textualism into a purposivism with extra steps? If I refuse, have I not made the law turn on a typo?

(b) Terms of art whose content was itself fixed by prior judicial decisions, and provisions whose conventional meaning was clear when enacted but which later amendments elsewhere in the code have rendered incoherent. Here the conventions of the enacting community either point outside the statute to judge-made content, or to a state of affairs the enactors could not have contemplated. Does the position have the resources to say anything non-arbitrary about which conventions govern?

(c) The general term and the new case: a statute that uses a category term — some functional kind rather than an enumerated list — and a case that falls outside anything the enactors imagined but inside the kind. What does the position say, and is what it says distinguishable from what a purposivist would say? If the answers converge, say so plainly.

Third, is there a class of provisions the position cannot even describe? I have in mind the possibility that some statutory text is not a communicative act at all — that it is a price, or a coordination device, or a delegation whose content is meant to be filled in later — and that a theory of linguistic meaning is simply the wrong theory for those provisions.

Fourth, and most important: has the position been insulated? I notice that it comes with escape hatches — the absurdity exception, the move from plain meaning to context, the canons of construction, the retreat to an objective purpose discerned from the statute as a whole. Each looks like a place where a determinate conventional meaning can be overridden without admitting that purpose is doing the work. I want an honest verdict on whether the position as I have stated it risks anything at all, and if it does not, whether that is a defect in the position or merely the normal condition of any view refined enough to survive contact with hard cases.

Sort what you give me by how decisive it would be: what should move me off the position entirely, what should force a qualification, and what is merely awkward. For each item, say whether a committed defender of the position would concede it as a cost or dismiss it as a misunderstanding. I would rather have five objections that survive the obvious replies than twenty that do not, so for each one, state the obvious reply and then say whether it works. Argue from the structure of the position rather than from the literature; I am not asking for a reading list.
