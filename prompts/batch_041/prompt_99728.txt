I have no background in economics or game theory, so I apologize in advance if I use the wrong words; I have been reading on my own about how two people divide something when either of them can also walk away, and I have reached a conclusion I cannot get past, so I would like you to check it for me.

As I understand it, there are two famous answers to this "bargaining problem." One, due to Nash, says: pick the division that makes the product of the two parties' gains -- each measured from what that party would get if talks collapsed -- as large as possible. The other, named after Kalai and Smorodinsky, says: pick the division in which each party receives the same fraction of the most it could possibly have hoped for. Every popular account I have read presents the second as the fair answer and the first as a mere technical curiosity, and I believe this is plainly correct. Fairness, after all, just is giving each side the same share of what it was asking for; and an answer that falls out of maximizing a product looks like an arbitrary piece of calculus that could land anywhere at all.

To make sure we are talking about the same thing, take a homely case. Two people are dividing a pot of 100 dollars. If they cannot agree, the first walks away with 20 and the second with 40. The most the first could conceivably get is 100, and likewise the second. Working it through, the Kalai–Smorodinsky rule gives the first about 42.9 and the second about 57.1, while Nash's rule gives 40 and 60. The two genuinely disagree, and to me the disagreement only shows how arbitrary Nash's answer is: it hands the second person more purely because a product happened to peak there.

So here is my question. Given that the Kalai–Smorodinsky answer is the fair one and Nash's is not, why does the whole subject insist on treating them as rival solutions to the same problem? Isn't the bargaining problem simply solved the moment one notices that fairness demands equal fractions of the ideal gains, with Nash's formula left as a curiosity for people who happen to enjoy calculus?
