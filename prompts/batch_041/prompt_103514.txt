I sit as the philosopher on a state licensing board's character-and-fitness panel; the other six members are practitioners and lay appointees. We decide about forty cases a year — applicants with convictions, academic-integrity findings, unpaid judgments, disciplinary actions — in roughly thirty minutes per file, and we must give written reasons that a court can review. Our sanctions run from admission with conditions to denial. The question we keep fumbling is the one the situationist literature was built to embarrass: what can a record of behavior tell us about someone's character? I need a decision procedure my non-philosopher colleagues can actually run, and I want it to take the situationist evidence seriously rather than argue it away. So the procedure should respect four points. First, the classic experiments establish inconsistency mainly in weak situations — low stakes, no observers, no prior commitment — so the strength of the situation the applicant was actually in is itself evidence. Second, on the trait-activation picture from industrial psychology, a disposition shows itself only when the situation supplies trait-relevant cues, so the procedure must ask what cues the applicant's surroundings offered. Third, those experiments arguably test for a global, cross-situational consistency that virtue ethics never required: on the Aristotelian model a virtue is a trained skill organized by practical wisdom, not a habit obliged to fire everywhere. Fourth, a disposition that explains little is still a disposition — the real question is how much weight one trait can bear, not whether it exists.

Give me the procedure with its questions in order, what counts as a sufficient answer at each step, where it stops with a denial, and where it hands the decision back to the panel as a judgment call. Then run it on three applicants: a decade-old DUI with nothing since; three academic-integrity findings at two institutions; and theft from an employer to cover a dependent's medical costs, disclosed candidly. At least one result should be one I won't like. Since character is formed by practice, say what our conditions are meant to do, and where the procedure is really predicting future conduct under situations rather than judging who someone is. And say where it will misfire — whether it can tell a character defect from a badly designed environment, and what the panel should do when it can't. Tell me how confident you are in the procedure and its applications, and single out the point you are least sure of.
