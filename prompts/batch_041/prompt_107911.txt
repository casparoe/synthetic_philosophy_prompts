I'm taking a metaethics course this term, and for a seminar presentation I tried to reconstruct the Frege–Geach objection to expressivism in my own words. What I ended up with looks airtight, and that is exactly why I no longer trust it: it seems to prove far more than the objection is supposed to prove. I'd like you to tell me which step to give up and what giving it up costs.

The argument:

(1) Expressivism holds that a sentence such as "lying is wrong," when someone asserts it, expresses a non-cognitive attitude — a state of disapproval — rather than stating a fact. Such an attitude is not the sort of thing that can be true or false.
(2) The words "lying is wrong" mean the same thing in the conditional "if lying is wrong, then getting your brother to lie is wrong" as they do when asserted. If they did not, the argument "lying is wrong; if lying is wrong, then getting your brother to lie is wrong; so getting your brother to lie is wrong" would equivocate, and its validity would be an accident of wording rather than a matter of meaning.
(3) In the conditional, however, nothing is disapproved of. Whoever utters it asserts no moral verdict and expresses no disapproval; she merely states a connection between two things.
(4) So the meaning of "lying is wrong" is present in the conditional, while the attitude is not.
(5) Therefore the meaning of "lying is wrong" is not the attitude, and expressivism is false.

Two things bother me. First, the argument uses nothing specific to ethics: it would rule out any sentence whose meaning is a non-cognitive state from occurring unasserted inside a valid argument. It would therefore also rule out imperatives, optatives and permissions. Many accounts of imperatives treat "shut the door" and "you may leave" as non-truth-assessable, and yet "if you're cold, shut the door" is not gibberish, and something like inference seems to go on with imperatives. If my argument is sound, all of that is impossible. Second, I have already tried the obvious repair — saying the conditional expresses a higher-order attitude rather than a first-order one — and it did restore validity, but I could not see what prevented the same manoeuvre from working for any vocabulary whatever, which made me suspect I had quietly stopped doing expressivism without noticing.

Where does this go wrong? I would most like to keep (2) and the idea that moral arguments are valid in the ordinary sense, and I would rather not surrender the claim that moral judgment is practical in a way that belief is not. If more than one repair is available, price them for me and tell me which is cheapest.
