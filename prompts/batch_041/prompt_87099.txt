I am a consultant in intensive care medicine, and I chair the working group at my trust that has to produce a written procedure for determining death — something a first-year doctor on a night shift can run at the bedside without a philosopher present. We have had three difficult cases in eighteen months and nothing on paper: a man on veno-arterial ECMO whose brainstem reflexes were absent and whose heart was still ejecting; a controlled donation-after-circulatory-death (Maastricht category III) donor whose family wanted the moment of death to be when the heart stopped while the retrieval team wanted five minutes of observation first; and a woman in a prolonged disorder of consciousness whose family insisted she had already died and were furious to be told she had not. Our regional group has proposed a scaffold that I am told is philosophically respectable but which is useless as written, and I want you to turn it into a procedure. The scaffold says that every determination must answer four questions in order: whether the patient has lost the capacity for integrated self-maintenance, as opposed to losing some particular organ, or consciousness, or personhood; whether that loss is irreversible; whether it is the whole brain or the circulation that has ceased; and — the one nobody can answer — who is entitled to be wrong. The group also insists on keeping two windows apart: an epistemic window (how long you must observe before you can be confident) and a metaphysical window (the moment at which, on your definition, death actually occurred), which I gather are not the same thing and come apart badly in DCD.

Please write the procedure. It should be a sequence of questions in the order they must be asked, with what counts as a sufficient answer to each, the points at which the procedure stops with a no, and the points at which it must hand the decision to someone else — an ethics committee, a coroner, a court — because the question has become one of values rather than facts. For every step, say whether it is the determination of a fact or the exercise of a judgement, and who signs it off. It has to fit on two sides of A4 and be runnable within the hour. Two distinctions I need preserved rather than smoothed away: first, the difference between a cessation that is permanent (it will not be reversed, because no one will attempt to reverse it) and one that is irreversible (it cannot be reversed), which our neurologist keeps raising in DCD on the grounds that we could always put the patient back on bypass; second, the difference between the patient's death and the family's bereavement, which our nurses think we conflate.

Then run the procedure on three cases: the ECMO patient, the DCD donor, and the woman in the prolonged disorder of consciousness — and please be straight with me about the last one, because the procedure will tell her family something they will not want to hear and I need to know how solid that answer is. I would also like you to mark clearly where the procedure is likely to misfire, particularly with a sedated two-month-old and with a family whose religious commitments reject brainstem criteria outright, and to end by saying what a procedure of this kind cannot do at all. Write in British English.
