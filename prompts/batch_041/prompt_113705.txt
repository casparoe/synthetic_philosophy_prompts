ok so my ethics reading group just wrapped the equality-vs-priority stuff — Parfit, the priority view, the levelling-down objection — and i want to close the last session with a poem instead of yet another summary. heres the thing i want it to do. the levelling-down objection is the one where a purely egalitarian view looks committed to saying that making the better-off worse off, with nobody gaining anything, is in one respect an improvement, because the gap shrinks. parfit's priority view is suposed to dodge this: it isn't bothered about the gap as such, it just gives extra weight to benefits that land on the worse off, so a pure loss to the better off is simply a loss.

i want a poem that enacts the levelling down rather than describing it. 16-24 lines, built out of two registers that start out unequal — a high ornate one (full rhyme, tight metre, fancier diction) and a low plain one (unrhymed, loose, flat). across the poem the high register gets progressively stripped: rhyme goes first, then the metre loosens, then the diction flattens, until by the last stanza the two are more or less indistinguishable. the poem literally levels down.

what i care about is the ending. it should not tell me whether that was a loss. a prioritarian reading should be able to say nothing of value was destroyed (the plain voice was never made better off, so who exactly got harmed?) and an egalitarian reading should be able to say the poem is finally just. both should still be live at the close — make the last two or three lines hard to cash either way. don't name parfit and don't use the phrase 'levelling down' in the poem itself; let the structure carry it.

someone who knows the debate should be able to see the two views pulling on that ending, and someone who doesn't should just get a poem about something elaborate and expensive being reduced to something plain, and feel weird about it.

after the poem, tell me how confident you are that it tracks the actual distinction (priority view: welfarist, no intrinsic disvalue in inequality; telic egalitarianism: the inequality itself is bad) and flag the single point you're least sure you've got right. and if you had to lean on a contested reading of Parfit to make the ending work, say so.
