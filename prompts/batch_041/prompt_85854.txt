The critics, Borel and Lebesgue most prominently, don't dispute the derivation; they dispute the principle, and the complaint is that it asserts existence without giving any rule for the choice, so in the cases that matter nobody can say which object got chosen. Hadamard more or less shrugs and says the existence claim is fine as it stands. Then Vitali extracts a non-measurable set from it, Banach and Tarski get the decomposition paradox, Zermelo redoes the proof in 1908 in a form people minded less, and eventually the whole thing is absorbed into ZFC as business as usual. Much later Solovay builds a model of ZF plus dependent choice in which every set of reals is Lebesgue measurable, assuming an inaccessible cardinal, and Shelah shows you can't drop the inaccessible for that measurability result; separately, with large cardinals you get that every reasonably definable set of reals is measurable and has the Baire property. Also, over ZF the well-ordering theorem and the axiom of choice are equivalent in first-order logic, but in second-order logic well-ordering is strictly stronger.
