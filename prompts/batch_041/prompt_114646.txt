So I was at a friend's place and two of her grad-school friends, Dana and Marcus, ended up going at each other for like an hour about secession, and she wants me to help figure out who was actually making sense. I couldnt follow all of it. Here's roughly how it went.

The setup they agreed on: a region -- call it the vale -- has been part of a larger country for about a century. No conquest or anything dramatic, just a lopsided unification deal. The vale has its own language, and around 70% of its residents now say they want out. The country as a whole is strongly against it. The vale also contains the only port the rest of the country can use, plus a big reservoir that was paid for out of national funds and supplies several other provinces. And there's a chunk of the vale that doesn't want to leave at all, and would rather stay with the country or maybe go its own way.

(1) Dana: A clear majority in a defined territory wanting to govern itself is enough to make secession legitimate, as long as it doesn't trample basic rights on the way out. You don't need prior oppression. Requiring that treats self-government like a reward for the state behaving badly, when it's just a right people have.

(2) Marcus: It's not just a right people have. Secession is legitimate as a remedy for serious injustice, not as a preference. If a majority preference were enough, then no state could ever bind itself to anything across generations, and you'd get cascades -- every region that dislikes a tax or a war exits, and whoever's left can't run anything. And yeah, nobody consented to the original state, but nobody consents to anything, so consent can't be doing the work here.

(3) Dana: ok, so require a supermajority, a waiting period, a clean negotiation over debts and borders. That handles the cascade worry. But it doesn't change who decides. And it has to be the people of the vale deciding, not the whole country. The country doesn't get a veto over whether it keeps ruling people who don't want it.

(4) Marcus: But that's exactly the thing. The vale's exit lands on people outside it -- they lose their only port, they lose a reservoir they paid for, their economy takes a hit. So why does the set of people who get to vote on the decision exclude most of the people the decision lands on? If affectedness is your test for who's in the demos, the demos is way bigger than the vale. If subjection is your test, then the vale's voters are deciding something that mostly lands on non-subjects. You can't use one principle to pick the voters and the other to justify the result.

(5) Dana: The all-affected idea proves way too much -- everyone's affected by everything, so it'd mean the whole world votes in every election. And anyway there's no neutral people sitting there before the vote; the vote is what makes the people. The sub-region inside the vale that wants to stay? It can't veto either, by the same logic, or you never get to any decision at all.

(6) Marcus: Then notice what you just did. You used majority-decides to entitle the vale to leave, and then denied it to the sub-region that wants to stay, because letting that majority decide would be inconvenient. If the majority inside a territory is the unit that counts, why isn't the majority inside the sub-territory the unit that counts? You didn't give a principled stopping point, you just picked the level that gets the answer you want.

They asked me who was right and I honestly couldn't tell. Can you go through it move by move, 1 through 6, and say who's got the better of each step, where each of them is overreaching, and what both are missing? I don't need a general theory of secession, just who's right about what they actually said, and who's more right overall.
