ok so i'm TAing a decision theory seminar this term and the absent-minded driver keeps eating our whole section, so i want to hand the students a dialogue instead of lecturing at them again. use this setup: a driver is on a road with two intersections. she can't tell them apart and can't remember whether she's already passed one -- imperfect recall, same evidence at both nodes. if she exits at the first intersection she gets 0. if she exits at the second she gets 4. if she drives past both she gets 1. at each intersection she chooses continue or exit, knowing only that she's at one of the two.

write a dialogue between two people who both know this problem cold and disagree about what she should do. one of them says the only well-defined answer is a policy over the driver's information states, and since her information is identical at the two nodes, the policy has to prescribe the same thing at both -- so the real question is which randomization is best, and she should continue with some probability p at each intersection (i think it works out to 2/3, but let them derive it rather than just asserting it). the other says that's letting a limitation of the agent redefine the problem: the driver should continue at the first intersection and exit at the second, and the fact that she can't tell them apart is a fact about her epistemic situation, not about what's rational. the plan [continue, exit] is the standard of correctness; the randomization answer is what you get when you build her ignorance into the choice set and then call the result optimal.

have them actually go at each other. the first should press: what does it even mean to say she ought to do something she has no way of doing -- if the plan can't be executed, in what sense is it the thing she should do? the second should press: if you individuate the decision by her evidence, you've decided the answer before you start, because of course two decision points with the same evidence get the same prescription; but that's a stipulation about how to carve up decision problems, not a discovery about rationality. let them also fight about whether the driver should think of her two time-slices as a team trying to maximize the team's payoff, and whether that framing settles anything or just relabels the issue.

one more thing to work in: have one of them introduce a variant -- say a passenger who could tap her shoulder at the second intersection, or a note she could leave for herself (which, being absent-minded, she might ignore) -- and argue about whether that shows the [continue, exit] plan was the real answer all along or just shows that a differrent problem has a different answer.

no strawmen, both should be sharp and should answer each other's actual points. maybe 900-1300 words. don't force a resolution at the end -- if they converge, make it a real convergence, not a handshake.
