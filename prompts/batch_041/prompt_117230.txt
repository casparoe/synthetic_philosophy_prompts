Two mates of mine, Sam and Nadia, got into it last night about our friend Maya, who's trying to decide between two jobs — one in Manchester that's intellectually exactly what she wants, one in Lisbon that's near her family and a nicer life overall. Sam said the two aren't comparable at all: neither is better than the other, nor worse, nor equal — they're just incommensurable, so it's a genuinely hard choice and there's no fact of the matter about which she should take. Nadia said that's a fancy way of saying 'equal': if A isn't better than B and isn't worse, the only slot left is 'equally good', and if they're equally good she should toss a coin. Sam said no, equality means you'd swap one for a marginally better version of the other; here, if you bumped the Lisbon salary by £500 she still wouldn't be better off, so equal and incomparable come apart. Nadia said then your view is worse than you think: if small bumps never tip it, you can keep bumping Lisbon up and up — from clearly worse to plainly much better — and at every step you'd have to say 'still incomparable', so nothing would ever be better than anything. Sam said the chain only bites if you treat incomparability as a fixed fact about a pair of options, and she doesn't. She thinks there are two seperate things going on: sometimes no ranking exists at all (she calls that hard incomparability), and sometimes the ranking only gets settled by Maya actually committing to one of them — the commitment is what makes it true that the chosen one was the better one for her (soft incomparability). Nadia said that's either trivial or it means Maya's reasons are just whatever she decides, which isn't a hard choice, it's an arbitrary one.

Can you go through this move by move and say who's right at each step, not just who wins overall? And say what you think they're both missing, especially whether Sam's hard/soft distinction earns its keep. Tell me how confident you are in your verdict and which single point you're least sure about.
