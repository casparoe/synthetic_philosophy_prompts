I teach the philosophy-of-medicine unit in an upper-level bioethics course, and I'm partway through grading the midterm. The unit has turned unexpectedly personal this term: I was recently handed a diagnosis that some clinicians treat as a straightforward disease and others treat as a variation that merely needs managing, and I've since spent a lot of evenings in the naturalism/normativism literature trying to work out whether that disagreement is about facts, about values, or about words. That is probably why I wrote the exam question the way I did — I wanted to see whether students could hold the two frameworks apart without collapsing them into a fact/value standoff.

Below is the exam question and one student's answer. I would like you to grade the answer and then give me the specific comments that justify the grade, rather than a general impression of its quality.

The exam question:

Dr. Aris proposes the following case. A 30-year-old has a neuroendocrine profile that is rare in the population — present in roughly one in ten thousand people. The profile reduces the functional capacity of a particular organ system relative to the species-typical range, but the person reports no pain, no distress, and no interference with work, relationships, or daily activities, and by every self-report measure leads a flourishing life. One clinician says the person has a disease but does not know it. A patient advocate says the person is different, not diseased.

(a) Explain how a Boorse-style naturalist would classify this case, and how a normativist would. (b) Assess whether Wakefield's harmful-dysfunction analysis can reconcile the two. (c) State what you take the upshot to be for whether disease is a descriptive or an evaluative concept.

The student's answer:

Boorse's biostatistical theory treats health as the capacity to perform all species-typical biological functions at statistically normal levels, and a disease as an internal state that reduces that capacity below the norm. Applied to Dr. Aris's case, this yields a clear verdict: the organ system's functional capacity falls below the species-typical range and the cause is internal, so the condition is a disease. The absence of pain or distress is beside the point for Boorse, because the theory defines disease in terms of function, not feeling. Boorse's account is meant to be purely descriptive — species-typical function is a biological fact, and no evaluative judgment enters into calling the case a disease.

A normativist sees matters differently. On this view, to call a state a disease is already to say that it is bad or undesirable — that it would be better if the person were otherwise. Since the person is flourishing, reports no suffering, and is not impeded in any activity she values, the normativist has no basis for the value judgment and so would deny that there is a disease here.

I then considered whether Wakefield's harmful-dysfunction analysis can mediate. Wakefield's analysis is a purely normativist account: it makes harm, which is a social value judgment, the criterion of disorder, so dysfunction alone never suffices. On that analysis the case is not a disorder, because there is dysfunction but no harm.

The two frameworks therefore cannot be reconciled. One is descriptive and the other evaluative, so they are not even answering the same question; there is no fact of the matter that could settle which is right, and the disagreement is ultimately about which vocabulary we prefer. It follows that disease is fundamentally an evaluative concept, and the clinician who says the person has a disease but does not know it is simply imposing a value.

What I need from you:

Give the answer a grade on our 100-point scale plus a one-line overall verdict, and then itemize your comments by part — (a), (b), and (c) — saying for each what is correct, what is imprecise, and what is outright mistaken, with a sentence or two on what a fully correct answer would have said instead. Where the student gets something right that a hurried grader might read past or wrongly penalize, say so explicitly. Where the student's error is the kind that reads smoothly and could slip through, name the exact sentence and explain why it does not hold.

Finally, tell me what would change your grade: which alternative reading of the student's phrasing would turn one of your criticisms into a fair point rather than a mistake, and which of your own judgments here is the one you would be most likely to revise on a second look.
