I write a column on science and ideas, and I am finishing a long piece about meritocracy — about why the argument that no one ever really deserves their advantages keeps being rediscovered, and why it keeps failing to change anyone's mind. I would like to end the piece with a poem rather than a paragraph of summary, and I would like you to write it.

I am asking for a pantoum, and the form is the reason. In a pantoum the second and fourth lines of each stanza come back as the first and third lines of the next, and the final stanza folds back into the first, so that a line is asserted, then reused as a premise, then reused once more with the ground shifted under it. That is the shape of the argument I want the poem to carry, which is a regress. The speaker should be someone who has done well and is trying to give an honest account of how. Each time they claim some part of it as their own doing, the line they have just used returns as something they were handed instead: the taste for the work, the temperament that made the work bearable, the household that made the temperament, the country that made the household. By the end, the same sentence should be doing both jobs at once, and someone who works on desert and the natural lottery should be able to point to the exact lines where the sense flips.

Two things I need from the poem that the standard version does not give me. First, let the regress run one turn too far, to the point where it also takes away the critic's standing to have diagnosed it — if effort is always inherited, so is the insight that effort is always inherited — and leave that problem standing rather than settling it. Second, keep hold of the difference between deserving and being owed: a person can be owed what a fair scheme promises them without deserving it in any deeper sense. I would like that to be what the speaker is left holding at the end, not as a consolation and not as a dodge, but as the one thing the regress does not reach. Five quatrains will do. Rhyme if it comes easily, but the repetition is the point and not the rhyme, and the poem has to be legible to a reader who has never opened a book of philosophy.

Begin the response with the poem itself, with no preface or commentary.
