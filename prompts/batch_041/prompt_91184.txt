I run a fortnightly reading group that mixes economics and philosophy graduate students, and I want to open the next session with a one-page diagnostic instrument: a decision tree, given as an indented outline rather than a picture, that carries a participant from the one point everyone in the room already grants — that the game below has an infinite expected payoff, and that almost nobody will pay more than a few dollars to play it — to a single resolution, so that we spend the hour arguing from where we actually stand instead of reciting the same three resolutions from memory.

The game in its familiar form: a fair coin is flipped until it comes up heads; if the first head falls on the n-th flip, the prize is 2^n dollars. The expected prize is infinite, and the median prize is two dollars.

Please key the tree to four cases, all of which must do work somewhere in it.

(i) The unbounded game as stated, with the prize schedule chosen by an adversary who sees your utility function for money first, so that no unbounded utility function gives the game a finite expected utility.

(ii) A capped version: play stops after sixty flips, and you collect 2^60 if all sixty come up tails. The expected prize is then exactly sixty-one dollars, since each of the sixty rounds contributes a dollar, while the median prize is two dollars and a prize above a million dollars arises roughly once in half a million plays.

(iii) A version whose stakes are not money but a good that many people take to be bounded in value — additional years of life free of pain, say — with the schedule otherwise as in (i).

(iv) A version in which the coin's outcomes are not alternative chances but branches of a single physical process, so that the factor 1/2^n is a measure over branches rather than the probability of one outcome.

Requirements for the tree itself:

- Do not organise it by which author one follows. My own guess at the first cut is that it should ask what the respondent takes the paradox to be evidence about: the realizability of the game, the shape of the utility function, or the standing of expected-value maximisation. If you think the first cut should be something else, say so in one sentence and build the tree your way.

- Every internal node must be a question a participant can answer without having read anything, with two or three options, each phrased as a commitment about one of the four cases rather than as a label; a node that asks whether one accepts expected utility theory is not a node.

- Nine to twelve leaves. Each leaf carries the position's name if it has a standard one, and otherwise the word unnamed together with a short descriptive handle, plus one sentence naming that position's most awkward commitment.

- Include a node on what the 1/2^n is being read as — a credence, a chance, or a measure over branches — since case (iv) turns on it.

- Where the tree cannot separate two leaves, say so plainly instead of forcing a split, and say whether the two share a commitment or whether my choice of cases is what fails to separate them.

- Where a combination of answers is left unoccupied, record it and diagnose it in a sentence: incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored.

- Finish with one line per leaf on what someone who lands there will say about case (ii), the capped game; that is the case I expect to do most of the separating.

No citations, no reading lists, no dates, no attributions. I want the sorting, not the scholarship.
