I recently attended a public lecture on Frederick Douglass's changing view of the American Constitution, and the lecturer's account was delivered with such assurance that I have been repeating it to friends. Something about it nags at me, though, and I cannot work out where the trouble lies. His summary ran roughly as follows.

'Douglass began as a disciple of William Lloyd Garrison, denouncing the Constitution as a proslavery compact and calling for the dissolution of the Union. In the early 1850s he reversed himself and declared that the Constitution, rightly read, is a charter of liberty. The explanation for the reversal is that Douglass came to believe the framers themselves were antislavery men: they had deliberately kept the words "slave" and "slavery" out of the text precisely because they meant to set the institution on a course towards extinction. Having satisfied himself that the original intent was antislavery, Douglass could argue that the document was antislavery too. This makes him an originalist avant la lettre—he held that a constitution's meaning is fixed by the intentions of those who drafted it, and that a faithful reading of those intentions yields a libertarian document. He added that Douglass's reading only holds if one averts one's eyes from how the Constitution was actually applied: the courts, Congress and the executive treated the document as protecting slavery, so the "liberty document" was really a statement of what the Constitution ought to have meant rather than what it did mean. The lesson he drew was that constitutional meaning is never simply found but always chosen—Douglass shows that the same text can be made to serve slavery or liberty depending on the story one tells about its origins, so that interpretation is ultimately a species of political rhetoric rather than a genuine constraint.'

I suspect this is mostly right and subtly wrong in ways I cannot pin down, and I am inclined to accept the final relativist conclusion, which is partly why I want it tested. Could you go through the account claim by claim and tell me what a historian of American abolition, or a constitutional theorist, would accept, what they would wince at, and what is straightforwardly mistaken? I am particularly unsure about three things. First, whether Douglass's argument actually rested on the framers' intentions at all, or whether he deliberately set that question aside—and if the latter, what he took the basis of constitutional meaning to be. Second, whether his treatment of the specific slavery-related provisions (representation, fugitives, the slave trade) was a careful textual argument or a rhetorical one, and whether it survives being stated precisely. Third, whether the lecturer's relativist conclusion follows from anything true in the summary, or whether Douglass's position actually presupposes a fairly demanding constraint on interpretation that would embarrass that conclusion rather than support it. I should also like to know whether calling Douglass an originalist is illuminating or an anachronism, and if the latter, what label would be more accurate. Please be specific about which parts of the account a defender of Douglass would concede and which they would reject outright.
