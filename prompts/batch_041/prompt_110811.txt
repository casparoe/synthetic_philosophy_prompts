I've recently changed jobs — I moved from a statistics group into a machine learning team, and the first model I was responsible for looked excellent in backtesting and then, once it was live, did no better than a coin flip on the cases that mattered most. A colleague of mine, who read philosophy as an undergraduate, told me I had simply met Hume's problem, and that the whole business of inductive bias in learning theory is a footnote to it. I was sceptical enough to join a small reading group that is working through A Treatise of Human Nature over the winter, and I have been given Book I, Part III, Section VI — 'Of the inference from the impression to the idea' — to lead. I can follow the general shape of the argument, but there are three sentences near the end of the section that I cannot get to hold still:

> Our foregoing method of reasoning will easily convince us, that there can be no demonstrative arguments to prove, that those instances, of which we have had no experience, resemble those, of which we have had experience. We can at least conceive a change in the course of nature; which sufficiently proves, that such a change is not absolutely impossible. To form a clear idea of any thing, is an undeniable argument for its possibility, and is alone a refutation of any pretended demonstration against it.

What I want is a close reading of that particular move, in Hume's own terms, rather than a general account of the problem of induction. How much of the work is being done by the conceivability claim, and how much by the earlier observation that no object implies the existence of another when the objects are considered in themselves? Is the second sentence an independent argument or a restatement of the first? And is the principle in the third sentence — that a clear idea establishes possibility — one Hume is entitled to, given that his own theory of ideas treats a clear idea as at bottom a recombination of simple ideas received in impressions? If recombination is all it takes, we can form clear ideas of a great many things, and I would like to know what in Hume's system stops the principle from proving far too much.

Then the comparison that started all this. In my field the no-free-lunch results are read as showing that no learning procedure can be guaranteed to beat chance across all possible target functions, so that generalization requires a prior commitment — a bias — about which functions are likely. My colleague thinks Hume is making that same point in a modal key. My suspicion is that they are different arguments: the no-free-lunch result is an averaging argument over a class of functions and concludes that no learner has an advantage on average, whereas Hume seems to argue from the separability of ideas to a conclusion about what can be demonstrated with certainty, which is a claim about justification rather than about advantage. If I am right that these come apart, tell me precisely where they diverge and whether Hume's version, read sympathetically, is stronger or weaker than the one I use at work; if I am wrong and they are the same argument, I would rather see the identification made in detail than asserted.
