I cover forecasting and grand strategy for a magazine, and I keep running into a move that I would like to be able to name in print.

The scene is always roughly the same. Someone is predicting the decline of a power, and they reach for a base rate. The median lifespan of the great powers that have come and gone since the sixteenth century is, let us say, somewhere north of two hundred years, so a power that is now two hundred and fifty years old is on borrowed time. Someone else, working from what appears to be the same list, reaches the opposite conclusion: if you drop a pin into the history of any long-lived power, you will almost always land on a year in which it has already been around for a very long time and still has a long run ahead of it, so age is not a risk factor at all. A third person observes that of every great power that has ever existed, one hundred per cent have eventually gone under, and treats that as the base rate that matters. Three rates, one archive, three policies.

Nobody in these arguments disputes the historical record. What they are disputing, without saying so, is what the rate is a rate over: polities, or years in which polities exist, or polities that have already finished. Each choice is defensible, each is made silently, and each one settles the conclusion before the evidence is consulted, which means the evidence cannot settle which choice to make. The outside view is supposed to stop an analyst from exempting the case in front of them, but the outside view requires a reference class, and choosing the class looks like exactly the kind of judgment the outside view was meant to police.

I know about the reference class problem, and it does not seem to be this: there, the trouble is that an individual belongs to indefinitely many classes and nothing picks one out. Here everyone agrees on the class — great powers since 1500 — and the trouble is one level down, in what you count within it. I also know about survivorship bias, and that is not it either, since nothing has been lost; both sides are reading the same list. So: is there an established name for this? Is it one phenomenon or three wearing one coat? I am not after a label — I want to know what exactly the thing is, how it relates to the nearest concepts I do know, which of those it fits best, and where its edges are. If nothing established covers it, coin a term, define it so that it excludes the two neighbours I mentioned, and tell me whether the choice of unit is a matter of taste or whether one of the three really is the right rate for the purpose.
