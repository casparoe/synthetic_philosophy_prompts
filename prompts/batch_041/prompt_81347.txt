I write compilers for a living, and I have gotten myself stuck on what I am fairly sure is the rule-following problem — the one Wittgenstein raises about following a rule, in the skeptical reading Kripke gives it. I would like your credence in a specific claim, built up from parts, rather than a tour of the literature.

The case that started this: our internal language has no written specification beyond a suite of 200 unit tests. The original interpreter passes all of them. A colleague then rewrote the interpreter; it also passes all 200, but the two implementations disagree on inputs that no test covers, and we found this out only when a customer hit one. Assume, for the sake of the question, that the two implementations are equally simple by any measure we can state, that there is no reference semantics, and that nothing anywhere records what the language was meant to do on the uncovered inputs. My colleague concludes that the language simply has no meaning there, so the choice is a product decision and not a question of correctness. My instinct is that one of the two implementations was already correct and that the tests merely failed to reveal it, but I cannot say what could make that true.

Give me your credence in this proposition:

(P) When an agent has applied a rule on finitely many occasions, there is a determinate fact of the matter about which rule it was following — a fact not exhausted by its past responses and not constituted by what its community would accept as correct.

I want a number between 0 and 1, assembled from parts. Please address each of the following, give it a rough probability, and say in one or two sentences whether, if it is the right account, (P) comes out true or false:

1. The problem is genuine: the finiteness of the evidence really does leave meaning underdetermined, and this is a discovery about meaning rather than a symptom of a confused picture of it.
2. The dispositional account: the fact of the matter is just the agent's total disposition to respond, including on occasions it has never actually faced.
3. The communal account: there is no fact beyond what the community is licensed to assert, and following a rule is a matter of how a shared practice runs.
4. The dissolution: once we stop assuming that every rule needs an accompanying interpretation, the puzzle evaporates, and following a rule is a trained capacity or practice rather than a state with hidden content.
5. The best-explanation account: among all the rules consistent with the past applications, the fact of the matter is fixed by whichever one best explains the agent's behavior — most compresses it, or is the most natural continuation of it.
6. The family-resemblance account: there is no single thing tracked by the word rule, so the demand for one determinate fact is the mistake.

Then tell me how you combine these into the number for (P). I realize they may overlap and may not cover the space, so please say what you did about that rather than presenting them as a partition.

Finally: which single sub-question is my number for (P) most sensitive to, and what argument or consideration — not a reading list — would move it most, and in which direction? For calibration, my own number is about 0.35 and a friend who works in logic puts it at 0.8; tell me whether our gap is better explained by a disagreement over item 1, over item 3, or over something I failed to list.
