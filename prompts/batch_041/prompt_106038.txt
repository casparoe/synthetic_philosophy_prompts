I've been TAing a course on well-being this term and I've somehow ended up defending a view I didn't expect to: that a life goes well for you just to the extent that you're getting what you actually want, wants taken as they come, no idealization, no "well if you were fully informed you'd want X instead" laundering. The experience machine is supposed to be the killer objection, but I've got a reply I'm kind of proud of: I say it isn't a counterexample at all, because when people report that they wouldn't plug in, they're just reporting a want — a want to stay in contact with the real world — and on my view a want is a want, so the refusal is already vindicated by the theory. The theory doesn't get embarrassed by the intuition, it contains it. A friend in the reading group says I've basically made the view unfalsifiable and that I should be worried about that, but I don't see why that's automatically a bad thing for a theory of welfare.

Anyway I want you to be adversarial about this, because I suspect I've made the view too cheap. Here's the wrinkle I keep chewing on. Picture a machine that doesn't just feed you experiences — call it the ratchet machine. It scans your actual preferences, then runs a simulated life for you in which, at regular intervals, it quietly rewrites your preferences so that whatever you happen to be experiencing right then becomes what you most want. So at every moment you're getting exactly what you actually want, and the edits never register as a cost, because each edit is made to the version of you that has the preference afterward. By my theory this is a maximally good life. That strikes me as obviously wrong, and I genuinely can't tell whether that's a real problem for the theory or whether I'm just smuggling in a rival intuition through the back door.

So treat my position as the thing on trial, not the machine. What would count against it? I don't just want the textbook objections — I want the ones that bite against the specific version I've stated, including anything the ratchet machine exposes that the ordinary experience machine doesn't. Sort them: which are decisive, which are merely awkward, which a committed defender would happily concede as the cost of doing business. And give me an honest verdict on whether my reply to the experience machine has insulated the view from any possible evidence, and if so whether that's fatal or just a feature of theories at this level of abstraction. You're a philosopher who works on theories of welfare, so please think it through carefully — it's fine to start by just dumping a list of candidate defeaters before you rank them.
