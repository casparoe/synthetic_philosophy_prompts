I'm on a small advisory committee a government set up to answer one question, species by species: does this animal get full welfare protection or not. We're not a lab, we can't run new experiments, and we meet maybe four times a year with about a month to prep each case. We're getting squeezed from all sides right now — the aquaculture people want to know whether humane slaughter for crustaceans is worth the money, the insect-farming people want to know if their larvae count, and the shellfish farmers think the whole question is absurd when it comes to an oyster. Some countries have already pulled octopuses and crabs into their animal-welfare statutes and everyone assumes we're next, but nobody has ever told us how to actually decide.

Act as a philosopher of mind who's spent years on animal consciousness and who has also had to sit in rooms like this one and watch theory collide with a deadline. Before you write anything, brainstorm a bit: what would a usable procedure even look like here, and where are the traps.

Then give me the procedure itself. I want the questions in the order we should ask them, what counts as a sufficient answer at each step, where the procedure stops and just says no, and where it hands the thing back to us as a value judgment we have to own rather than a fact we could look up. A few things I'd like baked in:

- Treat sentience as dimensional rather than a yes/no — a capacity for valenced experience, plus some way of asking how rich or integrated it is — instead of a single test you either pass or fail.
- Keep nociception and sentience seperate. Reflex withdrawal from damage is not the thing we care about; suffering is.
- Use markers or indicators rather than any one experiment, and say which markers we should trust most and why.
- Build in a precautionary rule, with a burden of proof that slides depending on how many individuals are affected and how bad the likely harm is — and be explicit that where the threshold sits is our call, not the evidence's.
- Say what we should count as evidence at all — behavioural, neuroanatomical, evolutionary — and where each kind is weak or circular.

Then run it on three cases and show your work: (1) the octopus, which everyone expects us to wave through; (2) a crab or some other decapod, where the evidence is alot thinner; (3) an oyster or mussel, which I'll admit I'd like you to protect just to be safe, but I want to see whether the procedure you built actually gets there or whether I'd have to move the threshold to get it — if it's the latter, say so plainly.

Finally, tell me where this procedure will misfire: the cases it can't handle, the steps that look like findings but are really us smuggling in values, and what we should do when we deadlock. Don't pad it, and don't hand me a reading list or a pile of citations — I need the thing itself, not a bibliography.
