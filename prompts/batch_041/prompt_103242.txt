I'm a features editor at a mid-sized ideas-and-science magazine — I commission the trends slot — and I keep getting into the same fight with my colleagues, which has now become a genuine editorial problem. Every fortnight or so someone pitches me a generation story: Gen Z are quitting hustle culture, Millennials are the loneliest cohort, Gen Alpha are the first AI-native generation, that sort of thing. And I have a persistent itch that half of these are doing something not quite legitimate — smuggling in a claim about a real kind of person when all they've actually got is a birth-year bracket plus a bit of survey data that probably says more about the year the survey ran than about the people in it. But I can't articulate the difference sharply enough to use it at the pitch meeting, and I don't want to be the bloke who just declares that generations aren't real and kills every story, because sometimes the cohort frame is exactly right and the age-bracket stories are the boring ones.

What I want from you is a decision procedure I can actually run at the desk — a checklist or a flowchart in words, with the questions in order and what counts as a sufficient answer to each — for deciding whether a pitch that treats a 'generation' as a kind is doing legitimate conceptual work or is just dressing up an age band as an entity. I'm told the philosophy of kinds has something relevant here (I half-remember the homeostatic property cluster account, and Ian Hacking's idea that classifications can end up making up the people they classify) and that demographers have a whole identification problem about separating cohort effects from period effects from plain age effects, so please use whatever of that is genuinely usable and translate it into things a non-statistician editor can ask in ten minutes. The procedure needs to tell me: what has to be true of a cohort before it's a genuine kind rather than a stipulated label; how to tell a cohort effect from a period effect from an age effect when the pitch gives me all three mashed together; when the variation inside the cohort is so large that the kind is doing no explanatory work at all; whether the boundary is being discovered or merely drawn; and, importantly, the point at which the procedure should stop and hand the decision back to me on values grounds — because sometimes we run a generation story as a useful fiction and I want the procedure honest about that instead of pretending it's a pure fact question.

Then run it on three pitches so I can see it work: (1) 'Gen Z are the loneliest generation'; (2) 'the people who came of age during the 2008 crash are a distinct cohort in their attitudes to work'; and (3) 'Gen Alpha are the first AI-native generation'. At least one of those should come out with an answer I won't like — I'd rather find out now than in the letters page. And please flag the cases the procedure simply can't settle, where I have to make the call myself, and be straight about which steps are just judgment dressed up as method. Write it in British English, and keep the register pitched at an editor rather than a demographer.
