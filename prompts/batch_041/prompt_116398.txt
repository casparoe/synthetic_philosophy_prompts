So I've somehow volunteered to run the is/ought gap for our cross-departmental reading group next month — it's a mixed bag: two economists, a biologist, a historian and a lawyer, no philosophers, and they'll have done no prep. Sixty minutes, whiteboard. I want to actually teach the thing rather than just say the slogan at them, so don't explain the gap to me, I've got that; plan the session for me.

What I need from you:

**The order.** What has to be in place before the question even means anything to them — my instinct is that they need the notion of a valid argument, and the difference between a premise being true and a conclusion following from it, before "you can't get an ought from an is" can be anything other than noise, but tell me if that's the right first move or whether something else has to come before it. Then say where the naturalistic fallacy and the appeal-to-nature thing slot in — before the gap, after it, or never at this level — and why. Be explicit about what depends on what, because I'm fairly sure the whole hour collapses if I get the first ten minutes wrong.

**The carrying example.** My plan was to open with "eating meat is natural, therefore it's fine" (or the vegan version, whichever lands harder), but I've got a nagging feeling that's the wrong example, because it seems to smuggle in two mistakes at once — natural therefore good, and unnatural therefore bad — and neither of them is actually the is/ought point. If it's the wrong one, say so and give me the right one: something where the slide from description to prescription is invisible on first hearing, where the hidden premise is genuinely tempting, and where the diagnosis generalises. And tell me what the example must not be, i.e. what it would teach them if I got it wrong.

**The misconceptions, in the order they'll actually turn up.** What I'm expecting: the economists do cost-benefit analysis for a living and will be convinced they derive oughts from ises all day, so I need something ready for "but we do this every Tuesday"; someone will conclude that if the gap is real then morality is just made up and anything goes; someone else will over-correct and decide that facts are simply irrelevant to ethics, which I'm fairly sure is wrong and definately not what Hume was on about; and most of them will assume the gap runs both ways — that you can't get an is out of an ought either — which I think is plainly false ("you ought to go and help him" gets you "there's someone who needs help"). Where do I head each of these off, and which one should I let them walk into and then take apart rather than pre-empting?

**The thing I've never known what to do with.** Years ago someone showed me a smart-arse proof that looks like it gets an ought out of an is and seems completely valid — roughly: he's a sea captain, so he ought to do whatever a sea captain ought to do. There's a wierd cousin of it involving tea and the English, and another where a moral claim pops out of a purely descriptive premise just by being tacked onto the end of an "or". I don't know whether these are real counterexamples to the gap, tricks with the word "ought" getting carried along inside the conclusion, or just sophistry — and I'd rather not have the lawyer produce one cold. Where do they belong in the session, and what's the honest thing to say about them?

**The check at the end.** One question or one task that would show me whether they've got the gap rather than just been able to repeat it back — ideally something where the right answer and the slogan answer come apart.

**And what to cut.** I'm already planning to refuse the whole metaethical zoo (non-cognitivism, error theory, the various realisms), and the question of whether the gap is a theorem of logic or just a methodological warning, since I'm honestly not sure myself and don't want to bluff in front of the lawyer. Tell me what else to leave out and where in the session I should say out loud that I'm leaving it out, so nobody goes home thinking I skipped something embarrassing.

No reading lists, please — they won't do them, and the ones who do will just look up the titles.
