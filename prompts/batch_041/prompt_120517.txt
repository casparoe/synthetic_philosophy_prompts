My closest friend from university is a behavioural ecologist — she spent her twenties working on the nesting behaviour of seabirds and has spent the last few years applying the same cold eye to humans — and she has become, in a way I find genuinely instructive rather than merely irritating, incapable of letting a human habit go past without sorting it into one of two boxes. Either the thing is built in: it is there because doing it did something for our ancestors, and you would expect to find it in us no matter how we had been raised. Or it is a spillover: an accidental by-product of something else we happen to have, not really for anything at all, the way a shadow is not for anything. She treats these as verdicts about a fact rather than about taste; when I suggested she was probably just choosing whichever label sounded nicer, she was genuinely offended. Her calls so far, roughly, are these. The retching, nose-wrinkling revulsion at the smell of rotting meat or faeces: built in. The way a baby's cry cuts through a crowded room and is almost impossible for an adult to ignore: built in. The fear of snakes: built in — and she holds to that even though other animals are frightened of snakes too, which I had assumed would count against it. A song stuck in your head for an entire afternoon: spillover. Explaining your day, in full sentences, to a dog who understands none of it: spillover. Shouting at a printer, or at the television during a match: spillover, even though everyone does it. Her colleagues think this whole way of talking is unfalsifiable storytelling, she thinks they are cowards, and I am stuck in the middle of that argument without knowing which side is better.

Now she has agreed to give a seminar in my department and has asked us to send in cases in advance, and I want to spend my slot well. The case I want to put to her is blushing: the way a person's face and neck go red when they are embarrassed, caught out, or suddenly the centre of attention. It seems to happen to everyone, it is entirely involuntary, and it gives you away at precisely the moment you would most like to disappear, which is a strange thing for a body to do if nothing is being accomplished by it. What I want from you is not really a verdict about blushing for its own sake. I want to know what she will say, and, more than that, what rule she is actually running — because I suspect that more than one rule fits every call she has made so far, and that they come apart on this case. So: say which rules are consistent with her record, say what each of them predicts about the blushing case, tell me which one you would bet on and why, and name the further case that would separate them, including a better one than mine if you think there is one.

I should add that I am not a philosopher by training — I did a master's in marine biology before drifting into science communication — so please do not assume I will fill in the standard moves myself; spell the reasoning out. I do not need references or a reading list; I need the argument. And I would rather you were honest about how much of this is inference and how much is me wanting there to be a method where there may only be a temperament. Tell me which consideration would actually change your prediction about her, and where your reasoning is most likely to be wrong. If the honest answer is that her record is too thin to decide, say so, and tell me what would thicken it.
