I am assembling the program for a small, well-funded two-day workshop on Moore's paradox: six papers, an audience of analytic philosophers of mind and language, a couple of formal epistemologists, and one or two researchers on delusion and confabulation who are there to keep the rest of us honest. I want to solicit work on the questions that actually matter, so what I need from you is not a survey of the area but a defended agenda: which open questions most deserve the six slots, ranked, with the ranking argued rather than asserted.

Make the criteria explicit and put them in tension with one another. Which questions are upstream of others, so that settling one would settle, simplify, or dissolve the rest? Which are tractable for this audience within a two-year project? Which would genuinely change how anyone thinks about belief, assertion, or self-knowledge if answered, and which are unresolved only because nobody has bothered to resolve them? Where the criteria pull apart, say which you would sacrifice and why, and say what would move a question up or down the list: a formal result, a new case, an argument that a dispute is merely verbal, a finding from the delusion literature. I also want an explicit list of the questions I should not spend a slot on.

Some candidates I already have in view, to be kept, cut, reordered, or replaced:

- What is the phenomenon such that the cases below fall under it at all? Is there one thing here, or a family held together by resemblance?
- Is the paradox fundamentally about belief, about assertion, or about a norm governing one of them, and can any of these be derived from the others?
- What does the absurdity of the first-person case show about the epistemology of self-knowledge — that some form of first-person authority or transparency is constitutive of belief, or only something about rational revision?
- Is there a general theory of self-defeating speech and thought, of which Moorean assertions are one instance alongside performed absurdities such as promising without intending or declaring that one is not speaking — and would such a theory explain anything, or only redescribe?
- Does the absurdity come in degrees, and does any account predict the gradient rather than merely accommodate it?
- What does the paradox constrain in the logic of belief — positive and negative introspection, the coherence of a creature with attitudes it cannot introspect?

The ranking has to answer to the cases. Say of each whether it is a genuine instance of the phenomenon or merely odd-sounding, and what its classification does to the ordering: the paradigm assertion that it is raining and that one does not believe it; the epistemic variants (it is raining but I do not know it; it is raining but I am not certain it is; it is raining but I am certain it is not); the plural and impersonal versions (it is raining but we do not believe it; it is raining but nobody believes it); the retrospective ones, where the past-tense belief form is plainly unobjectionable but the memory form is not obviously so; the practical form (I am going to stay home tonight but I do not intend to); the performed forms; the embedded forms, under negation, conditionals, questions, and reported speech; the collective case, in which a committee's minutes record both that the bridge is sound and that the committee does not believe it is; and the third-personal self-report, in which a speaker uses his own name to say that it is raining and that he does not believe it.

No reading lists and no citations — I want the argument, not the bibliography, and I do not want the ranking to lean on which questions happen to be fashionable. Tell me, finally, which single question's answer would most shrink the field, whether you expect that answer to be deflationary, and which of the questions above you suspect is a verbal dispute in substantive clothing.
