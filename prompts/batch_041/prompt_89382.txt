I write about science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and for the past two years I have been reporting on the small industry of people who try to forecast what the world will look like in fifty or a hundred years. The reporting has pushed me toward a position I now hold with some conviction, and I would like you to tell me what should count against it.

The position is this. When you want to forecast the fate of a large polity — a hegemon, an empire, a civilization — the disciplined move is to set aside the particulars, locate the case in a reference class of comparable entities, and reason from that class's base rates: how long such entities typically last, in what ways they typically end, and at what age the endings cluster. The particulars — this leader, this constitution, this technology — are mostly noise, and the inside-view conviction that this case is different should be discounted heavily, precisely because it is the conviction every member of the class generated about itself. Applied honestly, the method yields determinate forecasts with meaningful confidence intervals. And most of the error in this genre comes from failing to apply the method, not from applying it.

The machinery I have been using is where I would expect the pressure to show up, so let me put it on the table. I have been treating the survival of a polity as a hazard process, which has a consequence I cannot shake: if the hazard is roughly constant, the process is memoryless, and the fact that a polity has already lasted eighty years tells you nothing about how much longer it has; if the hazard rises with age, then age matters, and the outside view needs an inside-view datum to say which regime it is in. I have also noticed that I can only build the class out of polities that left enough of a record to be studied, and records are kept disproportionately by those that lasted long enough to keep them; and that I cannot finally say whether the present case belongs to the class until I know how its story ends, which makes the classification partly retrospective. My provisional fix has been to average over several plausible reference classes rather than committing to one. I have been extending the same logic beyond states, to the lifespans of institutions and even of ideas.

What I want from you:

- What would count against this position, sorted from decisive to merely awkward — not the generic complaint that prediction is hard, but the specific observations, arguments, and formal results that should move me.
- What the position predicts that its rivals do not. I take one commitment to be that detailed inside-view knowledge of a state's institutions, economy, or leadership should barely improve forecasts, and that a dramatic, well-documented reform should barely move the number. Make that explicit, and tell me what would show it false.
- Which defeaters a committed outside-viewer would concede and which they would dismiss, and on what grounds.
- An honest verdict on whether the position has been insulated into unfalsifiability — particularly by the move of averaging over reference classes, which I worry lets me absorb any contrary result — and whether that is a fatal defect or the ordinary condition of a forecasting method.

The thing I would personally find hardest to explain away is a documented case in which a forecast made from a well-specified reference class was decisively refuted while inside-view analysts, reasoning about the particulars, got it right. If no such case exists, say so. If such cases exist and the position survives them, tell me how.

I do not need citations or a reading list; I am after the argument.
