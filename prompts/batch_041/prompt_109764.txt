Question 4 (25 points). In this course we distinguished four charges that are often run together:

(1) anachronism: attributing to people in the past beliefs, motives, or concepts that belong to a later period;

(2) the historian's fallacy: explaining or judging a past agent's decision as though the agent had the information and perspective that became available only afterwards (named and defined by David Hackett Fischer in 1970, in Historians' Fallacies);

(3) Whig history: narrating the past as a march toward the present, so that past figures are praised or blamed according to how far they anticipate us (Herbert Butterfield, The Whig Interpretation of History, 1931);

(4) presentism: the charge that a historian's questions, standards, or verdicts are drawn from the present rather than from the past. Within (4) we drew a further line between selection presentism (the questions a historian chooses to ask are inevitably shaped by present concerns) and verdict presentism (the standards by which a historian condemns or excuses past conduct are drawn from the present).

Now consider this case. Prompted by a recent epidemic, a historian writes about how a city's authorities responded to a plague in the seventeenth century and concludes that they were negligent. Which of (1)-(4) are avoidable here and which are not? Does the selection/verdict distinction hold up under this case, or does the case collapse it? Answer in no more than a page, and make clear in each case what the historian would have to do in order to avoid the charge.

Student's answer:

The case separates the four charges fairly well, and everything turns on what the historian can actually control.

Anachronism is avoidable in principle. The historian need not say that the authorities believed in germs, or that they understood plague as a contagious disease passed between persons; the beliefs should be described in their own terms, as miasma, as divine punishment, as corruption of the air, without smuggling ours in. That is a matter of care with the sources, and it can be done.

The historian's fallacy is harder to avoid, because the verdict that the authorities were negligent is itself a present-day moral standard projected backwards. Fischer's point, as I understand it, is that we should not judge people in the past by the moral standards of the present; the question to ask is whether the authorities behaved as we would have wanted them to, and if not, we may call them negligent. Since we now know that quarantine and the destruction of infected goods worked, the historian who finds the authorities negligent is applying a standard they could not have met, and that is the fallacy.

Whig history is avoidable here, and the historian mostly avoids it: the account does not treat the seventeenth century as a stage on the way to modern epidemiology, and the authorities are not praised or blamed for anticipating us. Selection presentism, by contrast, is not avoidable. The historian chose the plague because of a present epidemic, and nobody chooses a topic from nowhere; the question itself is present-shaped, and that is not a fault.

But this is where the line between selection presentism and verdict presentism breaks down. If the questions we ask are inevitably shaped by present concerns, then the standards we bring to the answers must be as well, since the standard is what makes the question interesting in the first place. So verdict presentism is unavoidable too, and the only honest course is to state one's present commitments openly rather than pretend to a view from nowhere. The finding of negligence is therefore not a lapse but the price of writing history at all.
