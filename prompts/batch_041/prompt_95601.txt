I teach a ninety-minute session on the threefold division of liberty — negative, positive, and republican — to a mixed master's cohort in a technology-and-governance programme. Roughly half of them are engineers who will want the taxonomy stated before anything else is said; the other half are lawyers who will not accept a definition until they have watched it applied to a case. Almost none have read any political theory. I know the standard formulations and I am not asking you to rehearse them for me. What I need is a plan for the session: the order in which the components must be introduced and why that order rather than another, so that each step is visibly required by the one before it; the single case that should carry the whole ninety minutes; the misconceptions to expect, and the precise point in the sequence at which each should be headed off; the check at the end that tells me whether the distinctions have landed; and what I should openly refuse to cover at this level.

On the carrying case, I keep reaching for candidates and distrusting all of them. The first is the master who never interferes, either in its classical form or in a contemporary variant I would prefer: a company town in which a single firm owns the housing, the store, and the clinic, has never evicted anyone or punished anyone for their politics, and whose residents report feeling entirely free. The second is the reverse case, in which interference occurs without domination — a member of a housing cooperative, or a taxpayer, who is constrained by a rule she voted for and would vote for again. The third, which I am tempted by and suspect may be a distraction, is an automated scoring system that gates access to work or credit, where no human exercises judgement at any point and which, so far as anyone can tell, has never actually been triggered against a given person; is a power that no one can wield a case of domination at all? Tell me which of these, if any, should carry the session, whether the reverse case belongs in the sequence or should be held back for the final check, and what the carrying case must have and must lack — my suspicion is that a merely colourful case will do no work.

The misconceptions I anticipate, in the order I expect them to surface: that liberty is the same thing as ability, so that the person who cannot afford something is unfree; that domination requires an interfering master, and moreover a malicious one, so that a benevolent employer, or a landlord who holds a key he never uses, cannot be a dominator; that a power which is never exercised is not really a power, so that domination without interference is merely possible and therefore does not count; that the three conceptions are three vocabularies for one underlying thing, with the republican one amounting to the negative one plus a moralising adjective; and that the positive conception is a matter of inner self-mastery, a private psychological achievement. I am least sure where the last of these should be handled, and whether the positive conception belongs in this session at all rather than being flagged and set aside; tell me which way you would go, and what is lost either way.

For the final check I want a single task that shows whether the students can use the distinctions rather than recite them. Give me the plan as an ordered sequence with each misconception attached to the step where it should be intercepted, not as general advice about teaching. I am not asking for a reading list, and I would rather cut material than have the session end as a survey of positions.
