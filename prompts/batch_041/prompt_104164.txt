I'm a graduate student splitting my time between philosophy of science and an interpretability research group, and I keep deferring the front section of a paper I'm writing: a specification of what a theory of representation in neural networks would actually have to deliver before I'm willing to treat the claim "this model represents X" as something with a truth value rather than a useful façon de parler. Before I read more papers that each quietly assume a different answer to that, I want the desiderata on the table, not another survey. Please act as a philosopher of science and cognitive scientist with a working knowledge of mechanistic interpretability, think carefully, brainstorm the relevant phenomena first, and then compress your thinking into a requirements list rather than padding it with a literature tour.

The deliverable is the list: each requirement a theory of neural representation must satisfy to count as adequate, stated with the consideration or case that motivates it, and marked as either non-negotiable given my purpose (a theory good enough to guide interpretability work and to underwrite a safety audit that wants to say the model represents a goal it never expresses) or merely a preference of one camp. Anchor the list in the cases I keep colliding with, so the requirements earn their place: a linear probe decodes a property from a layer with near-perfect accuracy, yet ablating or steering along the probe direction leaves behaviour untouched, while a direction found by activation patching causally moves behaviour yet no probe recovers it cleanly; a single unit responds to many unrelated inputs, and a sparse autoencoder re-describes the same layer as many near-monosemantic features without changing the network's function at all; adding a steering vector makes the model produce more of a concept but also shifts behaviour it shouldn't; two networks with near-identical internal geometry, by a standard similarity metric, behave differently, and two networks that behave identically have quite different geometry; the same concept is decodable from different directions across seeds and layers even though behaviour is stable; and a feature that is plainly used in one task is inert in another.

The requirements must force the theory to confront, at minimum: the difference between information merely being present and something being represented; whether content is fixed by correlation, by causal role in the computation, or by the theorist's decoding procedure; how the theory accommodates distributed and superposed representations without turning "a representation" into a fiction; whether representations must be able to misrepresent; whether representation is a two-place or a three-place relation (network, content, and interpreter or downstream consumer); and what role the network's task or context plays in fixing content. If some subset of the requirements cannot be jointly satisfied, say so explicitly and say what has to give; I suspect the causal-role requirement and the misrepresentation requirement pull apart, and that any requirement demanding stable, context-free content collides with what the probing results show. Finally, briefly score the main families of view against the list (representation as decodable information, as causal or structural role, as task-relative posit, and as recovered sparse feature), but keep that part short — the criteria are the deliverable, and I'll do the full scoring myself.
