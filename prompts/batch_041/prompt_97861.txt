I host a small interview podcast on mind and perception, aimed at graduate students and interested outsiders, and next week's guest is a researcher who has spent a career arguing that perception is encapsulated: that what you see, hear, and taste is not altered by what you believe, desire, or expect, and that the apparent counterexamples are artifacts of attention, memory, or the way the task was posed. This is the descendant of Fodor's modularity picture, and the guest leans hard on something like Pylyshyn's old criterion that a genuine top-down effect has to be semantically coherent with the content of the cognitive state, not merely triggered by its arousal. I'd like eight to ten questions that make him do more than restate the thesis, and for each of the questions below I want a follow-up keyed to the answer he is most likely to give, so that I'm ready whichever way he moves.

I need at least one question on each of the following, and I'd rather they be sequenced so that they build. First, how he handles the counterexamples that do the most work for the other side -- coming to see an ambiguous figure one way after a hint, or being told that the coiled thing on the path is a snake rather than a rope -- without simply reclassifying them as shifts in attention or judgment; press him on whether that reclassification is an empirical finding or a stipulation. Second, where exactly he draws the line between attention and expectation, which he concedes do affect perception, and genuine cognitive penetration, and how he draws it without making the impenetrability thesis true by definition; the semantic-coherence criterion is the obvious place to press, since it threatens to sort every inconvenient result into the not-really-perception bin. Third, whether the thesis could in principle be falsified, and what a study would have to look like to count against it -- here I want the follow-up to force him to commit to a concrete result rather than a gesture at future work. I also want one question built around the confound that an effect which distorts perception would equally distort the measurement of perception, so that the very design meant to reveal penetration is the one that hides it; and one question on the charge that his camp defines perception by the criterion rather than discovering a boundary in nature. Close with something genuinely uncomfortable: what evidence, if it arrived tomorrow, would make him abandon the position, and what he thinks is doing the real explanatory work in the studies he dismisses.

Please keep every question answerable from the armchair, with no slides or figures, and assume the guest cannot refer me to specific papers on air -- I want him to argue, not cite. No warm-up biography questions and nothing that invites a survey of the field; each question should land on a specific joint in the position, and each follow-up should assume the reply I'll actually get rather than the one I'd like.
