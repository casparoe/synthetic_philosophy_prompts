'There are two theories of function, and they are rivals. The historical theory says the function of a trait is whatever it was selected for: hearts pump because pumping is why hearts stuck around, and a trait that wasn't favored by selection doesn't have a function at all, it just has effects. The systemic theory, sometimes called the causal-role theory, says the function of a part is whatever it does that contributes to some capacity of the whole system it belongs to — so the function of the heart is whatever the heart does that keeps you alive, and by the same token the noise your knees make when you crouch has a function too, since it's something the system does. On this view, saying what a part is for is just describing how a working system is put together, and you don't need to know any history to do it. The historical theory gets malfunction right — a stopped heart still has the function of pumping, which is why we can call it a broken heart and not just a lump of meat — but it pays for that by making function depend on a past that may have nothing to do with the present, so a trait that arose as an accidental side effect of something else and now happens to keep the animal alive has no function on the historical view, which is obviously absurd, since the trait is plainly doing something important. And the historical theory was effectively refuted once people pointed out how many traits are byproducts of other traits rather than adaptations, so most biologists today have quietly given up on it, and the systemic theory is the one that matches how physiologists and doctors actually talk about parts of the body.'
