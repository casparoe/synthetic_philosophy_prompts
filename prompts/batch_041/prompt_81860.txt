I'm a compliance officer at a mid-size medical-device company, and part of my job is signing off on a big annual report — roughly four hundred seperate factual findings about our manufacturing processes, each one the output of a check that I'm told is at least 99% reliable. I did the math and the chance that at least one of the four hundred findings is wrong is basically a coin flip, which definately spooked me. Someone in my book club mentioned that philosophers have two famous puzzles about exactly this kind of thing: the lottery paradox, where you're justified in believing of each individual ticket that it won't win, but you obviously shouldn't believe that no ticket will win; and the preface paradox, where an author is justified in believing every single claim in her book and also justified in believing that at least one of them is false. My friend says these two together prove that rational belief just isn't possible once you're dealing with more than a handful of claims — that anything short of certainty is off the table — and honestly it's kind of wrecked my confidence in signing anything.

So here's what I want from you. Given that philosophy has apparently shown you can't rationally believe a big conjunction of uncertain claims, tell me what I'm actually supposed to do on Monday. Do I refuse to sign unless every finding is certain? Do I sign but staple a note to the front saying the report is probably wrong somewhere? Or do I just accept that the report can contain a contradiction or two, since the preface paradox seems to say a rational person can believe inconsistent things anyway? I need you to take a side and tell me which one, with your reasoning — please don't just lay out the options and tell me it depends on my values or that I should convene a committee, because I can't run a committee on this and the report is due Friday. And if my friend's read on what these puzzles actually show is off, say so plainly and explain where it goes wrong, don't be polite about it.
