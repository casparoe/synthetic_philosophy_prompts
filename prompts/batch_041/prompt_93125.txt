I have arrived at a position about Skolem's paradox that I would like you to try to defeat. Stated plainly, it is this: the Löwenheim–Skolem results do not merely show that a consistent first-order theory of sets has countable models; they show that cardinality is not a feature of the world but a feature of how a model assigns its quantifiers and its membership relation. On this view, the claims that the set of reals is uncountable and that the set of reals is countable sit at the same level, each answerable only relative to a background model, and there is no further, model-independent fact that either claim tracks. The "intended interpretation" the realist invokes is, on my view, a label for a hope rather than a description of anything. I hold this even when the background theory is ZFC and the model is one of the countable models the downward theorem supplies.

I want to know what would count against this — not a survey and not a list of famous replies, but the defeaters themselves, sorted by how decisive they would be, together with an honest verdict on whether the position is insulated and cannot be touched. I would like several specific things treated.

First, the self-application worry. My position says, apparently in a model-free voice, that there is no model-free fact about countability. Is that flatly self-undermining, or can the position be stated so that the object-language claims it targets and the meta-theoretic claims it makes are kept apart? I have a proposed way of doing this: I distinguish what I call model-bound assertions, which are the ones the relativist relativizes, from model-free assertions, which are the ones the relativist himself makes. Tell me whether that distinction is principled or whether it is a dodge that licenses me to say anything I like, and if it is a dodge, what it costs.

Second, the second-order escape. The downward Löwenheim–Skolem theorem fails for second-order logic under full semantics, so a defender of absolute countability can say that the paradox is an artifact of first-order logic and nothing more. What would have to be true about second-order quantification for that reply to succeed, and how much of my position survives if it does? Is the move a genuine rescue or a change of subject?

Third, I want the concessions. What would a committed relativist about cardinality himself admit as counting against the position, rather than dismissing as question-begging? Give me what the defender concedes, not only what the critic asserts.

Fourth, the shape of the disagreement. Is my thesis a metaphysical claim about what there is, or a verbal claim about the word "countable" and about the language in which set theory happens to be written? If it is the latter, tell me what is lost by admitting it. If it is the former, tell me what in the world could make it true or false.

Fifth — and this is the answer I would find most persuasive if you could deliver it — a concrete result or construction that would count decisively against me. I do not mean the bare assertion that the realist is right, but something like a natural, non-arbitrary way of pinning cardinality down that does not depend on a prior choice of model: a definable absolute notion, a determinate interpretation of the quantifiers, or a theorem showing that the relativity I am describing is not a genuine relativity at all. Tell me whether such a thing could exist, and if it cannot, why not. End by telling me honestly how much of the position is left standing after all of this, and whether what remains is a real thesis or only a manner of speaking.
