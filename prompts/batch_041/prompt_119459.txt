Right, I need a hand writing the final exam question for a module I teach, and I'm too close to the material to see it straight.

It's an MA module on genocide and international law (human rights students, 90-minute paper, one essay, 20 marks). It's gone personal this year. My mum's family is from a part of the world where something truly awful happened a generation back, and over the summer my uncle and I ended up going at it about whether it "counts as genocide". He says obviously it does, and that anyone hedging about the word is doing the perpetrators' work. I said it depends what you mean by the word — a legal finding or a moral verdict — and he said that's a lawyer's dodge. I've been chewing on it alot since, and now I want the exam question to be about exactly that knot, because I think it's the thing my students most need to be able to think about and are least able to.

What I don't want is a question they can answer by reciting the Lemkin story. They all know it: he coins the word while the war is still on, the UN Convention follows in 1948, the offence needs a specific intent to destroy a protected group in whole or in part, and the drafting leaves political groups out of the protected list and drops the cultural-destruction elements he had wanted in. That's the memorised layer, and it should be worth maybe a third of the marks at most.

So put them in front of a scenario instead: a foreign ministry's lawyers advise ministers not to use the word "genocide" for an ongoing atrocity, on the ground that whether it is genocide is a matter for a court, and a diaspora organisation says the refusal is itself complicity. I want students to assess both positions and to say what each side has to assume about the relationship between (i) the legal definition, (ii) the moral accusation and (iii) the political act of naming, since those three get deliberately muddled by everyone involved. And I want a defended conclusion on whether the label should be treated as a scarce legal finding or as an available moral judgement.

What I need from you: the exam question itself (a short stem plus the instructions to candidates, maybe 120 words), a model answer of about 550 words, and a marking scheme out of 20 that names the specific moves a top answer has to make and the specific mistakes that should cost marks.

The moves I want rewarded: knowing the offence is much narrower than the everyday moral use of the word and saying precisely what does the narrowing; knowing that naming carries legal consequences, since the Convention obliges states to prevent and punish, so the incentives run asymmetrically — states under-name, victims' groups over-name; handling the point that crimes against humanity are not lesser evils, so a withheld label does its own distinctive harm; and seeing that the ministry's position is only coherent if you already hold that the legal definition is the only legitimate use of the word, which is a substantive claim wearing procedural clothes.

The mistakes I see every year, which should be flagged in the scheme: equating genocide with mass killing; thinking the intent requirement means proving they simply hated the group; treating the absence of a court finding as evidence that it isn't genocide; assuming the Convention protects political groups; and thinking "in part" means any number of victims however small.

No citations or bibliography — they have the reading list and I'm marking the argument, not the footnotes. Write it in British English please; I mark "recognise" with an s and I notice these things.

Hard limit: the whole thing under 1,000 words, and don't let the model answer run past 600.
