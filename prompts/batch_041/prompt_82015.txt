Ok so this happened in my civics class and I've been thinking about it all week. We were doing a unit on constitutions and my teacher said entrenchment clauses, the ones that make some parts of the constitution harder to change than ordinary laws, are basically a Ulysses contract. Like Odysseus getting tied to the mast so he can't jump after the sirens. Two kids in my class then went at it for like twenty minutes and I honestly could not tell who was winning. I wasnt on either side, I was just sitting there. Here's how it went, roughly:

(1) Dee said the analogy is bogus because Odysseus is the same guy the whole time. The one tying the rope and the one begging to be untied are one person, so he's only ever binding himself. A constitution binds people who werent born when it was written and never agreed to anything. She said thats the trick of the phrase: it makes a bunch of long dead guys look like one wise person making a decision about his own life, when really its one generation putting handcuffs on another one. Nobody signs a Ulysses contract for their grandkids.

(2) Marcus said the same-guy thing is doing way too much work. Odysseus is not the same guy at the mast, thats literally the whole point. If he were, he wouldn't need the rope, he'd just decide not to jump. His later self has different preferences and no veto, and thats what makes it a precommitment instead of a promise. So a longer time gap doesn't change the logic, it just makes it longer. And anyway a constitution isn't a one way thing, the people alive now get to entrench stuff for the people after them too, so its not like only the founders ever got a turn.

(3) Dee said thats the tell though. If the people alive now can amend it, then its not really a precommitment, its just a rule with a high bar. And if they cant amend it, its not a democracy. So the word either doesnt fit or it fits and thats an indictment. She also said precommitment only works in cases where you know the future you is in a temporary self-defeating state, like an addict or a guy panicking on a boat. Politics isnt a pathology. Disagreeing about tax rates isnt being drunk.

(4) Marcus said the line isnt pathology versus normal, its whether acting on the preference wrecks the very thing the preference is for. He gave this example: a norm like presidents not prosecuting their predecessors can get eroded one reasonable-looking step at a time, and every step looks fine by itself, but the sequence ends somewhere nobody wanted. Thats a drunk person at the group level. And he said the whole we'd know if it was temporary thing is backwards, because the guy tied to the mast doesnt feel temporarily insane, he feels completely clear headed. Thats why the rope exists.

(5) Dee came back with, fine, but who decided the founders get to be Odysseus? Somebody has to declare that all future majorities are the drunk ones, and whoever declares it is just grabbing power with extra steps. And at least Odysseus eats the cost of his own mistake. The people bound by an entrenched clause never got to make the bet and never get to find out if it was a good one, because you cant run the counterfactual. She said the honest test should be whether the bound people would re-ratify it, and if they would, you didnt need to bind them, and if they wouldn't, then the binding is doing something they dont consent to.

(6) Marcus said that test is rigged because it asks the wrong moment. The bound persons answer while the storm is on isnt the authoritative one, you ask what they'd say after. Dee said thats circular, you're just picking whichever moment gives you the answer you like, and this is how every precommitment argument ends, with somebody deciding which version of you counts as the real you.
