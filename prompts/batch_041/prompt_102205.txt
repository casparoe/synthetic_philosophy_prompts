For most of my working life I was a field entomologist, and since retiring I have been reading my way through philosophy of mind with the eagerness of a late convert and, I fear, the naivety of one. The idea that has snagged me is the one I have seen philosophers gesture at with the word "sphexish": the suspicion that a creature — or a person, or a machine — can put on a rich and adaptable-looking performance while remaining, underneath, a fixed routine merely playing itself out, its apparent choices being stages in a program no less preordained than the steps a solitary hunting wasp follows when she drags her paralyzed cricket to the burrow mouth, goes inside to inspect the chamber, and, if the cricket is nudged a few inches away during her absence, hauls it back and inspects the chamber all over again, as many times as one cares to move it. I would like you to build me a thought experiment to order, and I want it to make a sharp structural point rather than merely illustrate the worry. Let us fix two tests. Call an agent cue-responsive to the extent that its output is a non-trivial function of several independent features of the situation, so that varying any one of them varies the behavior and no single fixed sequence of steps describes what it does. Call an agent goal-guided to the extent that its behavior is a means to an end it holds, such that if the end were altered, or the customary means blocked, it would adjust in order to secure the end. What I want is a case with a single agent whose behavior is maximally cue-responsive and wholly sphexish — no goal-guidance whatsoever, its busy sensitivity to circumstance being itself just more routine — together with a mirror case, or a second phase of the same case, in which an agent is minimally cue-responsive, stereotyped and mechanical by that same test, yet genuinely goal-guided.

Please constrain yourself as follows. Each case should be one paragraph and should concern a real kind of organism or an ordinary artifact rather than a stipulated magical agent; no perfect predictors, no exotic physics, and no homunculus smuggled in to do the work. Crucially, neither verdict may be stipulated: the sphexishness of the first agent and the flexibility of the second must fall out of the described mechanism, so that a reader could in principle watch the thing run and check. Hold the two cases as comparable as you can, changing as little as possible between them, since the point I want to make in a seminar is that cue-responsiveness and goal-guidance are logically independent — and a case that simply declares its agent rigid or flexible would fail at exactly the thing I am trying to show.

After the two scenarios, walk me through the demonstration: for each agent, state its cue-variation profile and its goal-tracking profile, say what each of my two tests returns and why, and then tell me which single feature of the case is doing the work. Say what happens if that feature is removed — whether the case collapses back into the ordinary story about the wasp, or into something else — and how a skeptic who thinks my distinction is merely verbal, or that goal-guidance is nothing but cue-responsiveness of a higher order, would set about dismantling the pair. I should add that I am not at all confident I have set this up correctly, and I would think better of you if you told me plainly when the request rests on a confusion — if the distinction will not bear the weight I am putting on it, or if no single pair of cases can satisfy both halves at once — rather than manufacture something tidy to please me.
