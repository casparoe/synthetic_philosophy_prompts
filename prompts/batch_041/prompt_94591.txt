At a dinner party last week two friends of mine, Marcus and Ivy, got into an argument about something a mutual friend is dealing with, and I've been chewing on it ever since because I genuinely couldn't tell who had the better of it. Background: our friend runs a small community orchestra. A wealthy donor has offered to fund its hall for the next decade on one condition — that the board sincerely regard his late wife's compositions as major works and program them as centerpieces of the season. They're not major works. Everybody at the table agreed about that part. What they fought about is what the donor's money gives the board a reason to do.

(1) Marcus: it gives them a reason to program the pieces, and even a reason to say the pieces are major works if they want to be polite about it, but it can't give them a reason to actually think the pieces are major works. Reasons to say a thing aren't reasons for the thing said. Money doesn't make mediocre music good, so it's not evidence that it's good, and thinking is supposed to track how things are.

(2) Ivy: that's too fast. If the board could come to genuinely love the pieces, their lives would go better — they'd enjoy the season, they'd stop feeling like sellouts, the whole institution would be happier. And a reason to be in a state that's good for you is a reason to be in it, not just to fake it. She said she'd happily take a pill that made her love the music, and didn't see why that showed anything was off.

(3) Marcus: that's the tell, though — a pill that makes you think the music is good is a reason to want the thought, not a reason for the thought. He said the money is a reason about the board's state, reasons about a state are a different kind from reasons about the music, and only the second kind can make the belief correct. He admitted he couldn't say what "different kind" amounts to without saying "the kind that counts," which he said is fine, and which Ivy said is circular.

(4) Ivy: also, what does "major work" even mean here? If a whole city treats a piece as central to its musical life for fifty years, that partly makes it central. So maybe the money is giving the board a reason that's about the object after all — a reason to set in motion the recognition that would make the claim true. Marcus said fine for "central to civic life," useless for "well made," and that the pieces are being asked to be regarded as well made.

(5) Ivy came back with a case: a choir director who treats a weak singer as though she's already good, and by doing so turns her into a good singer. There, she said, the reason to treat her as good is grounded in merit she'll actually have. Marcus said the merit doesn't exist yet, so it can't be what grounds the treatment — what grounds it is a different reason, something about what she could become.

Neither of them would budge, they asked me to settle it, and I couldn't. You're a philosopher who works on value and on what makes an attitude appropriate or inappropriate — please think this through carefully rather than going with your first reaction, and feel free to lay out the competing positions on paper before committing to anything. What I want is a move-by-move adjudication: for each of the five, is it clearly right, clearly wrong, or somewhere in between, and why. Don't cop out with "it's complicated" — say who has the better of each exchange, and where each of them is missing something the other is seeing. And I'd definately rather have your own reasoning here than a list of who said what; I'm not going to check anyone's homework, so no references needed, just argue it out.
