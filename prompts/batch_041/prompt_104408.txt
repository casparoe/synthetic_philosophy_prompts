Question 4 (30 points)

In lecture we distinguished three positions on whether death is bad for the person who dies:

(A) the Epicurean view that death is nothing to us, since death and the living person are never present together;
(B) the deprivation account, on which death is bad for the deceased because it deprives them of the good life they would otherwise have had;
(C) the symmetry argument, which compares the time after death with the time before birth.

Consider the following case, which we did not discuss in class. Priya, at 25, learns that a condition will kill her painlessly at 40, and that her years from 26 to 40 will be happy and full. She is offered a treatment that would prevent this death and allow her to live to 90, but the treatment would leave her permanently apathetic: from age 41 onward she would care about nothing, value nothing, and have no wish to go on living. Her years from 26 to 40 would be the same either way. Priya declines the treatment and dies at 40.

Write an essay of roughly 1,000 to 1,200 words addressing each of the following:

1. What does the deprivation account imply about whether Priya's death at 40 was bad for her? State the account and apply it to the case.
2. Does the case create a difficulty for the deprivation account? If so, state the difficulty as precisely as you can.
3. Does the symmetry argument bear on this case? Explain how or why not.
4. What is the strongest response available to a defender of the deprivation account, and does it succeed?

Student's answer

The Epicurean view is that death is nothing to us. Epicurus argues that a thing can be bad for a person only if that person can experience it, and death is precisely the absence of experience: whenever we exist, death is not yet present, and whenever death is present, we no longer exist. Since there is no moment at which a living subject is also dead, there is no one for whom death can be an experienced harm, and so death cannot be bad for the person who dies. This does not mean we should feel nothing when someone dies, since their death may still be bad for the survivors.

The deprivation account, defended by Thomas Nagel, rejects this conclusion. On Nagel's view, death is bad for the person who dies not because it is experienced but because it deprives that person of the goods they would have had if they had lived longer. The badness of death is therefore comparative: we compare the life the person actually has with the life they would have had if they had not died when they did, and death is bad to the degree that the second life is better than the first. Nagel's central point is that a harm need not be experienced to be a harm; it is enough that death makes a person's life worse than it would otherwise have been.

Applying the account to Priya, we compare her actual life, which ends at 40, with the life she would have had if she had accepted the treatment and lived to 90. If we measured by length of life alone, death would deprive her of fifty years and would therefore be very bad for her. But the account is not about length; it is about the goods a life contains. The additional fifty years contain nothing Priya cares about and nothing she would want to live for, so the counterfactual life is not better than the actual one and may well be worse. Priya's death therefore deprives her of nothing worth having. The deprivation account implies that her death was good for her, and that dying at 40 was better for her than living to 90.

This is where the case creates a difficulty. The deprivation account tells us when death is bad, but it now appears to imply that death can be positively good, and it gives us no principled way of saying how good, or of comparing the good of death with the good of continued life. More troubling, it makes whether a person's death is a tragedy depend entirely on facts about a merely possible future, so that one and the same death could be a misfortune or a blessing depending on what would have happened. A full account of the value of death should explain why death is normally an evil, and the deprivation account seems to make that explanation contingent.

The symmetry argument, which Epicurus also used, compares the time after death with the time before birth. We do not regard the endless time before we were born as a misfortune, since we did not exist then and had no interests that could be set back; by parity of reasoning we should not regard the time after death as a misfortune either. Applied to Priya, the argument says that her nonexistence after 40 is no more a harm to her than her nonexistence before 25 was. It therefore supports the conclusion that her death was not bad for her, though for a different reason than the deprivation account gives: not because her future would have been poor, but because nonexistence is never bad for anyone. The two views thus reach the same verdict about Priya, by different routes.

A defender of the deprivation account can reply that the account was never intended to be a complete theory of the value of death. It is an account of why death is bad, and it says that death is bad just when it deprives a person of good life; when continued life would be worse than nothing, death is simply not bad. The defender can add that the account is meant to compare lives only when both lives are worth living. I think this reply succeeds, because the deprivation account only ever claimed to explain the badness of death, and Priya's case does not show that it fails to do so.
