A great deal of current work on why some nations are rich now runs on one methodological practice. Since we cannot run experiments on countries, the field treats certain episodes in the historical record as though they were experiments: it looks for variation that is plausibly arbitrary with respect to prosperity — settler mortality rates, borders drawn by colonial administrators through otherwise similar terrain, legal systems transplanted wholesale by conquest, the location of missionary stations, the timing of a slave trade's suppression — and treats such variation as licensing causal claims about institutions. Policy then follows: because the estimates say institutions cause prosperity, development agencies and finance ministries fund institution-building, and the geography and culture explanations are treated as the defeated rivals. The practice is exacting in one respect and permissive in another. Practitioners will refuse a causal claim outright unless the variation is plausibly exogenous, and will argue for years over a single identification assumption; yet they rarely ask whether the estimated relation would hold in a different region or century, and they seldom say by what mechanism the institution they have named is supposed to produce growth.

I would like to know what this practice presupposes. Does it require a regularity or covering-law view of causation, on which a cause is a relation that must hold across cases, or is it equally at home with a mechanistic view on which causation is a stable process operating in a particular setting, so that the estimates need not travel at all? Does treating institutions as something that can be instrumented presuppose that institutions are one manipulable kind of thing, rather than a heterogeneous bundle of arrangements with different causal powers? What view of culture is built into the practice's habit of using culture as the residual, the term that absorbs whatever institutions and geography leave unexplained? And what does the practice assume about counterfactuals in a history that happened once — that the record is a sample drawn from a population of possible histories, or merely a collection of local contrasts?

Reconstruct the implicit theory, then tell me which of these commitments the practice genuinely needs and which it merely fits comfortably with; where it is underdetermined, so that two incompatible accounts of causation would license the same research programme; and what would have to change in the practice itself, not merely in how its practitioners describe it, if the presupposed view were replaced by a mechanistic one.
