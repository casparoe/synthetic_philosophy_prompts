My father died in March of an infection that, in hindsight, was treatable with a course of antibiotics that his physician considered and decided against. I am not asking you to evaluate the medicine. What I cannot settle, and what I would like you to adjudicate, is the argument my sister and my brother had across the kitchen table the day after the funeral. Both of them made points I could not dismiss, and I stayed out of it.

My sister: The doctor killed him. Declining to prescribe was a decision, not a mere absence, and my father would be alive if the decision had gone the other way. Nothing turns on the fact that the fatal thing was a non-action.

My brother: The infection killed him. The doctor is a cause only if we stretch 'cause' to cover things that did not happen, and then the word stops doing any work, because an infinite number of things did not happen without which he would also have lived.

My sister: Then a lifeguard who watches a child drown has not caused the drowning, and a builder who skips a required inspection has not caused the collapse. That cannot be right.

My brother: Look at what is doing the work in your two examples. The lifeguard is on duty; the builder is paid to inspect. A stranger on the beach who does not swim out to a drowning child is not said to have caused the death. So the claim that an omission caused something is a claim about what someone was obliged to do, dressed up as a claim about what happened.

My sister: Counterfactual dependence does not pick out a cause on its own anyway. The doctor not prescribing is one of countless non-events without which my father would have lived -- the pharmacy not being robbed that week, the nurse not transposing a digit. An account that makes all of them causes makes none of them causes. You need a principle for selecting one, and obligation is at least a principle.

My brother: Which concedes my point. If obligation is what selects the cause, then the dispute about whether the doctor caused the death is a moral dispute about the standard of care, and the causal language is decoration.

Two things neither of them raised, which I suspect matter. First, the doctor weighed the risk of antibiotic resistance, so the omission was reasoned rather than inadvertent. Second, a physician we consulted afterwards said most doctors would have made the same call, so it is unclear that my father's survival counterfactually depended on this doctor in particular rather than on the prevailing standard of practice.

Please work through the exchange move by move, rating each move from clearly mistaken to clearly right and giving brief reasons for each rating. Then tell me what both of them are missing, given those two facts. Finally, answer the question I actually want answered: is 'the doctor caused his death' a factual claim, a moral claim in factual clothing, or a claim whose truth depends on the purpose we are putting it to -- and if the last, is the disagreement between my siblings merely verbal? I would rather have a clear diagnosis than a verdict on the doctor.
