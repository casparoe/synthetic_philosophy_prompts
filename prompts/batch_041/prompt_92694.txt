I spent most of last Thursday evening arguing with a close friend about whether there is a principled difference between curing someone and improving someone who was never ill, and we both left the table dissatisfied. She kept insisting that the difference is obvious to anyone who has sat in a clinic; I could not say why the obvious cases are obvious without appealing to something that, under pressure, turned out to be a fact about insurance paperwork.

In four weeks I am interviewing, for our department's public Q&A series, a philosopher who defends a position I will state plainly, since it is the position and not her name that matters for your purposes. She holds that the therapy/enhancement distinction is conventional rather than natural — settled by the internal norms of medical practice rather than by biology — but that it is not for that reason arbitrary, and that its moral significance is derivative: what matters is whether an intervention answers to a legitimate claim on the part of the person who receives it, and the therapy/enhancement line is a serviceable proxy for that, though only within a certain range. She also holds that the proxy fails at the margins, and that those failures are diagnostic rather than embarrassing.

Please draft eight to ten interview questions that press on the substance of that position rather than letting her restate it, with at least one question each on:

1. the case in which a single intervention — same molecule, same dose, same measurable functional effect — is classified as therapy for one person and enhancement for another, and what follows about whether the classification is doing moral work or merely epistemic work;
2. the baseline problem for traits that sit at the low end of a normal distribution, where there seems to be no fact of the matter about what the person's function would have been absent the condition;
3. drift in the baseline itself: interventions that begin as enhancements and end up as therapies as the population changes, and modifications to a person's environment that achieve by other means what a modification to the body would achieve, and whether her proxy survives either kind of drift;
4. the disability critique, namely whether, if the deficit in question is constituted by social arrangements rather than by the body, the intervention still answers to a legitimate claim;
5. positional goods, and whether her account ends up generating enhancement obligations in high-stakes roles and what that does to the line she wants to keep.

Include a closing question that forces her to say what would count as evidence that the proxy has come apart from the claim it is supposed to proxy. For every question, supply the follow-up keyed to the answer she is most likely to give, and where she is likely to deflect, a second follow-up that makes the deflection harder to use. Argue the points on their merits: no citations, no reading lists, and no question she can dispose of by describing her own view again. At the end, tell me which single question you expect her to be unable to answer, and add one question you think would actually settle the argument I had with my friend.
