Ok so I need help with something I've been dreading. My old college is giving me an honorary degree at commencement in June and they want me to give the address, and I've rewritten the opening like nine times because I can't figure out how to be honest up there. I spent basically my whole adult life in the prison reform movement — forty years of reports, testimony, op-eds, the whole thing — and the commencement genre wants me to tell a few hundred kids that their arguments and their passion will change the world. I'm definately not sure I believe that anymore, and I don't want to stand up there and lie to them, but I also don't want to be the guy who tells a graduating class that nothing they think matters, actually. Nobody wants that guy.

What's eating me is this line of thought I half remember — I think it's Appiah, and maybe Bernard Williams too (the philosopher, not the other Williams who did the slavery-and-capitalism thing, though maybe him too honestly). The idea is that the big moral revolutions mostly didn't happen because somebody won the argument. Slavery didn't end because the abolitionists had better premises. Dueling didn't end because people figured out it was dumb; it ended because the kind of honor that made you show up changed into a kind of honor that made you keep your temper. Something like that about footbinding in China too — it stopped when it got embarrassing for the family, not when it got proven cruel. Interests and honor, not arguments. And then the arguments come along afterward and take the credit.

So what I want from you is a commencement address, 6-8 minutes out loud, where the speaker is a version of me — genuinely unsure whether forty years of arguing ever moved anything — standing in front of students who want inspiration, their parents who want it over with, and a row of my old comrades from the movement who will notice instantly if I betray the story we told ourselves for forty years. The occasion has to be pulling toward uplift and the philosophy has to be taking the easy uplift off the table, and the speech has to find something true to say instead of a mush. Not "well, arguments and interests both matter, kids." Please.

Couple of things I want in it. I want the speech to actually keep the interests story and the honor story apart, because they lead to different advice — if interests do the work, you change the incentives, you organize and boycott; if honor does the work, you change what's shameful, you make the thing disgusting to the people doing it — and I don't want those two blended into a fog. I want at least one concrete case used properly, not just name-dropped. And I want the speaker to have to actually sit with the possibility that their own forty years didn't do what they thought it did, and still end on something a person could do on Monday. Don't cite anything at me, I'm saying this out loud and nobody's taking notes, and please don't turn it into a philosophy lecture with a graduation hat on.
