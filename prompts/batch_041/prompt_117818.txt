Okay so I've been trying to figure out what the replication-crisis reforms in psychology (and now a decent chunk of biomed) actually commit you to philosophically, and I keep getting tangled up. Wondering if you can untangle it.

Here's the practice as its actually run. You preregister your hypothesis and your analysis plan. Then you run the study and you look at whether p < .05. If a follow-up comes back at p = .34, that gets called a failed replication and people start saying the effect doesn't exist. But the exact same crowd will tell you, correctly, that a non-significant result isnt evidence of no effect, that you can't accept the null, that p = .049 and p = .051 aren't two different findings, that we should stop dichotomizing and report effect sizes with intervals. And meanwhile a multiverse analysis or a specification curve over the same dataset gets praised as the honest thing to do, even though it's basically an admission that the single preregistered test was arbitrary.

There's also the bit where a real effect will routinely fail to "replicate" in the significance sense, because the original estimate was inflated by having cleared the bar in the first place, so the second study is powered off a too-big number, lands at p = .12, and everybody calls it dead.

So: what theory of evidence does the preregister-then-check-for-significance workflow actually presuppose? My hunch is that it's a story about the long-run error rates of a procedure rather than about what any particular dataset tells you about the size of an effect, i.e. a frequentist story. But test the hunch, don't just agree with it, and tell me which rival views the practice quietly rules out.

Then separate what's strictly presupposed from what's just packaged together by sociology. Specifically: does preregistration itself need the frequentist story, or is it a commitment device against p-hacking that a Bayesian could endorse on purely practical grounds? Is there anything off about people now wanting Bayesian analyses preregistered too, when the likelihood supposedly doesn't care when you thought of the hypothesis? And does treating "replication" as a pass/fail certification presuppose that a study is an instance of a repeatable procedure with a sampling distribution — and what breaks if you drop that and treat every study as one noisy measurement of a shared parameter?

Last thing: if a lab or a journal actually took the Bayesian self-description seriously instead of using it as a coat of paint, what would have to change in how it decides what to publish, how it decides whether a grad student's dissertation "worked," and how it reads a null result? And where is the practice genuinely underdetermined, such that a frequentist and a Bayesian could run the identical workflow and mean completely different things by it?
