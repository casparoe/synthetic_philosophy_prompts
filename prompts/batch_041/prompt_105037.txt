I hold a position about which animals are subjects of welfare, and I want a map of its exposure — what would count against it, sorted by how decisive the defeaters actually are.

The position has three parts. The first is an evidential standard. A creature is a suffering subject, on this standard, only if it satisfies what I will call the trade-off condition: it will bear net costs in other currencies — accept injury or predation risk, forgo food, leave a preferred shelter, abandon a mate or a brood — specifically to escape or avoid a noxious stimulus; the size of the sacrifice scales with the intensity of the stimulus; the pattern is graded and reversible rather than a fixed reflex, and survives the obvious deflationary explanations (a general stress response, a hard-wired avoidance insensitive to the animal's other needs); and it is reinstated after extinction when the stimulus returns at higher intensity. The second part is the verdict: decapod crustaceans and fishes satisfy this condition, so in aquaculture they are not merely things whose tissue we should avoid damaging for our own reasons but beings to whom something is owed directly — which places routine eyestalk ablation in shrimp broodstock, live chilling and live boiling, asphyxiation on ice, and slaughter without stunning in the same moral category as comparable treatment of a mammal. The third part is a generalization rule: because the relevant machinery is plausibly homologous within these groups and the costs of error are asymmetric, evidence that one decapod or fish species passes licenses treating the whole group as covered.

What I want from you:

1. What would count against each of the three parts separately? Which observations, experimental results, conceptual arguments, or possible cases tell against the standard, against the verdict, or against the generalization rule?

2. Rank the defeaters from decisive to merely awkward, and identify the point at which a defender would have to abandon a part of the position rather than qualify it.

3. Which defeaters would a defender of the standard concede as counting against them, as opposed to the ones only a critic would press? I do not want the two lists blurred.

4. Is the trade-off condition a disguised restatement of the verdict — a definition of suffering that guarantees the answer — and does the generalization rule quietly reintroduce the precautionary reasoning I claim to be avoiding? If either is true, is that fatal or merely a cost?

5. What does the position predict that its rivals do not? Take seriously at least one rival: a mechanism-based standard requiring specific integrative structures; a standard on which valenced experience depends on some kind of self-model; and a flatly precautionary standard that treats the evidence as permanently underdetermined. Say where each does better than mine and what it pays for that advantage.

6. Give me the case that should embarrass me most: one in which the trade-off condition is met by something almost nobody takes to be a subject, or is failed by something almost everybody does. When the two directions of failure conflict, which way does my standard bend?

I will say in advance what would move me most: a demonstration that the trade-off condition is satisfied by systems no one is tempted to call subjects, since that would show the standard is too cheap to do the work I am asking of it. No reading lists or citations — I want the structure of the case, not the bibliography.
