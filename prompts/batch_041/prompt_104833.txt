I host a small speculative-fiction group that has lately been doubling as a philosophy seminar, and we keep failing to write our way into the problem we care about, so I want to commission a story instead. The distinction we keep circling is this. Some considerations are reasons for an attitude: they bear on whether the attitude is correct or warranted, the way a person's courage bears on whether admiring her is warranted. Other considerations are merely reasons to bring the attitude about: a reward for admiring someone, or a threat that will be carried out unless you admire him, gives you a reason to want to be in that state, to rehearse, to avoid counterevidence, to arrange to be in his company — but it appears to leave untouched the question of whether admiration is warranted. The second sort of consideration is not nothing; it genuinely rationalizes action, and that is exactly the trouble, because it seems to be the wrong kind of reason to make the attitude itself fitting, which is awkward for anyone who hopes to analyze value as the property of being a fitting object of some attitude. There is a further wrinkle we keep arguing about: the threat is not a reason to admire, yet it can be a reason to take steps that put you in the way of real reasons to admire, and the difference between the direct route and the roundabout one is where the whole problem lives. The cases that interest us most are the impure ones, where a consideration of the second sort attaches to an attitude that already has genuine grounds, so that the two kinds of reason are tangled around a single state of mind and cannot be peeled apart by inspection.

So: write me a short story of roughly 1,200 words, set in the near future in a company town whose employer runs a 'sincerity bonus' scheme — quarterly payments to employees whose enthusiasm for the company's flagship product survives an interview with a sincerity assessor, who is known by everyone to be fallible and to have been gamed before. Build it around one employee who needs the money, who has some independent grounds for enthusiasm that she does not entirely trust, and who does not want to become a person who fakes it. Both the direct route and the roundabout route should be live options for her at different moments, and at least one other character should take the direct route and either get away with it or fail at it, so that the reader can see what each route costs. End without resolving the puzzle. No character should explain the difference between reasons for an attitude and reasons to bring one about, no character should be a philosopher or quote any literature, and the story should never use the words fitting or warranted in their technical senses; I want the distinction earned by what the characters do rather than announced in dialogue. Append, outside the fiction, a note of three or four sentences identifying the scene you take to be doing the philosophical work and saying what would have to change about the setting for the story to be about a merely prudential dilemma instead — one where nothing turns on whether the attitude is warranted and only the payoff matters.
