I run a fortnightly reading group on Rorty this term — mostly epistemologists, a couple of metaethicists, and one historian of philosophy who comes mainly for the biscuits — and I've set them a written exercise that I'd like you to have a go at yourself. The charge I want the essay to settle is one that keeps surfacing in the group and that I've never heard answered to my satisfaction: that Rorty's antirepresentationalism is either too weak to be interesting or too strong to be believed. Read weakly, it says only that we shouldn't take the mirror-of-nature metaphor too literally, and shouldn't expect philosophy to underwrite our claims to have got reality right — a bit of therapy rather than a doctrine, and nobody who wasn't already in the grip of the picture needs curing of it. Read strongly, it says that our sentences don't answer to how things are, that there's no fact of the matter about which of two vocabularies gets the world right, and then it looks as though it saws off the branch it's sitting on: the claim that there's no such fact is itself put forward as true, and the antirepresentationalist presumably thinks the realist is getting something wrong — which is precisely the sort of thing she says can't happen. I want the essay to say whether that dilemma is real; and if it is, which horn the antirepresentationalist should take and why; and if it isn't, exactly where the dilemma equivocates.

To stop it sliding into a recital of the usual moves, I'd like it organised around a case I've been toying with, which I'll call the lagoon. Take a small island community whose entire vocabulary of the sea is, by our lights, systematically false: their word for the tide picks out something that doesn't exist, their reef is a fiction, their charts of the currents are, as descriptions, bunk. And yet they fish, they navigate, they don't drown; the practice is a triumph of coping by any measure you care to name. So: does the antirepresentationalist have anything to say about the lagoon-dwellers beyond "a different vocabulary, another way of going on"? If she doesn't, is any interesting distinction between coping and getting-it-right left standing, or has the realist apparatus been quietly smuggled out of the back door? If she does have something to say — something that licenses her to call the lagooners mistaken rather than merely different — then I want to know which resource does the work, and whether that resource is a representational notion in disguise. Along the way, lay out three or four distinct readings of the antirepresentationalist thesis and keep them seperate (a quietist/therapeutic one; a causal-naturalist one, on which vocabularies are just tools kept or binned according to how well they let us cope; a social one, on which truth comes down to what your peers will let you say; plus any fourth you think deserves a seat at the table), then say which of them the lagoon case actually discriminates between.

Don't pad it with a literature review — I know the secondary stuff and I don't want a bibliography or a tour of who said what about whom, so no citations needed, and please don't hedge by telling me it all depends on how one reads "representation". Pick the reading you think is most defensible, argue for it, and then give me a verdict on the original charge — trivial, self-undermining, or neither, and if neither, what the defensible middle actually looks like. Tell me briefly, too, what would shift you: which arguement or example would push you off the position you end up defending. I'd far rather have a slightly wrong essay that commits to something than a beautifully balanced one that doesn't.
