I'm writing a seminar paper for a metaethics course and I actually want to argue *for* something rather than just survey the field, so I'm hoping you can help me build the case properly. Quick background: our unit was on idealized-deliberation views of normativity, the family that says what you ought to believe or do is fixed by what some improved version of you would settle on — better informed, more coherent, not self-serving, unlimited time, whatever. My prof kept talking about what he calls the two dials, and I think it's a useful picture: there's the information dial (how much more the ideal agent knows than you do) and the procedure dial (how much better she is at reasoning with whatever she happens to know). His throwaway claim, which he never bothered to defend, was that only the procedure dial can do any normative work. Crank the information dial by itself and you've just described an oracle — what an oracle tells you is a fact about the world, not a standard for your beliefs — whereas the normative oomph has to come from the fact that the ideal agent is reasoning well from *your* evidence, and that's the procedure dial. Concrete case I keep coming back to: I'm bad at base rates and I'm trying to figure out whether my friend is lying to me. Is the norm for me "what I'd believe if I could actually do the Bayesian thing on the evidence I have," or is it "what someone who knew everything would say"? Those come apart and I don't think they can both be doing the work.

So here's what I need: make the strongest case that the procedure dial is the only one that can ground normativity. The catch is it has to survive the comeback I know is coming, namely that you can't turn the procedure dial up very far before you've changed the subject — a perfect reasoner with unlimited working memory and no biases isn't a better version of me, she's a different thing, and then why would her conclusions bind me any more than the oracle's do? And if you keep her close enough to still be recognizably me, the view seems to collapse into "you're justified in believing what you'd believe if you reasoned correctly from your evidence," which sounds circular since reasoning correctly is exactly what we were supposed to be analyzing. So the argument needs to (a) genuinely rule the information dial out, and (b) show the procedure dial can carry real weight without either drifting into changing-the-subject or collapsing into triviality. I don't need any citations or reading suggestions, just give me the argument itself, in your own words, and flag honestly where you think it's weakest — I'd rather find the holes now than in front of the seminar. We've been arguing about this at the bar after class for two weeks and I've got a bet riding on it.
