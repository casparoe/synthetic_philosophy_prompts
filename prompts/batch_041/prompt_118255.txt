I am preparing an application for a six-month seed grant from my university's humanities centre, which asks for a project description of no more than three pages. The panel is mostly not archaeologists — it includes a philosopher, a historian of science, and a museum curator — so the description has to be legible to them without being watered down into platitudes about "the human past." There is no money for excavation, survey, or laboratory work, and I would not want any; the project has to be carried out against material that is already published.

My half-formed idea is this. Our field moves constantly between two kinds of claim that we rarely keep apart: claims about what people took the world to be like, and claims about what people took themselves to be required or forbidden to do. Regularity is not normativity — a population can be perfectly uniform in what it does for reasons that have nothing to do with obligation, and conversely an obligation can be widely flouted. I have been calling the proposed fix the friction criterion: an attribution of a norm, as opposed to a custom, a convenience, or a by-product of technique, is warranted only where the record shows the regularity being maintained against something — traces of sanction or correction, deliberate marking of boundaries, or at minimum a pattern of conformity that was more costly to sustain than the nearest available alternative. I cannot yet tell whether this is the right necessary condition or a distinction without a difference, and that is what I want the grant to find out.

Please write the proposal. State the question so that it has a determinate answer; explain why the standard ways of getting from objects to minds — ethnographic analogy, optimality and behavioural modelling, and formal comparison of rival hypotheses — leave it open rather than settling it; set out the method, which should be conceptual work disciplined by two contrasting bodies of published material selected according to criteria the proposal itself specifies; give a work plan with phases and rough durations for six months; say what a successful result would look like and what would count as the project failing (including the failure modes I should be most worried about, e.g. that friction is systematically invisible in the record, or that the criterion turns out to license every attribution and therefore none); and be honest about what is too ambitious for the time and what I should cut. The deliverable should be one article-length paper plus a short, usable statement of the criterion that other archaeologists could apply to their own material. Arguments and positions are welcome; skip citations, bibliography, and any reading list — I will supply those myself.
