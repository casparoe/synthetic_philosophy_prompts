I hold the following view and I want an honest map of its exposure — what it risks, and where. The crane/skyhook contrast I have in mind is Dennett's: a crane is any process that does explanatory lifting while being itself the product of earlier lifting, whereas a skyhook is something asked to do the same work without having been assembled by anything, lifting merely because it is given. My position has three separable parts. First, there are no skyhooks in biology or cognitive science; every genuine explanation of design, or of a capacity, is crane-like. Second, greedy reductionism is a substantive methodological error rather than a matter of taste or impatience: it is the mistake of invoking a lower-level mechanism to carry explanatory weight at a level where that mechanism has not been earned, because the intermediate cranes have not been built. Third, and this is the part I trust least, the contrast is exhaustive — for any proposed explanation, either it is anchored in prior lifting or it is a skyhook, with nothing in between.

Tell me what would count against this, and be specific about which of the three claims each item tells against rather than lumping them together. In particular:

Consider the pressure from the autonomy of higher-level descriptions in physics, where macroscopic regularities hold across systems with wildly different microphysics — the same laws governing a magnet, a fluid, and a flock, with the microscopic details washing out. Such a description appears to do real explanatory work that no amount of lower-level bookkeeping would supply, and yet its lifting capacity is plainly not borrowed from anything below it. Is that upper-level explanation a crane, which would violate my own criterion, or a skyhook, which would be absurd? Does this show the dichotomy is not exhaustive, or that earned is simply the wrong criterion?

Consider the origin of life, where the anchoring sequence has to begin. Is that a gap in the picture, a skyhook I am quietly conceding, or nothing at all?

Consider cases where the greedy/non-greedy verdict flips with no change except how much we already know, which would suggest the distinction tracks our epistemic position rather than the explanations themselves.

Consider cases where one and the same explanation is a crane at one level and a skyhook at another — say a cognitive-level information-processing story that is respectable as psychology but that a neuroscientist regards as unearned.

Then: which of these would a committed defender concede as genuinely counting against them, and which would they dismiss? Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, and mark which are decisive rather than embarrassing. Say whether the position has been insulated — in particular, whether earned functions as a promissory note that can always be paid later — and whether that is fatal or acceptable for a methodological stance of this kind. Finally, say what the position predicts that its rivals do not, whether there is a case it cannot describe at all, and which single item would most move someone who, like me, would be most persuaded by a case in which the crane/skyhook verdict and the greedy/good verdict come apart — an explanation that is greedy but not unearned, or unearned but not greedy.
