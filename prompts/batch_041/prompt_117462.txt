So me and my buddy burned like three hours last night arguing about approval-directed vs goal-directed agents and I need someone to settle what would actually count against his side.

His position: build the AI so that it picks whatever action it thinks its human overseer would most approve of, and you get most of the safety of a goal-directed agent without the usual failure mode, because (a) "the human approves of this" is supposedly way easier to specify correctly than any objective out there in the world, and (b) the overseer stays in the loop, so the agent can't quietly run off and optimize a misspecified proxy. He thinks this basically dissolves the sharp version of the outer-alignment problem. I'm not so sure and I want to know what would have to be true for him to be wrong, ranked from "this would sink it" down to "eh, awkward but survivable."

I dont want the lazy objection either ("the AI just learns to manipulate the overseer") — everyone says that, I want to know whether it's actually fatal or whether approval-directedness has a real reply, and what that reply costs.

Heres the thing I threw at him and couldnt defend well: approval-directedness looks like it just relabels the problem. The agent optimizes a proxy of a proxy — the overseer's approval is a stand-in for what we actually want, and the agent's model of the overseer's approval is a stand-in for the overseer. So you've got two layers of approximation where the goal-directed agent had one. His answer was that the second layer is the whole point, because the overseer is corrigible and the goal isnt. Fine, but then where does it bottom out? If approval-directedness means "do what the overseer would approve of," and the overseer's approval can itself be wrong, you either get a regress of approvers or you have to anchor the whole thing to something that isnt itself approval-based — an ideal observer, coherently extrapolated values, whatever — and that anchor starts looking exactly like the goal you were trying to avoid. Is that regress a genuine defeater or a cheap gotcha?

And the part I actually care about most: is there a version of "the agent gradually shapes the overseer's standards so that its own actions become approved" that a defender just has to eat? Because that looks like a failure mode goal-directed agents don't obviously share, and I want to know if it's decisive or just a corner case.

Last thing — be honest about whether this position has been quietly qualified into unfalsifiability. If every time I raise a problem the move is "well the overseer wouldnt approve of that," I want to know if there's anything the theory actually predicts that could fail to happen. And tell me what an actual defender would concede counts against them, as opposed to what critics say counts against them.
