I am assembling materials for a graduate seminar on human enhancement, and I want to begin with the specification rather than with the candidates. Set out the desiderata that any adequate theory of the therapy/enhancement distinction must satisfy. Take the theory's purpose to be the one that makes the distinction do real work: it must supply a line that a health insurer, a sports regulator, and a clinical guideline body could each apply, publicly and defensibly, in deciding what they will pay for, permit, or recommend. Assume the theory may be revisionary -- it may tell us that some received classifications are mistaken -- but it may not be silent on the cases below.

For each desideratum, give the case or consideration that motivates it, and say what the requirement rules out. At minimum, address the following.

(a) Cases the theory must classify correctly, or at least classify without absurdity: corrective lenses for myopia versus corrective lenses for a fighter pilot who merely wants better than 20/20; growth hormone for a child with a diagnosed deficiency versus the same hormone for a short but healthy child; statins for someone with familial hypercholesterolemia versus someone with average cholesterol and a family history; cochlear implantation in a deaf infant versus in a deaf adult who regards deafness as a culture rather than a deficit; a hypothetical pill that reliably cuts sleep need from eight hours to four with no rebound; a hypothetical intervention that slows biological aging in people who are, by every current measure, healthy; SSRIs for major depression versus for ordinary shyness; and a gene edit that raises muscle mass in an athlete with no disease and no injury. For each, say what the theory should say, and whether the case is a genuine test of the distinction or a distraction from it.

(b) Distinctions the theory must not collapse: therapy versus prevention; enhancement of a trait versus enhancement of the person; the species-typical range versus the population-typical range versus the socially expected range; a medical need versus a medical want; and the difference between "this trait is statistically unusual" and "this trait is bad for its bearer." Explain why each is a genuine requirement, and where you think one of them is instead a prejudice of one camp, say so.

(c) Constraints the theory must respect: what counts as a disease or defect has changed historically, and some of those changes look like moral progress rather than the correction of an error (the removal of homosexuality from the diagnostic manuals is the standard example); the line cannot be defined as "whatever current medicine can and cannot do," or it will track technology rather than the underlying facts; and it must not classify as enhancement every intervention that improves a life, on pain of erasing the very distinction it was introduced to draw.

(d) The purpose-dependence of the question: is this one distinction or three? The insurer's question (what is owed), the regulator's question (what is fair), and the clinician's question (what is indicated) may not have the same answer, and a theory that assumes they do may be answering a question nobody asked. If you think the distinction should be abandoned rather than repaired, argue that as one of the options -- but only after the desiderata are on the table.

Then tell me which two or three desiderata are doing the real work -- which ones exclude the most candidate theories -- and whether you think any pair of your requirements is jointly unsatisfiable. If some pair is, that is the most interesting result and I would rather have it than a tidy list. Mark each desideratum as non-negotiable or negotiable, and say what would have to be given up in each case. Be explicit about your confidence: indicate which claims you would defend against a determined critic, and identify the single point in your list you are least sure of, and why.
