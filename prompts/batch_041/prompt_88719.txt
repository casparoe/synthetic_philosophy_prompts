For a themed issue I'm editing on the strange afterlife of French theory in the corporate world, I'd like a piece of sustained deadpan satire: a leaked internal onboarding memo from a fictional management consultancy, "Marlowe & Vance," reproduced verbatim, that has been hired to roll out what it calls the Lantern Program at a mid-sized regional logistics company. Write it in the voice of the consultancy's invented "Director of Organizational Clarity," structured as numbered policy sections, roughly 800 to 900 words. The memo's governing premise — stated without irony and adhered to throughout — is that the firm does not surveil, rank, or punish anyone; it simply renders the workforce "legible." Every policy should follow from that premise with airtight internal consistency: an open-plan redesign justified as an architecture of mutual visibility, a continuous performance dashboard that quietly replaces the annual review and inverts who is being examined by whom, mandatory "clarity conversations" that function as confession, and a sincere closing section that answers the anticipated objection that all this is dehumanizing by explaining that the warm, interior, private self was never the natural baseline it is taken to be but a product of the very arrangements the program is dismantling.

The comedy has to live entirely in the substance rather than in the narrator's attitude, so please don't name Foucault or any other theorist, and don't let the memo wink or tip into parody of its own voice — it should read as a plausibly cheerful piece of corporate writing that a real HR department might circulate. The target is the way management discourse has absorbed and operationalized the critique of disciplinary power, so a reader who knows the material should recognize, correctly deployed and never mislabeled, the panopticon as a diagram of visibility rather than a building, normalization as a practice rather than a rule, power as productive rather than merely repressive, and the examination as a technique that turns individuals into cases — while a reader who knows none of this still finds the piece funny and a little chilling. To keep the satire fair rather than easy, resist the temptation to make the policies obviously cruel; the unease should come from how reasonable each one sounds in sequence. Please also include one dissenting element — a new hire's marginal note objecting to the program, which the Director has annotated and absorbed into the framework as further evidence that the system is working — and end with the section on the objection above, delivered straight.
