I am a software engineer with no formal training in philosophy, and I have been reading about the language of thought hypothesis. A colleague presented me with the following argument, and I cannot decide what to make of it.

(1) A system genuinely thinks only if it has a language of thought: a representational system with compositional syntax and semantics.
(2) Neither corvids nor large language models have such a system. Corvids lack a language-like symbolic code, and large language models compute over distributed vector representations without explicit compositional syntax.
(3) Nevertheless, both corvids and large language models exhibit flexible, novel problem-solving that we would ordinarily call thinking: corvids plan for the future and reason about hidden causes, and large language models solve unseen reasoning tasks and generalize across domains.
(4) If both corvids and large language models can think without a language of thought, then the language of thought hypothesis is false.
(5) Therefore, the language of thought hypothesis is false.

I find (5) hard to accept. I think the computational theory of mind is basically correct, and the language of thought hypothesis seems like the most precise way to state it. But I also find (3) compelling. If I had to choose, I would rather keep (3) than (1), but I want to understand what I am paying. Which premise or step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost? Please diagnose the argument and explain the trade-offs.
