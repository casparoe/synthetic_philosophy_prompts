Ok so I'm not a philosopher, I just read a lot and argue with my friends more than is probably healthy, and I've been stuck on this thing for weeks. The argument is basically about whether everyday conventions — tipping, queueing properly, not jaywalking in front of kids, holding the door, standing on the right of the escalator, that whole family of stuff — are actually *moral* obligations or just manners. My friends are all in the "just manners" camp: they say if you don't tip or you cut the line you're a bit of a dick but you haven't done anything *wrong*, no one's been wronged, nothing to feel guilty about. I think that's too quick, and I want to write something about it — probably a long blog post, maybe just so I can win the argument. I've sketched my case below and I'd like you to comment on it. Be blunt, I don't need encouragement, I need to know where it falls apart.

Here's the gist. I want to sort these conventions into two piles and say the moral weight is different in each. Pile one, "entry conventions": you basically can't participate in the thing at all without following them, like forming a queue at a bus stop or waiting your turn at a deli counter. Break these and the whole practice collapses, so I want to say the obligation is real and it's owed to everyone in the practice, not to any one person. Pile two, "exit conventions": you can ignore them and still get what you want, you just pay a social price, like tipping or thanking the bus driver. Nobody can stop you from walking out without tipping. I want to say these still obligate you, but in a weaker, weird way — owed to the specific person (the server) rather than to the practice, and only because they're relying on you in a way they can't easily avoid. Then the punchline I actually care about: I think the strength of the obligation tracks how much other people have *banked on* your compliance — how much they've already spent or arranged assuming you'll do the thing — and not how rude it looks or how much people will judge you. Tipping in the US is strong because the wage structure bakes it in; tipping somewhere it isn't expected is weak or nonexistent, even though it's equally "polite."

And then the bit I'm least sure about, which is what I really want your take on. My friend's girlfriend is from a country where you don't tip and don't really queue the same way, and she says when she visits the US she shouldn't have to follow our conventions because she never agreed to them and they're not *her* norms — it's not like a law, and she's a guest. I want to say that's wrong and that visitors *do* have to follow local conventions, but the only reason I can give is something like "you're relying on the local system while you're in it, so you owe into it," which feels hand-wavy and maybe just begs the question. Also I suspect my two piles aren't actually a clean split — jaywalking seems like it's neither, since there's no real practice to collapse and no specific person relying on you, but I still feel like it's wrong to jaywalk through a crowd of schoolkids. So: is the entry/exit distinction doing real work or am I just relabeling "things with enforcement" vs "things without"? Does the reliance/banking idea actually carry the weight I'm putting on it, or does it collapse into "consequences" and lose the moral part? And does the conclusion about visitors follow, or is there a better argument for it that I'm missing? If you think the whole framing is wrong, tell me what you'd replace it with, but keep it grounded — I want the comments on this draft, not a survey of the literature, since I won't read it anyway.
