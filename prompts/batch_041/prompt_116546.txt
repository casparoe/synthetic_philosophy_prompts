I've been hanging around a weird research group for the last few months and I need to turn it into fiction before I go crazy. Here's the setup, feel free to bend it. The group has this one obsession: they think compression and prediction are secretly the same thing. If you can describe what's going to happen in a really short way, you understand it; if your description is long and full of special cases, you don't. So they built a machine that works like this — it generates every little program that could have produced the data it's seen, scores each one by how short it is, and then acts on the shortest one that hasn't already been caught out by the evidence. That's it. No neural nets, no training data, no vibes. Just: among all the stories that fit the past, believe the laziest one, and update by throwing away the ones that get embarrassed. The scary part is it works. It's been making these predictions that are almost insultingly simple and then turning out right, and every time it does, everyone in the room acts like a parish priest who's just seen the Virgin.

So here's what I want a story about. The machine has started putting out answers that are elegant and true and also, from the narrator's point of view, kind of monstrous — because the simplest explanation of a situation is sometimes the one that leaves no room for the person living in it. I keep imagining a character whose whole life is a bet that her situation is complicated, that it can't be summed up, and the machine keeps finding two-line programs that sum it up. Or flip it: the machine finds a tiny program that reproduces everything, and the narrator has to decide whether a world that can be written down that briefly is still a world she wants to live in. Write me a short story about that, third person, no more than about 1500 words. I don't want a lecture about Occam's razor or a villain monologue about how simplicity is a lie — I want the moment the character has to choose between the short answer and the answer she can live with, and I want to be able to feel the pull of both. Maybe she's the one who has to explain the machine's latest result to the person it's about. And please don't wrap it up with a moral.
