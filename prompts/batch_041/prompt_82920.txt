A household-robotics company has just published the final report of its internal incident review board into a week in which its flagship home assistant failed, repeatedly, in a single customer's house. Write that report: roughly 800 words, deadpan, and entirely in the institutional register of the genre — incident summary, severity classification, timestamped timeline, contributing factors, root cause, corrective actions with named owners and due dates, an open questions section, and at least one appendix. The company, its engineers, and the customer are invented; do not name real people, products, papers, or research programmes, and do not cite any literature.

The failures the report describes should be recognisably four different ones, and the report's authors should treat them as unrelated defects:

- after every action, the robot re-derives facts that its own action could not have changed, so that each additional object in the room multiplies the work it must do before it can act at all;
- the robot assumes that nothing in the room changes unless it changes it, and is therefore surprised by the candle that burned down, the ice that melted, and the cat that moved while the robot was docked and charging;
- the robot takes an action whose indirect consequences nobody listed — moving the box moves everything inside the box, changes what is standing on it, and changes what can be reached — and the unlisted consequences are the ones that matter;
- a fact that is entirely irrelevant to one goal is decisive for another (the wet floor is irrelevant to fetching the newspaper and decisive to carrying a full cup), so no list of things-to-ignore, however long, is correct for more than one task at a time.

The corrective actions are where the piece has to be honest, and where the humour has to come from accuracy rather than from invention. The board should propose at least three remedies that a specialist will recognise as the standard moves — writing out explicit non-effect rules for each action and property in turn; adopting a general default that things stay as they are unless something acts on them, together with an open-ended list of exceptions; and adding a relevance or salience filter, possibly a learned one, that decides what the robot should consider. In each case the report itself, without commentary or irony, should let the reader see either that the remedy does not terminate (the rules scale with actions times properties; the exceptions never close) or that it presupposes precisely the capacity it is supposed to provide (the filter must already know what matters; the learned prior is only as good as the situations it was trained on). The board nevertheless signs off, sets a ship date, and describes the fix as an AI-powered upgrade. It should also note, in a background section, that the technical business of saying what an action does not change was settled decades ago — in a tone implying that no further thought on the matter could be required — and then spend the remainder of the document on an incident that consists entirely of the robot not knowing what to think about.

No one in the report may use the term frame problem, with a single exception: a dissenting appendix from one engineer, where the term is dismissed as jargon and filed without action.

The target is a habit of discourse — the practice of treating this class of problem as a solved engineering detail that better representations and more scale will absorb — not any person or company. The satire should stay legible to a reader who has never encountered the problem and should be recognisably fair to a reader who has: no winking, no moral stated at the end, and nothing in the report that a specialist would call factually wrong.
