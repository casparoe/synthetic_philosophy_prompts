In a committee meeting about entrenching a balanced-budget rule in the constitution, a colleague disposed of the objection that this binds future majorities by saying that you can always 'tie yourself to the mast'. I accept that precommitment is a real phenomenon; what I cannot tell is what the slogan is asserting. Set out the distinct precise claims it could be making — that the later self is a different agent from the earlier one; that the earlier, cooler preference is authoritative over the later, hotter one; that binding works by removing an option rather than by raising its price; that the arrangement presupposes an enforcer who is not himself subject to the temptation, and who in a constitution would play the crew's part; that being bound is either the exercise of self-rule or its surrender — and for each say whether it is trivially true, substantive and defensible, or false, and which of them would actually license my colleague's conclusion. Give me the menu rather than a verdict on the analogy, and flag any reading on which the analogy, taken literally, undercuts the democratic case he thinks it supports.
