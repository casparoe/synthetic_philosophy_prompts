Late in a career spent mostly in the field and the laboratory, I have taken up the philosophy of science as a serious hobby, and I find myself stuck on Popper's treatment of Darwin. Popper appears to have held, at one point, that the theory of natural selection is not a testable scientific theory but rather a metaphysical research programme — a framework within which genuinely testable theories might be built — and then, in his later years, to have changed his mind and allowed that it is testable after all. I would like an essay that adjudicates between these two Popperian moments rather than merely recounting them, and I would like it to reach a verdict.

Please proceed in three moves. First, set out the strongest version of the objection to the testability of natural selection, and be precise about what the objection is aimed at: the compressed slogan that the fittest survive, or the fuller claim about heritable variation, differential reproduction, and the consequent accumulation of adaptive traits. I suspect the charge lands very differently on those two formulations, and I want the essay to say which one it convicts and which it leaves standing.

Second, reconstruct what could plausibly have turned Popper around — whether the change concerned the theory itself, his criterion of falsifiability, or his understanding of what a metaphysical research programme is — and say whether you think the recantation was warranted on its merits, rather than merely noting that he made it.

Third, and this is the part I care about most, take up the proposal that fitness be understood as a propensity: a disposition, possessed by an organism or a genotype in a given environment, to survive and reproduce. Does construing fitness as a propensity supply natural selection with the kind of independently specifiable content that falsifiability requires, or does it merely relocate the circularity — on the grounds that a propensity inferred from the very survival it is invoked to explain may be no more testable than the tautology it was meant to replace? I have a working biologist's suspicion that the answer turns on whether the propensity can be characterised in advance of the outcome, and I would like that suspicion either confirmed or refuted with reasons.

End with a real verdict. Tell me whether Darwinian selection theory is falsifiable in Popper's sense, and if it is, what the decisive kind of test looks like and why it counts; if it is not, tell me what follows for how a working biologist should regard the theory — as a research programme, a statistical framework, a family of models, or something else again. I spent decades testing hypotheses and never doubted that the work was falsifiable, so I would rather be told, on good grounds, that I was deluded than be handed a survey that declines to choose.
