Okay, I'm a novelist, about two thirds through a book, and I need help with one scene. I'd rather ask than fake it.

The setup, which is already in the book: small town, about twenty thousand adults. The guy who owns the local paper is an eccentric rich man, and for the paper's hundredth anniversary he runs a contest. Every household gets a postcard in the mail. The rules are printed on the back. Write a whole number on the postcard — that number is how many entries you're claiming. Mail it in by Friday. At the end, the paper adds up every entry claimed by everybody in town, divides one million dollars by that total, and hands that amount to the submitter of one randomly drawn entry. Postage is a stamp. You can claim as many entries as you want, but only for yourself — you can't claim anything on behalf of anybody else.

Two characters. Next-door neighbors, both in their sixties, both broke, both smart, sitting at a kitchen table the night before the deadline with postcards in front of them.

Iris. Retired schoolteacher. Reads alot of philosophy and has a theory about everything. She has decided to roll a die — a die with twenty thousand sides on it, as many sides as there are adults in town — and mail the postcard, claiming one entry, only if it comes up a one. She's got the die. She's serious.

Ray. Actuary. He's claiming a big number, and he says he can show you on the back of an envelope why: given whatever number everybody else claims, his best play is to claim exactly as many entries as all of them put together. If the town holds back, that's a small number and he takes a fat slice. If the town piles in, he piles in with them.

They're friends. It's going badly.

What I need from you: the dialogue.

The fight I actually want them to have is about whether "what if everybody did that" is a reason or a superstition. Iris thinks Ray's rule can't be followed by everybody at once — there is no number that every single person in town can claim — so it isn't a rule at all, it's a race, and a race where the prize gets smaller the faster everybody runs. Ray thinks that's beside the point; his number doesn't move anybody else's number, and if the race has no finish line, standing at the starting line is how you lose.

Stuff I care about:

1. Neither one is a dummy and neither one is a caricature. Iris is not a prig and Ray is not a cynic. Both arguments at full strength, the real versions, not the versions that are easy to knock over.

2. Real back and forth. My first pass read like two seperate essays stapled together. Each of them needs to be answering the actual thing the other one just said, not the thing they wish the other one had said.

3. Nobody wins, and no third character wanders in with the answer. But each of them should have to give ground at least once, and I want the reader to see why the concession doesn't budge the other one.

4. No citations, no name dropping, no "as so-and-so showed." I know both of these positions have famous names attached to them, and I know there's a name for the "what if everybody did that" test, but the second they start quoting a literature the scene reads like a term paper. I want the substance, not the bibliography.

5. Somewhere in there I want one of them to bring up the fact that this has been tried for real — a science magazine ran a version of it back in the eighties and it turned into a disaster, people mailed in numbers like a googolplex and the prize shrank to a fraction of a cent. Neither of them should get to use that as the last word, though.

6. Keep the arithmetic honest. If the numbers come out wrong, a certain kind of reader throws the book across the room.

About a thousand words. Straight dialogue, a line or two of stage business, I'll do the prose pass myself.
