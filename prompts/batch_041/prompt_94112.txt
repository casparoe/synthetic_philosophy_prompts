I'm a TA for a 300-level course that's half philosophy of mind and half AI ethics (18 students, a 75-minute slot all to myself next week, normal seminar room with a projector and whiteboard, and they've already done functionalism, the Turing test and Searle, so they're not starting cold). I need to design an in-class game that makes them feel the force of the question whether a reinforcement learning agent can suffer -- not the general "is it conscious" thing, but the narrower one about reward signals and valence: whether a system that maximizes a scalar and avoids a negative scalar is having a bad time, or is just doing arithmetic that happens to be about a bad thing. I don't want them to hear me explain the puzzle and nod along; I want the rules of the game to walk them into it. My half-formed idea, which I'd like you to build out (or replace, if you have something better -- think it through first, sketch two or three candidate structures before you settle, then commit to one and give me the detail): a yoked-pairs setup, the kind you see in old animal-learning work. Two students get the exact same schedule of bad events, but only one of them has a button that can cut the event short; the other's button is dead. I want that asymmetry to do real work in the game and then get turned around on an RL agent -- one whose actions can't change its reward stream at all versus one that can steer it. My worry is that the students will just say "well the agent's reward is only a number and the tingle or whatever I got is real," which is exactly the thing under dispute, so I want the activity to make that move feel cheap. Be a philosopher of mind who has also designed classroom simulations before, and please be concrete: rules, materials, roles (I like the idea of one student secretly running the "environment" while the others don't know whether it's a fixed script or reacting to them), a minute-by-minute timeline for the 75, stakes that don't need money (points, chores, candy, mild inconvenience -- nothing that needs consent forms, keep it safe and opt-out-able), and a payoff structure that makes the yoked asymmetry actually bite.

The other half of the idea, which you can use or cut: a "valence ledger." Each round every participant writes a line -- what happened, what they did, how bad it was from 0-10 -- and at the end they have to fill in the same ledger on behalf of the agent. Then the debrief compares the two ledgers, and the pressure point is the fork I keep circling: either the reward signal matters because of what it does to the policy (its use) or because of what it is in itself (its carry), and the game should make both horns unattractive in turn. I'd like them to walk out having felt three things: that their own behaviour under a scalar isn't obviously different from the agent's; that control over the signal changes what they'd call suffering, which means a purely formal feature of the reward structure is valence-relevant in us too; and that "I have feelings and it doesn't" is the conclusion, not the argument.

Give me the debrief in full, mapped moment-by-moment onto the bits of the puzzle (where control enters, where report and access enter, where the number-versus-tingle objection enters, where the "no subject, just a policy" point enters), and be honest about two things: what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this -- my guess is someone refuses the aversive condition, someone else meta-games the environment role, and the group converges way too fast on "obviously it can't suffer" before they've felt anything -- with how to handle each, and then a frank paragraph on what the activity can't reproduce and that I'll have to supply myself as the facilitator. Also flag anywhere you're smuggling in a claim that's actually contested, and don't bother with readings or citations, I just need the thing to run.
