Some friends and I do a fortnightly "defend a text you half-agree with" thing, and I got handed Simone Weil's essay arguing that political parties should be abolished. I went in smug and came out half-converted, which is annoying, because the argument as I've reconstructed it seems to reach way past parties and I can't find the leak. The numbering is mine, and the definition in (1) is my own attempt to make her point precise since she isn't exactly formal about it:

(1) Call an association party-shaped when it has (a) a doctrine members are expected to affirm as a package, (b) some mechanism for disciplining members who deviate on parts of it, and (c) an orientation toward winning -- office, votes, influence, market share, souls, whatever the field's version of victory is. (2) In a party-shaped association, when the collective's interest in winning collides with the truth of some part of the doctrine, members will, over time, treat the winning as outweighing the truth; that's just what (b) and (c) do to people. (3) A mind that habitually subordinates the truth of a claim to the fortunes of a collective has damaged itself as an instrument for finding truth, not only about politics but about whatever it's been trained on. (4) That damage isn't repaired by good intentions or by the doctrine happening to be true, because it's the disposition and not the content that's doing the harm. (5) So any party-shaped association is, by its form, hostile to the intellectual virtue of its members, and the better it is at (c) the worse it gets. (6) Political parties are the most party-shaped thing we have, and their doctrine-discipline happens to be about the one subject where being wrong is most expensive for everyone. (7) So parties should be abolished.

My problem is that (1)-(5) look fine and (7) is nowhere near the only thing they reach. Science departments, religious orders, unions, the editorial line at a newspaper, an army, and frankly our own reading group (we make people defend a text as a package, we do give each other grief for reading 2 uncharitably) all seem to satisfy (1). And if the harm in (4) really is a disposition rather than a content, then "our doctrine is true and our cause is just" buys no exemption -- which is exactly the move I want to make for the reading group and against the party, so I can't have it both ways. The Kolmogorov complicity thing also keeps nagging at me: in a lot of historical situations the live choice wasn't clean truth-seeking versus party discipline, it was party discipline versus being disappeared, and Weil's remedy -- a politics purged of collective passion -- looks like it needs the same kind of sustained, coordinated, doctrine-carrying organization to survive contact with a hostile state that she's condemning. So either (7) is much too narrow or (1)-(5) prove far too much, and I genuinely can't tell whether there's a flaw in the chain or whether it's valid and I just have to pay up at some step. I'd like to keep (7) if I can, but not by keeping a premise that also licenses abolishing the reading group. Which step do I give up, why did it look fine in the first place, and what does giving it up cost me -- including whether the cost is that I lose (7) too? Tell me how confident you actually are in your diagnosis, and single out the one point in it you're least sure about.
