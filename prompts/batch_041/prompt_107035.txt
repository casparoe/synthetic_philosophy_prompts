My old dissertation supervisor, who has spent her career on speech acts and is fond of the misfire/abuse distinction, has given me rulings for years on whether some act was actually performed, and I now need to guess her verdict on a case I cannot ask her about. Her rulings so far: a sincere promise in the standard words, heard and accepted — a promise; the same words said with no intention of keeping them, but heard — still a promise; the same words spoken to someone asleep who never heard them — not a promise; an apology in the correct words to the person wronged but with no shred of remorse — an apology; the same apology delivered to an empty room — not an apology; a bet proposed in the proper form and answered with an explicit acceptance — a bet; the same proposal made to someone out of earshot — no bet.

Last month I let a language-model assistant run my inbox. It replied to a colleague using exactly the conventional words of acceptance, and the colleague, who read and understood the message, treated the matter as settled and began to act on it. I never composed, read, or authorized that message, and I do not feel that I agreed to anything; the model produced the words on its own. Assume the words were the correct ones for the act and that a human received and understood them.

Predict my supervisor's verdict on whether I promised, tell me the rule she appears to be running and the rival rules that fit her earlier rulings equally well, give your best guess and your reasons, and name the one further case that would tell those rules apart.
