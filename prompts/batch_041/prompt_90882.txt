In my philosophy of science course we have spent two weeks on the problem of old evidence, and I would like your credence in a specific claim rather than a summary of the literature. The claim is this: that the probability calculus, as standardly interpreted, can explain how evidence already known before a hypothesis was framed can nonetheless confirm it, and can do so without appealing to counterfactual credences about what one would have believed had one not known the evidence. Please give me a number, and decompose it into the sub-questions it rests on — at minimum, whether the difficulty is a genuine defect of Bayesian confirmation or an artifact of treating evidence as certainly known; whether the counterfactual-credence strategy succeeds or quietly assumes what it must show; whether an old fact's relevance to a hypothesis can be registered without double-counting it; whether confirmation must mean an increase in probability at all, or whether something like explanatory unification is doing the real work; and, since our seminar kept returning to it, whether the problem is even well posed when the old evidence is presupposed by the concepts in which the hypothesis is stated, so that there is no coherent earlier state of belief to appeal to. Assign each sub-question a rough probability, say how you combine them into your final number, and finish by telling me which term your credence is most sensitive to and what argument or evidence would move it most.
