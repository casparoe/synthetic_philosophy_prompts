I was at a house party this weekend and got into it with a friend-of-a-friend about hate speech laws — the whole US-vs-Europe thing. I was on the absolutist side (I've read enough First Amendment blogging to be insufferable about it) and he was pushing the German/European line, that some speech just isn't protected because of the dignitary harm it does to a group, not because it's going to cause a riot next Tuesday. At one point, after I gave the usual move that being offended or feeling hurt isn't a harm the state gets to police, he rolled his eyes and said that the sticks-and-stones thing — words can't hurt you, only sticks and stones can — is just a nursery rhyme bullies recite to get kids to shut up about being tormented. And I didn't have a reply, because I think we were running "hurt" through about four different senses and I couldn't tell which one his arguement actually needed. So I want to take the proverb apart and see what's really in it.

Here's what I want: take that line — sticks and stones may break my bones, but words will never hurt me — and lay out the distinct precise claims it could be making. A menu, not a verdict on the "right" reading. Go from the readings that are basically tautologies or word-games all the way up to the ones that are substantive and load-bearing. For each one I want to know: what exactly it asserts (what kind of "hurt", whose hurt, measured how, over what time scale — immediate feeling, durable psychological damage, standing in the public order, the dignitary wrong of being put below others), whether it's trivially true, genuinely substantive and defensible, or just false, and — this is the part I actually care about — whether that reading is the kind that would do the work people use the line for in the free-speech fight, i.e. whether it really supports "hate speech shouldn't be regulated," or whether it's a non sequitur that only sounds like it settles things. I have a hunch that the interesting readings are the ones about the kind of harm rather than the amount, and that the proverb only bites if you've already bought a pretty thin picture of what a harm is — but tell me if that hunch is wrong, I keep wanting to taboo the word "harm" and see what's left. And a related thing, because it's why the argument felt stuck: his counter-move seemed to be that the proverb is false and the European approach is right precisely because words can wound in a way that matters to the state, so I'd like you to say which of the readings his move is actually attacking — because if he's attacking a straw reading, the two of us may not even disagree.

Format, please: start with a section called Bottom line (2-3 sentences — the single reading you'd bet the line is usually doing in these fights, and whether it survives). Then The menu (the bulk of it, roughly 500-600 words, one numbered reading per paragraph, trivial to radical). Then Verdicts (a short list or table, ~250 words, each reading tagged trivial / substantive / false, plus one line on whether it actually supports the anti-regulation conclusion). Then What he probably meant (~150 words). Please don't give me case law or a survey of doctrines — no citations, I don't need them — I need the disambiguation itself.
