I run a small working group on predictive processing and the free energy principle, and two of its members have now spent the better part of a year talking past each other. The first holds that the free energy principle is a genuine unifying principle of self-organisation: that anything which persists, maintains itself, and behaves like a cognitive system must be in the business of minimising a variational bound on surprise, and that this is a substantive explanatory claim about how the natural world works, not a redescription of it. The second holds that the principle is either unfalsifiable or vacuous — that the formalism is elastic enough to redescribe any behaviour after the fact, that the boundary apparatus does no work independent of the modeller's choice of partition, and that whenever the principle is made precise enough to forbid anything, it has quietly dissolved into ordinary Bayesian inference plus a stipulation about priors and loss. They agree about the mathematics, so far as I can tell, and disagree about everything it is supposed to buy. I have been asked to chair our sessions and I would rather not adjudicate; instead I want the two of them to produce, jointly and in writing, the document that a good-faith adversarial collaboration between them would produce. Neither of us has any appetite for a reading list or a survey of who said what — we cannot look things up, and we would rather have the argument reconstructed from first principles in our own words.

So please draft that document. It should open with a numbered section of claims that both parties can sign without reservation, pitched so that they are neither truisms nor covert concessions — including, if you can manage it, claims about what the principle formally asserts, about which of its parts are definitions rather than discoveries, and about what kind of thing it would be if it were true. Then a table of the residual disagreements, each one stated twice: once in the wording that the principle's defender would accept as a fair statement of her own position, and once in the wording that the sceptic would accept as a fair statement of his. Then the cruxes — the sub-questions such that settling one would move at least one of them — with, for each crux, the formulation of the question that both would accept as the right question, a concrete case, experiment, or argument that both agree would bear on it, and each side's prediction about how it would come out, recorded in advance. Four things keep recurring in our sessions and may or may not be the real cruxes: whether a Markov blanket is a discovery about a system or an artefact of a chosen partition; whether the dark-room objection is answered by the machinery of expected free energy or merely relocated; whether a framework that no single experiment could refute is thereby discredited or merely misclassified; and whether the standard decision-theoretic result that any admissible policy is optimal for some prior and loss function makes the whole enterprise unfalsifiable in principle. Tell me which of these are genuine cruxes, which are verbal, and which are red herrings. Close with a short section naming any part of the dispute you judge to be merely about words, and saying what the two of them would have to do — what they would have to specify or measure — for the remaining disagreement to become tractable.
