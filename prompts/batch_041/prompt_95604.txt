So I'm in this reading group that's been working through big-picture history books — Spengler, Toynbee, Ibn Khaldun, that whole organic school — and one of the guys in it, Marcus, has basically converged on: a civilization is an organism. It's born, it has a vigorous youth, it matures, it ossifies, it dies. Rome did it, Egypt did it, the dynasties Ibn Khaldun was writing about did it, and now he thinks we're in the old-age ward and there's nothing to be done. He's a cycles guy; I guess I'm more of a "things can actually get better if you fix them" person, which is where we fight. What bugs me is that he uses the analogy as an argument, not just as a colorful way of talking: since decline is just what organisms do when they get old, all the reform stuff I care about — fixing institutions, changing policy, whatever — is either futile or it's like giving vitamins to a 90-year-old. You can maybe slow it down, but the trajectory is fixed, so the analogy is supposed to license a whole fatalism about politics. I want you to audit it, not just wave at it. What maps onto what? If a polity is an organism, what's the genome, what's the metabolism, what's the immune system, what's aging itself, what's death? And then where does the mapping actually break? I'm especially curious about three things he never wants to talk about. First, organisms senesce on something like a schedule that's more or less fixed for the species, but do civilizations even have anything like a species-typical lifespan, and does the analogy survive if what we call aging in a society is damage accumulating rather than a program — because even in biology that's not settled, there are animals that basically don't age at all, so "everything gets old and dies" isn't even true of the source domain. Second, an organism's organs don't have their own agendas; they exist for the whole. Societies obviously have parts with their own agendas, and I suspect the analogy quietly recodes internal conflict as a symptom of disease, which is convenient if you're the part that's currently winning. Third, does an organism ever get to graft on another organism's organ, or copy its genome, or get absorbed by another organism and keep going? Civilizations clearly do that, and it's not obvious whether that counts as death, reproduction, or neither.

Also, does the analogy require the cause of death to be internal? Because as far as I can tell most societies that ended got ended by something outside them — a plague, an invasion, a drought, a trade route moving — and if the analogy has to insist the collapse was endogenous, that's a pretty big claim to smuggle in on the back of a metaphor about bodies. And one more meta thing: Marcus says the analogy predicts our decline, but it seems to me it can only retrodict, since he has to eyeball the age of our civilization by feel, and then every counterexample gets filed as "youth" or "not really a civilization." So what I want from you is the correspondence table first, then the disanalogies split into the ones that actually matter for his conclusion versus the ones that are just nitpicks, then what the analogy is smuggling in about internal causation and inevitability, and finally whether the fatalism survives once all of that is on the table. If there's a better comparison for whatever real thing he's gesturing at — institutional sclerosis, elite overproduction, a coordination trap, whatever — tell me what it is and what it would predict that the organism story doesn't.
