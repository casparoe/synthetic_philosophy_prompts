I'm a science journalist, and I've more or less talked myself into a view I keep hearing from historians and complexity people: that the big outcomes in history are chaotic in the technical sense, so tiny contingencies — a storm, a missed train, someone sleeping in — tip the whole thing, and the structural forces everyone cites (economics, geography, demography) don't really determine what happens. For want of a nail, stated strongly: if the nail had held, no kingdom falls.

What would count against that? I don't want the lazy "its unfalsifiable" objection, I want the actual defeaters, ranked from fatal to merely awkward. Specifically: does chaos theory even support the claim, or does the fact that chaotic systems have attractors and stable distributions mean structure constrains where a trajectory can end up, so small nudges mostly wash out? What would the historical record have to look like for the strong view to be right, and how do we square it with cases where wildly different paths end in the same place — several countries industrializing independently, say? How do you identify a genuinely pivotal contingency rather than just a point the trajectory happened to pass through, when you can't rerun the counterfactual? And do defenders of the strong version actually concede anything, or has it quietly weakened into "history is complicated and path-dependent"? If it has, tell me straight whether that's a problem for a view like this or just the price of admission.
