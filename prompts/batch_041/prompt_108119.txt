Adam Smith died in 1790, well over a century before any of the following existed: a global public sphere in which images of distant strangers' suffering arrive continuously; platforms engineered to maximize engagement by amplifying outrage; and statistical models that can predict, from large samples of human judgment, how people will appraise a given act. I want you to predict what Smith would have made of a proposal that could only be put forward now, reasoning from his account of sympathy and the impartial spectator rather than from his general reputation.

The proposal. A firm proposes a public service it calls an Impartial Spectator Engine. Before a person posts, speaks, or acts, the service reports how a well-informed, disinterested observer would appraise the conduct. Its designers claim to be automating and universalizing Smith's impartial spectator: by making the disinterested standpoint available on demand, they say, it corrects self-love and partisan bias in everyone alike. Assume the engine is accurate in the narrow sense that it reliably predicts what a representative sample of strangers would approve or disapprove of. It is used voluntarily, at scale, and it is cheap.

Write the analysis, in the following order.

First, the machinery. Reconstruct, from the substance of Smith's position and without relying on his reputation, the parts of his view that bear on the proposal: what the impartial spectator is and how it is formed in a person; the difference between being praised and being praiseworthy; the role of self-command; the way the imagined spectator is built out of real spectators and their actual responses; and the standing worry that a spectator assembled from other people's reactions might simply reproduce their prejudices. State plainly where the reconstruction is secure and where it is an interpretive guess.

Second, the verdict. Predict whether Smith would treat the Engine as a legitimate external support for conscience, as a corruption of the very thing it claims to replicate, or as something in between. Give the reasons he would give, and identify the feature of the proposal that does the most work in your prediction.

Third, the distance problem. Smith holds that our sympathy is naturally strongest toward those near us and faintest toward strangers we will never meet. Predict what he would say about the Engine's promise to make distant and near cases weigh the same. Would he regard the leveling of distance as an improvement in our moral condition, a distortion of a sympathy that is properly partial, or a change he lacked the resources to assess? Give the considerations on each side.

Fourth, the designers. Smith wrote about the man of system, who is fond of arranging people as if they were pieces on a chessboard and cannot bear that the pieces have their own principles of motion. Predict what he would say about the people who build and tune such an Engine, and about the claim that a single calibrated instrument can set the standard of approbation for an entire society.

Fifth, the open questions. Identify at least two questions the proposal raises that Smith's framework leaves genuinely undecided, and say precisely what is missing that would decide them. I care more about a well-argued "his view does not reach this" than about a confident verdict.

Sixth, the boundary of the prediction. Separate the parts of your answer that follow from Smith's principles from the parts that depend on the assumptions and prejudices of his time and place, and say which of your predictions you would retract or revise under a specific change in the scenario.

Close with a short verdict: one paragraph on what Smith would most likely have concluded, and one sentence on how confident you are and why.

Refer to Smith's arguments by their content, not by chapter or page, and do not supply a bibliography or citations; I will add those myself. Aim for roughly 1500 words.
