I have been keeping a list of my own verdicts about killing and letting die, and I would like to know what principle, if any, they add up to. For each case I record two things separately: whether I count what I did as killing the person or as letting them die, and how wrong I take it to be. Those two judgments come apart often enough that I suspect they answer to different principles, and I would like to be shown whether that is so.

1. Cutting the rope. A climber hangs above a drop, held by a rope within my reach. I saw through the rope. He falls and dies. I count that as killing him, and as wrong in the way murder is wrong.

2. The rockfall I started. I dislodge a boulder that pins a hiker's leg in a ravine, then walk away without sending for help. He dies of exposure before morning. I count that as killing him.

3. The ventilator. A patient with no prospect of recovery is breathing only because a machine breathes for him. I switch it off. He dies. I count that as letting him die, and I do not think it was wrong, or wrong only in the way that any death is a sad thing.

4. The canal. A stranger's car leaves the road and goes into the canal below my house. I am standing on the bank with a rope in my hand, and I could reach him easily and at no risk to myself. I do nothing. He drowns. I count that as letting him die, but as gravely wrong, nearly as wrong as the first case.

5. The kidney. A stranger will die unless he receives a kidney, and I am a match. The operation is painful and will cost me some weeks, but it is not dangerous. I decline. I count that as letting him die, and as not wrong.

6. The lookout. I am paid to keep watch from the bow. I fall asleep. The ship strikes a reef and a passenger drowns. I count that as letting him die, but I hold myself as responsible as if I had pushed him in.

7. The flood. A flash flood is coming down the valley and will drown my village. I dynamite the bank so that the water takes a channel leading to a single farmhouse instead. The farmhand there drowns. I count that as killing him, though I believe I was right to do it.

The case I keep going back and forth on is the ventilator. When I imagine that the patient had years of life ahead of him and wanted the machine left on, I find I want to say that switching it off would be killing him; when I imagine him already dying, I want to say it is letting him die. So I cannot tell whether my verdict in case 3 is about the act of switching off or about the fact that the machine was doing so little.

My tentative suspicion is that the killing line tracks who started the threat rather than who moved a muscle: I kill when I introduce or redirect the danger, and I let die when the danger was already there and I merely fail to stop it. That fits cases 1, 2, and 7 and the contrast between 4 and 5. But it seems to misclassify case 3, where the patient's danger was already there and I did not introduce it, yet I switched off the very thing holding it back; and it sits oddly with case 6, where I introduced no danger at all and only failed at a job I had taken on. It also leaves me unsure how to separate case 1 from case 3 at all, since in both I remove something that was the only thing keeping the person from death.

What I want from you: a principle, or a small set of them, that delivers these verdicts, stated precisely enough to apply to new cases. Say which of my cases forces each clause of it, and say separately whether the wrongness verdicts follow the same principle as the killing verdicts or a different one. Tell me what your principle predicts for the ventilator patient with years of life ahead of him, and for one further case I have not listed. And tell me plainly which of my seven verdicts it cannot save, and whether you would give that verdict up or keep it and pay the price in complexity.
