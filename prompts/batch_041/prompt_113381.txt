ok so I'm a second-year undergrad taking a rational choice / decision theory module, and our lecturer has this thing where everyone in the class drafts one exam question for the final and she picks a couple to actually use. Mine has to be on the two-envelope paradox. Every draft I write ends up being a question you can answer by reciting the one line everybody remembers from the lecture — that the switching argument secretly assumes a scale-invariant improper prior — which, fine, but it doesn't make anyone actually think. Can you write the question for me, plus a model answer and a marking scheme?

The thing I want it built around isn't in the lecture and I haven't seen it in the standard treatments either: pin the amounts down instead of leaving them totally unspecified. The host picks an integer m uniformly from 1 to 10, puts m in one envelope and 2m in the other, shuffles, you take one, open it, see x, and then choose whether to keep x or take the other envelope. You're risk neutral and you want to maximise expected money. I'm fairly sure the optimal policy is swap iff x ≤ 10, and that it beats never swapping by exactly 2 in expectation, even though always swapping gains you nothing at all in expectation — but please check that yourself before you build the whole key around it, if I've slipped somewhere I'd rather find out now than in the marking meeting.

That's the part I think will break the parroters. With the numbers pinned down, the naive argument (there's a 50/50 chance the other envelope holds 2x or x/2, so it's worth 1.25x, so swap) actually gives the right conditional answer at some values of x and the wrong one at others, so students can't just write "improper prior, done" and move on.

Three parts, please. (a) State the usual switching argument as carefully as you can and say exactly which step is unjustified, and why. (b) The concrete game above: derive the optimal policy and the expected gain over never swapping, and then explain how a positive expected gain conditional on a particular x is compatible with the ex ante symmetry argument that says there's no free lunch here. (c) The swap-twice version: if each swap really had the expected gain the naive argument claims, then swapping back and forth with a small fee per swap would be a sure loss you'd rationally march into — say what that shows about the naive argument. Model answer around 700 words, then a marking scheme with the must-hit moves, the marks for each (total 25), and one line per point on how students typically lose it.

Couple of constraints from the lecturer: it has to be doable in 45 minutes with no calculator, so keep the arithmetic humane; no reading lists obviously; and don't let it drift into the should-you-open-the-envelope-at-all / value-of-information stuff, that's a different week. Also she hates marking schemes that are just a list of topic names, so the marks need to attach to actual moves a student makes, not to vibes. And it should definately not be answerable by someone who's memorised the lecture and nothing else, that's the whole point of me doing this.
