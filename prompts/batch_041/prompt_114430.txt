I'm teaching a seminar on incommensurable values and hard choices, and every year I get the same complaint from students: the theory is all well and good, but it never touches anything with dirt under its fingernails. So I've been trying to build a unit around the Tellico Dam / snail darter fight, and I want your help thinking it through before I inflict it on them. The basic story, as I'll present it: TVA started building a dam on the Little Tennessee in the late sixties, mainly as an economic-development and recreation scheme rather than a power project; the reservoir would flood a stretch of river that was also home to multi-generational farms and Cherokee sites, including the village that gives Tennessee its name. In 1973 a biologist found a small fish, the snail darter, in that stretch of river, and it was listed as endangered; a law student and his professor sued, and in 1978 the Supreme Court held that the Endangered Species Act forbade completing the dam, since it would destroy the fish's only known habitat. Congress then amended the Act to create a cabinet-level committee -- the so-called God Squad -- that could grant exemptions, and it was required to rule on Tellico specifically. It denied the exemption, essentially on the ground that the dam's economic benefits didn't warrant wiping out the species. Then Congress turned around and exempted Tellico by a rider to an appropriations bill, and the dam was finished and the reservoir filled in 1979. Later the fish was transplanted to another river, listed as threatened, and eventually delisted; and there's a live taxonomic dispute about whether the snail darter was ever a distinct species at all, or just an eastern population of the stargazing darter. I'll mention that, but I don't want the whole exercise to hinge on it.

What I want from you is a two-directional analysis, theory into case and case back into theory. Start with the mapping: what are the actual values in play here -- species survival, the integrity of a river system, the ancestral land of the Cherokee, the farms, the economic prospects of a poor region, the rule of law and the credibility of a statute -- and who holds them, and who bears the losses? Then bring in the apparatus. A Raz-style incommensurability claim would say that some of these goods don't admit of a common measure, so there's no fact of the matter about whether the dam's benefits outweigh the fish; Ruth Chang's parity view says they can be comparable without either being better or worse, which is why the choice feels hard rather than merely uncertain. I want you to say which pairs of values here look incommensurable, which look merely on a par, and which are straightforwardly commensurable -- and to be honest about how much the distinction is doing, because I suspect alot of the apparent incommensurability is really about the indeterminacy of the value-bearers rather than about the values themselves. Then the interesting institutional question: the God Squad was, in effect, a commensuration machine -- a body legally charged with weighing economic benefits against extinction. Did it succeed in commensurating, or did it just make a decision and dress it up? What does it mean that it found the benefits insufficient, and that Congress then overrode it -- does that confirm that no genuine weighing was possible, so the override was pure power, or does it show that a weighing was possible but that the political process simply didn't accept the answer? Finally, what does the episode show about the incommensurability thesis itself: is it a confirming case, an anomaly, or a case the theory never anticipated -- and does the later taxonomic revision, if the snail darter wasn't a species, make the whole thing look like a hard choice about a pseudo-value, or does it leave the structure of the hard choice untouched? Don't force the theory to speak where it's silent; I'd rather have you say where the apparatus runs out.
