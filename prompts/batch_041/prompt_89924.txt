I teach a film-and-philosophy seminar and want to close the aesthetics unit with a case that strains the standard solutions to the paradox of tragedy rather than illustrating them. The film is Kenneth Lonergan's Manchester by the Sea (2016): Lee Chandler, a janitor living alone in Quincy, is named guardian of his teenage nephew after his brother's death, and the flashbacks disclose the reason for his isolation — a drunken mistake that burned his house and killed his three children. Read the film through the paradox of tragedy — the fact that we voluntarily attend to works that make us miserable and then call the experience good, even valuable — and press it hard in both directions.

Two features make this a hard case rather than a convenient one, and I want both held onto. First, the film withholds catharsis. Lee does not heal and does not accept forgiveness; the ending offers no insight, no reconciliation, no uplift, only his plain statement that he cannot get past what he did. So the conversion account (Hume's, in which eloquence and form turn painful subject matter into pleasure), the compensation account (the whole is worth the pain because it is beautiful or edifying), and the control account (we enjoy the sadness because we can switch it off) must all do their work without the usual payoff. Second, the film is funny: Lonergan's dialogue is deadpan comedy, and the audience laughs well before the film turns the screw. That threatens the paradox's premise, which is that the work makes us sad.

Which of the accounts survives, and where does each misdescribe what is actually happening in the theater? Take the meta-response account seriously — Susan Feagin's idea that we take pleasure in being pleased by our own sympathetic response — and then test it; and say whether the quasi-emotion reply associated with Walton, that our sadness at fiction is not real sadness but something else, solves the paradox or changes the subject. Then consider the possibility that the paradox is simply the wrong question for this film, because Lee's story is one of guilt rather than misfortune: the dominant affects may be dread, shame, and moral horror rather than pity and fear, in which case the frame needs replacing rather than repairing. Say plainly whether you think that is right.

Some particular things I want addressed. The police-station scene, where Lee, having just been told that no charges will be filed, lunges for an officer's gun: what is the audience's response, and is it pleasure? The scene with Randi, whose apology Lee refuses to accept: does the film make us want the forgiveness to land, and is that wanting a pleasure that has nothing to do with sadness? The closing stretch with Patrick, where a room in Boston and a boat and a shared routine are all the consolation on offer: is that a compensation after all, and does it compromise the film's honesty if it is? And the rewatch question — many viewers call this a great film they have no wish to see a second time: if the paradox says we seek out the painful work, what does that do to it?

Where the film resists the reading, say so rather than forcing the fit: if the humor, or the film's patience, or its refusal to punish Lee keeps it from being the kind of sad art the paradox is about, that is a finding, not a failure. Don't summarize the plot and don't cite literature — write from the film and from whatever positions you can reconstruct. I want a reading faithful in both directions: the film not reduced to an illustration of a thesis, the theories not stretched over scenes they do not fit.

End with a single line beginning Verdict: that states, in one sentence, whether the paradox of tragedy is a genuine paradox in this film's case and which account best explains our response to it.
