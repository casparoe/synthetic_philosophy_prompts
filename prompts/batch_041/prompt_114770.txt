I cover science and ideas, and for the past year I've been reporting on the question of which artificial systems, if any, are owed moral consideration. I've arrived at a position I'd like you to try to break. It has two halves. The first is the familiar one: what matters morally is a system's computational organisation rather than the material it happens to run on. The second half is the one I actually care about, because everything turns on it and I suspect it is where the view is most exposed, namely that for any system there is a fact of the matter about which computation it implements, fixed once we agree on the grain of description. Call that the attribution assumption.

Here is the apparatus I have been carrying around, so you can see the shape of the worry. Any finite physical system can be described as implementing indefinitely many mutually incompatible computations, depending on which mapping we choose between its physical states and the states of some formal machine. A clerk with a sufficiently large book of precomputed answers can be described as computing whatever a brain computes, or as computing the weather, or as running some vast automaton whose states are positions of ink on a page, and nothing inside him need resemble the computation we attribute. Two systems can share an input-output profile while their internal states are permuted relative to one another; two more can share a functional organisation while differing in causal microstructure, one continuous and analog, the other discrete and clocked. I keep noticing that my intuitions about moral status track the description I have adopted rather than the system in front of me. I have been tempted by a repair, which is to privilege the mapping under which the system's behaviour is most counterfactually robust, so that the clerk with the book is demoted because his outputs would survive only the smallest perturbations of the book. Tell me whether that repairs the assumption or merely relocates the arbitrariness.

So what would have to be true for the position to be false? I don't want the stock objections; I know them, and I want the ones that should actually move me. Which observations, arguments, intuitions, or formal results would tell against substrate-independent computationalism, and which would tell specifically against the attribution assumption? Rank them from fatal to merely awkward, and for each say what the view's defenders would themselves concede and what they would dismiss as question-begging. Tell me what the position predicts that its rivals don't, and whether any of those predictions are the sort we could in principle check, in the lab, in comparative neurobiology, or in the behaviour of systems we build. Tell me whether the view risks anything at all or has been insulated by qualification, and if it has, whether that is a defect for a view of this kind or simply the price of the subject matter. I am especially interested in whether it collapses into a rival, whether into the claim that moral status is projected by observers rather than discovered in systems, or into a pancomputationalism on which everything and nothing has status. And I want your honest bottom line on whether the attribution assumption can be made principled at all, or whether the whole position simply inherits its arbitrariness.

Please write this as continuous prose, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, in the manner of a long letter to a colleague who disagrees with you.
