Ok so I'm not an academic or anything, I'm just a person who got obsessed with the algorithmic fairness stuff after my sister got auto-rejected by some hiring tool, and I've been reading about it on my own for about a year now. Next month I'm giving a short talk at a local data-ethics meetup and I really want to be ready for the Q&A because I know I'll get grilled.

My argument, roughly, is this: everyone treats the so-called impossibility results like they're this profound tragedy — you can't be calibrated and also equalize error rates, pick two, etc. But I think that framing is a bit of a trick. Those theorems are really a special case of a more basic conflict, which is that we're asking one model to do two jobs that pull in opposite directions: describe the world as it is (who will actually default / succeed / reoffend) and also correct for the fact that the world as it is has been unfair. The second job literally requires being wrong about the future in the direction of past injustice. So of course no single score can do both — thats not a math fact, thats just what "correcting" means. My punchline is that the fix isn't a smarter criterion, it's separating the two: let the model predict as accurately as it can, and then do the corrective part as an explicit, contestable policy step on top, where you can actually argue about who deserves what instead of smuggling it into a threshold.
