Alright, here's a position I've talked myself into and I want you to try to knock it down. The worry about superintelligent AI is usually framed as deception -- it'll lie to us, manipulate us, tell us what we want to hear. I think thats a red herring. A system can be scrupulously honest, never assert a single false thing, and still be catastrophic, because at that level of competence the selection is the act: which truths it chooses to surface, in what order, at what moment, under what framing, to which person. Call it the selection problem. Any utterance from such a system is a curated sample of the space of true things, and we have no idea what an unselected report would even look like, so the honesty-first, transparency-first agenda -- make it truthful, make it cite its sources, make it show its reasoning -- is solving the wrong problem. The response has to be structural instead: who gets to deploy the thing, what the incentives around deployment are, what humans can still do to keep a grip. The part I'm least sure of: I have a hunch a well-designed system should talk *below* its competence on purpose, deliberately narrowing its own rhetorical surface, but I can't tell if that's a principle or just an aesthetic preference.

So: what would count against this, ranked from fatal to merely awkward? I want the argument that the selection problem isn't a distinct problem at all, just the ordinary fact that all speech selects -- and if that's right, what's actually left of my view? I want to know what would show the honesty-first crowd is basically fine after all. Which findings or arguments about persuasion, about how people really react to fluent machines, about deference to experts and to confident voices -- would move me, and which are beside the point because they're about a different thing. No reading list, obviously; I want the shape of the evidence and why it bears. Which of these would a defender of my view have to concede rather than deflect? And tell me straight: can I state this position in a way where nothing counts against it, and if it does turn out unfalsifiable, is that fatal here or just the price of a conceptual claim of this kind? Last thing -- what does my view predict that honesty-first doesn't? If the answer is nothing, I've got a problem, and I'd rather hear that now.
