ok so I've been trying to get my head around supererogation and I keep hitting a snag that the usual textbook treatments dont really address. The standard line is that an act is supererogatory when its morally good and praiseworthy but not required -- beyond the call of duty. Fine. But it seems to me "beyond the call" covers two pretty different things and I cant tell if they're actually the same deontic category.

First kind: duty asks you to give 5% of your income to charity, you give 20%. You did more of the same kind of thing duty already demanded. Second kind: duty doesn't demand anything in the ballpark -- you forgive someone who seriously wronged you when you had every right to stay cold, or you go far out of your way for a stranger who has no claim on you at all. Here there's no instruction from duty that you exceeded; there was never a call to go beyond in the first place.

Are these really one category, or does "supererogatory" equivocate between "above the threshold" and "outside the domain of duty altogether"? Is there a principled way to unify them? And relatedly, when we praise the act, is the praiseworthiness attaching to the act itself or to the agent's character -- because if it's really the character, I'm not sure the phrase "supererogatory act" is pulling any weight that "admirable agent" doesn't already pull.

I want a properly conceptual answer, not a literature survey. Tell me where the distinction is load-bearing and where it collapses, and be honest about where your own explanation is most likely to be wrong or to be smuggling in a contested reading.
