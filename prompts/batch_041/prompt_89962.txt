I was arguing with my friend Marisol for like two hours at a diner last night about when a bunch of things add up to being a single thing, and I woke up still annoyed about it, so I'm hoping you can settle this. Take the role of a philosopher of metaphysics who works on parts and wholes — mereology, the metaphysics of ordinary objects, that whole neighborhood — and please start by brainstorming the candidate principles and laying out the options before you commit to one, then work through my cases one at a time. Marisol has a name for this problem and a menu of four or five stock answers she recites at me whenever I try to talk about it, and honestly none of them fit what I actually think, so build up from my gut verdicts instead of starting from the menu. And think carefully about the difference between what's doing the work in my head and what I'm just saying because it sounds right.

Here's what I think. Two bricks sitting next to each other in a wall with nothing between them: not one thing, just two bricks. A brick wall with mortar in it: one thing, I'll die on this hill. A pile of sand on a beach: not one thing, just a bunch of grains. A sandcastle: one thing, definately — and yes, I know it's the same grains held together by nothing but friction and a little water, and yes, I know that's the same relationship the pile has, don't @ me, that's part of why I'm asking. A cat: one thing, obviously. A cat that's been dead a week: still one thing, I think, even though it's rotting. A swarm of bees moving as a unit from field to field: I want to say not one thing, but every time I actually watch one I get less sure. A beehive with a queen and workers: one thing? It's a bunch of individual organisms, but it also does stuff no individual bee does. A Portuguese man o' war, which as I understand it is a colony of seperate animals that can't live apart and hunts as one: one thing, it really feels like one thing, but then I don't see how the beehive isn't also one thing, and once I say that I don't see how the pile of sand isn't one thing if we're just going by how tightly the parts cooperate. A tornado: not a thing, it's an event. A cloud: not a thing. A river: not a thing, but I say "the river" constantly and I'd fight someone who told me the Mississippi doesn't exist. A corporation: not a thing, it's a legal fiction, except it owns my apartment so who cares what I think. A basketball team: not a thing. A forest: not a thing. A coral reef: one thing?? Marisol says a reef is one thing and the individual corals aren't, which is the exact inverse of what I want to say about the bees and the hive, and it makes me feel like I'm just making this up as I go. A cup: one thing. A cup that's been smashed into seven pieces and glued back together badly: still one thing, or one thing again? And a human being: one thing — but a human being is also kind of a colony of cells that would mostly go on living for a while if you scattered them, which is the bee problem all over again and I hate it.

I think my principle is something like: parts make a thing when they're organized so they do something together that they couldn't do as a heap — a function, a job, a unified go of it — and mere contact, shared origin, or physical proximity doesn't cut it. But I can't tell whether that's a principle or just a vibe, and it has obvious trouble: it seems to make the sandcastle a thing only because someone wanted it to be, which means the pile of sand becomes a thing the second I decide to build a castle out of it, and it seems to make the beehive a thing while the swarm isn't, which feels like a difference of degree dressed up as a difference in kind. So: is there a principle that gets all of these, or close? State it precisely, tell me which clause is doing the work for each case, give me a small set of rival candidates if the honest answer is that no single one fits, and tell me honestly which of my verdicts the best candidate can't accommodate and what I'd be paying to keep it. Then use it to predict what I should say about a case I haven't listed — a frozen embryo, a lake, a Wikipedia article, a marriage, a species, whatever you think is the sharpest test of whether the principle is really mine.
