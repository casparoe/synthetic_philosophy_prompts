You are a philosopher of mind with a working knowledge of personal identity, brought in to advise a novelist. Before you build anything, brainstorm briefly: list the distinct senses in which a self might be said to survive — continuity of memory, of character, of a self-representing pattern, of a person's hold on the minds of others — and note where these come apart. Then give me the procedure.

I am writing a novel set in a near-future clinic that copies a person's mind into several bodies; the copies begin identical and diverge. My problem is scene by scene: does the figure standing in the room count as the same person as the one who walked in? A widow's grief, a promise made to the original, an inheritance, a murder charge, two copies who have become strangers — each turns on an answer I am currently making by feel.

Write me a decision procedure I can run in a few minutes while drafting. Give the questions in order, say what counts as a sufficient answer to each, mark the point at which the procedure returns a no, and mark the points where it must stop and hand the decision back to me because the question is one of values rather than fact. Ground it in the best available account of a self that could genuinely be distributed across brains — Hofstadter's picture of the self as a self-referential pattern that other minds can carry, together with whatever the psychological-continuity tradition still contributes — but keep the steps free of jargon, so that a writer who has not read the theory can follow them.

Then run the procedure on three cases from my draft: first, a copy who has every memory but none of the original's character; second, a copy who shares almost nothing with the original yet continues her project and is recognized by everyone who loved her; third, two copies who each claim to be the person who made a promise, their claims having diverged past the point where either could be called the continuation. Include at least one verdict I will not like, and flag the verdicts that are unstable, where a small change of facts flips the answer. End with what the procedure cannot do: the cases it will get wrong, and the places where it looks like a test but is really a metaphysical decision in disguise.

I need this usable at my desk rather than in a seminar, and every step should yield something I can put on the page — a line of dialogue, a hesitation, a refusal. Answer in American English.
