So two people in my reading group got into a fight about the money-pump argument for expected utility maximization and I've been drafted to referee. Roughly how it went:

Marcus: if your preferences go in a circle -- you prefer A to B, B to C, and C to A -- then someone can offer you a string of trades you'll accept one by one, ending with you back where you started minus a fee. So a rational agent's preferences have to be transitive, and the same trick forces completeness too. That's basically the whole case for expected utility maximization: a rational agent has complete transitive preferences, and that just is being an expected utility maximizer.

Priya: the pump only works if you keep accepting the trades. Someone who makes a plan and sticks to it, or who just refuses to haggle with a stranger obviously running a scam, never loses a cent. So at most it shows a bad habit of trading, not that the preferences themselves are irrational.

Marcus: refusing an offer you prefer to the status quo is exactly the irrational thing, and you can't always precommit anyway. And the argument is about the shape of your preferences, not your willpower -- a cycle is a defect in the ordering itself.

Priya: that only bites if your preferences stay fixed across the whole sequence. If you re-evaluate at each step you're not still bound by the earlier cycle, so the pump needs diachronic stability as an extra premise, not just intransitivity. And second, even if you squeeze transitivity and completeness out of all this, that buys you a utility function, not expected utility maximization -- nothing in any pump hands you independence or a probability representation. So the coherence argument, stated honestly, gets way less than Marcus claims.

Marcus: if you bolt on a preference for 'not being manipulated' above the cycle, you haven't rescued the cycle, you've just changed the subject by adding a fourth option; the ranking underneath is still circular.

Priya: and if that's cheating, then so is assuming she has to accept every improvement -- you're assuming away the resolute agent.

Can you go through it move by move and say who's right at each step, and where each of them is missing something? I don't care who 'wins' overall, I want to know which moves actually survive.
