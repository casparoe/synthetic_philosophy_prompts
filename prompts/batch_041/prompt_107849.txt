Two camps in the philosophy of medicine have argued for decades without either side moving. The naturalist holds that whether a condition is a disease is settled by facts about biological function and the standards of the biomedical sciences, and that calling a condition a disease is not, at bottom, an evaluative act. The normativist holds that no such verdict can be reached without appeal to evaluative standards — to what is bad, harmful, or incapacitating for a person — and that the naturalist's favoured facts are either unobtainable or, where obtainable, insufficient. They have now agreed to an adversarial collaboration, and you are to draft the joint statement that both could sign.

The agreed-claims section must be substantive rather than diplomatic. Record only claims each signatory would defend in its own voice; include what each concedes to the other, what both now regard as a pseudo-question, what has survived the exchange intact, and what both agree the dispute is not about — including positions outsiders commonly attribute to them that neither in fact holds. The lowest common denominator is not acceptable.

Both signatories should work with three distinctions, and the statement should make clear which one is doing the work at each point of agreement and disagreement.

First, three candidate markers of disease: (i) biological dysfunction — a failure of a part or process to perform the function it was selected for or is standardly supposed to perform; (ii) statistical atypicality relative to a reference class; (iii) disvalue — suffering, incapacity, or the loss of something worth having. Which combination is necessary, which sufficient, and relative to what reference class, is exactly what is at issue.

Second, the type/token distinction: a condition type may be a disease while this bearer of it is not diseased, or the reverse. Where a claim is made at one level and tested at another, say so explicitly.

Third, the triage distinction between the diagnostic question (is this a disease?) and the licensing question (does it entitle its bearer to treatment, the sick role, or public resources?). Both sides must state whether they think the opposing camp has been answering the licensing question while claiming to answer the diagnostic one, and if so, which part of the dispute is thereby verbal.

Choose the cases so that they cross the three markers: dysfunction present and absent, bearer-level disvalue present and absent, third-party disvalue present and absent. At a minimum, address these four, and add a fifth if the design leaves a cell empty.

(a) A heritable variant, common in one population and rare elsewhere, that markedly reduces peak aerobic capacity relative to the species range while conferring near-complete resistance to a lethal endemic pathogen. Carriers are asymptomatic, work, and do not regard themselves as ill.

(b) A condition for which no biological abnormality has ever been found, whose entire character consists in the exclusion of its bearers from paid work by law and custom, with the consequent distress and incapacity that follow.

(c) A well-characterised metabolic defect fully compensated by a cheap daily supplement, leaving its bearer asymptomatic, statistically normal on every measured function, and free of stigma.

(d) A genuine dysfunction that causes its bearers neither distress nor incapacity, but that others find repellent and disvalue strongly; the bearers themselves are indifferent to it.

For each case, both signatories must record in advance what they predict, and both must agree on what observation would count against their own prediction. Treat the following as a shared datum to be explained rather than disputed: in quality-of-life research, people with conditions that clinicians and laypeople alike rank as severely disabling often report life satisfaction at or near population averages, sometimes above. State what each side predicts about this, and what either would have to concede if it holds up.

Then set out the residual disagreement as a table, one row per crux: the crux in neutral wording; the naturalist's formulation of its own position and the normativist's, each written so that the side in question would accept the wording as fair; the case, argument, or evidence both sides agree bears on it; and each side's prediction. Include at least one crux that is empirical, one that is conceptual, and one about what medicine is for. For each, say what would move at least one side and whether the disagreement would survive if that happened.

Close with two short sections: first, an honest note on which parts of the dispute the collaboration has shown to be verbal and why the rest are not; second, the joint list of red herrings.

Do not rest any claim on the authority of a named author or a body of literature; every claim must stand on its merits. Do not let the well-worn examples that appear in every treatment of this topic carry the argument. Write in the register of a formal joint statement, in British English, signed by both signatories.
