I'm a novelist — six books into a secondary-world fantasy series about a family of cartographers, and at this point most of my working life is canon management. There's the series bible, my own private wiki, and one rule about the magic I have never once broken on the page: nobody comes back from the dead, full stop, no exceptions, even when it would solve book four beautifully. Underneath that there's a whole layer of stuff I bend, patch and quietly rewrite whenever a book needs it — the timeline, the geography of the northern coast, exactly how the old empire fell, whether the second book's prophecy was literal. A friend with a philosophy degree heard me describe all this over dinner and said I'd basically reinvented Lakatos's theory of research programmes, which I'd never heard of. So I read up on it enough to get the shape, though I'm no philosopher. As I understand it: a programme has a hard core of commitments you refuse to abandon come what may, and they're protected not because anyone proved them but because the practitioners have decided not to aim their criticism there; around that sits a belt of auxiliary assumptions you're allowed to revise freely; there's some kind of strategy telling you which repairs count as productive development and which are just you refusing to touch the core; and then the whole enterprise is either progressive — it keeps generating new content and new predictions, and sometimes gets vindicated in ways nobody expected — or degenerating, where it only ever grows by patching itself after the fact to save appearances. Supposedly you can't tell which one you're in while it's happening; the verdict only comes out afterwards, over a long historical run.

What I want from you is a translation job. Take that whole picture and restate it in the vocabularly I actually think in — series bible, canon versus continuity, retcons, foreshadowing and payoff, worldbuilding rules, the promise the first book makes to the reader, plot armour, the difference between a book that opens up new territory and a book that's only plugging leaks. Be faithful; don't just staple my words onto his concepts where they don't fit. Then give me the residue report, which is the part I actually care about: what came across cleanly, what I had to fudge, and what in his picture has no counterpart in mine at all. My suspicion is that everything to do with a world that can embarrass you — a world that can turn out to be otherwise than you wrote it, that can make your predictions wrong — falls into that last bucket, and I want to know whether that's my vocabulary missing something important or whether it means his distinction is doing less work than it looks like. Related and I'd like a straight answer rather than a both-sides one: if there's no fact of the matter about my world independent of what I write, does "progressive" just collapse into "still selling"? No reading lists or citations please, I'm not going to chase them down.
