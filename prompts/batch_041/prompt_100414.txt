I'm on the board of a small worker-owned bakery — 12 of us, we all own it and all work in it — and I'm trying to reverse-engineer what one of our pay-committee members is actually doing when she votes. Her name's Nadia, she's been on the committee for like six years, and she definately always gives these very confident, very principled yes/nos, and I can never quite say what the principle is. Her whole shtick is that whenever we're deciding something she asks the room "would you still vote for this if you didn't know which of us you'd end up being?", which is charming but not exactly a decision procedure.

Here's her track record, as best I remember it.

1. We voted between a flat rate (everyone $700 a week) and a tiered one where our three most senior bakers got $1,000 and the other nine got $750. She voted for the tiered one.

2. Then there was a choice between (a) bumping the three seniors to $1,500 and leaving everyone else at $750, where the extra was a retention bonus to keep them from walking, and (b) bumping the other nine to $800 and leaving the seniors at $1,000. She voted for (b).

3. Last spring there was a deal that would have lifted our lowest-paid members from $750 to $780, but the ingredient supplier would have had to cut its pickers' pay to make the price work — they'd have gone from about $400 to about $350. We're not their employer, there was nothing we could do about it. She voted no, which really surprised me, because it made our worst-paid people better off.

Now the new proposal on the table. A retailer wants a long-term contract with us. Taking it would move our lowest-paid members from $750 to $780 a week. It doesnt hurt anybody else, there's no underpaid supplier involved, and as far as we can tell it doesn't shortchange any future members either. The catch is the retailer wants the three top spots — the higher-paid ones — permanently reserved for the current senior bakers. Nobody hired later, and no newer member, could ever move into them.

I'd like you to take the role of a political philosopher here and actually work this out rather than give me a vibe. Brainstorm the candidate rules first — there should be several that fit votes 1 through 3 — then narrow down which ones survive, and only then commit. Concretely: how does Nadia vote on the new proposal, and what's the rule she's really running? For the rules that survive her past votes, say what each predicts here and where they come apart. Give me your best guess and your reasons, and then tell me one further proposal I could actually bring to the committee that would most cleanly separate the rule you're betting on from its main rival.
