I'm in a decision-theory seminar and we just wrapped the superrationality unit — Hofstadter's Scientific American columns, the ones he later collected in that big anthology. The episode I can't stop thinking about is the lottery he actually ran on his readers. As I understand the setup: he announced a cash prize, I want to say a million dollars, and invited readers to mail him a postcard stating how many tickets they wanted in a draw for it. The catch was that the prize would be divided by the total number of tickets everyone requested, so if the readership collectively asked for N tickets, whoever's ticket came up collected the prize divided by N. One ticket total and you're rich; a few hundred thousand tickets and the winner gets pocket change. My professor says the results were a rout for the cynics, but I dont remember the actual totals and honestly I don't think they matter much for what I want to ask.

What I want is an analysis of that episode through superrationality, running in both directions. Start by being careful about the game, because I suspect it isn't the same shape as the prisoner's dilemma the theory was built on. In the PD the choice is binary and "everyone does the same thing" pins down exactly two candidate profiles, so you compare two numbers and you're done. Here the choice variable is a number you can make as large as you like, and the symmetric profile "everyone requests k" exists for every k, so the maximization step that made superrationality look like a theorem doesn't obviously select anything. Work out what the theory actually prescribes — I think the answer is request the smallest number allowed, but I want to see the derivation, and I want you to say whether the symmetric-play assumption is doing any work there or whether the same answer just falls out of plain joint-payoff maximization, and if it does, what's left of the distinctively superrational move.

Then the other direction: does the episode confirm the theory, embarrass it, or fall outside its jurisdiction? My suspicion is the last one, for a reason I can't quite articulate. Superrationality needs the agents to know they're symmetric and facing the same problem; the public announcement gives everyone common knowledge of the problem but not of each other's reasoning, and a readership mailing in postcards is not a set of identical agents anyway — some people were surely writing in to make a point, some to be cute, some to win. So tell me whether the experiment could have tested superrationality even in principle, and if not, what a real test would need — precommitment, no communication, identical choice sets, a known number of participants — and whether those requirements can all be satisfied at once, since common knowledge seems to require publicity while no-communication seems to forbid it. If the experiment can't test the theory, say so plainly and don't rescue it. And finish by comparing it briefly to the Platonia-type case we did in class, where a prize goes to whoever claims it alone and vanishes if two people claim it: is the difference just continuous versus discrete choice, or does the binary case have a determinate superrational answer for a reason the lottery lacks?
