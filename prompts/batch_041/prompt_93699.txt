Setup: I'm in Damascus. I bump into a figure who, as far as anyone can tell, always turns up wherever a person is the next day. He's been right every time anyone has bothered to check — nobody thinks he's literally infallible, but he's good enough that I'd bet my life on him. He clocked me and looked slightly taken aback, like he'd expected me somewhere else, and said he'd be seeing me tomorrow. So: wherever I end up tomorrow, that's where he'll be.

My friend's argument:

1. If I stay in Damascus, he'll be in Damascus tomorrow; if I leave for Aleppo, he'll be in Aleppo.
2. Either I stay or I leave.
3. So either way he's there. Staying, I die; leaving, I die.
4. So I die either way.
5. So my choice makes no difference to whether I die.
6. A choice that makes no difference to the outcome gives me no reason to prefer one option over the other.
7. So I have no reason to run rather than stay, and since I'm indifferent I might as well stay put.
8. And nothing in this turned on him being anything special. All I used was that where I'll be tomorrow is already settled: he knows it now, so it must already be true, so it must already be fixed.
9. But every one of my decisions is like that. By the time I'm deliberating, what I'll decide is already true of the future, and on the deterministic picture already settled by the distant past.
10. So no choice of mine ever makes a difference to anything, and deliberation is a pointless ritual.

I'm fine with 1 through 4, honestly — he's never wrong. But 8 through 10 can't be right, because if deliberation is pointless then so is the argument I just deliberated my way through. Where's the break? Give me the step to reject, why it looked airtight, and what I have to pay to reject it. If it's a choice between keeping 1-4 and keeping 10, I'd rather keep 1-4.
