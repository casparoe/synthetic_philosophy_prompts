I'm a retired sedimentologist — forty-odd years of poking at cores — and since I packed it in I've been doing an evening philosophy degree for fun, and I've gotten a bit stuck on something that keeps splitting the seminar room: when a community's memory of what happened and the documented record disagree, which one is meant to give way? Most of the philosophers around the table treat memory as a bad photocopy of history, a degraded copy of the real thing, but the longer I sit with it the less that sits right, and the more I notice the record is its own kind of remembering — selective in its own way, and doing work for the people who keep it.

So the reason I'm writing. I'd like a poem, and I've got a form in mind: a pantoum. In case you don't know it — four-line stanzas, and lines 2 and 4 of each stanza come back as lines 1 and 3 of the next one, and the last stanza folds back into the first, so the whole thing is a loop that never quite gets out. I want that loop to be doing the work, not just decorating it.

The setup: a small fishing town somewhere on a coast remembers a disaster — the night the sea took the church — as one night, sudden, and as a sort of judgement on them, and the town's year still turns on that date. The cores and the old tide logs say something much slower: thirty-odd years of the ground giving way underneath, no single night at all. Two tellings that can't both be the whole truth, and each one is doing something for the people holding it.

What I want from the poem: alternate the voices — one line the town's memory, the next the record, or one stanza each, your call — so the pantoum's returning lines make each version keep coming back and reasserting itself, over and over, the way they actually do in a place like that. And I want the swap at the end to mean something: by the closing stanza the lines should have traded places, so the record is now saying what the memory said and the memory is saying what the record said, and the reader feels that neither can be told without dragging the other along behind it. Don't resolve it — no tidy moral about who wins, because I genuinely don't think there is one, and the poem should sit in that discomfort rather than climb out of it. Five or six stanzas is plenty. It should be accurate enough that someone who works on this stuff would nod along, and still be a good poem for someone who's never thought about it.

No need for notes or sources or anything, I'm not citing it anywhere, its just for the reading group.
