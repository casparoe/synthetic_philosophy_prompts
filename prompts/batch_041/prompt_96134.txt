Two months ago I became the person at my organization who has to decide whether our field partners' customary payments to municipal officials count as corruption. The payments are made openly, are expected by the officials who receive them, and are described by everyone directly involved as gifts rather than bribes; our funders describe the same payments as bribes rather than gifts. What has unsettled me is that my colleagues and I do not disagree about the facts — we have the same facts — but about something conceptual underneath them, and I would rather have that disagreement made explicit than continue trading intuitions across it.

I would like your credence in a single proposition, given as a number between 0 and 1, together with the reasoning that produces it. The proposition is this: there exists a single, culture-independent criterion that correctly sorts transfers to public officials into corrupt and permissible, such that the same criterion returns the correct verdict both in a liberal democracy and in a society where personal-obligation networks are the ordinary medium of public administration. I am asking whether such a criterion exists, not whether anyone has stated it well; if you think the proposition needs tightening before it can have a truth value, tighten it and say how.

Please do not give me a bare number. Decompose it. Identify the sub-questions your credence actually depends on, assign each a rough probability with one line of justification, say how the terms combine into the final figure, and then tell me which term the number is most sensitive to — the one that, moved by twenty points, would move the total most. I expect the decomposition to touch on at least the following, though you should add, merge, or replace terms as you think correct:

- whether there are culture-independent standards for evaluating conduct in public roles at all, as opposed to standards that are only ever internal to a political community;
- whether corruption names a functional kind — the abuse of entrusted authority for private advantage — that recurs across societies, or a category whose boundaries are drawn by the society doing the classifying;
- whether the gift-giving and clientelist practices in question are a genuinely different functional kind (reciprocity, insurance, network maintenance) or the same abuse under a different name;
- whether the public/private distinction that most anti-corruption criteria presuppose is a parochial feature of modern bureaucratic states rather than a universal one;
- whether a criterion that is theoretically correct would also be usable by an outsider with limited access to local intentions and norms — that is, whether correctness and decidability come apart here;
- whether a universal criterion could be applied without becoming an instrument of domination, and whether that possibility should count against the proposition's truth or only against its use.

To make this concrete rather than abstract: I have been working with a candidate criterion, which is that a transfer to an official is corrupt just in case a fully informed and impartial observer who endorsed the office-holder's mandate would have forbidden it in advance, where impartiality ranges over all affected persons and not merely over the local political community. I would like you to say whether the interesting sub-question is whether that criterion is extensionally adequate, or whether it is even coherent for a society in which office and kinship obligation are not separable in the first place. Use it as a test case for your decomposition rather than as a definition to accept.

Finally, I am at about 0.3 on the proposition and a colleague who has worked in the region for twenty years is at about 0.8. Given both numbers, tell me where our disagreement most plausibly lives — which sub-question we are really divided on, whether we might instead be using the word corruption in two different senses without noticing, and which single piece of evidence or argument, if it arrived, would move your own number the most.
