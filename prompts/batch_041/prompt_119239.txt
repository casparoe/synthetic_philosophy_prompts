I have been reading a chapter on dispositions in a recent popular book about metaphysics — the kind of book that walks a general reader briskly through a live debate, confident and short on hedges. I know the rough shape of the territory from a seminar a few years ago, but not well enough to tell where the author is cutting corners, and I have no way to look anything up at the moment. I would like you to audit his argument for me. Here is the chapter as I have reconstructed it, close to his own words where I could manage it:

"The standard account of what it is to have a disposition is the conditional analysis: for a glass to be fragile is just for it to be the case that it would shatter if struck, for sugar to be soluble is just for it to be the case that it would dissolve if stirred into water, and so on. This was the received view for most of the last century, and it is now dead. The counterexamples come in two families, which I will call finks and masks, using the two terms interchangeably, since they make exactly the same point.

In the first family, the object has the disposition, but the stimulus condition, when it arrives, takes the disposition away before it can do any work. Picture a live wire wired to a device that severs the connection the instant anything touches the wire: the wire is live, it is disposed to conduct when touched, and yet if you touch it, it will not conduct. In the second family, the object has no such disposition at all, but something else brings about the very manifestation the disposition would have produced. A sugar cube that is not soluble at all will still dissolve when dropped into water that has been dosed with acid.

Both families show the same thing, namely that there is no counterfactual conditional that is true exactly when the disposition is present. Whatever refinement you offer — 'it would manifest if the object retained its causal basis', 'it would manifest unless something interferes' — I can construct a case in which disposition and conditional come apart again. Consider the double fink: a sorcerer who hardens a glass whenever a hammer is raised has himself been cursed to fall asleep whenever a hammer is raised. Nest finks inside finks and you have solved nothing; you have only postponed the difficulty, and there is no principled place to stop. The regress shows that the conditional analysis cannot be repaired from within.

What is left, then, is this. Dispositions cannot be cashed out in non-dispositional terms at all: when we call a glass fragile we are not describing any arrangement of its parts, and not even describing what it would do, but only marking it, from our point of view, as the sort of thing that behaves in a certain way. The general lesson is that everything is dispositional all the way down — there is no bedrock of purely categorical fact for the dispositions to rest on."

I am inclined to think he is basically right, at least about the conditional analysis, and I find the closing picture appealing, which is exactly why I want it checked. What I would like is an audit organised by claim rather than by paragraph: for each of the moves he makes, tell me what someone who works on dispositions would accept as stated, what they would accept only with a qualification (and what the qualification is), and what is simply wrong or misleading. I am especially interested in three things. First, whether the two families of counterexample really do make exactly the same point, whether the two labels he uses interchangeably are interchangeable, and whether his taxonomy leaves anything out — I have a nagging sense that the standard vocabulary here carves the cases more finely than he does, though I may be misremembering. Second, whether his double fink and the regress argument built on it do the work he claims: I want to know whether that example is a counterexample to anything, and whether the argument that any refinement can be undermined by a further stipulation establishes what he thinks it establishes. Third, whether the concluding metaphysical claim follows from any part of the argument that survives scrutiny, and whether the bottom line can be restated in a weaker form that is defensible.

Two practical notes. I do not need citations, a reading list, or names and dates — a bibliography would be useless to me right now, so keep to the substance and, where you think a point is genuinely contested among specialists, just say so in plain terms. And please do not be diplomatic: if the chapter's argument is a case of a true premise being asked to carry a conclusion it cannot bear, say that directly, and tell me which of his claims are load-bearing for the conclusion and which are decoration. Around eight hundred words, please.
