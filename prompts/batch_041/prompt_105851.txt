As a software engineer with no formal training in philosophy, I have been trying to get straight on the debate over whether calling something a disease is a matter of biological fact or of social evaluation: the naturalist idea that disease is subnormal biological functioning measured against some reference class of the species, versus the normativist idea that a condition is a disease only relative to values about what is undesirable or disabling. I would like a piece of satire that makes the two collide. Please write a terms-of-service agreement, roughly 600 words, formatted as numbered clauses, for a fictional subscription service that employers and insurers use to adjudicate what counts as a covered illness. The company should advertise a purely objective, value-free definition of disease while its actual clauses quietly encode contested choices: which reference class fixes normal function; whether a dysfunction must also be judged harmful before it counts; how the service treats a condition that is a disorder in one setting but a variation in another; what happens to a subscriber when the service reclassifies their condition; and an appeals clause that ultimately turns on who gets to decide. Keep the prose deadpan and bureaucratic, so the absurdity comes from the logic of the clauses rather than from jokes, and make the satire legible to someone who knows the naturalist-normativist literature without ever naming it. Finish with a single-sentence bottom line of no more than 25 words stating which side, if either, the service has actually committed itself to.
