Next month I'm giving a 40-minute talk at a small workshop on bounded rationality, and I'm basically the token decision theorist in the room. The crowd is mostly experimental and behavioral economists who spend their lives documenting preference reversals, framing effects and intransitive choice in the lab, plus two or three people from the ecological rationality / heuristics side who think the whole expected-utility apparatus is a fantasy imposed on agents who are actually doing something smarter, plus one Bayesian statistician who will want every step made formal. I'm defending a version of the coherence argument for expected utility maximization, but with two twists that I expect will be the entire fight. First, I drop completeness entirely -- I'm happy to say their are genuinely incomparable options and that my argument is simply silent about what you should do with them. Second, I run the argument on choice dispositions rather than on preferences. The constraint I defend is menu-consistency: if you'd pick A from {A,B} and B from {B,C}, you should be willing to pick A from {A,B,C}; if you won't, there's a finite sequence of menu offers that leaves you worse off by your own lights, with no change in your tastes, no new information, and no factual mistake on your part. What I claim to get out of that is a prohibition on a certain class of cyclic choice patterns plus a partial representation result -- not full expected utility maximization, and I say so out loud, repeatedly. (My flatmate, who does experimental econ, thinks the whole thing is a bait and switch, which is definately a preview.)

What I want from you is a Q&A prep sheet for that specific room: the eight or ten objections these people will actually raise -- not the ones I'd get in a decision theory seminar, and not the ones I'd get from the LessWrong crowd -- ordered by how likely each is times how much damage it does to me, each one phrased the way they'd phrase it, with a two-minute reply and an honest note on where the reply is soft, where I'm going to have to bite a bullet, and which objection I should just concede outright. Tell me what to give away in the first five minutes so the rest of the hour isn't about that, and which single objection has to be defused for the talk to count as having landed at all. Also tell me whether the menu-consistency move actually insulates me from the standard "the pump only works if you keep changing your mind" line, or whether it just relocates the problem, because I genuinely don't know how that goes down with this audience. And give me the question I'm hoping nobody asks -- the one aimed at the weakest joint in the whole thing -- plus what to say if it comes. No reading list, no citations, I've read the stuff; what I need is the actual question period.
