Bit of an odd request, I know. I'm a freelance journalist — I write about science and ideas for a couple of mid-sized magazines and a newsletter — and I'm about 2,000 words into a piece on whether cheap computation settles the old socialist calculation debate. Everyone I've interviewed is either bored of it or furious about it, and I've ended up with an argument from one of my sources that I've writen up and now can't see my way out of. It goes roughly like this, in his order:

(1) To allocate resources rationally you have to compare the importance of different uses of them against each other, and you can only do that with a common unit that expresses what's being given up — a price.
(2) Prices are only real prices when they come out of exchange between people who actually own the things being traded, because only then is something genuinely given up by the person who gives it up.
(3) Under socialism the means of production belong to everyone and no one, so capital goods are never exchanged between owners.
(4) So there are no prices for capital goods under socialism, only accounting figures that somebody has made up.
(5) So planners cannot know whether building the railway rather than the steelworks is the better use of the same steel, labour and land. They can have preferences, but they can't have a rational allocation.
(6) The obvious reply is Lange's: the planning board announces prices and then nudges them up when things are scarce and down when they pile up, exactly like an auctioneer calling out bids until supply matches demand. But my source says that just moves the problem back a step. To nudge a price up you already have to know there's a shortage, and to know that you already have to know what people wanted and what it was possible to produce — and that knowledge isn't lying around anywhere waiting to be collected. It's dispersed, it's mostly tacit, it's different in every workshop and warehouse, and it only comes into existence when people act on it.
(7) Which means the problem isn't that the planning board has too little computing power. Even a perfect computer can't do the sums, because the numbers don't exist for anyone to feed into it.
(8) So planning is impossible in principle, not just in practice. And that holds however cheap computation gets.

The bit that nags at me is that I don't think the market passes test (7) either. Nobody in a market holds that knowledge in a single head — that's meant to be the whole point of the price system, that it lets you act on information you don't have. So if (7) is enough to kill the planning board, it seems to kill the thing my source wants to defend as well. When I put this to him he said markets aren't an alternative to using dispersed knowledge, they're a discovery procedure, and a discovery procedure is a seperate kind of thing from a board setting prices. That felt a bit like having it both ways, but I couldn't say why.

So: which step do I cut, and what does cutting it cost me? I'd rather keep (5) than (2) if it comes to that, because (5) is where the piece actually bites, but tell me what I'd be paying. And I'd like to know where you're least sure — what consideration or argument would make you give a different answer, and which part of your own reasoning is most likely to be the weak link. Please write in British English.
