I sit on the ethics advisory board of the health insurer that covers my father, who manages a chronic condition. Last month I learned that his medication reminders are generated by a program the board is now asked to approve. The program, called the Adherence Support Initiative, combines pharmacy refill records, wearable step and sleep data, geolocation, and a sentiment score derived from call-center transcripts to identify moments when a member is especially likely to skip a dose. At those moments it sends a message framed as a question from the member's own care team, sometimes with a small reward for refilling. The message is not labeled as algorithmic, and the design team refers to the targeted moments as windows of least resistance. The insurer says the program raises adherence, reduces hospitalizations, and costs less than nurse outreach; it notes that members consented in the enrollment packet and can opt out at any time. The insurer's behavioral science lead adds that the program only changes the timing of a message the member would have received anyway. A colleague on the board argues that this is straightforward manipulation: it exploits fatigue, loneliness, or confusion rather than offering reasons, and its effectiveness may depend on members not realizing how the message was timed. Another colleague replies that all persuasive communication is designed to move people, that the program serves the member's own health goals, and that refusing to use available data would be paternalistic in the opposite direction.

Please draft the board's written opinion, which I will circulate before we meet next week. It should recommend a concrete course of action with a time frame -- approve, reject, or approve with conditions -- and give reasons the insurer's clinical and data teams could actually respond to. The memo should distinguish manipulation from legitimate persuasion in a way that applies to this program rather than to persuasion in general, and it should say whether the program's reliance on inferred vulnerability and unlabeled algorithmic authorship is decisive. It should also address whether a message can be manipulative when the member would endorse the outcome on reflection, and whether the opt-out provision and enrollment consent settle the ethical question. It should engage the strongest version of the argument on the losing side, including the claim that the program's health benefits and the timing-only defense justify approval. If conditions are appropriate, specify what must change before the next enrollment cycle and how the board should verify compliance. I have disclosed my father's enrollment and will recuse myself from the vote, but the chair has asked me to draft the opinion because I know the program's mechanics.
