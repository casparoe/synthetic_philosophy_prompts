I'm running a reading group for ML engineers who've drifted into safety work and haven't read much moral philosophy, and I want a one-page decision tree they can use to work out where they actually stand on "how do you put ethics into a machine?". Build it from the logical space rather than from a list of isms, and phrase every node as a question they could answer without already knowing where it leads. I'd like the first split to be about where the ethics is supposed to live: in what the system computes at inference time, in the distribution it was trained on, or outside the system altogether (in the deployer, the auditor, the regulator) — I keep finding that people who think they're disagreeing about rules vs consequences are really disagreeing about that. Then split on whether the system is being treated as a moral agent or as a tool, on how its meant to handle moral uncertainty (commit to one theory, hedge across several, defer to a human), on whether the position is about the system's decisions or about the process that produced them (a system can look fine and still be doing the wrong thing — post-hoc rationalisation, reward hacking), and on whether anything changes if the system is itself a candidate moral patient. Leaves should name positions where there are names to use — hard-coded constraints, consequentialist reward specification, imitation or human-feedback learning, character and disposition training, the hedge-across-theories ensemble, the "it's just a tool" line, ethics-as-external-audit — and "unnamed" where nobody actually defends the combination, with one sentence on the commitment that leaf would have the hardest time defending. No reading list obviously, it's a handout. Tell me which leaves the tree can't separate, and for the empty combinations say whether they're empty because the view is incoherent, unattractive, or just unexplored.
