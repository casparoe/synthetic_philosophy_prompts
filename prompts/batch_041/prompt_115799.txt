I host a social-science interview podcast, and next week's guest defends a deliberately uncomfortable reading of the so-called gender-equality paradox. She accepts that across countries the sex difference in measured occupational interests widens as material gender equality rises, but denies that this is nature revealing itself once constraints lift. Her claim is that once subsistence pressure stops funneling everyone toward similar work, occupational choice becomes a kind of identity consumption, and gendered self-signaling is among the cheapest identity goods on offer, so greater affluence and formal equality can widen expressed divergence in interests even if the underlying tastes are entirely socially constructed. She pairs this with a normative claim: justice turns on whether the sorting is produced by constraints, not on whether the differences are innate.

Write eight to ten interview questions that press the position instead of letting her restate it. Include at least one question each on (a) how the identity-consumption mechanism could be distinguished empirically from a plain preference-expression story, given that both predict the same cross-national correlation; (b) why the correlation is sharp in interest inventories and personality measures but much murkier in actual labor-force segregation, and what her mechanism implies about that gap; (c) which confounds she thinks survive scrutiny, including the country sample, the composition of the equality indices, and the possibility that the interest instruments are themselves culturally calibrated; and (d) how she reconciles the claim that justice concerns constraints rather than differences with the thought that inegalitarian sorting may itself be evidence of constraints. For each question, add a follow-up keyed to the answer she is most likely to give.
