The question she set was: "Q2 (20 points). Nozick's tracking analysis of knowledge (Philosophical Explanations) replaces the justification condition of the traditional analysis with two counterfactual conditions. (a) State the conditions of the analysis (4 points). (b) Explain how the analysis bears on the principle that knowledge is closed under known entailment, using the brain-in-a-vat scenario as your illustration (8 points). (c) Is the failure of closure a cost or a virtue of the theory? Take a side and defend it (8 points)."

And here's what the student handed in, typed as-is: "(a) Nozick says that S knows that p just in case four things are true: (1) p is true; (2) S believes that p; (3) if p were false, S would not believe that p (sensitivity); (4) if p were true, S would believe that p (adherence). The idea is that knowledge is a matter of the belief covarying with the fact — it's there because the fact is there, and absent when the fact isn't. What's gone is justification: Nozick doesn't ask whether S can cite reasons for p, or whether S's evidence makes p probable, only what S would have believed in nearby worlds. That's why the theory is externalist, and it's the main reason it looks like an improvement on justified true belief, since Gettier cases are precisely cases where a true belief is true by accident, and a belief that tracks isn't accidental. (b) Closure is the principle that if S knows p, and p entails q, then S knows q. Nozick's conditions don't transmit along entailment, so closure fails on his account. The standard illustration is the brain in a vat. I know I have hands, and that belief tracks: if I'd lost my hands in an accident I'd believe I'd lost them, and given that I have hands I do believe it. 'I have hands' entails 'I am not a handless brain in a vat', so if closure held I would know I'm not a BIV. But the tracking conditions aren't met for the anti-skeptical claim: if I were a BIV, I wouldn't believe that I wasn't one, so the belief isn't sensitive to the fact it's about. So Nozick says I know I have hands but I don't know that I'm not a BIV — what people sometimes call an abominable conjunction. The deeper point is that the two beliefs are held by different mechanisms: my hand-belief comes from perception, which is hooked up to the world, whereas the belief that rules out the vat comes from the same inner evidence that would be there either way. (c) I think the failure of closure is a virtue rather than a cost. The alternative is Moore's move — keep closure, and conclude that since I know I have hands I also know I'm not a BIV — which seems like exactly the kind of thing that makes philosophy look silly to outsiders. Nozick lets us keep the ordinary claim and drop the weird one, and it's principled rather than ad hoc, because what the tracking conditions really test is whether the alternative is a relevant one, i.e. whether it's among the possibilities that are live in the context of the inquiry. The vat isn't a live possibility in ordinary contexts, so the belief doesn't have to discriminate against it, and that's why closure has to go. Anyone who wants closure back has to explain why we should be able to rule out every possibility, including ones we couldn't in principle distinguish ourselves from, which seems like a much bigger cost than dropping the principle."
