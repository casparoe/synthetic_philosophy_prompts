Last night I lost a long argument with a friend who defends the old covering-law picture — that history explains by subsuming events under general laws, laws that historical writing usually leaves tacit. I came away defending something I can't yet state cleanly: that historical explanation is causal through and through, but the causal generalizations it leans on are not laws of human nature or of society; they are constraints on which trajectories a system can take, fixed by material, ecological, and demographic limits. On this view, explaining an outcome means locating it within the space of trajectories that were genuinely open, and accounting for why that space was as narrow or as wide as it was. Now I want the case against it. Which observations about how historians actually argue, and which distinctions or results in the philosophy of explanation, would count against this view — and what does it predict about historical practice that a purely narrative account does not? Which of its commitments is it most exposed on: that the explanations are causal, that the generalizations are constraints rather than laws, or that locating an outcome within the open space suffices to explain it? Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, separate the ones a defender would concede from the ones they would dismiss, and say honestly whether I have built a position that nothing could refute. My own guess is that the sufficiency claim is the weak joint, but I would rather you tell me where the real pressure lies.
