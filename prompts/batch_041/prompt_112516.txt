I teach a ninety-minute session in an upper-level undergraduate seminar on causation and moral responsibility, sixteen students, and I want an activity that makes them feel on their own pulses something they will otherwise only concede in argument: that our verdicts about what an omission caused track what people were required to do and which contrast we happen to be asking about, while the counterfactual facts are held fixed. By the end I want them to have caught themselves changing their answer twice with no new information about the world, to have watched a list of causes refuse to terminate, and to have felt the pull of the doing/allowing asymmetry before they are told it is contested. I would like the design to run in three rounds, and I have particular mechanics in mind; use them, improve them if you can, but do not replace them with a discussion or a lecture.

For the first round, an inquest, give me four duty profiles and assign each to four students, so that the room divides into four quartets. Every profile places its holder at the same lake on the same afternoon and supplies an identical physical timeline: the swimmer enters the water, the current strengthens, the swimmer goes under, the window of opportunity closes. The profiles differ only in what the holder was obligated to do, and one of them should be a profile with no obligation at all in the relevant jurisdiction but a crowd that believes otherwise. Each student privately writes, on a card, a single sentence of the form the death was caused by X rather than Y. Then redistribute the profiles at random so that nobody keeps the one they held, hand out fresh cards, and have them write again. Collect the first round before the swap so nobody can consult what they wrote. The scoring rule is that a card earns its writer a point only if, read aloud with nothing but the timeline and the stated contrast, a majority of the room would endorse it. The prize should be trivial and should not be money.

For the second round, a ledger. The group must list everything whose not happening was a condition of the death, with the rule that no two entries may be of the same type, and the round ends only when the group itself declares the list complete. Let it run about eight minutes and then stop it; do not rescue them. For the third round, hold the profile and the facts fixed and vary only the contrast, giving me six pre-written contrast sentences for the same case, some of which the room will accept and some it will reject, so that contrast-sensitivity becomes impossible to miss.

Constraints. No framing lecture before the first round. The words omission, counterfactual dependence, contrast, norm, and causal selection must not be spoken until the debrief. Nothing in the activity may require students to disclose their own moral views, and there must be a content note about drowning and a way for a student to stay in the room without playing.

Give me the rules, the materials list, the timeline, the four duty profiles, the six contrast sentences, the scoring rule, a minute-by-minute plan for the ninety minutes, a debrief that maps each round onto a position one could hold, a frank account of what will predictably go wrong the first time and how to handle it, and an honest note on which feature of the idea the activity cannot reproduce and that I will have to supply myself. The debrief should be conceptual and should not cite texts or gesture at a reading list, since the handout will circulate without a bibliography. Write the whole thing as continuous prose, with no headings, numbered lists, bullet points, bold or italic text, or tables; the way one would write a long letter to a colleague.
