I am a retired microbial physiologist — thirty-five years on the anaerobic side, mostly at the bench — who took up philosophy of biology after I handed back my keys, and I now sit on our institute's Advisory Committee on Novel Self-Replicating Systems. Because I am the only member who has been reading philosophy seriously, the chair has asked me to draft the written opinion that will go into the file. I should like your help in drafting it.

The case. A group in our chemical biology division has built what they call a protocell, and they want it reclassified. In outline: fatty acids in the medium (oleic acid with a little decanol) spontaneously assemble into vesicles; inside each vesicle sits a 190-nucleotide ribozyme that copies itself and copies nothing else. Nucleoside triphosphates, fatty-acid precursors and buffer are all supplied by the experimenter. The system cannot make its own monomers, cannot hold its own pH or redox gradients, and does nothing we would ordinarily call metabolism beyond polymerisation. What it can do is evolve: copying is error-prone, faster-replicating variants take over, and across some three hundred serial transfers the group has documented a lineage, including a variant that lost a stem-loop and now replicates about forty per cent faster than its ancestor. The vesicles themselves are not inherited — they form de novo from the medium, and their contents are split stochastically when the suspension is sheared — so it is not obvious whether the individual being selected is the vesicle or the RNA lineage inside it. The group has also found that the ribozyme survives in spent medium at four degrees for at least six weeks, in residual vesicles, and resumes replication when fresh monomers are added. Our treatment plant discharges into a phosphate-rich river. The safety officer's position is not that the construct would flourish there; it is that we cannot say that it could not.

The two classifications on offer. If the construct is a living organism, it falls under containment level 2, all waste is autoclaved, bench work stays in a cabinet, and we report annually to the national authority. If it is a chemical reagent, it leaves with the rest of the chemistry waste and no one outside this building hears of it again. The group wants the second and argues that a replicating molecule is not an organism. Our charter, drafted for planetary protection in the 1990s and inherited by us unexamined, defines what it governs in two clauses: a chemical system that maintains itself far from equilibrium, and a chemical system capable of Darwinian evolution. It does not say whether both are required or either suffices, and this construct is a clean case of the two clauses pointing in opposite directions.

What I need. A memo I can circulate before we meet on 12 June, recommending a decision the committee can act on and attaching a timetable to it. It must give reasons rather than a verdict by fiat, and it must state the opposing case at its strongest: the safety officer will be in the room, and I would rather she recognise her own argument in my memo than find a straw man there. It should also leave us a test we can apply to the next case, because there will be one — the same question is already waiting on a prion-like agent in the animal house and on a self-propagating mineral system in geochemistry, and we have deferred both once already.

Two further things. First, please argue from the facts above and from first principles, not from the literature on the definition of life; I cannot check references, neither can you, and a bibliography would be of no use to a committee that has to decide at that meeting. Second, I have a suspicion I should like you either to confirm or to kill. I suspect that "is it alive?" is the wrong question to put to this committee, and that what we actually need to decide is which purpose of the containment rule the case falls under — the rule exists, so far as I can see, to catch replicators that could persist and spread beyond containment, and this one replicates and persists, which settles the matter whatever we say about life. If I am right, write the memo on that footing and say so at the outset. If I am wrong, or if the move I have just made is a dodge that merely relabels a metaphysical question as a prudential one, tell me plainly rather than humouring a retired chemist who has read too much philosophy in four years. Where your reasoning depends on a genuinely contested reading — whether Darwinian evolution requires heritable individuals rather than merely heritable molecules, say — mark it as contested, give me your own leaning and your confidence in it, and tell me what argument or evidence would move you.

Format. Please use these headings, in this order, at roughly these lengths:

- Recommendation — no more than 150 words; the decision, the reasoning in miniature, and the date by which the committee should act.
- The case before us — about 250 words; the facts and the two classifications, set out so that a member who has read nothing else can follow the rest.
- Where the charter's two clauses pull apart — about 500 words; the conceptual work, including whether the construct satisfies each clause and what "self-sustaining" and "Darwinian" ought to mean here.
- The strongest case against the recommendation — about 300 words; the best version of the losing side, including the version of it that does not depend on any view about the nature of life.
- Confidence, and what would change my mind — about 150 words.
- Decision and timetable — about 200 words; the motion to put, the conditions to attach, and the dates.

British English throughout, please.
