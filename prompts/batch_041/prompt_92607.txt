A geologist friend and I are putting together a museum exhibit on the Anthropocene, and she wants its start dated to the mid-twentieth century on the strength of an analogy. The end of the Cretaceous is her model of a well-made boundary: an impact leaves a thin worldwide layer of iridium that anyone can find in the rock and date, and above it the fossils change. The industrial age, she says, has left the same kind of layer — fallout from atmospheric bomb tests, plastics, fly ash — so the Anthropocene is another boundary of that kind. She takes the analogy to settle two things: that its start is a discovery rather than a decision, and that the bodies which ratify the geological time scale would merely be recording what the rock already says. Anyone who calls the epoch's beginning a political choice is, on this view, confusing a technical question with a rhetorical one.

Audit the analogy rather than the dates. Set out the correspondence explicitly — what plays the role of the impact, the iridium, the fossil turnover, the spike driven into the outcrop that fixes the official marker — then say where the mapping breaks and which features of the impact story she is quietly importing into the Anthropocene. Separate the disanalogies that matter to her conclusions from those that are merely picturesque; ask in particular whether choosing a start date is the same kind of act as locating a boundary in a core sample, and what would have to be true for the second to settle the first. Then say whether her conclusions survive the breakdown, and whether some other transition in the history of the Earth models what we are in the middle of better than an impact does.
