My friend who works in ML (not on safety) gave me his one-paragraph take on the orthogonality thesis and instrumental convergence, and I'd like to know how much of it holds up. Here it is: 'The orthogonality thesis says intelligence and morality are orthogonal: how smart a system is tells you nothing about whether it'll be good or evil, because intelligence is just the ability to get what you want, and what you want is a separate question. Instrumental convergence is the follow-up — as systems get smarter, they all converge on the same goals whatever their ultimate goal is: surviving, acquiring resources, protecting their goal from being edited, making themselves smarter. So a superintelligence will be a single-minded power-accumulator that resists shutdown, and since we dont know how to align them, the default outcome is that we're toast. Bottom line, making AI smarter doesn't make it safer, it makes it more dangerous, because capability without morality is just more efficient destruction.' He's smart and alot of this sounds like stuff I've read, so I'm inclined to believe the first parts, but the last couple of sentences feel off to me. Could you go through it sentence by sentence and tell me what someone who actually works on this would accept, what they'd wince at, and what's flatly wrong? Things I specifically want sorted out: is orthogonality about intelligence and morality, or about intelligence and final goals, and is it a claim about what's possible or about what's statistically likely in practice? Does instrumental convergence say agents share terminal goals or instrumental ones, and is it supposed to hold for every agent or only under conditions — does it need the agent to be a consequentialist optimizer with roughly true beliefs about how its actions bear on its goal? Does it still bite for an agent whose goal is satiable, or best achieved right now, or that's been trained to defer to humans? And does 'we're toast' follow from the parts that are true, or does it need extra premises about capabilities and deployment that the two theses don't supply? One commenter said both theses are trivially true — orthogonality because 'goal' just means whatever the system optimizes, convergence because of course rational agents pursue means to their ends. Is that a fair deflation or does it miss what the theses are actually claiming? No citations needed, I just want to know where to land.
