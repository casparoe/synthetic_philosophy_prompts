For the past term I have been running a graduate seminar on modality and personal identity, and I have found that my own convictions about the necessity of origin refuse to settle into anything tidy once I press them against cases. I would like you to treat the verdicts below as data — they are mine, and I will defend them — and tell me what principle, if any, unifies them, how that principle has to be stated, and which of my verdicts it obliges me to surrender. I hold the following about myself. I could not have come from a different sperm or a different egg than the two cells that in fact fused to produce me. I could have come from those same two cells in a culture dish rather than in a fallopian tube, at a different hour, in a different country, gestated by a different woman, and raised by people who are not my parents at all, since the embryo could have been donated to strangers; all of that is still me. I could have come from those same two cells with a slightly different genome, since a mutation that in fact arose at the two-cell stage might not have, or a chromosome might have been lost from one blastomere rather than another, and I would then have been sicker or healthier, but not someone else. Yet I could not have come from a numerically different sperm carrying a genome indistinguishable from the actual one — something I gather can happen when a pair of homologues segregates without crossing over — and I find myself wanting to make this distinction even though I can give no reason for it that does not appeal to the sheer particularity of the cells. By the same token, a clone grown from one of my somatic cells, however genetically like me, is not me and is not a person I could have been.

What I cannot fit into that picture is a cluster of cases in which the gametes themselves are not well defined, or are not a pair. Take a tetragametic chimera: two zygotes fuse and a single person results, so that their tissues trace back to two sperm and two eggs, and there is no non-arbitrary answer to the question which of those four cells they came from. I want to say that such a person is one person, and that they could not have come from an entirely different set of gametes, but I cannot say which gametes are the essential ones. Take identical twins: one sperm, one egg, two people, so that sharing an origin plainly does not make for being the same person, and origin begins to look at best necessary and not sufficient. And take an organism that reproduces by fission — an amoeba or a bacterium dividing — where there is no sperm and no egg and the parent cell is the whole of the origin. My inclination is that neither daughter is the parent, but I hold that with far less confidence than I hold that the clone is not me, and I change my mind about it depending on whether I have been reading about identity across time or about origins. (I am a philosopher and not a biologist, so forgive any looseness in the biology.)

I have a rough principle of my own that I would like you to test and repair: that what is essential to me is the pair of particular gametes that fused, taken as historical individuals rather than as bearers of any particular genetic content, and that everything downstream of that fusion — genome, womb, parents, timing, even the number of persons the zygote subsequently becomes — is accidental. Say precisely what this principle delivers for each of the verdicts above; say whether it can accommodate the chimera, the twins, and the fission case, and if it cannot, whether a repair exists that does not simply restate my verdicts in general terms; and tell me honestly which of my verdicts it forces me to abandon. Then tell me what it predicts for one further case I have not listed — preferably one drawn from reproductive biology rather than from science fiction — and whether that prediction is one I should accept or treat as a reductio of the principle.
