I'm a PhD student in the philosophy of cognitive science, and I need a closing piece for a seminar session on Turing's 1950 paper on machine intelligence. Write me a pantoum of about five stanzas that takes the telepathy objection seriously. The formal idea is that a pantoum recycles whole lines, so let the interrogator's lines and the machine's lines get handed back and forth through the repeating structure, until by the final stanza the reader cannot cleanly tell which voice originated which line. That should enact the worry that if telepathy is a real channel, the imitation game cannot guarantee the separation of two minds that its verdict depends on. The turn should be Turing's proposed control — shielding the machine from telepathic influence — and the poem should hold both that this is a sensible experimental move and that it is a strange one, since it saves the test by adding a condition the test was never meant to need. Keep the diction plain, and make the philosophy accurate enough that someone who has read the paper recognizes the specific objection and the specific reply rather than the general topic. After the poem, add a short note on where it takes liberties with what Turing actually says — in particular how confident he really was about the evidence for telepathy, and whether the shielded room was offered as a practical proposal or as a concession that the game is only as clean as its physical setup — and say which alternative reading of that passage would have produced a materially different poem.
