I am assembling the guest interview for a constitutional theory podcast, and next week's guest is a legal scholar who defends weak-form judicial review of the Commonwealth type. His book's central claim is that such arrangements do not merely soften the countermajoritarian difficulty but dissolve it, by separating two things the standard debate conflates — whether the court has the last word on a particular statute, and whether the court has final authority over the constitutional question — and by recasting the judicial role as one of shifting a burden of justification onto the legislature rather than allocating final authority. I would like the interview to test that claim rather than elicit a restatement of it, so I will supply the institutional facts I want the questions to turn on.

The two models at issue are these. In the United Kingdom, courts cannot invalidate an Act of Parliament: the Human Rights Act lets the higher courts declare a provision incompatible with the Convention rights, but such a declaration leaves the provision valid, in force, and enforceable, does not bind even the parties to the case, and leaves it to Parliament to decide whether to amend. In Canada, courts may invalidate legislation under the Charter, but a legislature may respond with the notwithstanding clause — an express declaration in a statute that it is to operate notwithstanding the Charter's fundamental freedoms and its legal and equality rights. Such a declaration lapses after five years but can be re-enacted without limit, and it can be made pre-emptively, at the moment of enactment and before any litigation, as Quebec did in its 2019 secularism law.

Please draft eight to ten interview questions, ordered so that the guest's standing defences become progressively harder to deploy, each with a follow-up keyed to the answer he is most likely to give. I want at least one question on each of the following: (1) whether a declaration that leaves a statute fully operative can amount to more than an advisory opinion, and what dialogue can mean when the government's response to a finding is to pay damages and change nothing; (2) what the last-word/final-authority distinction is supposed to predict — what would be observably different in a jurisdiction where courts held final authority — and whether the distinction survives a legislature that answers a ruling by re-enacting its declaration every five years, or that immunises the statute from review in advance; (3) whether the model presupposes a legislature that is competent, responsive, and answerable to an electorate that cares about rights, and whether that makes the legitimacy argument circular, since democratic responsiveness is precisely what is in doubt in the cases where rights most need protection; and (4) whether the model requires a non-overridable core, given that the Canadian override cannot reach democratic rights, language rights, or the guarantee of equal rights for men and women — and if it does, whether that concession reinstates the countermajoritarian difficulty for exactly the rights that matter most.

Beyond those, I want a question on what empirical showing would vindicate the model's claim to greater legitimacy; a question on whether the override is a safety valve or an escape hatch, with a follow-up pressing the point that if he says its use should be rare, he has conceded that the model's value depends on its going unused; and a closing question on what evidence or argument would count against weak-form review rather than merely distinguishing it from strong-form review. No questions about how he came to the topic or what drew him to constitutional law.
