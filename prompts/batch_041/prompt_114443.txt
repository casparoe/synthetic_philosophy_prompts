I am marking the scripts for a second-year examination in a course on truth, meaning, and the epistemic theory of content. The question and one candidate's script are reproduced below. Please mark the script.

Question (30 marks)

The knowability principle states that every truth is knowable: for every proposition p, if p is true, then it is possible to know that p. Anti-realist and verificationist accounts of truth have typically been committed to it.

(a) Reconstruct the argument showing that the knowability principle entails that every truth is in fact known. Make explicit every logical and epistemic principle the argument relies on, and say which of them does the work. (12 marks)

(b) A familiar repair narrows the scope of the knowability principle, claiming it only for those propositions p for which it is not inconsistent to suppose that p is known. Does this repair block the argument? Is it independently motivated, or does it merely exclude the troublesome case? Does it leave the anti-realist with the thesis she wanted? (12 marks)

(c) Suppose instead that the anti-realist reads the possibility in the knowability principle as possibility for an inquirer at the ideal limit of inquiry. Does that reading rescue the principle, and does the paradox show that semantic anti-realism is false? (6 marks)

Candidate's answer

(a) Take a proposition p that is true but not known. The compound proposition that p is true and that nobody knows that p is then itself a truth. If every truth is knowable, this compound proposition must be knowable — it must be possible for someone to know it. But it cannot be known. Knowledge is factive: whatever is known is true. So anyone who knew that p and that nobody knows that p would know that p, and would also know that p is not known, which is a contradiction. The compound proposition is therefore not knowable. Since it is true whenever there is an unknown truth, the knowability principle fails unless there are no unknown truths at all. Contraposing: if every truth is knowable, every truth is known.

The principles used are the factivity of knowledge, the closure of knowledge under known implication (the argument has to get from knowledge of the conjunction to knowledge of each of its conjuncts), and the knowability principle itself, applied to the compound proposition. The derivation is intuitionistically valid apart from its final step, which turns the denial of the compound proposition into the conditional that if p then p is known; so an anti-realist who adopts intuitionistic logic does not escape by that route.

(b) The proposed repair restricts the knowability principle to propositions p such that it is not contradictory to suppose that p is known. This cannot do the work asked of it. The Fitch reasoning does not depend on any special feature of the compound proposition; it applies to any proposition whatever, since all it requires is a proposition that happens to be true and unacknowledged. Narrowing the principle's scope therefore changes nothing about the derivation. And if the restriction did work, it would work too well: the anti-realist wants to say that the truths we have not yet discovered are knowable, and those are just the truths the restriction would exclude. The repair also looks suspiciously well tailored to the single case that causes trouble, so I do not think it can be defended as independently motivated.

(c) On the ideal-limit reading, the derivation is unaffected: it needs only that knowledge is factive, and knowledge at the limit of inquiry is still knowledge. But the conclusion is no longer absurd on that reading, because the claim that every truth is known becomes the claim that every truth is believed at the limit of inquiry, which is precisely what the ideal-limit conception of truth asserts. So the paradox does not refute anti-realism. What it shows is that the moderate anti-realist cannot state her position as a thesis strictly weaker than the claim that all truths are known. That is an embarrassment rather than a refutation.
