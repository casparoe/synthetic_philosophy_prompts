At our town's annual fair this month I sat between two descendants of the families that founded the Harrow's Bend Volunteer Fire Company, and they spent most of the evening arguing about whether the Company still exists. Three years ago the county consolidated its fire services: the members voted to dissolve the Company's charter, the apparatus and roster were transferred to the county, and the volunteers now answer calls as County Station 4. The hall was deeded to a charitable trust controlled by the same families, and the annual fair — still billed under the old name — continues under that trust. The two of them agree on every one of those facts. Neither would move, and I have set down the exchange as fairly as I can.

A: The Company exists. Same hall, same families, same fair, same people answering the calls. What is gone is a piece of paper.

B: What is gone is the Company. A charter is not paper; it is what turned a crowd of neighbors into something that could own a truck, carry insurance, and make promises, and that thing was dissolved by its own members' vote. Name one thing the Company can do today that Station 4 cannot.

A: Put on the fair and hold the hall. The trust does both, and the trust is the families.

B: The trust is a separate entity that happens to have the same trustees. And the same families answering calls makes for a tradition; traditions outlive the bodies that carried them, which is exactly why the Company can be over while the fair goes on. On your view an institution can never be said to have ended, because some practice always continues.

A: Consider what your view needs. It says an institution's existence is settled by its own rules about ending. But an institution lives on recognition and practice, and the rules are part of the practice, not a verdict delivered from outside it. Notice, too, what we agree about: the vote, the transfer, the hall, the trust, the fair, the same volunteers. We agree on every fact and differ only over which of them deserves the name.

B: That difference is not small. Whether the Company exists decides whose names go on the plaque, who the trust is for, and whether the county inherited a promise or merely hired some people. And your test proves far too much: any two people who agree on the facts and differ over a description would be having a merely verbal dispute, which would make most disagreements in history, law, and art merely verbal. Disagreement about what to call a thing is often disagreement about what it is.

A: Then no fact settles it, and we are choosing which description to live with. You can draw the line at the rules, at the people, at the recognition, or at the money, and every one of those lines is defensible; nothing in the world marks one of them as correct. That is no scandal. "Exists" is not a property like "weighs forty tons," which we could go and check once everything else had been described; for something whose existence is constituted by our practices, the question is which practices we mean to keep.

B: Then the question has moved rather than vanished. If it is up to us, there is still a right and a wrong answer about what to do — whether the trust should be treated as the Company's heir, whether the county should be held to the old promise — and we can be right or wrong about that. And not every line is as good as every other: an institution is an agent that acts through its rules, so the rules that constituted it have a standing the fair and the plaque do not. Some existence claims here are simply true, such as Station 4's, and some false, such as the Company's since the vote; constituted by practice does not mean up to us.

I would like this adjudicated rather than summarized. Rate each of the four exchanges from clearly mistaken to clearly right, justifying each rating briefly, and say who is right on each specific claim — in particular, whether A's agreement-on-the-facts test shows the dispute to be about a word, whether B's charge that the test proves too much lands, and whether B is right that A's view cannot ever declare an institution ended. Then give an overall verdict on who has the better position, whether you would call the whole dispute verbal and what, if anything, would follow from that, and what both of them are missing. I am not on either side.
