I'm in a seminar on theories of history and I keep banging into the same wall. Every time we discuss some current crisis or conflict, someone says 'history repeats itself' like it's an arguement-ender — usually to imply there's nothing to be done, that this is just the cycle running its course, or that whoever's on top now will get knocked down like the last lot. My professor has a fancier version: he says history doesn't repeat itself, it rhymes, and he definately delivers it like it's a genuine upgrade on the dumb version. And last week a grad student said no, it's a spiral — same motion, but a level higher each time. I want to take something concrete to office hours. Can you lay out all the distinct precise claims 'history repeats itself' could actually be making, from the trivial to the genuinely radical — is it about time itself looping, about patterns recurring within ordinary time, about human nature being constant, about there being no cumulative progress, about us being able to learn lessons, about the pattern being something we impose as historians rather than something living in the events, or something else — and for each reading say whether it holds up and whether it actually delivers the fatalism people squeeze out of it? Same question for 'it rhymes' and for the spiral: do they add a real distinction or are they the same claim in a nicer coat? I'd especially like to know whether there's any reading under which the slogan doesn't imply we're helpless, since that's the one people seem to want. Put it in a list I can bring in. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence — which reading is really doing the work, and whether the helplessness conclusion survives — then the reasoning; no preamble, and don't restate my question.
