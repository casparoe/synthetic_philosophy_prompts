I'm writing a book where one of my characters is a very rich guy who's spent his whole life being told he's a parasite, and near the end he gives a big speech about why what he has is his. For research I read a newsletter by an econ writer I mostly trust, and it laid out Nozick's Wilt Chamberlain thing roughly like this:

Nozick's Chamberlain example is his proof that taxation is theft. You start with any distribution of goods you like — Nozick says it doesn't matter which, since he only cares about process, not about who ends up with what. Then a million people each voluntarily hand Chamberlain a quarter to watch him play, and he ends up with a quarter of a million dollars, which blows up whatever pattern you started with. From this Nozick concludes that Chamberlain deserves the money, that taking any of it back is exactly as bad as enslaving him, and that because no patterned theory of justice can survive a single voluntary transaction, Rawls's difference principle is dead on arrival. Nozick does concede that the question of how the original goods got acquired is a side issue, though he admits rectification is a loose end he never ties up.

How much of that is right? Go sentence by sentence and tell me what an actual entitlement theorist would nod along to, what would make him wince, and what he'd flat out deny. Then tell me whether the conclusion — Rawls refuted, therefore my guy's fortune is fine — follows from whatever is left standing after the corrections, and what the strongest objection to Nozick is that has nothing to do with the newsletter's mistakes, because my character needs an answer to that one too. Don't send me to a reading list, I just want the argument.
