I am a software engineer with no formal training in philosophy, and for several months I have been running a small online reading group on the ethics of belief. Two of the regulars have been arguing past each other, and I want to force the disagreement into a form that can actually be inspected.

One is a Cliffordian. She holds that it is wrong, always and everywhere and for everyone, to believe anything on insufficient evidence, and that this holds even when the question is momentous and cannot be avoided; where the evidence is inconclusive, the honest course is to withhold belief and act only on what is supported. She takes the shipowner — the man who talks himself into believing his emigrant ship is seaworthy, and drowns his passengers — as the emblem of what she is against.

The other is a Jamesian. He holds that where a question is a genuine option — live for the person, forced in the sense that refusing to decide is itself a decision, and momentous — and where the evidence cannot settle it, believing can be not merely permissible but required, because in some cases the belief is itself part of what brings the outcome about. He takes the mountain climber who can only clear the gap if he believes he can clear it as his emblem.

Please draft the joint statement these two could have signed if they had collaborated in good faith instead of trading objections. I want it in five parts.

1. Agreed claims. A numbered list of substantive propositions both can sign, about belief, evidence, action, and how far belief is under our control. Nothing vacuous.

2. The disagreement stated exactly. For each genuine disagreement, give the formulation the Cliffordian would accept as her own and the formulation the Jamesian would accept as his own, neither slanted nor empty.

3. Cruxes — the sub-questions such that settling one would move at least one side — and, for each, whether it is a real disagreement or merely verbal. I want at least these addressed: whether evidence means the same thing on both sides; whether the dispute is really about what to believe or about what to do and how to inquire; whether withholding belief is a genuine third option or itself a commitment; and whether belief is even the kind of thing one can choose.

4. Tests. For each crux, a case or argument both sides would agree bears on it, with each side's prediction written down in advance. Use the shipowner and the mountain climber, and add a case neither of them has raised: a woman deciding whether to believe that a reconciliation with her estranged father will succeed, where her believing it or not is claimed to change how she behaves and so to change the outcome.

5. One piece of work I think we have been missing. I have read enough to suspect a distinction we have never used might matter: the difference between believing a proposition and merely accepting it — that is, treating it as a premise for action without the corresponding belief. Say whether the two positions separate cleanly along that line, whether the Jamesian is really recommending acceptance rather than belief, and whether the Cliffordian's objection survives if he is. If the distinction dissolves part of the dispute, say which part.
