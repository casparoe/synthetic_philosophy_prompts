I'm a graduate student helping to run a formal epistemology reading group, and for our session on accuracy-first epistemology I've been asked to produce a one-page decision tree that takes a reader from the almost trivial thought that a credence should be accurate — that it is better the closer it comes to the truth of the proposition believed — to a specific, nameable position about how much of epistemology that thought can carry. The students keep collapsing everything into "so you just maximize expected accuracy, right?", and I want the handout to show them how many genuinely different commitments that slogan can be unpacked into. Please build the tree from the logical space rather than from a list of authors: design the branch nodes first, each phrased as a question a reader could answer without already knowing where the answer leads, and only then locate positions at the leaves.

The nodes I care about, roughly in the order I would ask them: (1) Is accuracy the sole fundamental epistemic good, or one good among several — say, alongside coherence, explanatory power, or understanding? (2) Is the epistemic value of a whole credence function fixed by the accuracy of its individual credences, or may it depend on relations among the propositions believed — on whether the state is coherent, or on whether it hangs together with the truth as a whole? (3) Must the ranking of credence functions come from a strictly proper scoring rule, or will any truth-directed measure do? (4) Is expected accuracy to be assessed from the agent's own point of view, so that the agent's own credences are by their own lights the ones that maximize it, or is there some external standpoint from which the comparison is made? (5) Does the programme deliver only synchronic norms, or also diachronic ones such as conditionalization and reflection — and if the latter, does accuracy do that work alone or only with an added bridge principle? (6) Is there a chance–credence link of the Principal Principle kind, and is it derived from accuracy or left standing as an independent constraint? I would like five to seven nodes in total and no more than eight leaves, and I would like at least these positions to appear as leaves if the space allows: an orthodox view on which accuracy is the sole good, is additive across credences, is scored by a strictly proper rule, and yields both probabilism and conditionalization; a pluralist view on which accuracy is one good among several; a globalist or non-decomposable view on which the value of the state cannot be read off the accuracy of its parts; a view on which accuracy constrains only synchronic rationality and diachronic norms must come from elsewhere; a permissivist or underdetermination view; and a view on which the chance–credence link is an independent constraint rather than a theorem.

Please use two concrete probes to test the branches, rather than resting on labels: first, an agent whose credences in a proposition and in its logical consequences are internally tidy taken one at a time, but which a whole-state measure and a per-credence measure would rank differently; second, a pair of agents who agree on each of several logically related propositions taken singly but differ on their conjunctions, so that additive and non-additive measures come apart. Each leaf should carry the name of the position — or "unoccupied" if you know of no one who holds it, in which case add one sentence on whether the combination looks incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored — plus one sentence on its most awkward commitment. Flag any pair of leaves the tree cannot separate, rather than pretending that a further question distinguishes them. And please name positions by their commitments rather than by the people who hold them: I want no author names and no citations, since the point is to make the reader choose, not to make them look up who said what.
