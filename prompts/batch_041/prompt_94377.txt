Two months ago a slow leak under my kitchen sink turned my apartment into an ant highway, my landlord sent an exterminator, and I have not been able to stop thinking about the ethics of pest control since. What unsettles me is not the killing; it is the language wrapped around it. The company's brochure is written in the register of environmental responsibility, the technician who came out described his gel bait as targeted and his stations as safe, and the website devotes a whole page to the company's commitment to doing right by the natural world. Meanwhile I crush ants on the counter without breaking stride and have never once counted it.

I would like a piece of sustained satire that takes that language seriously. The form I have in mind is the animal-welfare and environmental-responsibility section of an extermination company's annual sustainability report: the kind of document that opens with a letter from the chief executive, lists multi-year commitments, reports metrics against targets, and closes with a note on methodology. Invent the company, the numbers, and the executives. Do not refer to any real firm, product, study, or standard, and do not cite anything; the piece should stand on its own without a reader needing to look something up. Around 700 words, divided into numbered sub-sections with headings, in the flat, self-congratulatory register of corporate reporting.

Write it straight. No winking, no narrator surfacing to explain the joke, no obvious absurdity. The irony has to be carried entirely by the content, so that a reader who has thought about insect welfare recognizes a real argument being followed where it leads, and a reader who has not can still follow the joke. What I want satirized is not the company's hypocrisy but the logic: the ease with which welfare language and welfare accounting get extended to creatures whose moral status nobody has settled, and the way renaming a killing makes it possible to stop thinking about it.

The report should take positions without ever defending them. At a minimum it should:

- treat the question of whether insects can suffer as a matter for an internal committee, with a decision procedure whose outcome depends on something other than evidence;
- report a metric for how quickly its products kill, and treat speed as the measure of humanity;
- include an accounting in which insects killed are offset against suffering the company claims to have prevented, arriving at a net figure it is pleased with — I want a reader to feel the pull of that arithmetic before recoiling from it;
- explain why the vertebrates it occasionally traps get a welfare policy of their own while the insects get a different one;
- describe a formal channel through which affected parties can raise concerns;
- confront somewhere what becomes of the insect population that would have filled the space of the ones it killed, so that the question of whether a prevented insect life is a saved one hangs over the whole document.

Two further beats, if they fit: a claim that the company's growth-regulator program is its most humane offering precisely because it does not kill anything, and a passage in which the company distinguishes prevention from control and lets that distinction do moral work.

Do not resolve any of this neatly in the company's favor; the piece is better if the logic holds together all the way down. Keep the prose clean — the deadpan only works if the sentences are the kind a real report would print.
