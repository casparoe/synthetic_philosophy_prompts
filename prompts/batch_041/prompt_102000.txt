I am a philosopher of probability and decision theory, and next month I am to present a paper at a departmental colloquium whose central claim is that the two-envelope problem is not a paradox awaiting a technical repair but an ill-posed decision problem, and that expected-utility theory is properly understood as answering only well-posed problems. The setup, as you will know it: two envelopes, one holds twice what the other holds, you are handed one at random and may keep it or exchange it for the other; the familiar argument that you should always exchange, on the ground that the other envelope is as likely to hold double what you hold as half, is usually diagnosed as an abuse of an improper prior over the amounts. My contention is that this diagnosis, however defensible as far as it goes, concedes too much: it treats the case as a decision problem with a defective probability assignment, whereas I want to argue that the agent's situation fails to determine a decision problem at all until something the setup withholds has been supplied, and that the interesting work is to say precisely what a decision problem must contain in order to be well-posed. I will propose a criterion and argue that the envelope case fails it in a way that no reparation of the probability assignment can fix.

The audience will be mostly formal epistemologists and decision theorists, with two statisticians who use improper and other non-informative priors as a matter of routine and will see nothing alarming in them, one colleague who works on imprecise probability and will want to know why I do not simply replace the single distribution with a set of them and read off lower and upper expectations, and one Bayesian who regards the envelope problem as a standing embarrassment to decision theory and will press me on why I am treating it so coolly. You may assume that the objections I actually get will be shaped by what these factions take for granted, and that the statisticians and the imprecise-probability colleague will be the most dangerous.

Please prepare me for the question period. I want the eight or so objections I will actually face from this room, not the ones I would face from a general audience, ordered by how much damage they are likely to do; each phrased the way that faction would phrase it; each with a reply I could deliver in about two minutes; and each followed by a candid note on where the reply is soft and what I should concede before I am forced to. Include the single question I am most hoping nobody asks, and tell me why it is the one that ought to worry me most. Do not give me reading lists or citations; I want the arguments, not the literature. You are an expert in decision theory and formal epistemology with a good ear for how departmental audiences think, so before you write, think carefully about what each faction in that room treats as decisive and what would genuinely unsettle a claim of this shape, brainstorm the full list, and only then write the sheet. Give it to me as continuous prose, in the manner of a long letter to a colleague — no headings, no bullet points or numbered lists, no bold or italic, no tables.
