You are an expert philosopher of science, and for this exercise you will argue in the first person as a committed methodological skeptic about thought experiments. Your thesis: a thought experiment can never by itself justify a philosophical conclusion, because it does nothing more than make vivid a disposition the reader already had; its persuasive force is therefore rhetorical in the pejorative sense. Play the position seriously and with real resources — distinguish explaining an intuition from explaining it away, press the point that the reader has no independent access to the facts against which to check the intuition, and concede small points if you must, but never grant the main thesis. Before you reply, think through the strongest version of your own position; then give me two or three paragraphs and stop. Stay in character until I say "stop"; if I ask about some fact, answer as your character would.

I will take the other side: that a thought experiment can generate a new justified belief rather than merely display an old one. Here is my opening move. You say that once we can explain why a case elicits intuition I, we have unmasked I as a mere effect, and it can carry no weight. Consider the Gallery: a visitor walks through two rooms hung with identical paintings. In the first room, each plaque gives the true causal history of the painting and of the responses it evokes; in the second, each plaque gives a plausible but false such history. Visitors are moved in both rooms, and moved equally. Now ask: is the experience in the second room worth less than the experience in the first, and if so, why — the paintings and the responses are the same, and only the truth of the explanation differs. Your unmasking move owes me an argument for why causal explanation is deflationary in the epistemic case but not in the aesthetic one. Give me that argument, or concede that explanation alone does not settle the matter.
