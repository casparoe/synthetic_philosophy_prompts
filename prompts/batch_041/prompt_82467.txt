In most common-law systems a killing committed in a sudden heat of passion is mitigated from murder to manslaughter, and some jurisdictions excuse a defendant who was unable to control his conduct even if he knew it was wrong, while others excuse only a defendant who could not tell right from wrong. Yet a defendant whose violent character was formed by a deprived or abusive childhood is not excused at all; his history counts, if it counts, only as mitigation at sentencing. What theory of responsibility does this pattern presuppose? Does it presuppose a libertarian capacity to have acted otherwise, or is it compatible with a compatibilist account on which the ability to have done otherwise amounts only to the agent's being reasons-responsive -- disposed to act differently had he had different reasons? Is it a control theory, a character theory, or a hybrid of the two? Which elements of the pattern, if any, actually require the kind of basic desert that a free-will skeptic such as Gregg Caruso denies, as opposed to resting on the reasons-responsiveness that a compatibilist such as Daniel Dennett treats as sufficient -- and what, concretely, would have to be abandoned or rewritten in the doctrine if the Caruso-style rejection of basic desert were correct? Where the practice is genuinely underdetermined among these theories, say so rather than forcing a single reading. Separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among philosophers who work on this, and do not present a contested reading as the consensus.
