I've somehow ended up on the working group redrafting our city's gambling self-exclusion scheme, and the more I look at the whole thing the less the pieces fit together. The list works how you'd expect: you sign once and it binds you for life, no getting off it because you've changed your mind, the board won't hear you. Meanwhile the same council has a charter provision only it can change, needing two-thirds plus a waiting period, even though it passed by a bare majority, and someone called that a Ulysses contract and now everyones saying it. But the clinic our scheme refers people to will only honor a patient's advance directive refusing treatment against their later refusal if a panel decides the later refusal is out of character for them, and the payday-loan cooling-off register lapses after three years unless you re-sign. So in one place the past self wins outright, in another the present self wins unless a panel rules it isn't really them, and in the third the past self loses by default. What theory of the person over time, and of autonomy, does this whole mess presuppose? Which rival views does it quietly rule out, that there's no persisting self to be bound in the first place, or that only present preferences have authority? Is it even consistent, or is the in-character panel secretly doing the work everywhere and we should just admit we're all true-self theorists? And be honest about what's strictly presupposed versus what merely fits: could you run all three practices on a view with no enduring self at all, so long as somebody else, the casino or the state, is always there to do the enforcing? If we dropped the true-self talk entirely, which of the three rules would have to go?
