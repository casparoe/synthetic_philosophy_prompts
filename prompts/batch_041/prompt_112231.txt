I work in applied ethics and political philosophy, and I keep getting pulled into a dispute that has run for decades both in my own field and in parallel in development studies and anthropology: whether clientelism, patronage, and the exchange of gifts in political and bureaucratic contexts should be classified as corruption at all. The universalist side holds that there is a single functional core to the notion — an officeholder diverts a public resource or bends a public decision toward a private benefit — and that the label applies whatever the local moral culture makes of the exchange. The contextualist side holds that this description has already smuggled in an impersonal, liberal conception of office, so that a gift to a kinship patron, or a payment that discharges a socially obligatory reciprocity, is not the same act as a bribe; on this view, calling it corruption imposes an alien evaluative framework and then reads resistance to the label as backwardness. The two sides have been trading the same moves for years. The universalists say the contextualists excuse predation on the poor; the contextualists say the universalists mistake the analyst's categories for the participant's self-understanding. Nobody moves.

What I want from you is a diagnosis of the impasse rather than a verdict on which side is right — though I do want you to commit to a diagnosis. Concretely: Is this a genuine disagreement about the world, or is a substantial part of it a disagreement about what the word "corruption" is for — in one usage a coordinating device for institutional accountability, in another a term of appraisal directed at a person's character? Are the two sides simply working from different data — the universalist from cross-national indices and their correlates, the contextualist from ethnographic cases — such that they never meet on a shared case and talk past one another? Is a hidden empirical question doing the work (for instance, whether clientelist exchange actually functions as insurance and redistribution, or only as extraction) such that answering it would dissolve most of the dispute? Or does the impasse bottom out in a normative disagreement about whether institutions owe citizens impersonality at all, which no amount of ethnography or regression could settle?

My own hypothesis — which I would like you to test rather than adopt — is that the dispute is stuck because "corruption" is being asked to do two jobs at once: to pick out a kind of act and to pick out a kind of wrong. Those two criteria come apart precisely in the cases that matter — the gift that is also a duty, the payment that is also a survival strategy, the favour that is also a redistributive transfer — and each side privileges one criterion while treating the other as a confusion. If that is right, then the disagreement is partly verbal (not empty, but a disagreement about how to carve a concept whose extension is fixed by the function we want the concept to serve) and partly a genuine normative disagreement about which function to serve. If it is wrong, tell me where the picture fails and what the disagreement really is instead.

Take a definite position on the diagnosis and give your reasons for it, rather than laying out the options for me to choose among. Then tell me what would actually count as progress given that diagnosis: a sharper distinction, a case that would force one side to concede, a formal result, or a change of question — for example, retiring the broad term in favour of narrower predicates that name the act without carrying the appraisal. If you think no progress should be expected because the disagreement is at bedrock, say so and explain why. I have no use for citations or a reading list here; argue from the structure of the positions as I have described them, and correct my description where it misrepresents one side.
