So I read The Brothers Karamazov last year, mainly because I kept seeing people go on about the Grand Inquisitor part and I wanted to know what the fuss was — I'm not a philosophy person at all, I fix air con units for a living — and honestly that one chapter has been living in my head ever since, I keep arguing with it while I'm driving.

Here's what I've been chewing on. I've started calling it the quiet Inquisitor. There's a thing now where you hand over a load of small everyday decisions to a system: what to watch next, what to eat, who to text back, when to go for a walk, whether to stay in or go out. The selling point is that you end up measurably happier and less wound up, and apparently it actually works, the surveys keep coming back saying people sleep better and feel calmer. Nobody's forcing anyone, there's no dungeon, you sign up and you can quit whenever. It just quietly takes the choosing off your hands and hands you back a nice calm life.

What I want is your best guess at what Dostoevsky would have said about it. If you could sit him down in front of a working version with real numbers, would he say the Inquisitor was right all along and this is just his system finally arriving — bread, plus someone else to do the deciding — or would he say no, this is a different animal, because in the story the whole arrangement depends on the Church keeping people in the dark about where the authority really comes from, and here there's no lie at all, just results?

Second thing, and this is the bit I really want. I think the story cheats. The Inquisitor gets the best speech in the book and then Dostoevsky never shows us a society actually run his way — we never get to see wether the people in it are happy or miserable, we just get the speech and the kiss at the end. So the strongest case in the novel is never tested. Would he have been willing to give that side a fair run if he'd had the tech, or is the whole point that the test doesn't matter, because something in a person refuses to be managed even when the managing works? Because if you handed me a button that made my life calmer and someone else did all the deciding, I'd press it tonight, and I half think he'd say that just proves his point rather than disproves it.

So: what would he actually say, and where would the comparison break down? Would seeing it work change his mind about freedom and happiness at all? And does the rest of the book help here — the elder Zosima and the bit about actually doing things for people instead of just dreaming about loving everybody, Ivan falling apart at the end — or is that just him giving us his own answer and I should set it aside?
