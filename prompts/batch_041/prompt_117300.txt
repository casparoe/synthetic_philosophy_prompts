For the last two years I have helped run a small interview series attached to a public library reading group. It is not an academic affair: we invite one person to talk for forty minutes about something they have written on and then take questions from the room, and most of the audience, like me, has no philosophical training beyond a term or two of an introductory course. Next month our guest works on material constitution, and I have been trying to prepare properly. I have no degree myself — one term of an intro course, and then years of reading on my own — so forgive me if I state her position clumsily, but here is the line I take her to be running. She thinks the whole business of saying that the statue and the lump of clay are two things, or that the statue is constituted by the clay without being identical with it, is a mistake. On her view there is one object on the pedestal; the statue is that object and the clay is that object, and identity needs no third relation to do the work. What, then, of the fact that the lump could be rolled into a ball while the statue could not? She says this does not show there are two things, because claims about whether a thing could be squashed flat are not properties the object has by itself: they are true or false only relative to a way of classifying the object, much as being tall is true or false only relative to a comparison class. And she insists that this relativity belongs to the object rather than to our language — the object really does have the kind of history that supports the one classification and not the other. She is fluent and very hard to pin down; she tends to answer a question by restating her view in slightly different words, so I want questions with teeth.

Please write me eight to ten questions, arranged so that the interview builds rather than jumping around, and make sure the sequence includes at least one question on each of the following, each with a follow-up that anticipates the answer she is most likely to give and presses on her second move rather than her first: (a) what exactly makes the relativity she invokes a feature of the object rather than a fact about our descriptions, and how she answers the objection that any difference in what is true of the object must be grounded in some difference in the object; (b) how many things are on the pedestal on her view, and what she says to someone who points out that we seem perfectly able to count the statue and the lump and arrive at two, or who insists that how many objects there are cannot depend on how we happen to be counting; (c) the lump's earlier existence — the clay was dug out of the ground before anyone sculpted it, so if there is only one object, what was it before the sculptor arrived, and does her answer commit her to saying either that a statue was there all along or that the object was there while the statue was not; and (d) whether her view differs in any arguable way from the two-object views she rejects, or whether the dispute turns out to be about what we should say rather than about what there is. Add two or three further questions about a case where the classification itself is contested — something that is plausibly both a religious artifact and a piece of folk art, say — and finish with one question about what she would treat as evidence against the whole approach. Please leave out warm-up questions about how she came to philosophy or which books she admires.
