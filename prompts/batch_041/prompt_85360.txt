I am assembling the final examination for a graduate seminar on consciousness in Indian and Western philosophy. The last unit of the course pairs Schrödinger's late reflections on the subject with Advaita Vedanta, and I want a single essay question that tests whether students can think through the unity-of-consciousness thesis rather than reciting either the standard physicalist literature on the binding problem or a generic summary of Advaita. To keep the question fair, I will give students a short handout in the exam; it will state, in my own words, the package of views often associated with Schrödinger's late essays: that the plurality of conscious subjects is an appearance rather than an ultimate fact; that the subject of knowledge can never be made into an object of knowledge; that the Upanishadic identification of the innermost self with Brahman is meant literally, not metaphorically; and that physics can describe only objectified contents, never the subject that knows them. The handout will also note that these claims are often read as a form of numerical monism, according to which there is one consciousness and not many, though the same texts also speak of consciousness as a singular that appears as many. I do not want the question to depend on students having read any particular book, and I do not want it to ask for citations, dates, or reading lists. Assume that no one, including you, has web access.

Please draft the exam question itself, a model answer of roughly 750 words, and a marking scheme. The question should be answerable in 90 minutes and worth 25 marks. It should force students to distinguish at least three things: numerical unity of the subject, phenomenal unity within a single experience, and personal unity across time. It should also require them to confront a specific empirical-looking challenge—a split-brain case, or a constructed two-stream case, in which one organism seems to support two simultaneous streams of reportable experience. The question should ask whether such a case falsifies Schrödinger's monism, merely falsifies a claim about the unity of the person, or does neither; and it should require students to say what would count as a decisive consideration either way. The crux should be whether the monist can distinguish the subject of experience from the empirical person without making the thesis unfalsifiable. The marking scheme should name five must-hit points, assign each a mark so that the total is 25, and for each point give a one-line note on the mistake that typically costs students the mark; it should also make clear what separates a first-class answer from a solid upper-second. The model answer should not merely summarize; it should take a stand and defend it.

Finally, after the marking scheme, add a brief note—no more than 150 words—saying which part of the model answer you are least confident in, what argument or consideration would lead you to revise the exam question's crux or the model answer, and where your reasoning is most likely to fail. Do not include any bibliography, citations, or suggested readings.
