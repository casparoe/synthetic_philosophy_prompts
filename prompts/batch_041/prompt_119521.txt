I'm teaching a 90-minute session to about 14 upper-level students (mostly philosophy, a couple of econ people) and I want to run the doomsday argument as a game instead of lecturing it. They've all seen Bayes' rule and conditionalization, none of them have read anything on anthropics, and I don't want to hand out readings or have them look stuff up.

Here's the shape I'm picturing, feel free to redesign it: two opaque bags, one holding 10 numbered tokens and one holding 1000, and I secretly flip a coin to decide which bag is the real world. Everyone draws one token at random; that number is their birth rank, and nobody knows which bag they drew from. Then everyone privately writes down a 90% interval for the total number of tokens and a probability that the world is the small one, and we score them against each other with some proper scoring rule so there's a reason to care but no money changes hands.

What I want the session to do: make them feel in their gut that knowing your own position in the sequence is real evidence about the size of the whole, and then make them feel how much that conclusion depends on treating your position as a random draw from a population that already exists. I want the split between people who think you should update on your rank and people who think your own existence is already evidence about how many observers there are (so the two effects cancel) to fall out of the room rather than out of my mouth. Same with the reference class problem -- I want them to hit it themselves when they notice the answer swings depending on whether we count all humans ever, or all hominids, or every observer anywhere.

So: give me the full runnable thing. Rules, materials list (my budget is small), the exact sequence with timings, the scoring scheme, how to run the private-answer step so nobody free-rides or reads the room, and the reveal at the end. Include a second round that puts the cancel-out view on equal footing, because otherwise everyone just converges on the spooky answer and stops thinking. Also the debrief: map each moment of the game onto the piece of the argument it corresponds to, tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle each failure, and be honest about which feature of the real doomsday argument the game cannot reproduce no matter how I stage it -- I suspect its the fact that in real life theres no urn and no draw, but tell me if thats wrong and what else I'm papering over.
