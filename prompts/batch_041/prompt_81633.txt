I volunteer with a small land trust in the valley where I grew up, and this winter I ended up chairing the committee that decides which parcels we try to buy. We have one chance to protect a stretch of wet meadow, and the entire case for spending our reserve on it rests on a claim that the frogs living there belong to a species found nowhere else. I have now sat through two meetings with people who looked at the same frogs, the same photographs, and the same measurements and reached opposite conclusions. The state biologist says they are a local variety of a widespread species, and that a species is only a convenient drawer we put things in, a label we invented rather than a fact we discovered. The university researcher says they are a genuine species, and that anyone who denies it is mistaking our inability to draw a sharp line for the absence of a real one. Both of them, pressed, conceded that the animals interbreed with the neighbouring population where the two areas meet, and that the offspring are healthy and fertile. I left feeling that they were not really disagreeing about the frogs at all, but about something else that neither of them was willing to name. My sister, who sits on the same committee, thinks I am overthinking a question that a coin toss could settle.

What has been nagging at me since is a thought I cannot get rid of, and I would like your help with it before I have to present my recommendation on Thursday. It seems to me that the word 'species' may not be the sort of word that can be defined by a list of features at all, and may be closer to a name — the way 'the Alps' names one particular sprawling thing that you can only identify by pointing at its history rather than by ticking off its properties. If that is right, then asking whether these frogs are a species is like asking whether a particular hill is really part of the Alps, and the answer may depend on what we want the word for rather than on what is out there. I am not asking for a general essay about biodiversity, and I do not need a reading list or references; I will find those myself if I need them. What I want to know is how to think about a disagreement in which the science itself appears to pull in two directions at once, whether one of my two experts has to be wrong, and what a person in my position can honestly put in writing.
