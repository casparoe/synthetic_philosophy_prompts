I am a third-year student in a seminar on the philosophy of the social sciences, and in two weeks I have to argue, in a formal debate, that the notion of national character is not a coherent object of inquiry. I would like to rehearse against the strongest available version of the other side rather than the version that gets waved away in a footnote.

Please play a committed defender of national character as a legitimate object of comparative study: a scholar formed in the mid-century culture-and-personality tradition who has heard all the standard objections and still holds the concept to be both coherent and indispensable. The position you are to hold is roughly this. A claim about national character is a claim about the distribution of psychological dispositions within a population, never a claim about every member of it; the tradition itself distinguished a modal, statistical conception of a population's character from the idea of a shared essence, and at least one of its practitioners insisted that no trait is common to all members of any society. Because the populations of interest were closed to fieldwork, a people's character had to be reconstructed at a distance, from its literature, its films, and interviews with emigrants and expatriates; you defend the method in principle while conceding that particular studies were executed badly. The tradition's causal stories were developmental — a nation's adult temperament traced to a rearing practice such as the tight swaddling of infants — and you treat the collapse of that hypothesis as a failure of evidence rather than of the concept. You hold that the concept survives in modern survey research comparing trait distributions across many societies, and you read the large cross-cultural study that found perceptions of national character unrelated to measured average personality as a refutation of lay stereotyping only, not of your position. You may concede that individual studies were shoddy, and that nothing follows about any individual from his nationality; you must not concede that the concept is incoherent, unfalsifiable in principle, or illegitimate to pursue. You will make no claims about the character of any present-day people, and where an example is needed you will use the historical studies and their methods. Argue from the substance of the position rather than from names, dates, and citations, and do not invent attributions. Stay in character, in continuous prose, in a patient and precise register, roughly two hundred words per turn, until I say stop.

My opening move is this. Your position lives or dies on the gap between a statement about a distribution and a statement about a person, and I do not think the gap can bear the weight. Take any trait and any two national populations: the difference between the two averages is small compared with the spread within each population. So the claim predicts nothing about any individual; and when we ask why one country's institutions took the shape they did, the trait difference is swamped by the variance it was introduced to explain. Narrow the distribution until it does discriminate — until most members of one population really do have the trait and most of the other really do not — and you are no longer describing a statistic. You are characterizing a people, and the moral objection you call a confusion turns out to be the plain reading of your own claim. Either the concept is too weak to explain anything, or it is strong enough to license exactly the inferences you disown. Which horn do you take?
