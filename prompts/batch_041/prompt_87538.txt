Over the past several years I have been reporting on disputes over public monuments — statues removed in the night, streets renamed after long fights, plaques fastened to figures who have fallen out of favor — and a pattern keeps surfacing that I cannot square with itself. A city council will vote to take down a statue because the man it depicts held views the city now finds contemptible, and in the same meeting decline to rename a bridge for a donor whose fortune was made in a trade the city also finds contemptible, reasoning that the donor's money built something the public still uses. Another council keeps the offending statue but bolts a plaque to its base explaining the subject's record, as though the difficulty were one of information rather than of honor. And nearly everyone, whatever they decide, insists that they are not erasing history — even as they remove the most conspicuous historical object in the square.

What theory of commemoration do these practices presuppose? I would like you to reconstruct the implicit view. On the account the practices seem to assume, what is a monument for — a speech act by the community that raised it, a historical artifact that merely records what was once thought, an act of honor owed to the dead, or an instrument for shaping the citizens who will one day walk past it? Which rival accounts does the pattern quietly rule out, and which does it leave untouched? Is the pattern internally consistent, or does the treatment of the living-benefactor bridge and the explanatory plaque betray a second theory sitting uneasily beside the first? I would also like you to press on a distinction the practice seems to blur — between honoring a person and remembering one — and to say whether the mismatch between a monument's author (a past community) and its audience (the present one) does any real work in the reasoning or is merely decorative.

Then tell me where the practice is philosophically underdetermined — where several theories would license exactly what these councils do — as opposed to where it genuinely commits itself. Finally, say what would have to change if the implicit theory were replaced by one of its rivals: suppose, for instance, that commemoration were understood as remembering rather than as honoring, and tell me what that substitution would do to the statue, the plaque, and the street sign.
