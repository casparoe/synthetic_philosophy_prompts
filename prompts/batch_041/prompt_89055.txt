I'm stuck refereeing an argument between two friends and I need the spec before I can call it. One of them thinks it's fine, and sometimes unavoidable, to talk about national character -- that the French are rude, that Americans are optimistic, that Germans are rule-following, that Brazilians are warm, that Scandinavians are reserved. The other thinks any such generalization is either a statistical banality or an essentialist prejudice wearing a lab coat. Before I pick a side I want to know what a theory of national-character claims would actually have to deliver. So give me the desiderata: the requirements a theory in this area has to meet to count as adequate, with the case or consideration behind each one.

Fix the purpose this way, since adequacy is relative to it: I want a theory good enough that I can take a claim of the form 'the people of country X are F' and tell (a) whether it's even the kind of claim that can be true or false, (b) what would have to be the case in the world for it to be true, and (c) what does and doesn't follow from it if it is true. So the list is requirements on a theory that can do that job.

Here are the considerations I keep tripping over, and I want each of them either turned into a requirement or argued down as a red herring, with the reasoning shown:

- The measurement point: in the personality data, differences between countries tend to be small relative to the spread inside any single country, and the ranking of countries moves around depending on which instrument you use. Does a theory have to accommodate that, or can it say the instruments are measuring the wrong thing?
- The reference-group effect: when people rate their own traits they seem to be comparing themselves to their compatriots, so the self-reports may not be commensurable across countries at all.
- The mismatch: what people believe about their own national character often doesn't line up with what gets measured. Whose claim is the theory of -- the perceived character or the real one?
- The ecological fallacy: a true statistical fact about a population doesn't license a prediction about the individual in front of you. Is that a defect of the claim or just of the inference?
- Membership: diaspora, immigrants, children, dissidents, people who actively reject the national norm. Who counts as one of the X?
- Change: if character can shift inside a generation, what carries it? If it can't, then it can't explain the things it's usually invoked to explain -- revolutions, postwar transformations, sudden shifts in how a country behaves.
- Circularity: character gets used to explain a country's institutions while the institutions get used to explain the character.
- The comparative frame: rude relative to whom? What's the baseline, and does the claim survive once the baseline is made explicit?
- The normative slide: from 'X are F' to 'so it's fine to mock them / not hire them / not take them seriously', and the other slide from 'X are F' to 'so X can't be held responsible for what they do'. Are these failures of the theory or just abuses of it?

Once the list is up, tell me which requirements are genuine and which are just commitments of one camp, and flag any pair you think can't be jointly satisfied -- that's the part I most want. Say which single requirement does the most work in knocking out candidates. Then, briefly, in a sentence or two each, score the three standard moves against the list: treat national character as a shared essence transmitted down the generations; treat it as nothing but the aggregate of individual traits in a population; treat it as a pure stereotype that describes the perceiver rather than the perceived. Don't linger on the scoring, the list is the point. No reading list or citations, I'll chase those down myself if I need them.
