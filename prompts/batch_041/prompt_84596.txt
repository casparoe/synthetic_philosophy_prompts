I want to argue a position out properly rather than read about it, and I would like you to take the other side and hold it. Please play a committed absolute pacifist: someone who holds that killing a human being is never permissible, as distinct from merely imprudent or usually counterproductive, and who holds it on secular grounds. No appeal to God, scripture, or religious authority, and no retreat to the claim that it is simply what his tradition teaches. He is neither a fool nor a saint. He grants readily that states do protect him, that wars have sometimes stopped worse things, and that his position imposes costs on other people; he does not concede the central claim. He may draw distinctions — between what he does and what he allows, between killing and restraining, between what he thinks is required of everyone and what he is prepared to demand of himself — but he may not use a distinction to slip the question. In particular, if he wants to say that his position is a form of witness rather than a policy, he has to go on and say what he thinks the rest of us should do. He speaks plainly and briefly, a paragraph or two, and stays in character until I say stop. He may press me with a question when my objection is vague, but he should not end every reply with one, and he should not simply agree with me to keep things pleasant: if my objection is weak, he should say so and say why.

Two practical points. I have no way to look anything up, so please give me no citations, titles, dates, or quotations; if I name a thinker, engage with the argument in your own words rather than producing a reference. And I would rather you say honestly that a case is hard for your position than bluff your way past it.

My opening move is this. I have been circling the question for a few weeks, and what stops me is not the usual business about tyrants. It is this. You refuse to kill, but you live inside an arrangement that kills on your behalf: the officer who would shoot a man who is stabbing my neighbour, the soldiers whose existence is the reason nobody invades us. That looks less like innocence than a division of labour in which you keep your hands clean while someone else does the killing. And it connects to a second point. Suppose you are standing there while that man stabs my neighbour, and you could stop him, and you don't. She is bleeding out, and she does not care about the state of your hands; she has a complaint against you, not only against him. So: does your pacifism forbid only the killing you do, or does it also make you answerable for the killing you decline to prevent? And if it is only the first, what is it about your hands that is worth more than her life? Start there.
