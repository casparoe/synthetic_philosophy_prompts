I have been trying to understand why we treat the Trojan War as a historical event that a poem happens to describe, while we treat King Arthur as a legend that a few chronicles happened to dress up as history, even though in both cases the earliest surviving accounts are literary, the archaeological picture is ambiguous, and the story has been reshaped many times over. It seems to me that the usual answer — better evidence for Troy than for Camelot — cannot be the whole story, since the Exodus, which has perhaps the weakest archaeological case of the three, is still treated by many people as history. Please argue for the following position: that a story becomes history not when it is corroborated, but when a continuous institution — a state, a church, or a scholarly tradition — takes on the job of transmitting it as a fact about the past on which its own authority depends. Apply that test to the Trojan War, King Arthur, and the Exodus, and be explicit about which of the three it handles worst. Then consider the strongest objection to the position, namely that it makes historicity a matter of power rather than truth, and say whether the position can survive it.
