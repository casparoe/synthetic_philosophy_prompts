I serve as rapporteur to a citizens' assembly convened to recommend a statutory scheme on assisted dying, and next week the chair rules on whether one further submission enters our deliberative record. Our only guidance is our own practice, and five rulings make it up.

We refused a submission from a coalition of clergy urging that assisting a suicide remain a criminal offence. Its sole premise was that life is a trust held from God and that the civil law ought to mirror divine law; nothing else was offered in support of it. It was launched at a press conference at which its authors described members who disagreed with them as answerable to a higher authority and questioned whether the assembly had any standing to speak for the public at all.

We admitted a submission from a network of hospice chaplains supporting a permissive, tightly safeguarded scheme. It argued from a theology of accompaniment, but it also set out at comparable length the evidence from jurisdictions that have legalised and an argument from the state's duty to protect the vulnerable; its authors agreed to appear and be questioned.

We admitted a brief from a coalition of palliative care physicians, containing no religious content, urging that any scheme be confined to terminal illness and enforced by criminal penalty.

We refused a submission from a federation of congregations urging that doctors be forbidden, on pain of professional and criminal sanction, from raising assisted dying with patients. Its grounds were entirely religious, it offered no public argument, and it arrived alongside a campaign against the assembly.

We admitted a submission from a religiously observant nurse writing in a personal capacity, who urged that any scheme be paired with a large expansion of funded palliative care, arguing partly from her tradition's teaching about patience and partly from her own clinical experience of patients who changed their minds.

The new submission. A coalition of congregations from three traditions urges us to recommend that assisting a suicide remain a criminal offence carrying a substantial custodial sentence. It is offered quietly and in good faith; its authors have asked to appear and be cross-examined; they have undertaken to accept whatever we recommend; and no campaign accompanies it. Its argument has two parts. The first, which the submission itself calls the ground of its conclusion, is that life is a trust from God that no one may surrender. The second, which it describes as confirming the first, is a developed public argument: evidence about the pressure that legalisation places on the ill and the old, the record of safeguards in jurisdictions that have adopted it, and the state's duty to protect those who cannot protect themselves.

What I want. I do not need your view on assisted dying itself; I need the ruling. Set out the rules our five rulings are jointly consistent with — I suspect there is more than one, since the cases never seem to pull them apart — and say what each predicts here. Then give me your best guess, with your reasons, and name the precedent that is the closest analog and why. Tell me what principle we would be adopting if we admit the submission and what principle we would be adopting if we refuse it. And tell me what further submission would most cleanly separate the rule we are actually running from the ones that merely happen to fit our record, and how each of them would decide it.
