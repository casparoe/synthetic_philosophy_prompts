I'm a novelist and I'm deep in a book whose main character is a geneticist, so I've been reading around a fight in her field that I can't stop thinking about. When they map a genome, somebody has to go through all the long stretches that don't code for proteins and decide which ones to tag as functional and which to write off as junk. One crowd says a stretch is functional if it's doing something right now — getting transcribed, binding a protein, having some effect on the cell — and by that measure most of the genome turns out functional. Another crowd says no, functional means selection built it and kept it, it has a job in its history, and by that measure most of it is junk that's just along for the ride.

So here's what I want to know. Does the actual practice of anotating a genome require a particular idea of what 'function' means, or does it just need something cheap and operational and not really care? Does the practice quietly commit to one of those two views and rule the other out, or are the two camps fighting about something the practice itself is neutral on? And if you swapped the history-of-selection idea in for the is-it-doing-something idea, or the other way round, what would flip in the actual labeling — what starts getting called functional, what stops? I keep getting hung up on the in-between ones too: a stretch that clearly does something today but shows no sign of ever having been selected for, or one that was obviously selected for ages ago and now just sits there doing nothing. Which of those does each view have to call functional, and does either view actually match what the labs do?
