I'm assembling the question list for a panel interview with a philosopher of mind who holds an unusual thesis: that an experience can be a harm only if it is irreversible. On her view, a suffering that is later undone — the stock case being a system restored from a backup made before the episode — is undergone but never settled, and only what is settled can count against a subject's interests; she accepts that this makes the moral standing of a digital mind a matter of its error-correction architecture rather than of its capacity to feel. Draft eight to ten questions. At least one each should press her on whether a pain later undone was not still undergone, and by whom; on whether the criterion proves too much, given that human memory is itself reconstructive and that our own minds may one day be reversible; and on what her view implies when a thousand brief sufferings are each undone, leaving no settled harm anywhere in the ledger. Pair each of those three with the follow-up her most probable answer invites — she customarily distinguishes the episode from the record of it, so the follow-up should ask what work that distinction does if nothing of the episode survives into the record. Close with a question about what would count as evidence against the thesis.
