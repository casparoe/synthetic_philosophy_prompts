I am trying to formulate a single principle that distinguishes indicative from counterfactual conditionals, and I have a set of judgments that I cannot get to line up. Please examine the cases below, propose the principle or small set of principles that best captures them, and state plainly which of my judgments the principle forces me to give up.

My judgments:

1. The package is in Paris. I judge both "If the package is in Paris, it is in France" and "If the package had been in Paris, it would have been in France" true, and I see no difference in their truth conditions. The geographical fact is invariant.

2. The glass is fragile. I judge both "If you drop the glass, it shatters" and "If you had dropped the glass, it would have shattered" true; if the glass is actually made of steel, I judge both false. I see no difference. The disposition is invariant.

3. The draw is a red ball. Before the draw, I judge "If the draw is a red ball, I win" a correct prediction, while "If the draw had been a red ball, I would have won" is odd because it presupposes the draw was not a red ball. After the draw, if it was not a red ball, I judge the indicative no longer assertable, but the counterfactual true. The rule linking a red ball to winning is invariant, so the difference cannot be that the background facts are not invariant.

4. The picnic was cancelled. Suppose it did rain, but the picnic was cancelled because the park closed, not because of the rain. I judge "If it rains, the picnic is cancelled" false, because the rain did not cause the cancellation, but I judge "If it had rained, the picnic would have been cancelled" true, because the park closure would have cancelled it anyway. This suggests the indicative tracks actual causal dependence while the counterfactual tracks counterfactual dependence.

5. The butler did it. Given that the soup was poisoned, I judge "If the butler didn't poison the soup, the cook did" true, but "If the butler hadn't poisoned the soup, the cook would have" false, because the cook might not have acted if the butler hadn't.

6. The meeting is at nine. Suppose the meeting is actually at ten. I judge "If the meeting is at nine, we are late" not assertable, but "If the meeting had been at nine, we would have been late" true. Suppose the meeting is at nine. I judge the indicative true, but the counterfactual odd. So they differ.

7. The plant is a fern. I judge "If the plant is a fern, it needs shade" true when the plant is a fern, but when the plant is a cactus I find the singular indicative neither true nor false, while "If the plant had been a fern, it would have needed shade" still seems true. I am not confident about this verdict.

My tentative principle is this: an indicative conditional and a counterfactual with the same antecedent and consequent have the same truth conditions exactly when the consequent follows from the antecedent together with facts that are invariant across the actual world and the relevant counterfactual worlds. They come apart when the consequent depends on facts that are not invariant—the actual causal history, the actual outcome of a chance process, or the actual identity of a particular object. But this principle seems to predict equivalence in case 4 and difference in case 7, and I am not sure either prediction is right. It also seems to say nothing about why case 3 comes apart when the rule is invariant.

I would like a principle, or a small set of candidate principles, stated precisely enough to generate predictions. For each clause, tell me which case motivates it. Then apply the principle to at least two cases I have not listed—one where the antecedent is a chance event and one where the consequent contains a modal or probabilistic term. Finally, tell me honestly which of my judgments above the principle cannot accommodate, and whether you would abandon the principle or the judgment. If no single principle can save all the judgments, give me the best two or three candidates and say which judgment each one sacrifices.
