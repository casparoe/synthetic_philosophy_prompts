I write about science and ideas for a magazine, and I'm starting a book on the claim that a government can earn the right to rule by delivering the goods — growth, safety, schools, no drama — rather than by being chosen. China and Singapore are the exhibits everyone reaches for; the "Asian values" line from the nineties is the ancestor everyone reaches for after that. I've read enough by now to be bored of the two moves that come up every single time: the split between legitimacy that comes from what a government delivers and legitimacy that comes from who gets to pick it, and the old distinction between support that tracks current performance and support that outlives it. I want to get past those into the stuff that's actually unresolved.

So here's what I need from you. Take the role of a political theorist who works on comparative authoritarianism and also on the analytic literature about political obligation — someone who reads the China hands and the philosophers and isnt impressed by either. Before you rank anything, brainstorm out loud: what are the genuinely live open questions here, the ones where people who know the material disagree about the answer? Then rank five or six of them for me specifically — I've got about eighteen months and a book contract, and what I care about is which answers would change the shape of the whole debate rather than settle somebody's turf war. For each one: why it's upstream of the others, whether it can be settled by argument and reporting or whether it's really an empirical question I can't do anything with, and what would move it up or down my list. Be explicit about how you're weighing importance against tractability against how many other questions hang on the answer, and adress what you do when those pull apart.

Some things I've been chewing on — tell me if any of them are confused, already settled, or beside the point:

- Is legitimacy bought with results a flow that has to be renewed every year, or does something accumulate? And when a regime that runs on results hits a bad stretch, do expectations ratchet up or quietly adapt down? My hunch is that it's a loan and not a stock, but I couldn't tell you what would settle that.
- Does the reciprocity family of theories of political obligation — the ones that say a state which gives you a decent life has a claim on you — end up doing the work of justifying a benevolent autocracy? Everyone I've floated this past hates the conclusion and nobody has a clean way out of it.
- The information problem. Ruling by results means knowing which results people want, but the same machinery that keeps the regime safe distorts what it hears. Does that make performance legitimacy self-undermining over a long horizon? And how would anyone know what people in these places actually believe, when so much of what gets said in public is said for the room?
- Succession and crisis. Is there any mechanism by which one of these regimes outlives its founder, or is that the whole ballgame and everything else is decoration?
- Which legitimacy is really doing the work in these debates — the sociological kind (people accept it) or the normative kind (it has a right to rule)? Does the gap between them have practical consequences, or is it a philosophers' quarrel?
- Singapore: is it a case at all, or a selection effect — city-state, entrepot, no hinterland, a ruling party that got lucky with timing?

Skip the reading list and the citations. I know you can't look anything up, and for what I'm doing the reasoning is the whole point. Do flag how confident you are as you go, though, and at the end single out the one claim in your answer you're least sure of — I'd rather know where the floor is thin than get a confident tour.
