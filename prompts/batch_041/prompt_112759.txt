I'm writing up something on natural kinds and I want to use a colleague's verdicts as the foil. Priya is a philosopher of biology, very no-nonsense, and her track record runs roughly like this. Gold, water, electrons: yes, obviously, she'd be embarrassed to be asked. Jade: no — its two quite different minerals sharing a name. Fish: no, though she insists that's not for the usual paraphyly reason but because it's a leftover bin rather than a real cluster of anything. Biological species: yes, even though she'll cheerfully admit there's no essence in them. Species in bacteria: no, at best a useful fiction. The molecular gene: no. Race as a biological category: no (as a social category she says that's a different question and goes back to her coffee). DSM categories like depression: no. And she gets genuinely cross if you suggest a natural kind is just a compliment we pay to classifications we happen to find handy — she thinks some of these are real joints and some aren't, and that there's a fact of the matter about which.

Next week I'm giving a paper arguing that warm-bloodedness — endothermy — is a natural kind, and I want to know how she'll come at me. It's an awful case for anyone who thinks kinds are lineages: it's turned up independently in mammals, in birds, in a few fish, in some big insects, so there's no common ancestor to point at. But there is a fairly stable bundle of traits — high metabolic rate, insulation, keeping yourself warm on purpose — held together by a real mechanism, selection for keeping the engine running, and physiologists get a lot of mileage out of generalising over the whole lot of them at once.

So: will she say yes or no, and what's the rule she's actually running when she says yes or no? I want at least two rules that fit everything above equally well, what each of them predicts about my endotherm case, which one you'd bet on and why, and then the case that would actually settle it between them — because I suspect my case doesn't. I keep thinking she's a descent-of-lineages person, and then she says something that makes me think she's really after property clusters and the mechanisms that hold them together, so I definately don't know.

Write it in British English please, it's going to a colleague in Leeds and she'll only complain about my spelling otherwise. And don't bother telling me what's been written on all this, I've read it; I want your reasoning.
