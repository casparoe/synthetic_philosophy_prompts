For the first meeting of a jurisprudence seminar, I want a diagnostic handout rather than a lecture: a decision tree that sorts students by what they actually think about the relation between law and morality, built out of the logical space of answers rather than out of a list of theorists. The debate I have in mind is the one between Hart and Fuller, but the tree has to be usable by someone who has read neither and has never heard either name.

Start the tree at the case everyone has a verdict on. A legislature with unquestioned authority, acting in perfect procedural order, enacts a statute that is morally monstrous: it strips a religious minority of its property and bars its members from employment. The constitution says nothing on point, no higher court has spoken, and the statute is unambiguous. The first node is the student's verdict on whether that statute is law.

Then build out. Two rules for the nodes. No branch may use the vocabulary of the positions: positivism, natural law, inclusive, exclusive, and the names of any theorists may appear only in the leaf labels, never in a question. And every node must be answerable by someone who has no idea which position it leads to, so phrase nodes as verdicts on cases or as flat claims about what legal validity depends on, not as invitations to pick a school.

I want four questions kept apart that my students always collapse into one: whether a norm is legally valid at all; how anyone tells whether it is; what an official — a judge, a police officer, a licensing clerk — may or must do with a norm once it is valid; and whether the word "law" is doing descriptive work or work of praise. A position that answers one of these and is silent on another should be shown as silent rather than rounded off. I also want a node separating two views that sound alike but are not: that the tests for legal validity themselves include moral tests, and that the tests for validity are purely social while officials are morally permitted to refuse to apply a valid norm. Add a node on whether the verdict attaches to a single norm or to the legal order taken as a whole, since the two can come apart, and a node on whether the answer is relative to how decent the system is overall, so that the monstrous statute in an otherwise decent order comes out differently from the same statute in a systematically wicked one.

The tree has to route these cases, so use them as branch points. Second case: a statute enacted in the same flawless way that is morally unobjectionable but so vague that no official can tell what it forbids. Third: a statute that is clear, morally fine, and duly enacted, but that no official has enforced in forty years and that officials openly call dead. Fourth: a norm that appears in no statute book and was never enacted by anyone, yet every official treats it as binding and has followed it for generations. Fifth, the postwar problem: a woman denounced her husband to the authorities of the old regime under a statute then in force, which was unambiguous, and he was executed; the courts of the new order must decide whether to punish her, and their only tools are a retroactive statute and the thought that the old statute was so unjust that it never was law. Sixth: a trial judge's decision resting on no statute and no precedent, morally admirable, which later becomes the settled law of the jurisdiction. Seventh: a procedurally perfect constitutional amendment that abolishes judicial review and immunizes the ruling party from legal challenge, after which the courts go on hearing ordinary cases as before.

Leaves should be named, and should include exclusive legal positivism, inclusive legal positivism, Fuller-style procedural natural law, substantive natural law in the Finnis vein, Dworkin-style interpretivism, Radbruch-style non-positivism about extreme injustice, and at least one deflationary position holding that the dispute is verbal or has no single answer. Note the combinations nobody occupies and say briefly why the gap is there — incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored — and flag any two leaves your tree cannot separate given the cases I have supplied.

I need the whole thing as continuous prose, since it goes into a handout I will read aloud: no headings, no numbered nodes, no bullet points, no bold, no table. Write it as a walk through the tree in sentences, naming each node and each leaf in the flow. Do not cite anything or hand me a reading list; I know the literature, and I want the tree to stand on its own. American English, please.
