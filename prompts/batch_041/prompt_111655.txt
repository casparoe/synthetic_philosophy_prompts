I'm putting together a unit for a seminar on promises and contracts, and I keep running into the same problem: every account of why promises bind seems to be built to handle two or three pet cases and then quietly ignores everything else. My brother-in-law, who does commercial law, insists none of this matters because consideration does all the work, which is exactly the kind of thing I need a proper spec to adjudicate. So before I make the students read any more of these accounts, I want the desiderata first. Not a survey and not who-said-what: the requirements themselves, each with the case or consideration that puts it on the list, and for each one your call on whether it's non-negotiable or just a house preferance of some camp. Assume the thing has to be good enough that I can actually use it to decide a hard case in class without hand-waving, and that it's a theory of promising specifically, not of obligation in general.

Here's the sort of thing I expect on the list, though add what I've missed and cut what's just prejudice. It has to explain how a few words can manufacture a reason where none existed — the bootstrapping trick — without also licensing me to obligate other people by fiat, and it has to say what promising does that contracts, gratitude, role obligations, and merely causing someone to rely on me don't already do. It has to handle the promise the promisee never learns about, doesn't rely on, or wouldn't have cared about; the vague or open-ended promise, like telling a friend at a wedding that I'll always be there for them, and the deliberately blank promise where I agree now to do whatever is asked of me later; the promise to someone who's since died, the promise to my future self, and the promise made to a group rather than to any particular person; the promise extracted by a threat and the promise to do something plainly wrong; and release — why only the promisee can let me off the hook, and whether a release given under pressure leaves any residue behind. I want it to be honest about whether it's a theory of a social practice, so that the bindingness is contingent on the practice being around, or of something that would bind even in a society with no such practice. And it should say what happens to a promise made on your behalf — say your assistant, human or otherwise, tells a colleague you'll have the draft by Friday and you never agreed to anything. Finally it has to locate the wrong where the wrong is: when I break a promise, the promisee seems to have a special standing to resent it and not just a reason to be disappointed, and the theory should earn that rather than assume it.

Then flag the collisions — the places where two items on your list can't both be had, or where satisfying one quietly makes another trivial. My own suspicion is that the bootstrapping requirement and the standing requirement pull against each other, and that anything grounding promising in the value of reliance is going to be embarrassed by the promisee-who-doesn't-care case, but tell me where I'm wrong. Skip the scorecard of the usual suspects at the end; I'd rather you spend the words on which single requirement does the most to eliminate candidates, and why.
