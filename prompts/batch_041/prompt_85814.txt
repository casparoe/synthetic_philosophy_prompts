Last spring I agreed to sit on the incident-review panel of a mid-sized hospital that had recently deployed a machine-learning triage tool. When a patient deteriorated after the tool ranked her as low-priority, the panel spent six weeks discovering that every person in the chain could truthfully say the final decision was not theirs alone. I am now helping write the panel's standing protocol, and I need a procedure we can actually run, not a seminar. Please translate the philosophical discussion of the responsibility gap for autonomous systems into an operational decision procedure for a non-specialist panel with two hours per case, access to logs and interviews, and no authority to make legal findings. The panel must produce a written allocation of forward-looking responsibilities: who must explain, who must repair, who must change the system, and what cannot be allocated at all. I do not want citations or a reading list.

I have been using a three-way distinction that I have not seen made cleanly, and I want the procedure built around it: a gap in answerability, where no one can give an adequate account of why the system acted; a gap in liability, where no one can be made to bear the cost; and a gap in repair, where no one has the standing or capacity to change the system. An incident can have one, two, or all three. The procedure should diagnose which is present before assigning anything.

Then run it on three cases. First, an autonomous infusion pump that delivered a dose outside protocol because a nurse overrode an alert. Second, a triage model that systematically under-ranked patients from a demographic group because its training data came from another hospital. Third, a surgical robot whose manufacturer pushed a software update changing force limits after the hospital's biomedical team had approved an earlier version. For each, give the panel's output in the procedure's format. Include at least one case where the procedure returns an answer your own philosophical sympathies would resist. Be explicit about the points where the panel must decide a value question, such as how much residual risk to tolerate or whose consent counts, rather than discover a fact.

The procedure should say what to do when logs are missing, when the relevant designer is a defunct contractor, and when the system is a vendor's model plus a hospital's workflow plus a clinician's judgment. It should not ask us to settle metaphysical free will; it should say where that debate would matter if it did, then set it aside. It should distinguish responsibilities assignable to a named person now from those assignable only to a role or institution.

End with a single machine-readable line, \answer{...}, containing a short label for the panel's default allocation when all three gaps are present, for example answerability-first, liability-first, repair-first, distributed, or no-default.
