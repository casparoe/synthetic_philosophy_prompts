I spent most of last night arguing with a friend about the run-up to the 2003 invasion of Iraq — the aluminum tubes, Curveball's mobile labs, the forged Niger documents, the October 2002 National Intelligence Estimate, Powell's presentation to the UN — and we ended on opposite sides of a question I actually care about. He says it was just herd behavior: everyone fell in line and nobody thought for themselves. I think that's too quick, because the whole point of an information cascade is that the people inside one can be behaving perfectly rationally, each treating the prior consensus as evidence, which is what makes the theory disturbing rather than merely an insult. But when I tried to run the case through the theory myself, it wouldn't sit still, and I want you to settle it.

Treat the march to war as a candidate informational cascade and work out what the theory really says about it. Be precise about the mapping: who the agents are, what the sequence of decisions is, what counts as a private signal versus a public one, where the cascade starts, and whether it ever becomes common knowledge in the way the models require. Then be just as precise about where it breaks. The standard account assumes private signals are conditionally independent given the truth, and that a cascade is fragile — a little credible public disconfirmation should shatter it. Neither assumption looks safe here: the supposedly independent sources were reading one another, and the disconfirmations (Blix, the IAEA's exposure of the Niger forgery, dissent inside the State Department and DOE) did not break the consensus before the war. So tell me which variant of the theory — informational cascades, reputational herding in the Scharfstein–Stein sense, or something else entirely — actually fits, why the fragility prediction failed, and what the episode shows about the theory rather than the reverse. Take a definite position and defend it; don't lay out the options and leave the choice to me.
