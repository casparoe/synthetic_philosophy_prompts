So I'm a backend/infra engineer, no formal philosophy background, and I've been going down a rabit hole reading about AI alignment lately. There's one distinction that keeps coming up and I think I get it but probably don't: some people seem to want "worst-case" guarantees about an AI system, and others think that's the wrong frame entirely and that we should care about average-case or typical-case behavior. In my world I actually know this split pretty well from other angles — a type system or a static verifier gives you something that holds for every input the compiler can see, whereas a test suite or a week of canary traffic only tells you how the thing behaves on the inputs you happened to hit, and a worst-case execution time analysis is a totally different animal from a p50/p99 latency dashboard. Same flavor with failures: most of my systems break for boring reasons, but a security person assumes someone is actively trying to make it break, and that changes what "works" even means.

What I want from you is the most important implications of this worst-case vs average-case split for someone who actually has to ship things. Concretely, say I'm building a code-review assistant that a human merges behind, and some advisor tells me "you need worst-case alignment guarantees." What does that actually commit me to that I'm not already doing with evals, red-teaming, and staged rollouts? I want to know where the distinction changes a real engineering decision — gating, monitoring, rollback, how much I should trust an eval suite that only covers the distribution I generated myself — versus where it's just a philosophical preference dressed up as rigor. And the other direction too: if I take the average-case view seriously, what am I quietly betting on, and when does that bet become unreasonable? Sort the implications by how much they'd actually change what I do on Monday morning, and please include the ones that cut against how ML engineers like me instinctively operate (we mostly ship on vibes plus a dashboard and call it done).

One more thing I'm unsure about: is this even a clean dichotomy, or is it more of a spectrum? It reminds me of the sound-but-incomplete vs complete-but-unsound tradeoff in program analysis, where nobody gets both and the interesting part is what you give up. If the alignment debate is structurally like that, I'd like to know which horn is which, and whether the people insisting on worst-case guarantees are basically saying they don't trust the incompleteness, or saying something else entirely that doesn't map onto my analogy at all.
