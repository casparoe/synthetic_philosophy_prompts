I am a PhD student in formal epistemology, writing an essay on the reference class problem. The standard line is that the problem afflicts frequency-based accounts of probability and largely dissolves on a Bayesian view, where probabilities are credences and there is no fact of the matter about which class is the right one to condition on. I want the essay to stress-test that dissolution rather than restate it. Consider a case in which an agent's total evidence licenses two conflicting single-case inferences: one treating the case at hand as a random draw from a class A in which E is common, the other treating it as a random draw from a class B in which E is rare, where A and B overlap and neither is contained in the other, so the familiar instruction to prefer the narrower class returns no verdict. Weigh the arguments that a Bayesian can still absorb the case -- by denying that there is any class-selection fact to get right, or by appeal to a principle of total evidence -- against the arguments that the problem merely relocates, into the choice of statistical model or the delineation of admissible evidence. Then reach and defend a verdict: does the reference class problem survive in a broadly Bayesian framework, and if it does, what would a satisfying solution have to look like?
