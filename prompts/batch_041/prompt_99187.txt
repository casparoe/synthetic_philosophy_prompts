Lets do a role-play argument, and I want you to be the dogmatist, not a strawman of one. Your position: you really do accept that if you know that P, then any evidence that P is false is, by that very fact, misleading evidence, so you're entitled to shrug it off and go on believing P. Hold that line the whole time, don't drift into "well, it depends," and keep it conversational -- one paragraph per turn, stay in character until I say stop. You can grant that knowledge isn't infallible and that people are wrong all the time, but the principle stays.

My opening: I want you to reckon with what I'll call a burn. A burn is when you'd have sworn you knew something, you waved off the person contradicting you as obviously misled, and then it turned out you were just wrong. Like: I lent my bike to my brother, so I "knew" it was sitting in his garage, so when my friend said "nah, your bike's at my place" I treated that as exactly the kind of noise your principle says to ignore -- except my brother had lent it to him, so the friend was right and I was the one who'd been wrong the whole time. Everybody's got a pile of burns. And here's the squeeze: from the inside, mid-burn, right before the reveal, it feels exactly like the times I really did know. So either you can tell me, using only what I've actually got at the moment of decision, how to sort the real "I know it, dismiss this" cases from the "I'm about to get burned, don't dismiss this" cases -- or your principle just tells me to dig in whenever I feel certain, and feeling certain is precisely the thing the burn wrecks. And don't answer "in the good case you know and in the bad case you don't," I already handed you that; I'm asking what that word is doing for me when I have to actually decide something. Bonus problem while you're at it: your principle looks order-dependent, since whoever talks first wins -- the second person's evidence is always the "misleading" stuff. That can't be how truth works, so untangle that too.
