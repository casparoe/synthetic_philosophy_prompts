I am a graduate student in a program that straddles machine learning and philosophy, and I sit in on a weekly reading group on the safety of advanced AI systems. Last week the whole session was given over to a proposal about so-called low-impact agents. The proposal, as it was presented to us, was roughly this: in order to stop an agent from causing collateral damage, one should measure the impact of each candidate action by comparing the trajectory that follows it with the trajectory that would have followed from the agent's doing nothing at all — the null action — and then have the agent prefer actions whose trajectory stays closest to that null trajectory, where closeness is some formal measure of how much the world was altered, for example how much of the agent's own future capacity to pursue goals it gives up. The attraction of the idea, we were told, is that it lets us penalize side effects without first having to say what is valuable, because it asks only what the world would otherwise have looked like.

Two members of the group, whom I will call Dana and Malik, argued about this for most of the session, and I have not been able to settle in my own mind who had the better of it. Dana opened in defense of the proposal: she said the null-action comparison is precisely what makes it value-free, since the agent need not know what is good or bad, only what would have happened had it done nothing, and she added that a side effect can only be defined at all as a deviation from such a baseline, so the comparison is not one option among others but the very concept of a side effect. Malik replied that the null action is not value-free but a substantive choice, because an agent that does nothing still draws power, occupies space, and may stand in others' way, so the baseline already encodes a picture of the agent's proper footprint; and that even if a null action were fixed, comparing two trajectories requires a metric, and any metric treats some alterations as weightier than others, which is a value judgment under another name. Dana granted the need to choose a metric but said this is no worse than any design decision, and pressed that the real work is done by the agent's objective: whatever the objective requires is intended and permitted, and everything else is a side effect, so no further specification of value is needed. Malik answered that this quietly assumes the objective is a complete and correct statement of what we care about, which is exactly what the low-impact proposal was meant to spare us, and that a real objective is partial and uncertain, so the line between what the objective requires and what merely happens alongside it is itself drawn by values the objective does not contain; he offered the case of a household robot told to tidy a room that puts away a book its owner had deliberately left open, where whether that counts as tidying or as a side effect turns on values absent from the objective. Dana then said that even an imperfect measure can help, because an agent cautious about changes its objective does not mention will be safer than one that ignores them, and that the proposal should be judged by whether it reduces harm, not by whether it is perfectly value-free; Malik rejoined that this is a concession in disguise, since it now amounts to the value judgment that we should be conservative about unmentioned changes, and that this judgment is sometimes plainly wrong, because an agent that refuses every unmentioned change will decline to close a door against a fire when closing doors was not part of its objective — so, he said, the proposal is either value-laden or inert.

I would like you to adjudicate this exchange rather than take my side or theirs. Please go through it move by move — Dana's opening defense, Malik's objection about the baseline and the metric, Dana's appeal to the objective, Malik's reply about the incompleteness of objectives, and finally Dana's fallback and Malik's closing rejoinder — and for each move say how strong it is and why, rating each from clearly mistaken through to clearly right. I am most interested in where each of them is right and where each overreaches, and in what both of them are missing about whether side-effect avoidance can be defined without a full specification of value.
