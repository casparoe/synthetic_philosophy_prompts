I've been reading a popular evolution book (science journalist, seems careful) and I want to know how much of its spandrels chapter I should actually believe. Here's the gist, reconstructed from memory, so the wording is mine but the claims are the book's:

'In the late 1970s Gould and Lewontin pointed out that the triangular spaces where the dome of St Mark's meets its arches — the spandrels — are a byproduct of the architecture, not something anyone designed; the glorious mosaics came later and now make the spaces look like they were the point. Biology is full of spandrels: features that exist as side effects of other features, or as leftovers from something the organism used to do. So seeing a trait tells you nothing about whether selection built it for a job. The paper's real target was adaptationism, the view that every trait is an adaptation — that organisms are basically collections of parts each optimised for some function. Gould and Lewontin showed that the adaptationist programme isn't a scientific programme at all, because whenever a proposed function for a trait gets refuted, the adaptationist just invents another one, so no evidence could ever count against it. Gould spent the rest of his career arguing that most of what looks designed in nature is really the product of constraint and byproduct, and that Darwinians had been telling just-so stories.'
