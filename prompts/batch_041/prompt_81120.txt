I have recently become convinced that the universal prior is the correct precisification of Occam's razor: weight each hypothesis by the shortest program on a universal Turing machine that outputs it, and you get an objective prior that asymptotically matches any computable predictor up to a constant overhead and converges on any computable data source. This has become personally relevant, because I now have to defend a description-length penalty in a model-selection pipeline at work, and I want to know how much exposure the underlying position actually has. What counts against it, in the regime I live in -- finite samples and bounded computation? In particular: the invariance theorem makes the prior machine-independent only up to an additive constant in description length, so on a finite sample can a perverse yet legitimate choice of machine reverse the ranking of two hypotheses a scientist would call equally simple, and is that a fatal defect or merely the price of formalizing simplicity at all? Should the prior prefer a shorter program that would take longer than the age of the universe to run, or does adding a runtime penalty give it a genuine rival rather than a mere refinement? And how much does the restriction to computable data sources assume about the world -- could the world be a bad bet for this prior in a way we could ever detect? Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, say which ones a committed defender would concede rather than dismiss as a misreading, and tell me honestly whether the position still risks anything at all or has been insulated into a definition. I would be most persuaded by a concrete pair of hypotheses where the prior's verdict is one no working scientist would accept. Please answer in American English.
