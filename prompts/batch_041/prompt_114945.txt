I run a monthly reading group for grad students from outside philosophy -- mostly biologists, a couple of economists -- and next month I've got one 90-minute session on whether insects, fish, and other invertebrates are conscious and can suffer. Dont explain the topic to me, plan the session: what has to be in place before the question even makes sense to them, the one example I should build the whole thing on and what it needs to have and not have, the misconceptions they'll walk in with and the order they'll surface in and exactly where in my sequence I head each one off, how I'll know at the end that they got the question rather than just the vocabulary, and what I should tell them we're deliberately not settling at this level.

Two things I want baked in. First, I keep watching students fall into what I've been calling the reflex trap -- reading any withdrawal or avoidance response as evidence of suffering -- and the resemblance trap -- treating how mammal-like the nervous system is as the test for a mind. I want the sequence to make both of those feel like temptations they've been led into on purpose and then defused, not assertions I hand down from the front, and I definately want the payoff to be the trick of leaning only on evidence that rival theories of consciousness would all have to accept anyway, rather than picking a favorite theory and letting it decide the case. Second, I want to land somewhere more useful than a ranked list of maybe-conscious animals: I want them to see why the practical upshot is that the question stops being "which ones are conscious" and becomes "whose interests do we treat as live possibilities when we decide what to do," and why that shift moves the burden of proof. And don't let them settle into the idea that this is just an empirical gap that better data will eventually close -- the session should make the conceptual part of that visible.
