Last night I lost an argument, or at least failed to win one, with a friend who works in actuarial science, and the dispute was about a sentence I teach but had never had to defend at that altitude. The sentence was of the form "there is a 35 percent chance that this particular person will do X." My friend insists that such a sentence states a fact about the person; I kept insisting that it states a fact only about a class the speaker has silently chosen, and that nothing in the case determines which class that is. He produced the obvious rejoinder — you simply take the narrowest reference class that is still statistically relevant to the outcome — and I said that this either regresses until the class is the person alone, at which point the frequency is zero or one and tells you nothing, or it stops at an arbitrary point. We went around that loop four or five times and neither of us could close it. I would like my students to feel exactly where the loop is, from the inside, rather than hear me describe it.

I run a philosophy of science seminar: twelve students, mostly second- and third-year, and I have a single ninety-minute session. Design a classroom activity — a game or a structured exercise — that stages the reference class problem so that the students generate it themselves. I want them to commit, in writing, to a probability for a single case and to the reference class they used, before they have any idea that the choice of class is the interesting part; then I want the exercise to make the alternative reference classes impossible to ignore, and to let them discover for themselves that the rule they reach for does not terminate and does not help. By the end they should see three things: that the number was not read off the case but imposed by a class they chose; that for any class they name, a defensible alternative class yields a materially different number; and that the usual repair either regresses to the singleton or halts arbitrarily. The idea has to show up in what happens to them, not in what I say afterward.

Give me the full apparatus: the case or cases to hand out, the materials, the rules and the scoring, a timeline for the ninety minutes, and the moves I make as facilitator to keep the exercise from collapsing early. I especially want the mechanism by which the students are induced to choose a reference class without noticing that they are choosing, and the moment at which the alternative classes are revealed — I want that to land as a surprise rather than as a lesson. Then the debrief: map what happened at each stage onto the parts of the problem — the frequency interpretation and where it requires a class, the single-case problem, statistical relevance and the narrowest-class rule, and the subjectivist retreat in which the number is merely a degree of belief and the class is whatever the believer happens to find salient. Note which of those the activity actually earns and which I have to supply by talking.

Two further things. First, tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle it — I am particularly worried that the students will litigate the base rates instead of the choice of class, that they will refuse to commit to a single number, or that the scoring will make them think the number is what is being graded rather than the class. Second, be honest about what this activity cannot reproduce. I suspect it can make the conflict vivid and can kill the naive narrowest-class fix, but that it cannot reproduce the positive proposals on either side, nor the metaphysical question of whether there is any fact of the matter about the individual's chance at all. Say so, and say what I will have to add myself for the session to amount to more than a demonstration that a problem exists.
