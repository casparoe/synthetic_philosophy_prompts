I was at dinner last week with two friends — one does brand strategy for a clothing company, the other runs HR for a chain of grocery stores — and I realised halfway through that they were both doing the exact same thing and neither of them noticed. My brand friend kept saying things like "Gen Z wants authenticity, they can smell an ad" and "Millennials will pay for experiences but not for objects," and my HR friend kept saying "Gen Z won't take a phone call" and "Millennials need constant feedback." And it struck me that this is now just how everyone talks about taste, work habits, politics, loneliness, spending, everything: you sort people by the year they were born into Boomer, Gen X, Millennial, Gen Z, and then you use the label to explain the behaviour. I'll admit I do it too — my last novel got a jacket blurb calling it "a novel for Millennials," whatever that means, and I didn't object. So here's what I want to work out. What does this practice actually presuppose? Does it only make sense if generations are real kinds — things in the world with something like a shared essence, a formative experience baked into everyone born in the window — or could all of it survive on a deflationary view where "Millennial" is just a name we hang on an arbitrary slice of birth years and the apparent differences are really just age effects (a 26 year old is broke and hates meetings because they're 26 and broke, not because of when they were born)? Which rival views does the practice quietly rule out, and does it even need the realist reading, or is the realist reading just something people slip into out of convenience?

The reason I can't stop chewing on it is that the practice seems to be at war with itself. The cutoffs are just birth years somebody picked — nobody agrees where Gen X ends and Millennials start, they've been redrawn, and the people who publish these ranges will happily tell you the boundaries are fuzzy conveniences, that a generation is an analytical tool and not a fault line in nature. Fine. But then the same people, and all the rest of us, turn around and explain a 30 year old's politics by saying "Millennials." And we do this even when there's a much more obvious explanation sitting right there — that they're 30, that they're renting, that they were 19 during a particular recession. You also can't pull the effects apart from a single snapshot: what looks like a generation being different might be people being young, or a period being bad, and there's no way to tell from one survey. Plus generations get named after the fact by people who weren't in them, and the same cohort gets re-described every twenty years. So: reconstruct for me the implicit theory of what a generation is that this whole way of talking commits us to, and be honest about the difference between what the practice strictly presupposes and what it merely fits comfortably with — I suspect a lot of it is underdetermined and would be licensed by several incompatible theories of social kinds at once. If the parts presuppose inconsistent things, say which part has to give. And then tell me what actually changes if you swap the realist reading for the deflationary one — concretely, in my friend's marketing decks, in the HR policy that says don't call the Zoomers, and in the fact that my publisher thinks "a novel for Millennials" is a selling point. Is there a middle position where generations are real social kinds without essences, real because shared events or because the label itself changes how people behave? And if so, is that what everyone was presupposing all along, or is it a repair job?
