I'm assembling materials for a seminar on the ethics of everyday life, and I keep watching two friends have the same unproductive fight. One holds that the small local conventions we live among — how much to tip, where to stand in a queue, whether to cross against the light on an empty street — can be genuine moral obligations, so that a visitor who ignores a local tipping expectation, or who cuts into a queue, is doing something morally wrong and not merely gauche. The other insists these are at most matters of etiquette or prudence, and that a visitor is morally free to deviate whenever no one is harmed or deceived. They have been circling each other for months, and I suspect they are in part talking past each other, so I don't want another round of the argument; I want the document the two of them should have produced together.

Please draft the joint statement of an adversarial collaboration between these two camps — call them the convention-realists and the convention-minimalists — on the moral status of everyday conventions and on what visitors owe to local ones. Begin with a numbered section of agreed claims: what both sides will grant about what a convention is, what makes one "local," the difference between a convention's being expected and its being enforced, what counts as harm or unfairness when a convention is broken, and what (if anything) a visitor owes a community she has not joined and will soon leave. Then a disagreements table. For each surviving disagreement, give the sub-question in neutral terms; then each side's own position written in wording that side would accept; then the crux — the smaller question such that settling it would move at least one side; and, for each crux, a case, thought experiment, or piece of evidence that both sides agree would bear on it, with each side's prediction recorded in advance. Include at least one crux turning on whether a convention's being a fair solution to a shared problem gives it moral force beyond the harm its breach would cause, and at least one on whether such obligations are owed to participants who have tacitly accepted the practice rather than to anyone who merely passes through it.

To keep the whole thing from collapsing into the examples everyone reaches for, build the agreed claims and the cruxes around some deliberately awkward cases: a town where a service charge is already included in the menu price and leaving an extra tip reads as a mild insult, so that the visitor's home instinct to tip generously is itself the deviation; a queue that is really a take-a-number system with an informal courtesy gap for people who need to slip out; a convention that is visibly dying, so that many locals no longer observe it themselves; and a purely coordinating convention, such as which side of a narrow stairway to walk on, whose breach is costly to one party and costless to everyone else. Finally, say plainly whether any part of the two friends' quarrel is merely verbal — for instance, whether they mean different things by "moral obligation," one requiring decisive weight and the other any genuine normative reason — and if so, which part and why. There is no need to cite any literature; argue from the cases.
