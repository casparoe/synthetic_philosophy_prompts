I'm a backend engineer, no philosophy training, and I've gone down a rabbit hole on Nomic — Peter Suber's game from 1982 where changing the rules is itself a move, players vote on the changes, and even the rule about how you change rules is fair game. The part that hooked me: in his original setup the rules come in two tiers, mutable and immutable, and an immutable rule has to be transmuted (turned mutable) before you can touch it, with immutable beating mutable when they clash. He says the whole point of the two tiers is "self-paternalism" — insurance against your own future weak moments, so a brief wave of fanaticism can't undo decades of structure. The victory condition is just first to 100 points, and he's admitted he made it deliberately boring so people would immediately rewrite it.

I want to use that as a lens on a real episode: the fight over India's basic structure doctrine, around 1973. What I know: the Constitution has an amendment procedure, Article 368, and for a while the Supreme Court said Parliament could amend anything, including Article 368 itself. In 1967 (Golaknath) it flipped and said fundamental rights were off-limits; Parliament then passed amendments to overturn that. Then in Kesavananda Bharati v. State of Kerala (1973), a 13-judge bench split 7-6 and held that Parliament can amend the Constitution but can't destroy its "basic structure" or essential features — except nobody ever wrote down a list of what those features are, the court just decides case by case. Later the court used the doctrine to strike down the 39th Amendment (Indira Nehru Gandhi v. Raj Narain, 1975) and parts of the 42nd (Minerva Mills, 1980). So the real question underneath it: is an amendment that removes your ability to amend still an amendment?

Map the episode onto Nomic properly, in both directions, and be disciplined about it:

- Who's the player, what's a move, what's the rulebook, and who's the referee in each case? In Nomic there's an agreed ruleset, a winning condition, and everyone opted in; in India there's no author of the game and no opt-out. Does that break the analogy or just relocate who counts as a player?

- The mutable/immutable split. India's basic structure smells like an immutable tier, but it's unwritten and discovered by the referee case by case rather than declared in the rules. Suber's "immutable" rules aren't literally immutable either — they're just harder to change and given logical priority. Is India's doctrine the same kind of thing, or a different animal? And is an unwritten immutable core even expressible in Nomic, which seems to need its rules written down? If not, is that a limitation of the game or of my analogy?

- Suber frames the "paradox of self-amendment" as something that dissolves rather than bites (I'm probably mangling this — correct me). Does the Indian court's move amount to the same dissolution, or something else? Concretely: is it equivalent to adding a Nomic rule that says "no rule change may be adopted if it would make some future rule change impossible"? Is that rule even consistent? Can Nomic host it without the referee stepping outside the game to enforce it?

- Does Nomic have an escape hatch India doesn't? I have a vague memory of a rule about what happens when the rules become contradictory or can't decide whether a move is legal — if that's real, is it doing the same work the court does, or is it a completely different kind of exit?
