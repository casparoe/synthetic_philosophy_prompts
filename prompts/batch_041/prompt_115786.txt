I'm writing the final for my upper-level epistemology seminar (mostly 3rd/4th years, plus three MA students who are just along for the ride) and I need one long essay question on van Fraassen's Reflection principle — 45 minutes, 25 marks. The problem is that every model answer I get is the same three beats: state the schema (my credence in A, given that my future credence in A is x, has to be x), say it's basically conditionalization in a wig, then wheel out the drug case where you know that tomorrow you'll be given a pill that makes you certain you're Napoleon, and pronounce Reflection dead. I want a question where those three beats are worth maybe 8 of the 25 and the rest has to be earned by actually thinking.

What I've been drilling into them all term is that Reflection is either trivially true or plainly false depending on what you feed it, and I want the exam to make them pick a side. If your future self is a conditionalizer, then your future credence in A is just your present credence in A conditional on whatever your total evidence will be by then, and the event "my future credence in A is x" is a union of those evidence-cells — so Reflection falls out of the probability calculus for free. On that reading it isnt a norm at all, its a theorem. But the moment the future credence isnt a conditionalization of the present one — because future-you is drugged, or because you're deferring to a machine or an expert, or because you'll have forgotten your own reasoning — Reflection suddenly says something substantive, and then it tends to be just false. I also want them to see that the expectation version (my credence now should equal my expected future credence) is much weaker than the conditional version: the expectation version only says the future credence is unbiased on average, while Reflection says it's sufficient — that once you've got the number, nothing else you know matters.

So here's the case I want to hang it on. I made it up, so they can't have read it anywhere. Ada is certain that tomorrow morning her forecasting service will text her a number, either 0.8 or 0.2 — she thinks it's a coin flip which — and that she will then just adopt that number as her own credence that it will rain, no further thought. She also knows the service's record: on days it texts 0.8 it rains about 30% of the time, and on days it texts 0.2 it rains about 70% of the time. Her credence in rain right now is 0.5.

Please write the question, a model answer of about 650 words, and a marking scheme built on five must-hit points worth 25 marks between them, each with a one-line note on how candidates typically blow that particular point.
