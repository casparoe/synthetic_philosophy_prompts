I am taking a comparative philosophy course on the self and the community — roughly half the term on Confucian and other relational conceptions of the person, half on the liberal-individualist tradition — and next week each of us must circulate a short position statement for the others to attack. I have drafted mine, but I have a nagging sense that I have assembled a package that cannot all be true at once, and I would like an audit before I send it round. Here is where I have landed.

(1) A person is socially constituted: her identity, her values, and even her capacity for practical reasoning are formed by — and partly consist in — her relationships, roles, and community. There is no self that exists prior to or apart from its attachments. The claim that a person simply is the sum of her roles, with nothing standing behind them, is closer to the truth than the picture of an individual who merely happens to occupy roles.

(2) An action is morally attributable to an agent only if she could have critically assessed the norms she was acting on and endorsed or rejected them. Autonomy in this sense is a precondition of responsibility, not an optional extra.

(3) Some moral standards hold across cultures: certain practices are wrong whatever the local community believes, so no tradition is beyond criticism from outside.

(4) Treating the individual as the basic unit of moral and political analysis is not a neutral starting point; it encodes one particular, largely modern Western, conception of the person, and it is a mistake to treat it as the default against which other traditions must justify themselves.

(5) In a just society a person must be able to exit the roles and obligations she was born into — an adult child should be free to refuse an expected life of caregiving, or to marry outside the community, even when doing so damages the family.

Which of these can be held together, and which cannot? I am fairly sure at least one pair is in outright conflict, and I suspect at least one other pair only looks like one. Tell me which is which; rank the genuine conflicts by how much they cost me and say what each would force me to give up; and then commit to the single revision that resolves the most while conceding the least. I want a definite verdict on which commitment actually has to go, with your reasons, rather than a survey that leaves the choice to me.
