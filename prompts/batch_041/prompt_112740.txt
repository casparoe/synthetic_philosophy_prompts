Bit of an odd one, but I've got myself stuck and I'd rather get a straight answer than another "it depends."

I've been messing around at home with a small system where a string of letters gets rewritten by rules. The rules are little recipes keyed to pairs of letters -- you read along the string, find a pair, swap in something else -- and I finally found a string that, after a long chain of rewrites, spits out an exact copy of itself. Felt like magic. My friend, who does molecular biology, was unimpressed: she says the string isn't doing anything, the recipes are doing the copying, and I wrote the recipes, so it's just me copying it with extra steps. Fair enough. But then I tried putting the recipes inside the string, so the string encodes the very machinery that reads it, and I hit a wall: something has to read the string before the string can build its own reader, so I had to hand-seed the first reader. She says that proves her point -- I'm the reader, so I'm the copier, and it never bottoms out.

I dont think she's just being a pedant, but I also don't think she's obviously right. She did concede that the genuinely strange thing about real cells isn't that they copy, it's that the instructions for the copying machine are written in the same stuff that gets copied, and that the traffic only runs one way. I'm writing this up for my blog and I don't want to be the guy claiming artificial life when what i built is a fancy for-loop.

So: what is the argument between us actually about; does her "you're the copier" line have a principled version, or is it a regress that would swallow anything; what would my string have to contain before it's honest to call it self-replicating; is the chicken-and-egg wall fatal, or is that just what every cell deals with; and how should I describe the project so I'm not overclaiming? Exactly five numbered points, a sentence or two each, no intro and no wrap-up.
