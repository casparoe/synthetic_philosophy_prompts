I'm a retired biologist — 35 years in population genetics, mostly fish, so I know what a species is supposed to be — and since I retired I've been reading philosophy and sociology on the side, which is how I got into this. My neighbor is a sociologist, great guy, and he's dead set on the idea that generations — Boomers, Millennials, Gen Z, the whole parade — are real things, not just a marketing label someone invented to sell sneakers. His favorite move is to say a generation is basically a species. A cohort gets born, shares a formative environment, grows into its own character, and eventually dies off and gets replaced by the next one. He says: you accept that species are real units in biology, so you should accept that generations are real units in history. And he uses that to license treating 'Gen Z' as an explanatory category — they vote like this, they work like this — the same way I'd explain a fin's shape by pointing to the species it belongs to. He even says the boundaries between generations are like speciation events.

I have a strong feeling the analogy is doing far too much of the work, but I can't say where it goes wrong, because I know species too well and generations not at all. Can you audit it for me? I'd like:

- the correspondence laid out explicitly (a table is fine): what plays the role of the population, the gene pool, the reproductive barrier, the shared/selective environment, heritable variation, and extinction;
- where the mapping breaks — and, importantly, which of the breaks actually matter for what he wants the analogy to do, versus the ones that are just noise;
- what the species picture quietly smuggles in about generations that generations don't actually have;
- and then the verdict: does his conclusion — generations are objective, causally-unified kinds — survive in any form once the disanalogies are on the table, or was the analogy carrying the whole claim? If there's a better analogy for what a generation really is, I want that too.

One thing that nags me: a statistician friend once told me you can't cleanly separate the effect of being born in a given year from the effect of living through a given period from the effect of just being young, and that this is a known headache with a name. I don't remember the name. If that's relevant, work it in.

Also — I've been burned before by confident-sounding answers that turned out to be contested among the people who actually study this, so please tell me how sure you are of each part, and flag the single point where you're least sure. And definately don't hand me a reading list; I just want the reasoning.
