I do a small interview podcast about big-picture philosophy -- anthropics, decision theory, the end of the world, that sort of thing -- and next weeks guest has spent most of her career defending the doomsday argument. Her new book isnt a restatement of it: her line is that the argument isnt really about extinction, it's a constraint on any estimate of how many people there will ever be, so anyone doing longtermist expected-value math without letting it shrink their number is cheating. That claim is what I want the questions aimed at, not the argument itself, which my listeners have heard a hundred times.

Shes also very good at killing objections with a single move: whatever you raise, she says it only works if you've already accepted self-indication, and the room goes quiet. I want a run of questions that makes that move progressively harder to use.

Give me eight to ten questions, each with a follow-up keyed to the answer she's most likely to give. At least one question each on:

- the reference class -- why humans ever born, and not primates, or mammals, or every observer in the universe, and what happens to the conclusion when the class gets bigger, including minds we might build or meet;
- the self-sampling versus self-indication fork, and whether she can keep the doomsday conclusion without swallowing the presumptuous philosopher case, where two hypotheses about how many observers the universe contains fit all our evidence equally well and one of them contains vastly more observers than the other;
- the worry that the same rank-in-a-class reasoning can be run on anything you're a member of (the number of times I'll blink, the number of flats I'll live in), so what stops it from proving too much;
- the charge that the argument uses my birth rank while ignoring everything else I know about the world -- which if taken seriously looks like it makes the argument disappear, and maybe makes every anthropic argument disappear;
- what it's supposed to do for action, since if it's right it also shrinks the number of future people whose risk we're trying to reduce.

Then two cases I want to spring on her, one question each. First: we learn tomorrow that another intelligent lineage exists in the galaxy with a history roughly as long as ours -- does the argument now predict that the total number of such lineages is small, and isnt that in tension with what we just found? Second: if we build a trillion digital minds, do they count as observers for the purposes of the argument, and if they do, does that make our rank early or late, and does it change what we should do about building them?

End on what would actually count as evidence against the argument -- something specific enough that we could go and look, not 'a better theory of anthropics'.
