I am assembling the final examination for an upper-level undergraduate seminar on realism and anti-realism, and I need a single long-form essay question on the knowability paradox. The students will already have seen the standard derivation in lecture, so a question that asks them to reproduce it, or to summarise the familiar move of restricting the knowability principle, would reward recall rather than understanding. I want the question to oblige candidates to reason about the derivation itself, and to that end I intend to hand them a specific proposed repair and make them decide whether it does any work. Please write the question as the candidates would see it, a model answer of about 700 words, and a marking scheme.

The setup the students will be given is as follows. The knowability principle states that every truth is knowable: for any proposition p, if p is true then it is possible that p be known. Grant two further assumptions about knowledge — that it is factive (if p is known then p is true), and that it distributes over conjunction (if a conjunction is known then each of its conjuncts is known). These entail that no one can know the conjunction of p with the proposition that nobody knows that p. For if that conjunction were known, distribution would yield knowledge both of p and of the proposition that nobody knows that p, and factivity would make the second entail that p is not known, contradicting the first. So the conjunction is unknowable. Yet if p is a truth that nobody in fact knows, the conjunction of p with the proposition that nobody knows that p is itself a truth, and the knowability principle pronounces it knowable. That is a contradiction, and so the principle implies that there are no unknown truths. An anti-realist who wishes to retain the principle must restrict it in some way.

A student in the class has proposed the following repair, and I want the examination question to turn on it: the knowability principle should be asserted only of propositions that are not self-referential, on the model of the restrictions that keep the liar and other semantic paradoxes at bay. The thought is that the paradox is a self-reference paradox, and that fencing the principle off from self-referential propositions will fence it off from the trouble.

The question should require the candidate to do three things. First, to determine whether this self-reference restriction actually blocks the derivation, and if it does not, to identify precisely what it fails to engage. Second, to state and defend a restriction that does block the derivation. Third, to say plainly what that restriction costs the anti-realist — that is, what must be conceded or abandoned for the knowability principle to remain at once non-trivial and anti-realist in spirit.

The model answer should show why the offending proposition is not self-referential in the relevant sense, so that the proposed restriction leaves the derivation untouched; should explain what feature of the offending proposition any workable restriction would have to exclude; should propose such a restriction; and should confront the dilemma that such a restriction is either circular — it excludes the proposition by a condition that already invokes knowability, so that the principle collapses into the claim that knowable truths are knowable — or non-circular and ad hoc, or broad enough to strip the principle of its anti-realist content. It should also say what becomes of the anti-realist who tries to escape by rejecting one of the other assumptions instead — factivity, distribution over conjunction, or classical logic — and why each of those routes is either untenable or ineffective.

The marking scheme should be out of 20 and should separate a first-class answer from a merely competent one by naming the specific moves each must make, with a one-line note for each move on how candidates typically get it wrong. The examination is a fifty-minute in-class final, and this essay is the only question on the paper.
