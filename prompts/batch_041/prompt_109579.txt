I have been following rationalist and adjacent decision-theory blogs for years, and I keep returning to Pascal's mugging not as a curiosity but as a stress test for how a bounded agent should actually live. Suppose the mugger is not offering literally infinite utility but a finite reward that dwarfs the value of the entire observable universe, and suppose there are many such muggers, each with mutually exclusive offers, each demanding a small but non-zero share of your resources, and you have neither the time nor the computational capacity to evaluate them all. I am interested in the most important implications of this set-up, not in a survey of the literature, and I do not want a bibliography or a reading list.

Please set out what you take to be the most consequential implications for (a) the choice between expected-utility maximisation and rival decision rules such as maximin, risk-aversion, or lexicographic thresholds; (b) the epistemology of testimony when the stakes are astronomically large and the evidence is thin; (c) the practice of assigning and acting on tiny probabilities in domains like existential risk, AI safety, and effective altruism; and (d) the broader question of whether a bounded agent can be rational at all if it must sometimes ignore Pascal-mugging-style considerations in order to get anything done. Where two implications pull in opposite directions, say so, and tell me which one you think a rationalist should treat as the more fundamental constraint. I would rather have a compact map of the tensions than a list of conclusions.
