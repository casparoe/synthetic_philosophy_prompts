I'm in a seminar on perception and we've spent what feels like three weeks circling the old puzzle about the person who's been blind since birth, can tell a cube from a sphere by touch, and then gets their sight back — does he know which is which just by looking? Everyone nods along and then nothing gets decided, and I've realised why: the classic version of the case is compatable with basically every position at once, so it can't discriminate between them. What I want from you is a version built to order that actually does the work. My half-formed idea, take it or leave it: the subject has been blind from birth, no light perception at all, and for a few years before the operation he's been using one of those sensory substitution rigs — camera on the head, signal driving a grid of vibrating pins against his back or his tongue, the sort of thing people really do use — and he's gotten good with it, so he's had a lot of practice with how the signal changes as he moves, turns, leans in, reaches out. By the time the bandages come off he has a deep grip on the laws connecting his own movements to changes in the input, he's just never had any of it in a visual format.

The three positions I want to separate: (a) the old empiricist line — recognising across the senses is learned from experienced correlations between them, so with no prior visual experience he should fail flatly; (b) the nativist line — the visual system arrives with its own spatial machinery, so he should recognise the shapes straight away, history be damned; and (c) my own hunch, which is that seeing a shape is really a skill, and the skill is about the movement-dependence of the input rather than about which organ is delivering it — on this view he should succeed, but with a signature: fine when he's allowed to move around the object and watch the signal change, hopeless when the thing is just flashed at him for half a second. Build the scenario so that those three verdicts fall out of the three positions and aren't smuggled into the story.

Constraints: keep the medical side mundane (surgery and devices that actually exist, no magic, no perfect prosthetics), the scenario itself no longer than about 200 words, and don't give him any visual experience whatsoever before the operation.
