I'm building a small collection of philosophical short fiction and I need one on Pascal's tangle about justice and force — the bit where justice without force is useless and force without justice is just thuggery, but the real kicker is his claim that the justice we actually obey isnt grounded in reason at all: it's whatever custom has settled on and force keeps settled, which is why the move everyone makes is to call the strong "just" rather than make the just strong. And a further strand of his: when people do notice this, they come in three flavors — most never notice and take the current order to be just by nature; a second batch see it's arbitrary and rage that it's only force in a wig; a third batch see it exactly the same way and obey anyway, because established beats true. (I'm paraphrasing badly and probably mangling him — you don't need the fragments, the above is the whole brief.)

Give me a short story that lives inside that, 1500-2500 words, concrete place and people, a real plot, characters with no idea they're illustrating a thesis. Please dont do the stock version — lone brave dissident against a cartoon tyrant. I want the force side to have a genuine case, and I want the person who sees through the order to be the one who has to choose. Take a definite line: plant the story firmly in one of those three positions and let the ending deliver a verdict instead of shrugging.

Start with the story itself, no preamble. Then, after the story, three or four sentences on where you took liberties with the idea or leaned on a contested reading of it — e.g. if you stacked the deck for the "just obey anyway" crowd, own up to it.
