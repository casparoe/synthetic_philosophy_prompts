I went vegetarian about six months ago, mostly for climate reasons, and I'm already sick of the "but what difference does one person make" conversation — mainly because I never have a decent answer to it. A friend of mine in a philosphy PhD pointed me at the inefficacy objection and the complicity replies (the idea that I'm not causing the harm, I'm doing my part in something that does), and I get the shape of it, but every version I've read seems argued over toy cases.

So run a real one through it. Britain, 1791: Wilberforce's abolition bill has just been voted down, and the abolitionists start a boycott of West Indian sugar — a pamphlet tells readers that "If we purchase the commodity we participate in the crime", hundreds of thousands of people pledge to give it up. No one who stopped putting sugar in their tea did anything to the plantation economy; the act is a rounding error. And yet something happened politically. Map it: who counts as a contributor, what is the act, is the best description causing harm, being complicit in a wrong, or doing my bit in a collective project — and then tell me where the theory runs out. I've got a hunch I want you to test, which is that the boycott didn't work by reducing harm at all but by making it visible how many people cared and changing what was politically sayable, in which case the whole causal-individual framing is asking the wrong question. Does the complicity literature have room for that, or does it just relabel the same causal story? And what does the case do to the theory — friendly example, or one it never anticipated?
