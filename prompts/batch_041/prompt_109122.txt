OK so I've been chewing on logical uncertainty for a few months now (longtime reader of the rationalist blogs, the kind of person who has opinions about whether Bayes is a theorem or a lifestyle), and I've built up a pile of commitments that I'm starting to suspect dont actually fit together. I don't want a survey of what other people think or a reading list — I want an audit of my own pile: which of these are pulling against each other, which pairs only look like they conflict, how bad each real conflict is, and what's the cheapest single thing to weaken that cleans up the most.

Here's the pile. (1) Credences are probabilities and rational change of mind is conditionalization on evidence — there's no second, non-probabilistic mode of rational endorsement that gets to run in parallel and hand me extra conclusions. (2) A bounded agent can rationally hold a credence strictly between 0 and 1 in a mathematical statement it can neither prove nor refute, and that credence is an opinion about the statement itself, not merely about which proofs it will eventually stumble on. (3) Heuristic arguments are real evidence. Concretely, I take it as genuine evidence for a claim that its falsity would require a giant coincidence — that if it were false, the world would have had to arrange itself specifically to look like it's true. (4) The engine behind (3) is the presumption of independence, and the version I've actually been running with is this: a property should be presumed to hold of an object unless the shortest description of that object has to mention the property, or something correlated with it. Randomness is incompressibility, structure is compressibility, and the presumption is just "don't posit structure you haven't paid for." That's the version I want audited, not a hand-wavy "assume it's random." (5) Mathematical realism: mathematical statements have determinate truth values that don't depend on us or on what any bounded reasoner can do, so when I say I'm 0.7 on Goldbach I'm 0.7 on something already settled. (6) Idealization: the standard of correctness in rationality is what an ideal reasoner would do; our boundedness explains why we fall short of it, it doesn't change what counts as rational.

I keep getting a bad feeling about this pile but I cant localize it — every time I try to say what's wrong I end up defending the thing I was about to attack, so I'd rather you do the tracing than me. Please rank the tensions by severity, separate the ones that are real from the ones that dissolve once stated precisely, and for each real one say exactly what has to give and what it costs. And if some of these are so vague that they can't actually conflict with anything yet, say so — I'd rather be told to sharpen a claim than get a fake diagnosis.
