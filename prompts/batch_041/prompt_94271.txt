I'm in a seminar on moral obligation and we've been on promising for three weeks now, and I've ended up with an argument I cant shake. It's annoying because I'm pretty sure the conclusion is false and I can't see the bad step. Here it is. (1) What makes breaking a promise wrong is that it lets down someone who was counting on you — the whole point of promising is that the promisee is entitled to rely on you. (2) If that's the ground, the words and the little ritual aren't doing independent work: what binds me is the reliance, not the saying. (3) But I can get someone counting on me without promising anyone anything — I tell a friend I'll be at the airport, I let my brother plan his wedding around my driving him, or I just keep showing up every Friday until my roommate stops bothering to arrange a ride home. (4) So in all those cases I'm bound to come through exactly as if I'd sworn an oath, and a promise is just a loud way of manufacturing reliance. (5) And then other people can do it to me: my neighbor announces to the whole block party that I'm driving him to the airport; the contractor starts ripping out my kitchen before we've agreed on a price, knowing I'll feel like a heel if I stop him; some stranger reads a post about me online and rearranges his week around my being somewhere. If foreseeing that reliance is enough, I'm bound by all of it. (6) At the limit I'm bound to keep doing whatever anyone has come to expect of me, which would make me a servant of other people's predictions. (7) That can't be right, so (1) is false, and promises must bind in virtue of something other than the reliance they produce.

But then I don't see what's left except the words themselves doing magic, which is exactly the thing that made me want (1) in the first place. (We read the usual stuff early on — Hume on promises as artificial, then the contemporary practice and assurance views — and none of it gave me a way out that I could actually state.)

So: where does the argument go wrong? Is it one of the steps, or is it the either/or I've stuck myself with at the end? I'd honestly rather keep some version of the reliance idea than accept (7), but I want to know what I'd be paying for that. And I'd like you to say which of my cases — the airport, the wedding, the Friday rides, the block party, the contractor, the stranger — actually bind me and which don't, and what distinguishes the real promise cases from the rest if it isn't the reliance. If you think (3) and (5) are smuggling in something that isn't in (1), name it and tell me whether (1) survives once it's out in the open.
