I have never studied philosophy formally, but over the past year or so I have been trying to work out a picture of how communication actually functions, and I have ended up committed to something I would like you to attack. The picture is this: whenever a person means something by an utterance, what they mean is a matter of the thought they intended their audience to grasp, and understanding just is the audience working out that intention — the hearer asks what the speaker could have been getting at, given the words used and what the speaker could reasonably have assumed, and settles on an answer. On this picture, indirectness and politeness are not a separate phenomenon bolted on from outside: a hint, a euphemism, a pointed remark about the weather, a question that is really a request, are all simply cases in which the intention is left for the hearer to infer rather than stated outright. And the literal content of the words is only ever a clue — an extremely good clue, but a clue. I gather something along these lines is associated with Grice, though I arrived at it on my own by brooding about my own conversations, and I am not asking you to explain the received view back to me. Please take the role of a philosopher of language with a strong working knowledge both of pragmatics and of the empirical work on how people actually understand one another, and begin by brainstorming the full space of possible objections before you rank anything, so that you do not simply reach for the famous ones.

Here are the cases I keep returning to, and I would like you to say which of them a defender of my picture would have to concede count against it and which could be waved away. A note on a door saying the occupant will be back in five minutes, with no speaker anywhere in sight; a sign in a shop window; a recorded announcement on a train; a message typed by someone half asleep and understood perfectly by the person receiving it. An exchange in which one person remarks that it has turned chilly, intending only to fill an awkward silence, while their companion hears a request to shut the window and shuts it — has communication occurred, and if so, what has become of the intention? A wedding vow or a formal apology, where what the words accomplish seems fixed by the ceremony rather than by what the speaker privately meant. A phrase of my grandmother's that nobody in the family could translate but which we all somehow understood. The way a small child seems to grasp a hint long before it could articulate anybody's intentions. And, more recently, the fact that people appear to understand what a chatbot is getting at, and to feel understood by it, when there is nothing in there that could have intended anything at all — I will admit in advance that this last one unsettles me most, so I am suspicious of my own eagerness to explain it away. Beyond the cases, I want the arguments: whether the picture survives being qualified so that only some meaning is intention-based, whether that qualification leaves it with any content, and what the picture predicts about misunderstanding, ambiguity and translation that a rival account would not predict. Sort the defeaters by how much they should genuinely move me, from the ones that would sink the picture to the ones that are merely awkward, tell me which of them the position's own defenders would themselves grant, and be honest at the end about whether what remains is a real thesis or a set of hedges. Please write in British English, and do not trouble yourself with citations or a reading list — I would far rather have your reasoning than a bibliography.
