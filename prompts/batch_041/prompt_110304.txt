Last week two of my friends, Ines and Marcus, got into an argument at my dinner table that I completely failed to referee, and I'd like you to take a shot at it. It started with a case a mutual friend of ours hands out in a first-year ethics class: a city's reservoir is silting up, and unless someone opens a sluice the whole town loses its water in three weeks; opening the sluice floods a farmer's fields and wrecks his entire year's crop. He's away and out of contact. Nearly everyone says open it. Then the class gets the version where the farmer is reachable and says no — and most people flip to saying you can't flood his field, even though the numbers are identical. That's the whole thing, no reading required. (I write novels, so whether a made-up scene can prove anything is not an academic question for me.)

(1) Marcus said the flip is a confession. The case only ever worked, he said, because everyone was smuggling in a premise — that a person's say-so outweighs a heap of consequences for strangers — and the scene hid that premise behind a picture. That makes it circular: it moves you only if you already believe the thing it's supposed to establish, and if you don't, you feel nothing and get called cold-blooded.

(2) Ines said that's backwards. The flip is the thing doing the work: vary one feature, watch the verdict move, and you've learned which feature your judgment is actually tracking. A case you can't flip is the one to distrust, because then you're not testing anything. She also said a counterexample to a general claim just is an argument, so the story isn't a trick played on the argument — the story is the argument.

(3) Marcus said the results don't hold still long enough to count as evidence. Change a detail nobody thinks matters, ask people to explain their answers, run the cases in a different order, and the verdicts wander. And unlike a physics experiment, he said, there's no independent check to calibrate against, so a wobbly instrument with no way to test it means you're measuring your own taste and calling it a discovery.

(4) Ines said every method looks like taste when it's young, and the fix for noise is better cases and better controls, not throwing out the observations. She said a verdict on one case still has to answer to every other case and to whatever theory you're holding — take the farmer's consent as decisive here and you've bought a commitment that will bite you somewhere else. Marcus said fine, but notice that what does the biting is the feeling that adding an exception would be ad hoc, and that feeling is the scene again, not an argument.

They're both my friends and I stayed out of it. Can you go through the four moves and say who's right on each one — not just who wins overall — and then tell me whether the reservoir case is doing anything an argument couldn't do, and what both of them are missing? I'd really apprecite it.
