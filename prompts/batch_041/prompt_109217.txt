I'm running a session in a literary translation workshop next month and I want to do Cyrano de Bergerac, because I've half-convinced myself the play is secretly about us. Here's what set me off. Cyrano is a ghostwriter: he feeds Christian the lines, Roxane falls for the face in act one and for the poetry soon after, and she never knows whose poetry it is until the last act, at the convent, when he's dying and reciting the letter she's kept for years and she finally hears the voice that wrote it. And the whole play is in rhymed twelve-syllable couplets, so any English version has to pick between keeping the rhymes and bending the sense, or dropping them and losing the music. Even the duel in act one is a stunt about exactly this: he fights Valvert while composing a ballade, and the refrain lands on the thrust. That's the translator's job description — satisfy the form and the sense at the same time, with no time to stop and think.

What I want is a reading of the play through the thesis Hofstadter spends Le Ton beau de Marot on — that in a poem the form isn't packaging, its part of the meaning, and a translation is a solution to a constraint puzzle rather than a copy of a content that was sitting there waiting to be carried across. And I want you to test a way of putting it that I made up, so don't tell me it's just the standard view, just tell me whether it earns its keep: Cyrano is a translator, and Roxane is a reader who falls in love with a translation and takes it for the original. On that reading the play already has a verdict, and it's Hofstadter's — she wasn't fooled, because the words really were the man, and she says as much when she tells Christian she'd love him even if he were ugly, now that she's read the letters.

But I don't trust it to be that clean. Christian is beautiful and tongue-tied and honest about being a fraud, and Roxane was moved by his looks before any of the words arrived, so the play is also in the body-is-part-of-the-person business. And the play is besotted with its own panache, the white plume, as if the style were the soul — very convenient for a playwright. And the ending kills the man whose words won her, which doesn't feel like a clean vindication of anything.

So give me the honest version, both directions, and tell me whether the play dramatizes something the theory misses. Don't quote the play at me at length, I've read it, and skip the reading list — I know the criticism is out there.

Sections, with these headings in this order: "The setup" (~150 words, just what the play gives us that matters here); "Cyrano as translator" (~400 words, the reading itself — the ghostwriting, the ballade-duel, the couplets, the letters from the siege); "Where the play backs the thesis" (~350 words); "Where the play pushes back" (~350 words, and I want the counter-evidence named scene by scene, not gestured at); and "What the play adds" (~250 words, what a translator gets from it that Hofstadter's argument alone doesn't give).
