I'm a novelist — three books, the last one about a locksmith — and I've gotten myself into a weird side project. My new novel has a character who checks into a private rehab clinic that makes you sign a contract: leave early and they take your house. Writing those scenes got me thinking about Ulysses contracts, and I ended up drafting a short essay (about 2500 words) for a friend's newsletter. It's the first bit of straight nonfiction I've written and I genuinely can't tell if the argument holds up, so I'd love your comments. Here's the spine of it, roughly:

Everyone talks about Ulysses contracts as a way of beating your future self in a fight of wills. I think that's backwards. Odysseus didn't out-will anybody — he changed the world his future self would wake up inside. The ropes were furniture. So a precommitment isn't self-control, it's engineering: you build a room your later self can't leave. Which means its power rests on something nobody talks about. The bound self has to be able to see that the ropes are still tied. If at the moment of temptation you can't verify the constraint is still in place — the clinic has closed, the friend you gave the keys to moved away, the escrow account is just a number on a screen — then you're not bound by anything, you're just a person who remembers making a promise, and memory is exactly the faculty that fails. So every working precommitment needs an auditor, some person or device that certifies the mast is still there. And then the whole question shifts: it's no longer about your willpower, it's about whether the auditor has any reason to keep doing its job. A constitutional clause saying an article can never be repealed is only as strong as the court willing to read it that way. And if the auditor is just you, later — you leave yourself a note, you hide the cigarettes — then you're back where you started, because the note-reader is the same guy you didn't trust. I call this the mast problem, or the auditor's regress when I'm feeling fancy.

What I want from you: is that middle move legit, or am I smuggling something in when I slide from engineering to auditing? And is my claim too strong — is there a real precommitment that works fine without the bound self being able to verify it's still in place? If there is, that's the counterexample I need to deal with, and I can't find one that convinces me. Also, and this matters as much, does it read as interesting to someone who isn't already into this stuff, or is the whole thing just a fancy way of saying promises are hard? The ending is a bit cute and I'm worried it undercuts the argument, so tell me if I should cut it. Be blunt, I'd rather find out now than after its publised.
