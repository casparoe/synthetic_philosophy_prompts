I've been arguing with a friend about conspiracy theories and I want you to argue for my side, properly, the way a philosopher would — not just hand me the usual survey of views.

The standard line, the one everybody repeats, is that what makes a conspiracy theory epistemically bad is that it's self-sealing: it can't be refuted because any evidence against it gets folded back in as part of the cover-up. I think that's mostly a myth. Almost no real conspiracy belief is unfalsifiable in that strong sense — people who hold these views will usually tell you what would change their minds, they just set the bar for the official story way higher than the bar for the conspiracy, and they treat certain official sources as presumptively compromised before they've even looked at them. So the actual failing isn't unfalsifiability, it's that asymmetry of evidential thresholds plus the built-in presumption against official sources.

What I want you to argue: that this is the right diagnosis; that the unfalsifiability charge is a strawman that lets critics skip the hard question of when distrusting official institutions is actually warranted; and that on this view a lot of what gets dismissed as conspiracy thinking is epistemically continuous with perfectly respectable suspicion of institutions — the difference being one of calibration and degree, not of kind. Push it as far as it honestly goes, and deal with the obvious objection that some conspiracy theories really are unfalsifiable, don't just wave it off.

At the end, tell me how confident you actually are in the main claim, and be honest about which part of the arguemnt you're least sure of.
