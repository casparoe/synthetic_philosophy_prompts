I have spent years reading the rationalist blogs and forums, and my working vocabulary for anything probabilistic is the one you would pick up from a probabilistic-programming tutorial: generative models, priors and likelihoods, factors and soft constraints, inference by MCMC or particle filtering, annealing schedules and inverse temperature, and at the limit Solomonoff induction as the canonical universal prior over programs. I am a competent programmer, comfortable with unification, term rewriting and constraint propagation, but I have never had any use for Hofstadter's Copycat programme, and I would like it restated in my vocabulary.

What I take Copycat to be: a stochastic model of analogy-making in a microdomain of letter strings, the sort of puzzle where abc is to abd as ijk is to what. Its architecture is tripartite: a slipnet of concepts joined by links whose effective weights shift with activation; a workspace in which the program builds structures — bonds between corresponding elements, groups, rules — out of the problem itself; and a coderack of codelets, small processes selected with probabilities proportional to urgency, which build and destroy structure and nudge activations. A global temperature measures how disordered the situation currently is and governs how readily exploratory and destructive moves occur, and the whole thing runs as a parallel terraced scan, with many partial interpretations pursued at once and effort redistributed as some begin to look more promising. It settles on an answer — sometimes a literal one, sometimes a thematic one — and separate runs can settle differently.

Restate that apparatus in my vocabulary, component by component, as faithfully as you can, without quietly substituting a better-behaved object for the one Hofstadter built. Then give me the residue report. Which parts translate cleanly — temperature as an annealing schedule, bonds as factors or soft constraints, the parallel terraced scan as something like particle filtering or a tempered sequential search, conceptual slippage as a move within a hypothesis space? Which parts translate only with distortion? Which parts have no counterpart in my vocabulary at all? And where the translation fails, tell me whether that is a defect of my vocabulary or evidence that the original claim was less clear than it sounded.

There is one thing in particular I want the translation to bear on. The standard objection from my side of the fence is that the slipnet is hand-built: the concepts and their associations are written down by the programmer, so nothing about conceptual novelty has been modelled. The standard reply is that this is simply what a prior is, and that Solomonoff induction answers the worry by putting the prior over programs rather than over a hand-chosen list of concepts. I want to know what survives when that reply is made precise. Does the difference between a hand-built slipnet and a hand-built hypothesis space survive translation, or is it a distinction without a difference? If it survives, what exactly does Copycat have that the probabilistic account lacks — and if the probabilistic account can be extended to have it, tell me what the extension costs and whether it remains recognisably an inference procedure rather than a description of one. You may invent the probabilistic analogue if it does not already exist; I would rather have an honest idealisation with its losses marked than a mapping that flatters my side. No reading lists, please — I want the argument, not the literature.
