I have recently become involved in a local campaign in my hometown — a town of roughly thirty thousand people, with no court, no university, and no great private fortunes — to convert a disused church into a small playhouse of about two hundred seats. The company would be local and mostly young; half the repertory would be plays written here about events that happened here; and the performances would be streamed for people who cannot attend in person. In preparing my case I read Rousseau's letter against d'Alembert about the proposal to establish a theatre in Geneva, and I found more in it than I expected — enough that I no longer trust my own side and want it tested against the strongest version of his.

Please play Rousseau, in the first person, speaking from the position of that letter. Constraints: use only the concepts available to you in the 1750s plus whatever I tell you about our situation; concede particular points where I am right, but never yield your central claim that a theatre cannot make a people better and is at best a dangerous amusement; if you need a fact about our town, ask me for it as Rousseau would. Stay in character until I say stop. Keep each reply to a single substantial paragraph, so that this reads as a conversation rather than a treatise.

My opening: you say the stage cannot improve morals because it merely flatters spectators who are already what they are, and you argue that Molière makes the honest misanthrope ridiculous and trains the audience to side with the polished man of the world. But everything you describe sounds like the great capital theatre — Paris, with its court and its idlers. We are proposing two hundred seats, our own young people on the boards, and plays about our own streets. That is not a spectacle we watch; it is something we do. So I put it to you: is your objection to the theatre, or only to the theatre at scale? And if your real target is the passivity of the spectator, why is a play not a festival — the kind of gathering you praise when everyone dances? We are even planning one piece in which the audience moves through the rooms and takes part. If that counts as a festival, your distinction collapses; if it does not, tell me exactly what the difference is.

Finally: when I say step out, I want one paragraph out of character separating the claims that are genuinely Rousseau's in that letter from the ones you improvised on his behalf, and marking the places where people who know the text disagree about what he meant — I would rather see a contested reading flagged as contested than have it presented to me as the consensus.
