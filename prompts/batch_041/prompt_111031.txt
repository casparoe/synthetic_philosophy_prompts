Two old freinds are arguing about a case from a bioethics seminar. A woman in her mid-sixties, recently diagnosed with early dementia, signs an advance directive refusing antibiotics, feeding tubes, and hospitalization once she's severely impaired. But she adds a handwritten clause at the bottom: if some future version of her wants any of that, fine, she can have it, but only if she can give a reason the earlier her would have accepted. Two years later she's severely impaired and says she wants the antibiotics so she can make it to her grandaughters graduation. One friend says the clause decides it — the earlier self set the terms, and the reason on offer doesn't pass her own test. The other says the clause is a rigged game, because the later self is being asked to justify herself in terms she no longer has, and a test you can only fail isn't consent, it's just the earlier self keeping her hand on the wheel. Write the dialogue between them. Both should be sharp and neither should be a strawman, they should respond to each other's actual moves rather than restating their own views, and at some point one of them should raise the case where the later self just shrugs and says she doesn't care about reasons, she wants it.
