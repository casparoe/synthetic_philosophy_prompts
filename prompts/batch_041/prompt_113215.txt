For a graduate seminar session on the intertheoretic-comparison problem, I need one worked case rather than an illustration of the problem. Construct a realistic decision — an ordinary professional or personal choice among three options, no trolleys and no fantastical quantities — faced by an agent who takes exactly two moral theories seriously and can state each of them in a sentence. The two theories must rank the three options in exactly opposite orders, and must differ sharply in scale: one sees large differences in choiceworthiness among the options, the other treats all three as permissible and nearly equally good. Give a choiceworthiness table with explicit credences, then show by computation, not assertion, that the three standard rules come apart in this pattern: maximizing expected choiceworthiness endorses the option the flat theory ranks last; maximin endorses the option the steep theory ranks last; unweighted minimax regret endorses the middle option that neither theory ranks first. Both theories must be statable independently of the numbers, and the numbers must be defensible as what those theories would say about the options; a table reverse-engineered from the verdicts I have asked for will not do. Then the debrief: give the credence at which expected choiceworthiness changes its mind, and say whether the other two rules are sensitive to credence at all; identify the single feature doing the work; report what happens when you renormalize the two theories' scales so that their spreads match, and whether the divergence survives; and say whether any version of the case could produce this three-way split without the assumption you identified. Close with the strongest objection open to a defender of whichever rule fares worst, and what you would concede to it.
