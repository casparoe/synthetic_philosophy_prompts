My friend works in the micro lab at our city water utility and we got into a stupidly long argument at a party about whether "alive" is a real category or just a bureaucratic one, and I havent been able to stop thinking about it. Her whole job is basically drawing that line: she plates a water sample on a medium, incubates it, counts the little colonies, multiplies by the dilution, and that number -- the colony forming units -- goes into a report that can force the city to shut down a well or issue a boil-water notice. Same deal with "sterile" in a hospital: an autoclave load counts as sterile if nothing grew when they tried to grow it. But she'll say in the same breath that the plate misses most of what's actually in the water, that there's this whole viable-but-nonculturable limbo where cells are obviously respiring and metabolizing but just refuse to form colonies, that the assay mostly catches the easy stuff, and that a lot of what actually makes water dangerous -- viruses, which never form colonies anywhere -- isn't detected by the test at all. So the legal test for "is there life in this water" is essentially "did some particular subset of bacteria reproduce under our lab conditions," and the viruses that are the whole reason the rule exists are invisible to it. And the borderline cases pile up: spores that sit inert for years, prions, a frozen embryo, a virus, a cell that's alive but starving and won't divide.

What I want from you is not the usual list of textbook definitions of life (metabolism, reproduction, evolution, whatever) -- I know those, and they're not the point. I want the view of life that this practice actually presupposes. My suspicion is that it's treating "alive" as an operational or pragmatic kind, so that alive just means "grows under our conditions," which would make it more like "tall" or "bald" -- a vague predicate whose line we draw wherever is useful -- than like "gold" or "water," a natural kind with a hidden essence our tests could get wrong. But maybe I have it backwards and the practice only makes sense if there's a fact of the matter about which cells are alive and the plate is just a lousy instrument for detecting it. Can you tell which it is, and be careful to separate what the practice strictly requires from what it merely fits nicely with, since my guess is that a couple of different views would license the exact same lab bench? Does it commit to a sharp threshold, or could it accommodate life coming in degrees (a spore vs. a dividing cell vs. a virus vs. a prion)? Which rival accounts does it quietly rule out -- say, the view that life is a cluster of properties with no single necessary one, or the pluralist view that "life" is just a word we slap on a bunch of unrelated processes and we should stop hunting for the one thing? And is the practice internally consistent, given that it uses the one word two ways at once (alive-as-culturable and alive-as-metabolizing) and leans on a test that doesn't detect the very things the rule was written to prevent? Last thing: if we decided the operational view was wrong and went with the natural-kind one instead, what concretely changes -- different media, different instruments, different thresholds -- or does the whole idea of a pass/fail number for a water sample fall apart?
