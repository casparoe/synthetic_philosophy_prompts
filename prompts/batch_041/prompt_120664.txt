I've been chewing on a dinner-table arguement all week and I want to stress-test where I landed. I write a column on science and ideas for a general-reader outlet, so I spend a lot of time watching smart people talk past each other on the great-man question — would there really have been a Nazi Germany without Hitler — and last week the table split cleanly in two. I ended up in the middle, which I now suspect is the hardest place to defend. My view: the regime was massively overdetermined by structure — Versailles, the Depression, the disintegration of Weimar's party system, elite terror of the left — so some authoritarian, revanchist, antisemitic dictatorship was near-unavoidable regardless of who was in the room; but the Holocaust as it actually unfolded, its timing and its industrialized, bureaucratically driven character, depended on Hitler specifically, and I don't think any other plausible German leader of that decade reproduces it. So: dispensable for the dictatorship, indispensable for the genocide. Tell me if that's a real position or a hedge that collapses the moment you press on what it even means for the genocide to be the one that actually happened.

What I want is a map of my exposure — not the standard objections from people who already think great-man history is bunk, but the things that should genuinely move me, sorted by how decisive they'd be. Which comparative cases, and which findings from the functionalist side of the historiography (genocidal policy escalating from below, the center ratifying rather than originating, radicalization outpacing what was ever ordered) would actually break the indispensability half, and which are just awkward? Same question for the overdetermination half — what would it take to show the dictatorship wasn't overdetermined at all? I'm also hung up on a more formal worry: in the usual possible-worlds treatment of counterfactuals the nearest world is the one that departs least from actual history, so the closest world to ours where Hitler is absent might be one where he simply never gets near power rather than one where somebody else takes his slot — and in an interventionist, causal-graph way of thinking, the answer to whether the outcome depends on who leads flips depending on which other variables you hold fixed. Does that make the question ill-posed, or just under-specified, and does anything change if I drop without Hitler and ask instead whether the outcome is robust across the plausible range of background conditions?

One more thing: I'd find it most persuasive if you told me what the strongest version of the great-man view would itself concede, and what it would refuse to concede, because I don't trust a map of defeaters that only comes from one side. Give it to me as exactly five numbered items, a sentence or two each, no intro and no conclusion — just the five.
