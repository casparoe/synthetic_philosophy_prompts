I am a barrister. My working life is documentary evidence: disclosure, provenance, chain of custody, documents written to serve somebody's interest, and the inferences a tribunal is invited to draw from what a party has kept and what it has let disappear. I have never studied ancient history, and I want to know whether what historians call source criticism — the questions Herodotus and Thucydides were already asking about what they had been told and how they knew it, and the form those questions take in modern work on the distant past — can be restated in the vocabulary of evidence and proof that I actually use.

Restate it systematically, strand by strand, rather than by loose analogy. Take the main moves of source criticism one at a time — the genuineness and date of a document, the interest and position of its author, corroboration and independent attestation, the force of silence, forgery and interpolation, and the selectivity of what survives at all — and for each one tell me three things: what the nearest counterpart in my vocabulary is (authenticity, admissibility, weight, the rule against hearsay and its exceptions, corroboration warnings, the best-evidence principle, adverse inferences from non-production, the standard of proof); what has to be stretched or fudged to make the translation fit; and what the historian can do that I cannot, or the reverse. My whole apparatus assumes a tribunal that can summon witnesses, put questions to them and punish them for lying, and that must eventually reach a binary verdict on a burden of proof. The historian has none of that, and is not trying to reach a verdict at all.

Then give me the residue report, which is the part I actually want. Which elements of source criticism have no counterpart whatever in the law of evidence, and is that because my vocabulary is missing something, or because the historians' claims are vaguer than they look? I am particularly interested in two candidate false friends. The first is the pairing of the question whether a source is what it purports to be with the question whether what it says is true: is that really the same distinction as admissibility and weight, or does it come apart, given that my exclusionary rules are mostly about policy rather than reliability? The second is the problem of the selectively surviving record, an archive shaped by what somebody chose to copy, keep or forge rather than by what happened: is the historian's version of that problem expressible in the inferences I am allowed to invite a tribunal to draw, or is it a different kind of epistemic predicament altogether?

I am not asking for a reading list and I do not want citations. Tell me what translates, what does not, and why.
