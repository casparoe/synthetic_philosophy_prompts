I teach a graduate seminar on decision theory and the philosophy of probability, and I am writing the essay section of the final. The question below is meant to take a strong student about ninety minutes and roughly eight hundred words, and it must not be answerable by reciting that Ellsberg's subjects violated the sure-thing principle or that ambiguity aversion is just a taste for known odds. Please keep the substance of the question, polish the wording if it needs it, and then produce a model answer and a marking scheme out of 20 that names the specific moves an A answer must contain, the moves that earn a B, and the specific mistakes that should cost marks.

The question:

"Three urns each contain 100 balls, red or black.

Urn A: you are told it contains exactly 50 red and 50 black.

Urn B: its composition is fixed, but you are told nothing about it.

Urn C: before you arrived, a fair coin was tossed; on heads, 40 red and 60 black were placed in the urn, on tails, 60 red and 40 black. You are not told the outcome of the toss.

In each urn a single ball is drawn at random and independently; you may bet on red or on black; a correct bet pays $100, a wrong bet pays nothing.

A subject reports: (i) a bet on red in A is strictly preferred to a bet on red in B; (ii) a bet on black in A is strictly preferred to a bet on black in B; (iii) she is indifferent between a bet on red in A and a bet on red in C; (iv) a bet on red in C is strictly preferred to a bet on red in B.

(a) What do (i) and (ii) establish about the existence of subjective probabilities for her? Do they violate the sure-thing principle? If not, describe the version of the Ellsberg problem that does, and explain why the two-urn version does not.

(b) What does (iii) tell you about whether she satisfies the reduction of compound lotteries axiom? What does the contrast between (iii) and (iv) tell you about the source of her aversion to B — is she averse to second-order uncertainty as such, or to something narrower?

(c) Could her pattern be represented by (1) subjective expected utility, (2) maxmin expected utility, or (3) a smooth-ambiguity model with a symmetric second-order prior and concave second-order utility? Answer each, with reasons.

(d) Specify one further case — a new urn, or a change in how the choice is presented — that would separate the comparative-ignorance hypothesis, on which ambiguity aversion appears only when an ambiguous prospect is evaluated alongside an unambiguous one, from an explanation in terms of her second-order beliefs, and say what each predicts about it."
