I'm a novelist and I'm writing a chapter about readers who find things in a book the writer swears aren't there. I want a thought experiment I can put in it, engineered so that some views about what a text means come apart in a specific way. The views are: (1) it means what the author actually meant; (2) it means what a reader who knew everything about the author and the circumstances would reasonably conclude the author meant; (3) it means whatever reading makes the text best as literature. I also want to test a theory a friend of mine keeps pushing, its that a text means what the author would have meant if he'd been smarter and a nicer person.

Here's the spec. The author must really have meant M, and the evidence for that must be out there and decisive, so views 1 and 2 both land on M. But the best-as-literature reading, M', has to be different from M and genuinely supported by the words on the page. And M' must not be what the smarter, kinder author would have meant either, so my friend's view lands on M too. Constraints: present day, no sci-fi, the author isn't lying or confused or joking, no tricks where the author turns out to be a character inside the story, and you can state the whole case in a paragraph.

Then walk me through why each view gives the verdict it does, tell me which feature of the case is doing the work, whether removing that feature collapses it into the boring version where everyone just reads the better book, and how someone who holds view 3 would go about attacking my example.
