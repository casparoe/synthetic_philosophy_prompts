In the study of national mythology, two ways of converting a military defeat into a durable source of collective identity are often distinguished. On the first — call it the redemptive-sacrifice model — the defeat is accepted as a defeat but recast as a moral or spiritual victory whose full meaning is realized by later generations: the fallen are said to have died for something that outlasts the battle's outcome, and the loss itself becomes constitutive of the community's self-understanding. On the second — call it the deferred- or stolen-victory model — the outcome is not accepted at all: the cause is said to have been betrayed from within or overwhelmed by illegitimate force, so that history's real verdict is merely postponed rather than delivered.

Compare these two models. Treat the American Confederate Lost Cause as one case, and choose at least one further case from the wider history of national myths (the Masada tradition, the Kosovo legend, and the Dunkirk story are all available, though you need not be limited to them). For the cases you discuss, address the following: What does each model explain well, and what does it leave unexplained? Are the two genuinely distinct, or does one tend to collapse into the other under pressure — for instance, when a movement must insist both that its cause was noble and that it fell only to treachery? Which model better accounts for a myth's ability to persist across generations and to license present-day political claims, and why?

Be concrete about mechanism rather than trading in generalities: attend to mourning and commemorative practice, ritual and pedagogy, the cult of the fallen, the role of defeat in the founding narrative, and the way each model handles the question of who is to blame.

Separate clearly what is settled from what is genuinely contested among historians and theorists of nationalism, and do not present a contested interpretation as though it were the consensus. Where your comparative judgment rests on a debatable reading, say so.
