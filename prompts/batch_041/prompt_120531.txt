The midterm question (short essay, 20 points) was this: Mencius and Xunzi both hold that anyone can become a sage — Mencius that anyone can be a Yao or a Shun, Xunzi that the man in the street can become a Yu — and yet they disagree about human nature (xing). Explain what they actually disagree about, using the distinction drawn in lecture between the modal question (can anyone become good?) and the grounding question (what makes the goodness we achieve good — is it native to us or produced?). Then explain what ritual (li) and deliberate effort (wei) do in Xunzi's account that the four sprouts (si duan) do not do in Mencius's.

Two notes on the lecture framing the answer presupposes, since you weren't in the room: the modal question is answered affirmatively by both thinkers, and the grounding question is where the instructor locates the real dispute; and the instructor glossed Xunzi's wei as producing a second nature — genuine and stable goodness that is nonetheless not natural goodness.

Rubric: accurate account of each thinker's conception of xing (6); correct use of the modal/grounding distinction to locate the disagreement (6); account of the roles of li and wei as against the sprouts (4); internal consistency and support for the claims made (4).

The answer: "Both thinkers agree that human beings can become sages: Mencius says anyone can be a Yao or a Shun, and Xunzi says the man in the street can become a Yu. They disagree about what human nature (xing) is. For Mencius, xing is good. We are born with the four sprouts (si duan) of benevolence, righteousness, ritual propriety, and wisdom, which show themselves in our spontaneous reactions — the alarm we feel at a child about to fall into a well, for instance — and moral development consists in extending and filling these out rather than manufacturing something foreign to us. He grants that the sprouts can be starved, as in the image of the mountain stripped of its trees, but that is a failure of nurture, not evidence about nature. For Xunzi, xing is simply what we are born with, and what we are born with is a set of desires; since those desires, unchecked, produce contention and disorder, he concludes that human nature is evil. The remedy is to transform nature and raise artifice (hua xing qi wei). Like warped wood that must be steamed and forced straight against the press-frame, we become good only by being worked on from outside — by teachers and, above all, by ritual (li), which the sage-kings devised. So Mencius and Xunzi disagree about whether we can become good at all: Mencius thinks goodness is native and needs only to be brought out, while Xunzi thinks it has to be built from nothing.

That disagreement is really about the grounding question. For Mencius the ground of goodness is xing itself; for Xunzi it is wei, human artifice. Since wei is the product of the sage-kings' invention, Xunzi is committed to holding that the content of morality — what the rituals prescribe — is a human convention with no basis in nature, and that the sages might in principle have prescribed otherwise. This is what ritual does for Xunzi that the sprouts do not do for Mencius: it supplies the content of goodness, whereas for Mencius the content is already given in the heart. So the character that wei produces is a second nature in the instructor's sense — real goodness, but not natural goodness. It also explains why, on Xunzi's view, the transformation must be total: since we are born actively wicked, taking pleasure in the very things ritual forbids, the desires themselves have to be eliminated before ritual can take hold, and so we can never relax our effort."
