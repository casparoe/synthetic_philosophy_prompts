Write a joke about consent — specifically about what makes a person's consent valid or invalid. I have no training in philosophy; this is just something I have been turning over on my own, so please build the joke on the actual mechanics of the problem rather than on the usual jokes about paperwork and fine print.

Here is the knot I keep getting stuck on. Take coercion first. When we say that someone's consent was coerced, I take it we usually mean that the threat made a difference — that but for the threat, they would not have agreed. The effect of the threat on the decision is the whole point. Now push that test as far as it will go. Suppose a man demands my wallet and threatens me if I refuse, but I was about to offer him the money anyway, out of pity. By the standard of whether he changed my decision, his threat changed nothing, and so on that logic my consent was never undermined at all. Extend it and you arrive at a mugger careful enough to rob only the generous: a man who, on this account, has never coerced anyone in his life.

Deception seems to run the opposite way. If someone lies to me about what I am agreeing to, we do not ask whether the lie changed my mind. We say that I agreed to something that was not real, and so never agreed to the thing that actually happened. What matters there is not whether the falsehood made a difference to my decision but whether the description I accepted matches the world. So one test tells me that if I would have done it anyway, my consent stands, and the other tells me that my having done it anyway is precisely what makes it not count.

And competence fits neither test: a person can agree freely and with full knowledge and still be someone whose agreement we say does not count, because of who they are rather than how the choice was made.

I want the joke to land on that collision — the way the coercion test and the deception test point in opposite directions on the very same fact about what I would have done anyway. It should be funny to someone who already knows this material, so the punchline has to turn on the substance of the distinction and not merely on philosophers being fussy. A short setup and a punchline would be ideal, and please do not end on the observation that consent is complicated; I already know that, which is why I am asking.
