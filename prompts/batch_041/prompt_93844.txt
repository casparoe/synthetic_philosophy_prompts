I write about the machinery of collective decision-making for a general-readership science-and-ideas desk, and I'm putting together a piece on the gap between judging a group's verdict issue-by-issue and judging it on the bottom line alone — what the literature tends to call the discursive dilemma. I've been going back and forth with a colleague about whether the paradox is really about the voters or about the propositions, and I've come to suspect it's the latter, so what I want from you is a pair of matched cases built to order that isolates the variable I think is doing the work. In the first case, three members of some ordinary institution — a hiring committee, a grant panel, a hospital ethics board, take your pick, so long as it's a real-world body and not a stylized puzzle — must reach a collective verdict on three propositions, one of which is tied to the others by a genuine logical connection (a conditional, a rule of the institution, a definitional link), with each member's own set of judgments complete and internally consistent, and yet voting proposition-by-proposition yields a collective set that no single member holds and that is itself inconsistent. In the second case, hold everything fixed — same number of members, same distribution of individual verdicts, same institutional texture — but make the three propositions logically independent of one another, and show that under that single change the two ways of aggregating (tallying each proposition separately versus tallying the members' bottom-line verdicts) can no longer come apart. Please don't reach for the textbook breach-and-damages or three-friends examples, and don't let the members' disagreement be stipulated as incoherent; I want something I could print without it reading as a reprint.

For each case, walk me through what the proposition-by-proposition tally delivers and what the bottom-line tally delivers, and say why each gives the verdict it does. Then tell me which single feature of the first case is carrying the result and what happens when you strip it out, and — since I don't want to stack the deck — give me the strongest line a defender of the bottom-line procedure would take against the first case, together with how a defender of the proposition-by-proposition procedure would answer it. Throughout, keep the seam between what's settled among people who work on this and what's genuinely contested clearly visible; I don't want a contested verdict on which procedure is correct presented as though the field had reached consensus. Lead with the two cases themselves, stated compactly, before any commentary — no preamble and no restating of my question.
