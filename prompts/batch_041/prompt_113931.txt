I serve on the historical marker committee of a mid-sized American city, and I have to draft the written opinion I will circulate before next week's vote. The case has split us, and I would rather have a memo I can defend than a compromise nobody believes.

A neighborhood association has petitioned to place a marker at the site of what it calls the Walnut Street raid. By the association's account, in the spring of 1971 city police and agents of a private landlord evicted several families from a building that had served as the hub of a tenants' movement, and a teenage boy was killed in the confusion. The account rests on the testimony of surviving residents, gathered over years, and on a single photograph that the association dates to the eviction and locates at the building; two members who were children at the time independently remember the boy's name.

Our staff historian has now gone through the municipal record and recommends against the marker as proposed. The police log for that year records no operation at that address. The coroner's register for the relevant months lists no death of a teenage boy. The building's demolition permit is dated 1974, three years after the alleged raid, which the historian says is inconsistent with a forced eviction that emptied it. The one contemporaneous newspaper item that comes close describes a disturbance two blocks away, on a different date, with no injury reported. On the photograph, the historian notes that the vehicles and street signage visible in it are of a type that, on the best evidence available, postdate 1971. The historian's conclusion is that the memory is sincere but mistaken, probably a conflation of the 1971 disturbance with the 1974 demolition.

The association's reply is not that the record is wrong in its details but that the record is the wrong instrument. It argues that these registers and logs were kept by the same institutions the community is accusing; that a police log's silence about its own conduct is not evidence of anything; that the newspaper served the landlords' interests; and that a city which has renamed streets and removed monuments on the strength of oral testimony and moral certainty should not now demand of this neighborhood a documentary standard it has never applied to anyone else. Two of my colleagues find this persuasive as a matter of principle and want to approve the marker as submitted, on the ground that the committee's job is to honor a community's memory, not to adjudicate it.

We have no policy that speaks to this. The relevant line in our handbook says only that markers must be "historically accurate," which everyone reads differently. The three live options are: reject the petition; approve the text as submitted; or approve a revised marker that presents the community's account and the record's findings side by side, each attributed, with a note on what remains unresolved. I am also being pressed to propose a change to the handbook itself, since we will see more of these.

Please draft the opinion I will circulate. It should recommend a concrete course of action on the Walnut Street petition -- which of the three options, or something else -- with reasons a colleague who disagrees could actually engage, and it should say what, if anything, we should do about the handbook, including a realistic timetable for any further archival work before a final decision. I want the strongest argument on the losing side stated in its own terms rather than knocked down, and I want a candid account of the distinction the association is relying on: between a record that denies something and a record that merely fails to mention it. If you think that distinction does the work the association needs, say so and say why; if you think it collapses, say where. Treat the layered-marker option seriously rather than as a dodge, and say what it would commit us to if we adopted it as a general practice.

Close with an explicit statement of what would change your recommendation -- which piece of evidence, if it surfaced, or which argument, if it were sound, would move you to a different verdict -- and identify the point in your own reasoning where you think you are most likely to be wrong.

Write in American English.
