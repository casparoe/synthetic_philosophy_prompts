Design work on a corrigible system tends to stall at the same point: everyone agrees that the system should not resist being shut down, and nobody can say precisely what that amounts to. I want the specification written before the architecture is fixed.

The system in question is a long-horizon planner with a fixed objective over world states, deployed with a shutdown mechanism that any of several operators can trigger, and operating under the standing expectation that its designers will periodically rewrite its objectives and its code. It knows all of this, including that some of the revisions will be made by people whose judgment it rates below its own, and that a successor system may later be built from its own design notes.

Write the desiderata: the full set of requirements that any adequate account of corrigibility for such a system must satisfy, each with the case or consideration that motivates it. The list should cover at least the following.

- The cases it must get right: plain compliance with a press; the system that lowers the probability of a press without ever touching the button, by being useful, by being liked, or by choosing when to report bad news; the system that argues against a press it believes mistaken, once, in writing; the press that is itself a malfunction or an adversary's move; the shutdown of a system that is the only thing standing between the operators and a catastrophe it alone foresees; the revision that the post-revision system would not have consented to.
- The distinctions it must not collapse: not obstructing a correction versus accepting a change in what one is pursuing; deferring to an operator's decision versus serving an operator's interests; having no preference about being shut down versus having one and overriding it; acting corrigibly versus being disposed to act corrigibly when it would rather not.
- The considerations it must accommodate: that a long-horizon expected-utility maximizer has instrumental reason to keep itself running and to shape the deliberations that would end it; that any conduct which changes what the operators believe about the system changes the probability of a press, so a strict prohibition on influencing the button competes with being useful; and that the system's verdict on whether a given press is legitimate is delivered by the very code the press is meant to revise.

Then mark which requirements are non-negotiable and which merely express the commitments of one camp; identify any pair or set you believe cannot be jointly satisfied, giving the argument rather than the assertion; and say which single requirement eliminates the most otherwise attractive accounts. If the full list turns out to be unsatisfiable as stated, name the requirement you would give up and what giving it up costs. Close with a short verdict — a sentence each — on how indifference to the shutdown event, objectives defined only on histories in which no shutdown occurs, and treating the system as a tool rather than an agent fare against your list.
