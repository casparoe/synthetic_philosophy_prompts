I have just finished teaching a graduate seminar on social choice and the theory of rights, and I keep running into the same frustration. The impossibility result that links a minimal liberalism condition to the weak Pareto principle is now so thoroughly digested that students can recite it, list the standard escape routes — restrict the domain, weaken Pareto, treat rights as alienable, reformulate them as game forms — and then ask, reasonably enough, why anyone would still work on the topic. My own suspicion is that the area is not exhausted so much as misdescribed, and what I want from you is a defended research agenda rather than a survey. Here is the concrete setting, because importance is relative to a budget. I supervise a doctoral student with a strong formal background — comfortable with Arrow-style machinery, fixed-point arguments, and the rudiments of implementation theory — but only a moderate appetite for the exegetical side of the field. She has roughly three years and wants to write a dissertation on rights and aggregation. I can also carve out one semester of my own time, and perhaps convene a small reading group of three or four people, to support a single line of work. So the question of what to prioritize is a real budgeting question and not a rhetorical one.

Two half-formed ideas of my own, which I would like tested against the state of the field rather than flattered. First, I have been drawing a distinction between rights understood as vetoes over outcomes — the right-holder fixes the social ranking of a pair — and rights understood as claims on the process by which outcomes are generated, so that what the right-holder controls is the shape of the choice situation rather than a comparison within it. My hunch is that the impossibility is an artifact of the first reading, and that the second has never been given a formulation precise enough either to be proved consistent or to be shown to reproduce the paradox. Second, I have been wondering whether the difficulty is better described not as a conflict between liberty and efficiency but as a conflict between two roles a value can play in an aggregation: as something that is counted, like a preference, and as something that structures the counting, like a constraint on admissible profiles. On that reframing the result looks less like a paradox about rights and more like a theorem about the limits of any procedure that must simultaneously register individual attitudes and be insulated from them. A colleague, for his part, insists that the right move is to model rights as powers over agendas rather than over pairs, so that the right-holder chooses which comparisons become live rather than how a fixed comparison resolves, and that on this reading the impossibility either dissolves or returns in a different guise, as a result about agenda manipulation.

What I want from you is an ordered agenda. Which open questions or lines of work in this neighborhood most deserve the next three years of a competent formal philosopher's attention, in what order, and on what criteria? I am interested in which questions are upstream of which — what would have to be settled before other things could be settled at all — which are genuinely tractable with the tools a philosopher rather than an economist would bring, which are neglected for bad reasons, and which, if answered, would change something downstream: in normative political philosophy, in how constitutional and institutional designers think about entrenchment, in mechanism design, or in the philosophy of rights more generally. Where your ranking rests on a contested premise, name the premise and say what would move the item up or down; if something on the standard list strikes you as a dead end that nonetheless keeps attracting papers, say so and defend the judgment. I would rather have a short, defended ordering with the trade-offs made explicit than a comprehensive map, and I do not need a bibliography or a set of attributions — if you are unsure who held which view, describe the position without naming anyone.
