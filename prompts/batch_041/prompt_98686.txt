I'm in my third year and taking a course on rationality and choice -- decision theory plus some philosophy of action -- and I'm trying to write the framing section of a paper on diachronic rationality, i.e. rationality for agents that persist over time. Before I start comparing anybody's theory I want the spec first: what does any adequate theory in this area actually have to deliver? So if you'd act as an expert on decision theory and practical rationality and start by brainstorming the candidate requirements before you commit to a list, I'd appreciate it. Then give me the list: the cases the theory has to get right, the distinctions it can't just collapse, and the results from the rest of decision theory it can't contradict -- with the consideration behind each one, and a verdict on which are non-negotiable and which are really just commitments of one camp.

Some of what's making me confused, so you know where I'm coming from. The standard move is the money pump: someone whose preferences go in a circle (she'll pay a little to swap A for B, a little to swap B for C, a little to swap C back to A) gets bled dry by a patient trader, and that's supposed to prove the cycle is irrational. But I keep noticing the pump seems to trade on two different things at once -- the structure of her preferences (the cycle) and the fact that she'll keep trading and can't commit -- and I'm not sure which one is doing the work. And then there's the case where she doesn't reverse any preference, she just changes her mind for good reason (she learns something), and a trader who anticipated the change can pump her anyway; surely that's not a proof that learning is irrational. So I want the requirements list to force a clean answer about what a pump is evidence of and what it isn't. Related: the "resolute" move, where the agent decides in advance to stop trading and then sticks to it -- does a theory have to count that as rational, or as a kind of cheating? And the addict/Odysseus cases, where the agent's own future preferences are the threat, and the old puzzle about whether a preference reversal is irrational or just a change of tastes the theory has no business policing. I also care a lot about the distinction between a requirement on the agent's preference set at a time and a requirement on her decision procedure over time -- I suspect a good chunk of the disagreement in this literature is people quietly answering different questions, and I want the list to keep those apart.

One thing I'd find really useful: flag any pair of requirements I can't have together. My gut says the demand that the theory return a determinate verdict in every case, the demand that it not require more of agents than bounded, forgetful, tempted humans can actually do, and the demand that it count at least some preference changes as rational are definately not all going to fit at once -- if that's right, tell me which one has to go and what that does to the surviving theories. And tell me which single requirement rules out the most otherwise-popular theories; that's the bit I most want for the paper. Please be careful and explicit about separating what's settled among people who work on this from what's genuinely contested -- I don't want a contested reading served up as the consensus. Thanks!
