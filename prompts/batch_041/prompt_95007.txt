I've been reading up on inverse reinforcement learning -- the setup where you watch what an agent does and try to recover the reward function it's maximizing -- and it's struck me that the whole thing is a pile of philosophical commitments dressed up as an estimation problem. So: what does this practice actually presuppose? It seems to assume the preferences are already there, latent, one fixed utility function per person, with behavior as a noisy readout of it, and that people are near-enough optimal that deviations are just noise or bounded rationality. Which views of preference does that quietly rule out -- preferences constructed in the act of choosing rather than revealed by it, values that won't sit on a single commensurable scale, acting against your own better judgment, caring about the shape of the process and not only where it ends up? And is the package even internally consistent, since you can't both treat behavior as the ground truth about preference and treat departures from optimality as mere error without some principled line between the two? Reconstruct the implicit theory, tell me what's strictly required versus what's just convenient for the math, and say what actually changes if you swap it for something constructivist or multi-valued.
