I'm the parent rep on our school district's instructional materials review committee — I'm not a teacher, I run a landscaping business, they just asked me to sit on it. We've got a formal challenge on the agenda for Thursday and I have to circulate a short written opinion before the meeting.

Here's the case. A mom has filed the paperwork to have a two-page handout pulled from the 10th grade biology unit on evolution. It's not a textbook — the teacher wrote it himself, and it's optional reading. It's about what Darwin personally thought about God, and about where our sense of right and wrong comes from. Her complaint, as best I can follow it: the handout tells kids morality is just an evolved instinct, so nothing is really right or wrong, and that Darwin was basically an atheist who thought religion was leftovers. She says a biology class has no business making those claims to her kids, and she definately wants it out of the unit. The teacher says he's being censored and that the handout is just history of science.

I went and read Darwin myself, because I didn't want to vote on something I hadn't actually looked at. A few things stuck with me:

- The moral sense stuff is a lot more interesting than the handout makes it sound. His story is that we start with social instincts, like the ones that make animals look after each other; then memory and a little reason let us look back on our own past behavior and judge it; and then sympathy gets stretched wider and wider by habit and teaching — family, then tribe, then everybody, and he even includes animals. He leans hard on wanting other people's approval and hating their disapproval, and says that gets built into you over time so you feel it even when nobody's watching. But he doesn't say it's fake. He seems to think the moral sense is the most real thing about us, and that moral progress actually happens.
- On God he's slippery. He says he's not an atheist, that he never denied God existed, and the word he liked for himself was "agnostic" (a friend of his made the word up, I guess?). He went from being fairly religious to not being able to pray or sit through church. One thing that clearly bothered him was wasps that lay their eggs inside live caterpillars so the babies eat them from the inside out — he said he couldn't believe a good and all-powerful God would design that on purpose.
- Also, apparently his son published the autobiography right after he died and cut out a bunch of it, including the parts about religion, and those bits didn't get put back in until the 1950s.

Where I get stuck, and this is really why I need the memo, is that it feels like three totally different questions are getting smooshed into one fight. (1) What did Darwin personally believe? (2) Does his theory force you to be an atheist, or does it leave God out of the picture either way? (3) Does explaining where our moral feelings come from say anything about whether those feelings are right? My brother-in-law keeps saying #3 is a "genetic fallacy," like explaining why someone believes something automatically has nothing to do with whether it's true. But that doesn't sit right with me either — if I found out I only think stealing is wrong because I got scared into it as a kid, wouldn't that knock my confidence down at least a little?

What I need from you: the written opinion I'll circulate before Thursday's meeting. Give me a clear recommendation — pull the handout, keep it as is, or keep it with specific changes spelled out — plus a time frame that works, since the unit starts in two weeks. Keep it short, a page and a half at most, nobody reads more than that. I need reasons that both the mom and the teacher could actually respond to instead of just feeling dismissed, and I want the strongest version of her side in there honestly, even though I'm leaning the other way. The chair is already saying we should just pull it to keep the peace, and I dont think that's right, but I need help saying why without sounding like I'm sneering at her.
