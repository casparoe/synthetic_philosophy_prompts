I'm a novelist, not a philosopher, but I've been asked to run a 90-minute session for about 18 undergrads in an ethics elective, and what I want is for them to walk out having felt the pull of the procreation asymmetry — the thing where it seems obviously wrong to bring a suffering child into the world and yet not remotely required to bring a happy one, even though those two attitudes don't obviously fit together. Don't let the name into the room until the debrief. Put on your philosopher-and-game-designer hat, think it through carefully, and brainstorm two or three mechanics before you commit to one.

What I'm picturing: tables of four play a "Registry of Births" that must issue exactly one rule — "the Registry shall refuse a life iff ___" — and apply it to a stack of case cards I hand out, each describing only the life that would result, never anyone currently existing. Then the twist: rules get swapped between tables, and each group has to defend somebody else's rule against a printed sheet of objections, including the one about nobody being worse off when nobody exists either way. And a round where their own rule, applied honestly, drags them to a verdict they hate.

Give me the case cards written out, a seperate objection sheet, the timeline, the debrief that maps each round onto a piece of the asymmetry, what predictably goes wrong the first time I run it, and be honest about which part of the idea the activity can't reproduce and I'll just have to say out loud. American English, please.
