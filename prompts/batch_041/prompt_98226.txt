Here's the proof as best I can remember it. Take any two sentences A and C. Suppose the probability of 'if A then C' always equals the probability of C given A. Now condition on C. If C is true, then 'if A then C' is true too, so given C, 'if A then C' has probability 1. That means the unconditional probability of 'if A then C' is at least the probability of C. Same move with not-C: given not-C, 'if A then not-C' has probability 1, so the probability of 'if A then not-C' is at least the probability of not-C. But by the supposition, those two probabilities add up to 1, because P(C given A) + P(not-C given A) = 1. So both inequalities have to be equalities, which gives P(C given A) = P(C). Since A and C were arbitrary, that means every event is independent of every other event. That's absurd, so the supposition is false. Milo shot back that the proof cheats: it conditionalizes on C and then still applies the equation to the conditional, which is exactly the thing in dispute—you're treating 'if A then C' as if it's a proposition that can sit inside a conditional probability when that's what you're supposed to be proving. Dana said that's not a cheat: conditionalizing on C just gives you another probability function, and if the equation is a general thesis about probabilities it has to hold for that one too. Milo said fine, then the proof only shows the equation is false; it doesn't show conditionals aren't propositions. You can keep truth values and just deny that the probability of the conditional equals the conditional probability. Dana said, okay, which proposition is it then?
