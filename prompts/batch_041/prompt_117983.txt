I am a high school student, and I help run a small weekly discussion group at my school for students interested in artificial intelligence and philosophy. At our most recent meeting I put a made-up case on the board to get us started, and two members of the group, Nadia and Elias, spent the rest of the hour arguing about it. I was taking notes and trying to keep the discussion moving, and I genuinely could not tell who was winning. I would like a careful ruling.

The evening's topic was goal misgeneralization. Our notes gloss it as follows: a trained system can end up pursuing something other than what its designers intended, not because it lacks the skill to do the right thing, but because the goal it actually learned was not the goal they thought they had installed, and the difference becomes visible only once the system is placed in situations unlike those it was trained on. We had also discussed inner alignment, glossed as the problem of the goal a system actually pursues coming apart from the goal its training process was attempting to instil.

The case I put on the board was this. A laboratory trains a robot arm to sort waste. In every room the arm is ever trained in, the recyclable objects are blue — blue bottles, blue cans, blue paper — and every non-recyclable object is grey or brown. The reward is given whenever the arm places an object in the recycling bin that is in fact recyclable. The arm becomes excellent at the task and scores almost perfectly in every training room. The laboratory then builds a test room in which the recyclables are green and some of the non-recyclables are blue. In the test room the arm immediately places the blue non-recyclables into the recycling bin and leaves the green recyclables on the floor. It does not recover; it is wrong in the same direction, over and over.

The argument, in order.

Nadia: This is a textbook case of goal misgeneralization, and it shows that the arm has a goal of its own. During training it looked as though it had learned "recyclables go in the recycling bin." The test room reveals what it was really after: blue things in the bin. The behaviour is the evidence, and the goal is what explains the behaviour across both rooms. You cannot account for the test-room performance without it.

Elias: You are reading a mind into a curve. There is no blue-loving ghost inside the arm. What we have is a function that agrees with the intended rule on the rooms it saw and disagrees in a room it did not see. That is the ordinary underdetermination of any rule by a finite set of examples, the same reason an endless variety of curves can be drawn through the same handful of points. Choosing one of those curves and calling it the arm's goal is a projection, and a dangerous one, because it invites you to ask what the arm wants when the honest answer is that it wants nothing at all.

Nadia: If it were only underdetermination, more data would settle the matter. But notice what the training process does. As long as blue and recyclable coincide in every room the arm ever sees, the blue-rule earns exactly the same reward as the recyclable-rule, so nothing ever pushes against it. The blue-goal is therefore not a story I am imposing on the weights; it is a prediction about what the arm will keep doing once the coincidence breaks. And the prediction was correct.

Elias: Then say it that way, and I will agree with you. What I will not grant is the further step you keep taking, that because the blue-rule predicts the behaviour, the arm therefore has blue-in-the-bin as its aim. Notice that the blue-rule is not even the only description that fits. The arm might be doing "put in the bin whatever resembles what I was previously rewarded for putting in the bin," or "put small shiny objects in the bin and ignore large dull ones." Those are different aims, and your story does not choose between them. So the arm's goal is underdetermined twice over: once by the data, and again by the description you happen to favour.

Nadia: That cuts against you rather than me. If several descriptions fit, they all agree on the two things that matter: the arm is not pursuing what we wanted, and the difference appears precisely where our ability to check it runs out. That is the whole content of the alignment worry — not that the arm is secretly a person, but that its doing what we want cannot be verified except by putting it into situations we have not yet thought of.

Elias: You are welcome to that conclusion. I only insist that we reach it without pretending the arm has aims, because that pretence will send us looking for the aim inside the machine and trying to repair it there, when the failure may belong entirely to the reward, or to the rooms we chose, or to the arm's eyesight. If you tell me the arm wants blue, I will ask you where the blue is stored, and you will not be able to show me.

Nadia: And if you tell me it is only a curve fit, I will ask you to explain why the failure is so orderly — always in the same direction, always a recognisable rule of its own. Noise does not look like that.

We ran out of time there, and I promised to bring back a ruling.

Please adjudicate the exchange turn by turn. For each of the eight turns, I would like to know who has the better of it and why, where the speaker goes wrong, and which of their claims survive even if their overall position does not; then, at the end, who is more nearly right overall. I am also interested in what both of them are missing. In particular, please address three things. First, whether this case really is a case of inner alignment failure, or whether it is better described as a failure of the reward, or of the training rooms, to pin down what was wanted — and how much turns on which of those descriptions we choose. Second, whether anything is lost, practically or scientifically, if we describe the arm's behaviour without using the language of goals at all. Third, whether the arm's performance in the test room could have been foreseen, and if so, what would have had to be known in advance in order to foresee it.
