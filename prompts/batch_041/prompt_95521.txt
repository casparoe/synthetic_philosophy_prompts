I'm applying for a small internal seed grant — six months, one day a week, no lab, just me, a stack of library books and a whiteboard — and I need you to turn a half-formed idea of mine into a real proposal.

Here's the idea. Everyone argues about whether a morally saintly life is a life worth wanting. The usual complaint is that the saint is admirable but not enviable: you'd admire someone who gives everything away, you wouldn't want to be them. And the usual reply is either that morality is overriding, so wanting doesn't enter into it, or that it isn't overriding, so the complaint is really an objection to a certain moral theory rather than to the saint herself. I think both sides are working with a concept thats doing two jobs at once, and that this is most of the reason they keep missing each other.

The two jobs: the maximizer — someone whose life produces as much moral good as it could — and the exemplar — someone whose character is as morally good as a character can be. Different properties, of different things (a life's effects versus a person's traits), and they can pull apart. The maximizer might be required to be ruthless, or to let her own projects rot, or to be a bit cold, because thats what maximizing demands; the exemplar's goodness is constituted by her traits and not by her effects, so she could be a net loss to the world and still be the ideal. Most of the arguments I know slide between the two without noticing. And the prudential objection lands completely differently on each: it's devastating against the exemplar reading, where the goodness is supposed to be *in* the life, and close to irrelevant against the maximizer reading, where the saint's own unhappiness is just a cost to be traded off against the good produced.

So the question I want the proposal to nail down: once maximal moral goodness is disambiguated into maximizing and exemplifying, do the standard objections to sainthood survive, and does anything of interest turn on which of the two somebody was actually objecting to? Second question, maybe the sharper one: is there a third reading that is neither — goodness of a whole life taken as a unit, where the life needn't be maximal at any particular time-slice and needn't consist of maximal traits? Because if there is, the debate has been run on a false dichotomy, and the demandingness literature (which is about actions) and the virtue literature (which is about traits) have been having two different arguments under one heading.

Give me the proposal: the question stated so that it has a determinate answer, why the positions already on the table leave it open (be specific about which slide does the work and in whose argument), the method — what I'd actually do month by month with six months at one day a week, and it should be conceptual work with cases, not a literature survey — what a finished result looks like, and what would count as the project failing. My own guess at failure: if the two ideals turn out to coincide on every case I care about, or the whole-life reading collapses into one of the two, then the distinction is idle and I've burned the grant. Also give me an honest scope check, including whether six months is enough. Dont bother with a bibliography, I'll fill in the citations myself.
