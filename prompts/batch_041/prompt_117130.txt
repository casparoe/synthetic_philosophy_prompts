I direct a small, foundation-supported research unit with roughly three researcher-years and a mandate to propose reforms to working-time law and workplace practice in a high-income democracy. Our aim is to reduce overwork-related harm, including karoshi-type health collapse and death, and to expand genuine leisure, without overclaiming to settle every question of distributive justice. The backdrop is a question I take to be genuinely open: who has a right to work, who has a right to leisure, and how do those rights interact? We cannot undertake original empirical research; we can do conceptual and normative analysis and synthesize existing evidence. The surrounding culture retains a strong Protestant-work-ethic presumption that paid work is the primary site of desert and social worth, and the live policy proposal before us is a statutory 35-hour week. We are considering whether to organize our work around a distinction between time sufficiency, understood as having enough hours free from paid work, and time sovereignty, understood as having control over when, whether, and how much one works. We are also drawn to a further distinction between leisure as merely discretionary time and leisure as activity valuable in itself, and to the idea that the relevant right is not a right to a quantity of free hours but a right against domination by employers' time demands -- a right to refuse overtime, to predict one's schedule, and to keep work from colonizing the rest of life. A 35-hour week might increase time sufficiency while decreasing time sovereignty for workers who want or need more hours, and karoshi law might be more responsive to hours than to control. I need a prioritized research agenda, not a survey.

Which open conceptual questions or lines of work in this area matter most for that agenda, and in what order? Please identify five to seven candidates and defend the ordering. For each, explain why it is upstream of other questions; how tractable it is for philosophers and policy theorists rather than empirical researchers; how neglected it is relative to its importance; and what evidence, argument, or policy development over the next few years would move it up or down the list. I am especially interested in questions that would change what we recommend about the 35-hour week, about the legal recognition of overwork harms such as karoshi, and about whether leisure should be treated as a right, a good, or a reward for work. Also identify one or two questions in this vicinity that are overrated -- because they are already settled for practical purposes, or because their resolution would not change much downstream -- and explain why. Conclude with a concrete allocation of our three researcher-years across the top priorities, with a sentence of justification for each allocation.
