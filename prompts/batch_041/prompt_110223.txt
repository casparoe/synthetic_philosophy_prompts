I took early retirement from a research lab four years ago — thirty-odd years of spectroscopy and method development, the last ten managing a group — and I've been using the free time to read all the philosophy I skipped in my twenties. The library reading group here just finished Kuhn's Structure of Scientific Revolutions and I've been stewing about it for a week. My problem is this. Kuhn says people working in different paradigms are incommensurable, that the phlogiston chemists and Lavoisier's generation might as well have been living in different worlds, and that a paradigm change has more in common with a religious conversion than with a proof. Fine, I can accept that the textbooks tidy up the history more than they should, and that nobody in 1775 was running a controlled trial. But then take Lavoisier himself. His opponents eventually came round — that happened, they didn't all die off and get replaced by students. And they came round because he could predict things they couldn't, and because they could watch it happen on a balance with their own eyes. If incommensurabilty means what I think it means, none of that should have been possible: they shouldn't have been able to understand his claims in the first place, or to agree with him about what would count as a good test, and the whole thing should have been settled by who had the better patrons at the Academy. So which is it? Was the argument actually doing any work across the gap, or wasn't it?

And here's the part that really bothers me, because it's about my own thirty years and not about the eighteenth century. If paradigm choice isn't settled by evidence but by something like conversion, then when my group spent two years building an instrument to decide whether a peak was real or an artifact of the sample prep, we weren't getting any closer to how the stuff actually behaves — we were just solving a puzzle inside a framework, and sooner or later somebody swaps the framework out and our answer looks as quaint as phlogiston. I don't think that can be right, and I suspect I'm misusing one of Kuhn's words, but I can't see where I've gone off the rails. My daughter (philosophy minor, twenty-three, knows everything) says I'm reading Kuhn like a positivist, which I take it is not a compliment. So tell me straight: what have I actually got wrong, and what does Kuhn really mean by incommensurable — because if it just comes down to people with different training noticing different things and using different words for them, then I don't understand why the book caused such a fuss in the first place.
