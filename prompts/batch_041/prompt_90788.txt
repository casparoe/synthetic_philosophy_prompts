I am the chief scientist at a state agency's evidence review unit. Six staff, all trained scientists, none with any background in philosophy, and we are the office that has to say in writing whether a claim is established. Each month we triage two or three questions and twice a quarter we issue a formal determination; the determinations get quoted in the press and read into the legislative record, so a hedge costs us about as much as an error. Two staffers get ninety minutes at a table with the case file — the studies, exposure estimates, and modeling reports that other people have already assembled — and no time or mandate to go find new literature. Anything we escalate goes to me, and above a threshold it goes to a public board.

Please write us a reusable determination procedure whose governing idea is the underdetermination of theory by evidence. It has to do one job well: separate the cases where the evidence settles the question from the cases where two or more options survive the evidence and the choice among them is being made by something other than evidence — a default assumption, a precautionary tilt, a cost trade-off, an institutional habit — and route those two kinds of cases to different rooms with different sign-offs.

The procedure should be a sequence of questions, and for each one: what counts as a sufficient answer, what disqualifies the case from going further, where it stops with a verdict, and where it hands the decision to a named person who must state on the record what they are deciding and on what grounds. Give it four verdicts — Settled; Gap Closeable; Gap Structural; Question Malformed — renaming or adding a fifth if you think one is needed. Keep the checklist itself to something a staffer can actually run in the ninety minutes, and put your explanations and the philosophical reasoning in a separate section after it.

Four things I want built into the procedure, stated in operational terms rather than as allusions, and with a note on which distinctions can be dropped without loss and which have to survive the translation:

1. The difference between the data being compatible with a hypothesis and two named rivals fitting the data equally well. Compatibility is nearly free and should not count as confirmation of anything.
2. Where the gap sits: in the central hypothesis or in an auxiliary assumption — the dose-response model, the exposure estimate, the measurement instrument, the extrapolation across species or populations.
3. Whether the gap is closeable: could a study of a kind we could plausibly fund, and that our staff could actually evaluate, close it, or would any feasible study leave the rivals standing? Treat those as different verdicts, not as degrees of the same one.
4. The fact that the rivals anyone can name are not the rivals there are. Tell us what to do when the case file contains one hypothesis and a residual something else, and why an empty rival list should be a warning rather than support.

Then run the procedure on three cases and show the verdict and the paperwork each would produce:

A. A solvent causes liver tumors in rodents at high doses; the human studies are null but small. Question: does the evidence establish that it causes cancer in people at exposures measured near the facility?
B. A cluster of a rare illness in a town beside a facility. The agency has already said publicly that the facility is the likely cause. Our review found the exposure estimates too coarse to separate exposed from unexposed, and that the town's age profile and recent in-migration could produce the same pattern. Question: does the evidence establish the link?
C. Question: is the safety margin we currently require adequate?

One of these should come out in a way we will not like, and I expect it to be B, so do not soften it. Close with the failure modes: where the procedure will hand down a confident-looking verdict that is really a judgment in disguise, where it can be gamed by whoever writes the case file, and what it cannot do at all. American English.
