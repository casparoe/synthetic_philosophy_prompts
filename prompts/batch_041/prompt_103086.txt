I'm finishing a master's in public health and I keep tripping over the same shape of thing in my data work, and in my notes I've started calling it "the flip," which I know is not a real term. Here's what I mean. In one dataset, people who took a certain supplement had higher rates of some condition than people who didn't — but when I split them by age band, the supplement users looked better in every band. My adviser said that's the famous one and moved on. Then a few weeks later I was working with a hospital sample and found that two conditions that are basically unrelated in the general population looked strongly linked among the patients in that sample; nothing about either one causes the other, the link just showed up because I was only looking at people who ended up in the hospital. And in a third case, a classmate and I had the same variables and the same data, we ran what felt like equally reasonable analyses, and we came to opposite conclusions about whether a variable mattered — neither of us did anything obviously wrong.

My classmates keep telling me these are "basically Simpson's paradox," but they feel seperate to me, and when I try to line them up they don't quite stack. In the first one, the grouping variable was there all along and was muddying the overall picture. In the second, the grouping variable is an effect of the things I'm studying — the hospital stay — and slicing by it seems to manufacture a link rather than reveal or hide one. The third is stranger still, because both analyses seem defensible and I can't tell which is the "real" answer. So what I want to know is whether these have seperate established names, what exactly distinguishes them, and whether there's a broader label that covers all of them, or whether I've just been lumping unrelated things under one nickname.

I'm honestly less interested in a bare name than in the joints: for each of my three cases, what makes it that kind of thing rather than one of the others, and where does my second case fall if it turns out the hospital stay is partly caused by one of the conditions but not the other? If you think I'm wrong to separate them at all, say so plainly, and tell me what would change your mind — like a case that looks like my second one but turns out to really be the first, or a definition under which all three collapse into one.
