I've been reporting on the longtermism people for about a year now, and there's one argument that keeps following me around — I can't tell if it's obviously broken or obviously right, and the people who make it are so serene about it that it spooks me. A researcher I've been shadowing (he asked me not to name him, so: D.) walked me through it again last week. Roughly:

(1) There's a real chance — he puts his own between 1 and 5 percent — that we're living at the hinge of history: the stretch of time whose outcome settles the whole rest of the story.
(2) If we are, the difference between a good hinge and a bad one isn't millions of lives. It's every life that would ever have existed, everywhere, forever. He added that if space is actually infinite and we ever get out there, the number isn't "very big," it's infinite.
(3) So the expected value of nudging the hinge is that number times the few percent, which is still that number.
(4) Set that against any ordinary moral consideration — a war, a famine, somebody's civil liberties, a promise you made — and the ordinary thing is a rounding error. Finite against infinite, or at least finite against 10^30.
(5) So any policy that raises the odds of a good hinge, even slightly, is worth almost any price. If the price is that everyone alive now loses their freedom, or a whole generation gets conscripted into making the hinge go right — he didn't flinch — you pay it. The math says pay it.
(6) Here's the part I can't shake: nothing in (1)-(4) needs us to actually be at the hinge. It only needs a nonzero chance. And he cheerfully agreed that a 12th century monk could have run the exact same argument with the Last Judgment where he's got the far future, and by the same math should have torched everything for it. His version of the argument is, as he put it, "probability-insensitive" — it doesn't care whether the chance is 5% or 0.0001%. He thinks that's a selling point.
(7) When I said the monk thing felt like a reductio he just shrugged and said if that bothers me, I'm not taking the stakes seriously.

I dont think I'm just being squeamish, but I also don't want to be the guy who waves off the future because the numbers are annoying. I'd like to keep (1) and (2) if I possibly can. (5) and (7) I can't live with. So which step is actually doing the damage, why does it look airtight right up until you push on it, and what's the bill if I give it up — do I have to quit using expected value for this kind of question, or quit caring about the far future, or just learn to tolerate a little fanaticism in my own thinking? And if you think the argument is valid and (5) is just true, say that plainly and tell me what I owe the monk.
