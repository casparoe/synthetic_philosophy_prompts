In my ethics club we spent a session arguing about whether people with comfortable incomes are obligated to give a large share of their money away, and one member defended her position with an analogy: moral requirements are like debts, and everything beyond them is like a gift. A debt is owed to someone who can claim it, its amount is determinate, and paying it discharges it; a gift is voluntary, no one can demand it, and it is praiseworthy precisely because it was not owed. From this she concluded that large-scale generosity and heroism are gifts rather than debts, so no one is required to perform them.

How far does that analogy actually hold? Please lay out the correspondence explicitly — what plays the role of the debtor, the creditor, the debt, the gift, and the recipient (when a soldier smothers a grenade, for instance, who is the creditor and who is the recipient?) — and then tell me where the mapping breaks. In particular: is there anything in morality that corresponds to a creditor's claim? Does the optionality of a gift have the same source as the optionality of a heroic act? And what does the analogy quietly assume about what we own and are entitled to keep? Which of these disanalogies matter for the conclusion she draws, and which are incidental? Does that conclusion survive once they are on the table, or was the analogy carrying it all along? If there is a better analogy for the same point, I would like to know what it is.
