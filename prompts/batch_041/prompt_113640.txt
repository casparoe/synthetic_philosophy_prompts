Something I've been chewing on and don't have the words for. I've spent time in Singapore and did a couple of stints in China, and both places run on a bargain nobody ever writes down: you don't get much say in who runs things, but the streets are safe, the trains come, and every year there's more money around than the year before. Singapore is the naked version. They pay ministers over a million a year, and the defense is always the same, pay peanuts and you get crooks or idiots, and the same government will point at the public housing, the subway, the crime rate, and basically say thats what you're getting for your patience. In China the story I heard is that local officials live and die by their growth numbers, promotions follow GDP, and the whole system's claim on you is look what we built. A friend calls it 'getting your money's worth out of the government.' I laughed, then thought about it for a week.

So here's what I actually want to know. If you take that whole arrangement seriously, paying the rulers like executives, grading them like salesmen, answering critics with results, what does it commit you to about where a government's right to rule comes from? Does it mean legitimacy is an output, something earned quarter by quarter, so the right to rule expires when the growth does? Because nobody involved acts like that. When things slow down they point at order instead of prosperity, or at the last thirty years instead of the last three. Which makes me wonder whether the results are the source of the authority at all, or just the reciept, or a sales pitch, or a stand-in for something else like plain competence.

Can you lay it out for me? What view of political authority this bargain really presupposes, what rival views it quietly rules out, and where it's just loose enough to fit two or three different stories. I'd also like to know if the fairness claim fits with the price-tag claim, no donors, no screaming matches, everybody gets a share, versus we are worth what we're paid, or whether those are two different arguments wearing the same coat. And the test I keep coming back to: if Singapore got twenty years poorer but stayed just as safe and clean, would the government there be illegitimate, or only unpopular? Those feel like different things, and which one you say probably tells me which theory I'm actually looking at. If the honest answer is that the performance story is mostly a story and the real premise underneath is that whoever keeps the peace gets to keep the keys, then say that, and tell me what it means for whether prosperity and order can justify rule or only explain it, and what would look different about these places if they ran on the other view instead.
