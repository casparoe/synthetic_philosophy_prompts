I run a small metaphysics reading group in the back room of a pub — grad students, a retired chemistry teacher, and one guy who insists that everything is either a particle or a fiction — and we've just finished a unit on dispositions. Everyone was perfectly happy with the party line that calling a glass fragile is just a roundabout way of saying it would break if you struck it, right up until I raised the finkish cases and the masking cases, at which point the whole thing came apart and we burned an hour arguing about the vase in my kitchen, which is genuinely thin-walled and brittle but has one of those cheap anti-cat gadgets inside it that stiffens the whole thing the instant anything moves toward it, so it has never broken and, as things stand, would not break if you hit it with a hammer. I want to turn that mess into a handout for next week. Not a list of who-said-what — I don't need citations, and I'd actually rather there were no bibliography at all, since I'm just photocopying it — but a map of the logical space of positions on what disposition ascriptions mean and what they pick out, with the real views located inside it. If I've attached a name to the wrong view anywhere, please just say so; I get these muddled.

Here's what I'd like. Build me a decision tree whose branch nodes are questions someone could answer without already knowing which position it leads to. Something like: do you think a disposition ascription is analysable at all in terms of what the object would do in certain circumstances, or is dispositionality a basic, unreduced feature of the world; if it is analysable, is a plain counterfactual enough, or does the analysis need a clause that keeps the object's intrinsic basis in place, or does the link to counterfactuals have to be given up entirely in favour of something chance-based or potentiality-based; do finks and masks show that the analysis needs patching, that it is hopeless, or that they aren't really counterexamples in the first place; and where do you land on the categorical-versus-dispositional distinction — two kinds of property, one reducible to the other, two ways of picking out one and the same property, or everything on one side of the line. That last branch matters most to me. I'd also like a branch about whether a disposition needs a single categorical basis in the object, or whether it can be relational or structural — my favourite case there is a key, which is disposed to open a lock it has never met, and whose basis looks like it's spread across two objects. The leaves should be actual named positions (the plain conditional analysis, the reformed one, the primitivist or dispositional-essentialist camp, the identity or limit view, Humean categoricalism, pan-dispositionalism, potentialism, and whatever else belongs there), each carrying one sentence on its most awkward commitment. Six or seven branch nodes, and somewhere between ten and twelve leaves. Please use these cases, refining them if I've described them badly: the anti-cat vase above; the plastic chandelier at my parents' house that would shatter if struck, but only because a hidden hammer fires at it whenever you swing (surely not fragile?); a crystal that is brittle in itself but has a lattice of foam grown into it, so the mask isn't a separate thing you could peel off; a shy wire that is live but cuts its own current the instant anything touches it; and a lump of plutonium that will decay in the next hour with probability one half, with a device that resets it whenever it is about to — a chancy disposition with a fink attached. If any of those is just a standard fink or mask in a costume, say so.

Format, please, in this order. First a section called The cases, about 150 words, restating each case in a line and saying which standard case, if any, it is a version of. Then The branches, about 500 words, the nodes in order, each phrased as a question with the options it splits into. Then The leaves, about 600 words, each named position with its most awkward commitment in a sentence. Then Empty rooms, about 200 words, on the combinations of answers nobody actually occupies, and whether that's because the view is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. Then Blind spots, about 150 words, on which leaves the tree cannot seperate from one another and what extra question would be needed to prise them apart. The audience is bright second- and third-years plus the pub guy, so keep the prose plain, but definately don't dumb down the positions themselves.
