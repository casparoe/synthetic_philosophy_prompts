I'm putting together materials for a reading group on the philosophy of mind, and I want a piece of speculative fiction to open the session with. The setting I've sketched is a near-future jurisdiction in which the question "is this a mind?" has become a matter of administrative law: a body called the Substrate Equivalence Tribunal issues certificates of mentation, and its governing doctrine is functionalist through and through — a mental state is whatever plays the right causal role within the right organisation, and the material that happens to realise that role is, in principle, beside the point. The Tribunal applies a two-stage test it calls "role first, realiser second", and its dissenting members have long grumbled about what they nickname the grain objection: that the medium is never quite as irrelevant as the doctrine pretends. I'd like the story to take the functionalist principle seriously — no cheap gotchas — and to find its drama in the technical pressure points rather than in generic robot melancholy. Among the things it should touch on: the difference between identifying a state by its causal role and identifying it by its physical realiser; the thought that one and the same role might be realised in radically different stuff; the suspicion that if we are generous enough about what counts as "realising" a program, then absolutely any physical system can be read as implementing any program, so that certification collapses into triviality; and the quieter worry that any functional decomposition already presupposes a boundary around the system being decomposed, which makes collectives, ecosystems and markets awkward candidates while rendering ordinary persons surprisingly hard to individuate. A reader who knows the literature should be able to spot the standard moves — the population of China as a mind, the possibility of a system that is functionally flawless yet phenomenally blank, the objection that multiple realisability turns mental properties into disjunctive gerrymanders — without any of them being named.

Please write the story at around 1,300 words. I'd like a concrete case before the Tribunal to carry it: say, a tidal-warning network grown from cultured slime mould that a coastal town has come to treat as a colleague, and an assessor who must decide whether to certify it and who is uncomfortably aware that her own certification, years earlier, rested on precisely the triviality she is now expected to reject. Give the setting enough texture — the hearing room, the town, the grain of the mould itself — that the philosophy arrives through the world rather than through speeches, and let the ending be genuinely undecided rather than a twist. Structure it as exactly five numbered scenes, each a few paragraphs long, with no prefatory note, no framing introduction and no closing commentary from you: the first scene should simply begin the story, and the fifth should end it.
