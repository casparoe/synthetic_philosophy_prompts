I am a postgraduate student in the philosophy of language, and I have spent the better part of a term trying to get to the bottom of Quine's indeterminacy of translation — the field linguist, the rabbit, the analytical hypotheses, the whole apparatus — only to find that a conversation with a friend in a computer science department has left me more confused than I was at the outset. He works on machine translation, and the systems he uses are trained purely on text: no bilingual informants, no shared perceptual situation with the speakers of the source language, no dictionary, and no access to the circumstances in which the source sentences were uttered. You feed in a corpus in a language the system has never encountered, and it returns English renderings that competent speakers judge fluent and, on the whole, faithful. He takes this to be a refutation in practice: if a machine can settle a translation without any of the guesswork Quine describes, then the indeterminacy was merely an artefact of the field linguist's poverty of evidence. When I pressed him, he added a further point that I found harder to answer — that the system can also be made to emit an explicit manual, a set of rules mapping source expressions to English ones, so that the translator's analytical hypotheses are now not hypothetical at all.

What I should like from you is a careful reconstruction of Quine's own response, written as though the situation had been explained to him and he had been given time to think it through. This is a prediction, not a summary of what he published. Would he say the machine has produced a translation, or only a serviceable correlation of sentences that happens to satisfy its users? How would he bring his notions of stimulus meaning, observation sentences and analytical hypotheses to bear on a system that has never been in perceptual contact with the speakers it translates? Would the existence of the manual change anything — and in particular, would he expect an alternative manual, incompatible with the first yet fitting all the available evidence equally well, to remain possible, and what would it show about the machine's output if it did? I am also keen to know how he would distinguish the indeterminacy of translation from the inscrutability of reference in this case: does the machine's output settle whether the rabbit-word is about rabbits, undetached rabbit parts or temporal rabbit-stages, or does it merely select one of those options without excluding the others?

Finally, I should like an honest account of the limits of the exercise. The scenario is not one Quine addressed in his lifetime, and I do not want a verdict smuggled in from his general behaviourism if his considered view would have been more guarded. Tell me where the case falls outside the range of examples he actually considered, which of his commitments it strains, and whether it exposes a genuine weakness in the indeterminacy thesis or merely a limitation of the analogy between a field linguist and a statistical model. Please write in British English, and please do not give me a reading list or a set of citations; I know the texts, and what I want is the argument.
