I read a lot of cosmology blogs and pop-sci, so I know just enough to get confused. Heres the thing that bugs me.

There's a norm in cosmology, more folk norm than formal rule, where you look at a model — eternal inflation, some multiverse setup, whatever — and you ask how many Boltzmann brains it produces relative to ordinary observers like us. A Boltzmann brain being a lone brain that fluctuates into existence out of the vacuum with fake memories, thinks for a second, and falls apart. And the norm is: if your model says BBs vastly outnumber normal observers, thats a strike against it, maybe a fatal one. People say the model has a Boltzmann brain problem, and they go hunting for a new measure, or they declare the model dead. The reasoning being roughly: if the model were right, the odds are overwhelming that I'm a momentary hallucinating brain rather than a person typing this, and that's absurd, so something's wrong with the model.

Fine. But this looks inconsistent to me with something the same people do. A lot of the same crowd uses observer selection to take the sting out of fine-tuning: yeah the constants look tuned for life, but if the ensemble is big enough then of course we find ourselves in a life-friendly corner, since only those corners get observed. Same move, no? In one case my position in the distribution is treated as informative (I'm not a BB, so the model is wrong); in the other my position is treated as unremarkable (someone has to be in the good corner). When I push on this, people say it's about measures, or typicality, or self-locating evidence, and I dont follow.

So what does the practice presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit theory behind a model that predicts too many BBs being a bad model: what view of evidence, of probability, and of what my own first-person situation is allowed to tell me about the world. Does the norm need facts about which observer I happen to be to count as evidence, so that me not being a BB is data? What has to be true about how observers are counted for that to work, and does it need one particular counting rule, or could several very different ones license the same norm? And does it presuppose that a Boltzmann brain is a defective observer rather than just a rare one — is mostly-BBs even a prediction about observers, if the BBs can't observe anything or check it?

Then the consistency question. Is the BB move the same move as the fine-tuning move, or not? Someone told me the difference is that a theory which says I'm a BB also says my reasoning is garbage, including the reasoning that led me to the theory, so it undercuts itself in a way fine-tuning doesn't. Is that right, or does it prove too much? Where exactly is the asymmetry, and is it principled or just that people like one conclusion and not the other?

Be clear about what the practice strictly needs versus what it's just bundled with — I suspect it's underdetermined and you could keep the norm while swapping out the philosophy underneath. And last: if you replace the presupposed view with the main rival, the one where self-locating facts carry no evidential weight at all, so I learn nothing about the universe from seeming to be an ordinary person, what happens? Does the BB objection evaporate, and what else goes with it — the fine-tuning response, the whole anthropic toolkit — or does the norm survive on some other footing? I don't care who said what and I don't want a reading list, I want the reconstruction.
