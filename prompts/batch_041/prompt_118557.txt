ok so help me think through something that's been bugging me about online games. in most modern fighting games the netcode does this: your machine guesses what your opponent is about to press, runs the game forward with the guess, and shows you the outcome immediately -- you see your jab connect, you react to it. if the guess was wrong, the game rewinds the last handful of frames, re-runs them with the inputs that actually came in, and jumps forward again. so the jab you saw land, and flinched at, and maybe changed your whole plan over, never happened. nobody thinks this is a bug; it's strictly better than making everyone wait for the round trip, and it's what the genre runs on. but look at what it implies: what happened in the match is whatever the authoritative machine says happened, and your screen was showing you a cancelled preview. meanwhile plenty of other games (older shooters, a lot of peer-to-peer stuff) let the client be authoritative, so when two players' machines disagree about whether the shot landed, each one is right from where they're sitting and the disagreement just never gets resolved.

what i want to know is what that whole setup presupposes, metaphysically, about virtual worlds:

(1) does authoritative-server-plus-rollback require that there be one privileged description of the world -- a real state of the machine that your rendering only approximates -- so that a virtual event is real just in case it's in that state? or could you get the exact same engineering out of a view where the server state, your rendered frame, and the saved replay are all equally real (or all equally fictional) and "what happened" is just indexed to who's asking? i want to know which theories of virtual objects the practice actually rules out versus which ones it merely happens to fit.

(2) related: as i understand it some of these games save the replay as a list of inputs rather than a video, so the official record of a match is a recipe that gets re-cooked on playback and can come out different after a patch. does that break the idea that there was a fact of the matter about the match, or is it just a boring point about records?

(3) is the player who flinched at a jab that got rolled back owed anything? not legally. i mean is there a fact of the matter about whether a harm occured to them, and if so is it a harm in the virtual world or in this one?

and push back on me where i need it -- if some of this is just the old appearance/reality thing wearing a headset, say so, because i honestly can't tell.
