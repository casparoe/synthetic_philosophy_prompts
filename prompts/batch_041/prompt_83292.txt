Last month, at a seminar dinner, I was seated between two colleagues, Ines and Tomas, who spent most of the evening on the knowability paradox — the argument that 'every truth is knowable in principle' collapses into 'every truth is known'. They each asked me to concede the point, and I could not. I have no stake in either position and would like the exchange assessed as they actually made it, move by move, rather than a general essay on the paradox.

Ines set out the argument. Take the anti-realist's principle that every truth is knowable in principle: for any proposition p, if p is true then it is possible that p should be known. Suppose some truth p is in fact never known. By the principle it must be possible to know the proposition that p is true and that p is never known. But knowledge is factive — you cannot know what is not so — and it distributes over conjunction, so anyone who knew that conjunction would know p and would also know that p is never known, from which it follows that p is not known. Contradiction. So the conjunction cannot be known; by the principle it therefore cannot be true; so there is no truth that is never known, which is to say that every truth is known. Ines concluded that the principle is refuted as stated, and that the interesting question is what an anti-realist should put in its place.

Tomas replied that the proof is valid but its target is narrower than it looks. The only proposition it exploits is one that says of an unknown truth that it is unknown, and such a proposition is unknowable for a reason we can see in advance: supposing it known yields a contradiction. He proposed what he called the self-defeat restriction: the knowability principle covers every truth except those whose being known would by itself yield a contradiction. The paradoxical proposition is precisely such a case, so the proof cannot touch the restricted principle; and no ordinary unknown truth is excluded, since nothing contradictory follows from knowing, say, how many scratches there are on the underside of a particular bench.

Ines objected that this is a repair indistinguishable from special pleading. The restriction exempts exactly the propositions that cause trouble; it fixes the exemption by appeal to the very notion of knowledge whose scope is in dispute; and it licenses the anti-realist to exempt any truth she cannot see how to know by declaring its knowability self-defeating. A doctrine that shrinks by precisely the amount needed to escape each counterexample is not a doctrine but a habit. She added that the proof can be rebuilt for any notion that is both factive and distributive over conjunction, so a repair aimed at this one proposition is a local patch on a general fault.

Tomas answered that the restriction tracks a real distinction rather than a convenience. Propositions about what is and is not known have their sense fixed by the very practice the knowability principle is a thesis about, and so cannot serve as independent test cases; the situation is like objecting to the law of non-contradiction with 'this sentence is not true', which is not a counterexample but a malformed input. He granted that 'would yield a contradiction' needs sharpening — it invites the objection that we cannot tell in advance which propositions those are — but held that the shape of the repair is right.

Ines's last word was that even granting the shape, the result is no longer an anti-realist thesis about truth: 'every truth is knowable unless knowing it would be contradictory' is true on any view of truth, the realist's included. A principle both parties accept does no work. She conceded that she could not herself say what the anti-realist should assert instead.

One thing came up that neither of them pursued. Tomas remarked in passing that the proof goes through unchanged if 'is known' is replaced by 'is verified'; Ines agreed, and said this showed the fault was general rather than local, but neither of them said what follows from that.

Please adjudicate move by move: which of Ines's and Tomas's claims are right, which are wrong, and where each is talking past the other. Then say what both of them are missing — in particular, whether the argument requires a step that both took for granted, and whether there is a third response to the paradox that neither considered. Finally, tell me who has the better of the argument overall, and what an anti-realist would have to mean by 'knowable in principle' for the losing side to be right after all.
