I'm trying to nail down where I actually stand on advance directives, and the case thats bugging me has a wrinkle I havent seen discussed. A man in his early 50s, fully competent, signs a directive refusing all life-prolonging treatment and artificial nutrition if he ever gets advanced dementia. Fine, standard stuff. But he adds a clause: if the later, demented version of him appears to enjoy his life and asks to be kept alive, that apparent contentment is itself to be treated as a symptom of the disease and disregarded — the directive pre-emptively disowns any future preference to the contrary. He gets dementia. He does seem content. In his halting way he asks to keep going. Whats your credence that the earlier self's directive should be honored? Give me a number, then decompose it — how much rides on whether the later self counts as the same person, on autonomy-at-the-time-of-writing versus welfare-at-the-time-of-living, on whether his contentment is genuine or an artifact of the disease, on whether a directive can validly contain a clause that pre-emptively discounts the later self's own testimony, and any other terms you'd add. One line and a rough probability each, then how they combine. Which term are you least sure about, and what would move it? And how confident are you in the final number?
