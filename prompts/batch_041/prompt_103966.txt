In a seminar on the ethics of procreation, a colleague repeatedly invokes the old saying that it is better never to have been born as though it settles the matter against any claim that bringing someone into existence can be a benefit to that person. I want to see exactly what that saying could be asserting. Please lay out the distinct precise theses it might express, from the trivial or near-tautological to the substantive and controversial. For each reading, specify what it says about the comparative value of existence and non-existence, whether it concerns the welfare of the person who might exist, the reasons of the prospective parent, or impersonal value, and whether it presupposes that non-existence is a state that can be better or worse for anyone. Then indicate which readings would actually support my colleague's use of the saying to defeat pro-natalist conclusions, which would merely restate the asymmetry, and which would collapse into a claim no one denies. I would also like the readings ordered by how much metaphysical and axiological commitment they carry, so that I can see which a defender of the asymmetry can hold without taking on the rest.
