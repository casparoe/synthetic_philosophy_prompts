I am a doctoral candidate in philosophy of science, working mainly on decision theory and the foundations of statistics, and I have spent the last two weeks arguing with a colleague over an argument he treats as a clean reductio and I treat as a machine for proving too much. It concerns the reference-class problem for singular credence, and it goes like this. (1) A rational credence about a singular event must be grounded in the relative frequency of events of that kind within some reference class to which the event belongs. (2) When several such classes are available, rationality directs us to the narrowest one for which we have reliable frequency data, on the grounds that a narrower class conditions on more of what is relevant and so should dominate. (3) But for any event and any class containing it, a still narrower class can be described by conjoining another true predicate the event satisfies, and this can be iterated until we reach the class whose only member is the event itself. (4) The relative frequency in that singleton class is one or zero, depending on whether the event in fact occurred. (5) So by (2), rationality requires a credence of one or zero in the event, that is, certainty about it. (6) Yet it is plainly not rational to be certain about contingent, uncertain events, and the rule was offered precisely as a rule of rational credence. (7) Therefore the narrowest-reference-class rule, which I take to be a fixed point of Reichenbach's frequentism, is incoherent, and any frequentist reconstruction of singular credence falls with it. (8) Worse, the same pattern appears to show that no credence short of certainty about any singular event can be rationally required at all, which would render the whole practice of probabilistic forecasting, in insurance, medicine, and meteorology, rationally arbitrary. My colleague reads this as a refutation of frequentism; I suspect it is a refutation of too much, but I cannot locate the point at which it goes wrong, and I would rather keep (1) than (5) if I have to choose.

Please act as an expert philosopher of probability with a firm grasp of the frequentist, subjectivist, and propensity traditions, and think this through carefully rather than reaching for a stock objection. Begin by restating the argument in your own words and marking the joints where a slide is most likely, since I would like to see where you think the equivocation or the quantifier shift, if there is one, actually occurs. Then commit: name the single step or premise you would reject, explain why it looked unobjectionable to begin with, and price the rejection honestly. In doing so, tell me what a subjectivist, a propensity theorist, and someone who keeps frequencies but repairs the rule by adding a relevance or reliability clause would each have to concede to stop the argument, whether the repair is ad hoc or principled, and whether giving up my premise (1) is the cheapest exit or whether I am better off surrendering something else. I am especially interested in whether the move from (3) to (4) is legitimate and whether a class defined by a predicate that already entails the outcome can do any evidential work at all. Finish by saying what the argument, once repaired, still teaches us about the difference between a frequency in a class and a credence in an event.
