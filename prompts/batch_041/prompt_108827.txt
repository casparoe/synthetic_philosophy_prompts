I am a novelist, and I am trying to write a dinner-party scene in which a mathematician corners a philosopher of mathematics with an argument I first met as an undergraduate and have never been able to shake. I want the philosopher's reply to be the real thing rather than a polite deflection, which means I need to know where the argument actually breaks; and I would rather not be fobbed off with the news that this is only Skolem's paradox, and that Skolem himself was already a relativist about it. Here is the argument as my character gives it.

(1) The Löwenheim–Skolem theorem tells us that any consistent first-order theory in a countable language with an infinite model has a model whose objects can be enumerated by the natural numbers. (2) Set theory, as it is ordinarily formalized, is a first-order theory in a countable language; so if it is consistent it has a countable model, call it M. (3) Cantor's theorem is a theorem of set theory, so M must contain a set that M regards as uncountable, which is to say that M says there is no bijection in M between that set and the natural numbers of M. (4) But M's objects are enumerable, so there is, from where I stand, a bijection between that set and the natural numbers, and the set is therefore really countable. (5) So what M means by 'uncountable' cannot be what I mean by it. (6) Nothing in any of this turned on a peculiarity of set theory; the same reasoning will run for any first-order theory one cares to name. (7) So no first-order theory fixes the meaning of its own predicates, there is no fact of the matter about whether a given collection is uncountable, or countable, or even finite, and set theory — mathematics generally — is a game with marks on paper rather than a description of anything.

My character delivers (7) as a discovery about the world, and the philosopher is meant to answer that the argument has proved too much. I can smell something, but I cannot put my finger on it. Step (5) looks like the place where the meaning of a word is quietly transferred from the model to the person speaking, and I do not know whether that transfer is legitimate or a slide; step (6) looks as though it might be smuggling in a claim about all predicates on the strength of an argument about one. And (7) has a consequence I cannot shake, because it seems to devour the argument that produced it: if there is no fact of the matter about what can be enumerated, then there is no fact of the matter about which collections can be enumerated, and that includes the collection of sentences of the theory invoked in (1) and the collection of objects in M — so the theorem doing the work in step (1) loses its content, and the whole thing eats itself. I would like to keep the shock of (6) and (7) if I can, since the shock is what the scene is for, but I would rather have an argument that survives than one that merely dazzles.

So: tell me which step to press on, why it looked innocent until it was pressed, and what I must give up to repair the argument so that it proves only what it should. If the honest answer is that the argument is valid as it stands and I have to choose which premise to surrender, say that plainly and put a price on each of the choices. Write it back as continuous prose — no headings, no bullet points, no bold, no tables; a letter, not a handout. You may refer to my steps by their numbers inside your sentences, but do not break the reply into a list.
