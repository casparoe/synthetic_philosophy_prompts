My position is this: the separateness of persons is not merely a vivid intuition pump but a genuine objection — any morality that aggregates welfare across lives treats the boundaries between persons as morally optional in a way that no defensible morality can. I want a map of its exposure. What would count against it? In particular: does it survive its own reach, given that the same reasoning seems to condemn the ordinary trade-offs we accept within a single life, where present pain is imposed for future benefit and the time-slices are "separate" in an equally thin sense? Would reductionism about personal identity, which makes separateness a matter of degree rather than an all-or-nothing fact, deflate the objection or only soften it? And what becomes of the objection once I grant that persons can voluntarily pool their fates — as in a case where two people contract to share every fortune, so that a benefit to one is a benefit to the other, and aggregating their welfare looks not merely permitted but required? I also want to know whether the objection's real content is a person-affecting restriction, and if so whether it inherits that view's own liabilities. Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, distinguish those a committed defender would concede from those they would dismiss, note which have already been absorbed by weakening the claim, and say honestly whether the position as usually stated risks anything at all or has been insulated into unfalsifiability. Please answer in American English.
