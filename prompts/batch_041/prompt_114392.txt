I am leading a ninety-minute session for a group of narrative designers and interactive-fiction writers — people who think hard about how stories move audiences but who have no philosophy training and are mildly allergic to it. The topic is the paradox of fiction: the apparent tension among the claims that we are genuinely moved by fictional characters, that we believe those characters do not exist, and that being moved by something requires believing that it exists. I do not want the explanation itself; I want the plan for delivering it.

Give me the order in which the components must be introduced so that the tension registers as a genuine problem rather than a verbal trick — which claim the room must first be brought to accept, which claim is the pivot, and why the sequence cannot be run in any other order without collapsing the puzzle into a truism. Then give me the single carrying example to build the whole session around, and justify it against the obvious alternatives: state what features it must have and must lack for the puzzle to bite, and explain why the natural candidates — a jump-scare in a horror film, a viewer weeping at a documentary about real people — are the wrong choice for the center of the session.

Next, give me the misconceptions this particular audience will bring or form, in the order they will surface, and the exact point in the sequence where each should be headed off. I expect to meet at least three: that this is all explained by "suspension of disbelief"; that the emotions in question are not really real; and that the whole thing is a semantic squabble about how we use the word believe. Say what each misconception gets right, so that heading it off does not mean simply denying it.

Late in the session I want to spring one hard case on them that I suspect resists the standard moves: an interactive-fiction writer who feels genuine grief while scripting the death of a character she invented and controls, reading that character's lines directly out of her own code. Tell me where that case belongs in the sequence, what it is meant to expose, and whether it threatens the solution the session has been building or merely stress-tests it. At that point I also want the room to see the fork between the two live ways out — one that denies the emotions are the real thing and treats them as a species of make-believe, one that denies the premise that being moved requires believing the object exists — and to understand which horn the hard case pushes them toward.

End with the single question I can put to the room that would reveal whether they have grasped the problem rather than merely learned its name, and tell me what I should explicitly decline to resolve at this level, with a reason why leaving it open serves them better than closing it.
