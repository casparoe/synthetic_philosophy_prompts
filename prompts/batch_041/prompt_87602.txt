Argue for the following position: the slogan that competence can exist without comprehension is systematically ambiguous between two different inversions of explanatory order, and the ambiguity is not innocent. To make the argument concrete, adopt the following stipulated distinction. A productive inversion occurs when a mechanism that does not represent a norm nevertheless reliably generates outcomes that satisfy the norm, so that the mechanism explains the competence. A descriptive inversion occurs when a system is explained by treating it as implementing a competence that it does not itself represent, so that the competence is attributed from an explanatory stance rather than produced by the mechanism. Argue that Darwin's inversion is productive and Turing's is descriptive, and that the computational theory of mind illicitly borrows the explanatory prestige of the former when it is entitled only to the latter. In developing the argument, address at least the following: (i) what comprehension must mean if the contrast is to be more than a verbal one; (ii) whether the distinction survives a deflationary account of representation, on which representation is exhausted by causal or functional covariation; (iii) whether the notion of a program or algorithm in cognitive science already presupposes the descriptive inversion, and if so, whether that makes computational explanations interpretive rather than causal; and (iv) the strongest objection to your thesis, namely that natural selection is itself descriptive because biological function is observer-relative, and why you think that objection fails or succeeds. Do not rely on citations or historical exegesis; reason from the stipulated distinction and from ordinary examples. Your conclusion should state clearly what turns on the distinction and what does not.
