My cousin finally got her citizenship last month — fourteen years here, two kids born here, runs a bakery — and she's asked me to give the short welcome-to-the-franchise bit at the naturalization ceremony in a few weeks. I'm a poli sci PhD student, I said yes immediately, and I'm now realising it's a minefield.

The room: the new citizens, obviously. Their families, and a big chunk of those family members are not citizens and aren't going to be — her husband's on a work visa, her mum's on a family visa, one of her older kids aged out of the pathway she thought they were on. Then there's the local officials and two people from the electoral commission who will be sitting in the front row and will not enjoy being told the whole thing is a stitch-up.

Here's the problem. I believe two things that are really hard to say in the same room.

First, I don't think citizenship is the natural home of the vote. The principled ways of drawing the line are either everyone affected by a decision or everyone who's subject to it, and on the first one half of my cousin's family should be voting and on the second one so should every person here who has to obey the law — which is also them. Plus the old problem that you can't use a vote to decide who gets to vote, because the vote already assumes you know who the demos is. So I can't get up there and act like the boundary is anything other than arbitrary, and I can't act like it's a scandal either, because I'm the one handing out the certificates.

Second, I think voting is a duty and not just a right — I'd make it compulsory, with a fine, like Australia does — and I think the age should come down to sixteen. But a lot of this room came from places where voting was compulsory and meant nothing, where the turnout number was the whole point and your ballot was a formality, and some of them know that better than I ever will. I can't stand there and say vote, it's the law, without sounding exactly like the thing they got out of. And the best objection I know to compulsion is that not voting is itself a way of saying something, so forcing a ballot is compelled speech — I'd like the speech to deal with that rather than skip it.

So write me the speech. Six minutes, give or take. It has to welcome people in without pretending the line they just crossed is principled; it has to make a case for voting as a duty that a person who fled a sham election could genuninely accept; it should say something real to the family members sitting there who can't vote and know they can't, without turning them into props; and it has to land for the electoral commission people too, who need to leave satisfied that I didn't tell a room full of brand new citizens that the franchise is a trick. Don't try to solve the boundary problem — I don't think it's solvable — but don't paper over it either. I'd rather the speech be honest and a bit uncomfortable than smooth.
