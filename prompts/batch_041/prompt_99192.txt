I chair the bylaws and elections committee of a scholarly association with roughly four thousand dues-paying members. We elect a single president each spring. The bylaws currently say only that the candidate with the most votes wins, with a runoff if no one clears forty percent; write-in votes are permitted, and for the past three cycles the ballot has also carried a "none of the above" line. Turnout is low, three-way races have become the norm, and two of the last four presidents took office with well under half the votes cast. The board has asked this committee to bring a recommendation to the spring business meeting on whether to replace plurality with something else, and I have to circulate our position before we meet.

Four proposals are live. One member, who teaches social choice, wants approval voting: let members mark every candidate they find acceptable and elect the most-approved. A second wants a Condorcet-consistent method with a plurality fallback in case the pairwise results cycle. A third wants to keep plurality but add a safety valve letting the board set aside a result it judges to have produced a president without a genuine mandate. A fourth has floated replacing the election outright with a lottery between the top two finishers, on the grounds that if every method can be gamed, we might as well be honest that the choice is partly arbitrary. The first and fourth members both keep invoking the Gibbard–Satterthwaite theorem, and they draw opposite conclusions from it.

Draft the written opinion I will circulate. It should recommend one concrete course of action; specify the ballot format, the tie-breaking rule, and how the write-in and "none of the above" lines are to be handled; distinguish what we can do by committee policy from what would require a bylaws amendment; and give a timeline that gets a change onto this spring's ballot. Give the strongest case for each losing proposal rather than a strawman, and be explicit about the strongest single objection to whatever you recommend.

The part I most need help with is the impossibility result. Tell me what it actually rules out and what it leaves open. In particular: what changes if we restrict the domain of admissible preferences — this association really does have a left–right axis, and members' preferences over candidates may plausibly be single-peaked along it; what randomization buys and what it costs, and whether the lottery proposal is as arbitrary as its sponsor suggests; whether "our members vote sincerely anyway, so manipulability is irrelevant" is a serious answer or a dodge; and whether the theorem should push us toward one of these rules, away from all of them, or merely toward admitting that the choice is not neutral. If the honest conclusion is that it does not settle the question, say so plainly.

About nine hundred words, addressed to the committee. I don't need citations or a reading list; I need reasoning I can defend in front of colleagues who will push back.
