I chair the ethics advisory panel attached to our defence ministry's targeting review. The review meets a week from Thursday to settle a single clause in the standing directive: the one that keeps an adversary's cities at risk as a declared retaliatory option. The clause has been in every version since the early 1980s, and as far as the panel can establish it has never been the basis of an operational plan; the retired planner among us calls it alliance theatre. One member has tabled a motion to strike it, resting the case on the argument Elizabeth Anscombe made against Truman — that choosing to kill the innocent as a means to one's ends is murder no matter how much good it buys — and then extending that argument from the deed to the threat itself.

Two objections will be pressed hard, and I need both handled at their strongest. The first says a threat is not a killing, and that an intention conditional on an event we are committed to preventing — a first strike on us — is not an intention in the morally loaded sense; the whole point of the option is that it is never used. The second is consequentialist and probably has the votes: the option has kept the peace for forty years, and a declaratory policy that narrows it invites the war it is meant to avert. What I most want the memo to face squarely is whether the directive's existing clause — the one borrowed from the principle of double effect, which separates intended from merely foreseen harm — is doing real work in this case or is a fig leaf, since the civilians' deaths are not a side effect of the threat; they are the threat. If that clause is being asked to license the very thing it exists to forbid, the panel should know it before it votes.

Please draft the short written opinion I will circulate before the meeting. It should come down on one of three courses — keep the clause, strike it, or keep it only after a specific rewrite — and state the recommendation as an instruction the review board can act on, with reasons the dissenting members can actually respond to rather than a restatement of doctrine. Do not tell me what Anscombe or anyone else wrote, or where to find it; the panel wants the argument, not the citations, and nothing in the memo should depend on our knowing what a future adversary would have done otherwise. Write it as continuous prose, with no headings, no numbered points, no bold, and no tables — the way one writes a letter to colleagues who will read it on a train.
