I write a science-and-ideas column, and I'm closing a long piece on the unity of the proposition with a poem rather than a summary. The subject is Bradley's regress, and I want the verse to perform the argument instead of paraphrasing it. The argument, as I'll lay it out for readers: take the proposition that a stands to b in relation R. R cannot by itself explain why a, R, and b hang together, because R must in turn be tied to a and to b, and each of those ties is itself a further relation that must be tied to its terms, and so on. Any ingredient offered as the glue starts the same slide. What I want the poem to test are the two exits my interviewees keep handing me. The first says unity was never an extra ingredient at all: a concept is unsaturated and simply requires completion, so there is nothing left over that still needs tying down. The second says unity is contributed from outside the proposition altogether — by the single act of judging, which takes up the items at once, so that nothing inside the sentence has to do the binding. The poem should make the difference between those two felt, and should be honest about the suspicion that the first only restates the problem while the second makes the unity of what we think depend on our thinking it.

Form: exactly five numbered stanzas, each handing its last line to the next stanza as its first line, so that no stanza stands on its own and each one needs the next to close. The fifth must end either by returning to the poem's opening line or by breaking the chain outright, and I should be able to tell from the ending which of the two exits it enacts. Keep the diction plain enough for a general reader; no title, epigraph, or closing note, and no names of philosophers unless the verse earns them.
