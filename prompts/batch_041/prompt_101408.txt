I am an undergraduate taking a philosophy of mind course this term while also enrolled in a machine learning sequence, and I have been trying to get the two courses to talk to each other. In the philosophy course we have been working through the higher-order family of theories of consciousness: the view that a mental state is conscious just in case it is the target of a suitable higher-order representation of it. The variants differ over whether that higher-order representation is thought-like (a higher-order thought) or perception-like (an inner sense), and over whether it represents the first-order state from the outside or is somehow folded into the state it represents (the self-representational version). I can state the general shape of these views, but I want to see them in the vocabulary I actually use when I build models: layered networks, learned representations, latent codes, a module that takes another module's representation as input and classifies or predicts it, self-supervision, prediction error, and so on.

Please restate the higher-order family as faithfully as you can in that vocabulary. Treat the first-order state as a representation computed by one component of a system and the higher-order representation as the output of a second component that takes the first component's representation as its input. Make explicit what plays the role of the higher-order representation's content, what makes a state the target of such a representation rather than merely correlated with it, and where the variants diverge once they are put in these terms.

Then give me the residue report. I want three things.

First, which claims in the higher-order literature translate cleanly and which translate only under distortion. In particular, follow two claims through the translation: the claim that a higher-order representation can misrepresent the first-order state it targets, so that the conscious character is fixed by the higher-order representation rather than by the first-order state; and the claim that the higher-order representation must be about the state in a way that is more than predictive or merely functional.

Second, what has no counterpart at all in the modeling vocabulary. I am thinking especially of the phenomenal or what-it-is-like aspect, and of any epistemic or normative dimension, for instance that the higher-order representation has to be a belief or a judgment with a certain standing rather than a mere estimate.

Third, whether the residue tells me that the modeling vocabulary is missing something real, or that some of the higher-order claims were less clear than they looked and seemed substantive only because of the mentalistic idiom they were stated in.

Keep the translation honest: do not quietly redescribe the views in terms that already assume the modeling framework is the right one, and do not treat a functional or predictive surrogate as though it were the original claim. Where a translation succeeds, say what it preserves; where it fails, say precisely what was lost.
