Two friends of mine, both lifelong readers of science fiction, spent most of a dinner party arguing about the dark forest hypothesis — the suggestion, popularized by a well-known Chinese science-fiction trilogy, that the reason we detect no other civilizations is that any civilization that makes itself visible is promptly destroyed, so the rational policy is to stay silent and, where possible, to strike first at anything that reveals itself. I have an undergraduate background in philosophy but never studied game theory, so I could follow the shape of the dispute without being able to referee it, and what I want is not a summary of the idea from the novels but an assessment of the argument as my friends actually made it.

Maya opened with the case for the hypothesis. You can never verify another civilization's intentions, she said; you can never be sure it will not undergo a rapid technological leap that leaves you permanently outmatched; and the universe's resources are finite. Under those conditions the only safe assumption is that anyone you meet is a potential existential threat, so concealment is rational and a preemptive strike is rational whenever it is likely to succeed. Ben objected that this is a security dilemma with a cosmological vocabulary. Every one of her premises, he said, is load-bearing: if a first strike is uncertain rather than decisive, if resources are abundant rather than scarce, or if communication is cheap enough for reputations and agreements to form, then cooperation becomes stable, as it sometimes has on Earth. Maya answered that interstellar distances break the analogy — a round-trip message can take centuries, so the institutions that stabilize cooperation cannot form, and in the interval needed to exchange two messages one side may have transformed itself. Ben replied that the argument was running two very different claims together: that you should hide, which is a claim about how a cautious agent ought to decide under uncertainty, and that aggression is what rational agents will in fact do to one another, which is a claim about an equilibrium — and uncertainty supports the first at most, and even then only if you are willing to decide by assuming the worst case, while the second requires a first-strike advantage and the absence of retaliation that Maya had not established. Maya then made a point I had not heard before: once a civilization has been leaking radio and heat for a century, concealment is a lost cause, so the rational strategy reverses — you should broadcast loudly and try to attract allies, because silence no longer buys you anything. Ben said this conceded his point, since it shows that the hide-or-die conclusion rests on an empirical assumption about detectability that the original argument never justifies.

I would like a point-by-point adjudication rather than a verdict on the whole thing. For each move — Maya's opening premises, Ben's security-dilemma rebuttal, Maya's time-lag reply, Ben's distinction between hiding and aggression, Maya's broadcast reversal, and Ben's claim that this concedes his case — tell me who has the better of it and why. Please also flag any premise that is doing more work than either of them admits, say whether the two of them are really disagreeing about the same question or talking past each other, and finish by saying which of them is closer to right overall and on which specific points.
