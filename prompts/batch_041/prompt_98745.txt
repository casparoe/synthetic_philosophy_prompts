I'm a novelist, about halfway through a book where the main character dies in chapter 3 and the rest of the novel follows the people he left behind, plus a few chapters from his point of view in some kind of limbo that may just be the narrator's invention. Anyway, I've been reading a lot of philosophy of death for it, and I've ended up with a set of views I'd like you to stress-test. I think death is bad for the person who dies mainly when it deprives them of the good things they would have had if they'd lived longer. I also think that nothing can be bad for you after you're dead, because there's no you there to be on the receiving end. I think the old Epicurean line—that death is nothing to us because where death is, we aren't—gets this wrong, because harm doesn't have to be felt to count. And I think the symmetry argument, the one that says we don't mourn the time before we were born so we shouldn't fear the time after we die, also gets it wrong, because the goods before birth weren't ours to lose in the same way. But I also think the badness of death isn't located at any particular time; it's not that you're worse off at some moment, it's that your life as a whole is worse. I think the badness of death is more like a broken promise than a toothache: it's not a state you're in, it's a relation between you and a life you didn't get. And I think that if someone's future would have been nothing but unbearable suffering, death can actually be a benefit to them. Finally, I think grief for the dead is rational and is really grief for them, not just for the survivors, and I think the dead can be wronged by what happens after they die—like if we betray their wishes or trash their reputation.

What I want to know is whether this package hangs together. I have a nagging feeling that some of these are pulling against each other, but I can't tell which. For each pair that's in tension, tell me how bad it is and what would have to give; and if some of them only look like they conflict but actually don't, say why. I'd rather have a ranked list of the real problems than a general lecture, and please don't tell me to go read someone—I'm just trying to figure out if my own views are coherent enough to put in the novel's acknowledgments without embarrassing myself.
