I've spent the better part of a leave reading my way into the old quarrel over the Trojan War, King Arthur, and the Exodus, and I've ended up defending a position I did not expect to hold. I now think the line between legend and history is not about whether the events occurred at all, but about the stance a transmitting community takes toward its own narrative. Call a narrative history, on my view, when its keepers treat it as answerable to evidence — when they date it, cross-check it against other records, and count a discovered discrepancy as a reason to revise — and call it legend when it is carried instead for the sake of identity, instruction, or edification and is insulated from that kind of correction. On this criterion most of the Trojan War cycle counts as legend even if a war at the site usually identified with Troy really happened, and the Exodus counts as legend too, while a dry chronicle or a boastful inscription counts as history even when it is full of flattery and error. The upshot I find hardest to swallow, and the reason I want your help, is that on this view a narrative can be entirely true and still be legend, and entirely false and still be history.

What I want from you is a map of what would count against the position. Not the cheap objection that it lets legend off the hook for being false — I know it does, and I take that to be a feature — but the real exposure. Which cases would the criterion classify in a way I cannot accept: is there a narrative that a community plainly treats as answerable to evidence and that we would still insist on calling legend, or one transmitted purely to constitute an identity and that is nonetheless plainly history? Does the criterion secretly presuppose what it is meant to explain — can we even identify a community's "stance" without already knowing which genre the narrative belongs to, and whose stance counts when a story is carried across a thousand years and many communities, each with different habits of correction? Does the view quietly collapse into something more familiar and less interesting, such as the claim that history and legend are just two literary registers, or that legend is history with the truth-claims left unasserted? And if the position is worth holding at all, what does it predict about a given narrative that its obvious rivals — the plain "did the events happen" view, or the pure genre view — do not?

Tell me honestly whether the position is falsifiable, or whether I have insulated it by defining my terms so that no possible find could dislodge it; and if it is insulated, whether that is a defect in this kind of conceptual proposal or just what such a proposal looks like. Rank what you give me: which of these would be decisive against the view, which would be awkward but survivable, and which a defender like me could simply concede and move past. I'd find it most persuasive if you could put your finger on a single clear case where the criterion misfires rather than a general worry about fuzzy boundaries, since I already grant that the boundary is fuzzy.
