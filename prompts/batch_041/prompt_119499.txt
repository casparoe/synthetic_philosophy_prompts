I've more or less talked myself into Sartre's radical freedom, the real version rather than the strawman. Not that circumstances don't shape what you do, but that there's no fixed self sitting behind the choosing — consciousness is just the for-itself projecting into its possibilities, so saying I'm just the kind of person who does X is always already bad faith, and the waiter playing at being a waiter is hiding from his own freedom. I'm the sort of person who's read too much existentialism and too much LessWrong, so make of that what you will.

What I want is an honest map of what would count against it, and I want the in-the-weeds stuff, not determinism versus free will 101. Specifically: (1) the self-deception problem — bad faith is supposed to be a lie I tell myself, which seems to need a liar, a lie, and someone who half knows the truth, but the theory says there's no self behind the act doing the lying. Does Sartre's fix, where bad faith is itself a free project and sincerity is just another way of fleeing, actually close that gap or just move it back a step? (2) If bad faith is the default and sincerity is also bad faith, is there any state left that isn't, i.e. does the account make authenticity impossible, or trivially automatic? (3) His own biographies of Baudelaire and Genet read like they explain the man from a childhood original choice; if that choice is fixed and everything follows from it, hasn't existential psychoanalysis smuggled back exactly the character the ontology denies? (4) Later Sartre piles on the practico-inert, seriality, scarcity, the group — is that a real defeat or just the same guy changing his mind?

Sort these by how much they'd actually hurt, say which ones a committed Sartrean would just wave off as not getting it, and tell me straight whether this is a view that can be wrong about anything, or whether every counterexample gets re-described as bad faith. If it's insulated, say so, and say whether thats fatal or just the cost of doing business.
