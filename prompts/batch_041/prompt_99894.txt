Consider the following examination question and a student's answer to it. I am the instructor of record and must return a grade with written comments. Please assign a grade out of 10 and, more importantly, supply the specific comments that justify it.

The question (10 points total):

In this course we used two readings of a sentence. On the content reading we assess the consistency of the proposition the sentence expresses; on the act reading we assess the coherence of the speech act the speaker performs in uttering it. A sentence is Moore-paradoxical when it is consistent on the content reading but defective on the act reading. We also defined a blindspot as a proposition that is consistent but cannot be truly believed, or known, by the very subject who entertains it.

(a) (3 points) Take the two forms

(i) p, but I do not believe that p
(ii) p, but I believe that not-p

Say what is supposed to be absurd about utterances of each. Is the absurdity of the same kind in the two cases? Justify your answer.

(b) (3 points) Classify (i) and (ii) under the content/act distinction, and describe the defect that each exhibits on the act reading.

(c) (2 points) Do the following remain Moore-paradoxical?

(iii) p, but she does not believe that p
(iv) p, but I did not believe that p
(v) p, but I will not believe that p

What does your answer show about the source of the absurdity?

(d) (2 points) Explain the relation between a Moore-paradoxical sentence and a blindspot.

The student's answer:

The absurdity in (i) is easy to state and easy to misstate. The speaker asserts that p and then, in the same breath, reports that she does not believe that p. If she does believe what she asserts, the second clause is false; if the second clause is true, she is in no position to assert the first. Yet the sentence is not, strictly, a contradiction: it is perfectly possible that p be true and that I fail to believe it, since I may simply be ignorant of the fact. Both clauses can hold together. The air of contradiction therefore comes not from the content but from the act: in asserting that p a speaker represents herself as believing that p, and the second clause cancels that representation. So (i) is Moore-paradoxical exactly as defined: consistent on the content reading, defective on the act reading.

(ii) is the mirror image and is absurd for the same reason. Here too the speaker asserts p and then reports a doxastic state that clashes with the assertion, and here too both clauses can be true at once, since one may believe a proposition that is in fact false. So (ii) is content-consistent as well. In fact (i) and (ii) are instances of a single schema — p together with the speaker's attitude toward the negation of p — and both are absurd because asserting p commits the speaker to a doxastic stance that the second clause disowns.

The upshot is that the absurdity belongs to the pragmatics of assertion rather than to logic. The sentence is not self-contradictory; it only sounds so because assertion carries an implication of belief, and the second clause cancels that implication. It follows that no epistemic norm governs belief or assertion here: the oddity is a by-product of the way we use assertions to convey what we think.

The variants in (c) bear this out. (iii) remains just as odd as (i): it is strange to be told that p and that she does not believe it, since if p is being asserted we assume a situation in which the belief is at least available. (iv) is likewise paradoxical, because it still sets the truth of p against the speaker's own doxastic history; (v) behaves in the same way. The absurdity thus survives changes of person and tense, which shows that it is not a peculiarity of the first-person present tense but attaches to any assertion accompanied by a denial of belief in the same content.

As for (d), the blindspot and the Moore-paradoxical sentence are two names for one phenomenon: in each case we have a consistent proposition that the speaker cannot coherently avow. This is why the variants in (c) inherit the unavowability, and it confirms that the source of the paradox is the impossibility of believing what one is asserting.
