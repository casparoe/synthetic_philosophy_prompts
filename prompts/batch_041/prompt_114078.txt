I'm a senior and the student representative on my high school's discipline appeals panel, which meets Thursday to review a three-day suspension. A teacher reported that a student shoved her during a crowded hallway changeover; the student says he lost his footing when someone bumped him and never made contact; the hallway camera was down for maintenance, and the only other student nearby says he was looking the other way. The complication is that the same teacher had written this student up two weeks earlier for looming over her and had told a colleague she was keeping an eye on him. Because I was the one who raised the question of how much weight her account should carry, I've been asked to draft the panel's written decision.

Please draft it. It should recommend a concrete disposition — uphold the suspension, reduce it to a lesser sanction, or dismiss — and state what would have to be true for the panel to reach a different result. It should also work through the ways a witness's prior expectations can shape her report without making her dishonest or her testimony worthless, keeping at least these possibilities apart: what she attended to, what she literally perceived, how she resolved something ambiguous, what she now remembers, and how she chose to describe it. Some writers call this the question of whether cognition penetrates perception, and I have read skeptics who argue that in careful experiments the effect usually turns out to be a change in judgment rather than in seeing. The memo should then give the strongest case against my position its due: if the panel begins discounting eyewitness reports because of what the witness expected, it may reach a point where no testimony can support a finding, and the panel has to decide by Friday.

One practical point I want addressed directly: our usual remedy is to ask more careful questions. I want the memo to say when further questioning can correct a distorted report and when it cannot.
