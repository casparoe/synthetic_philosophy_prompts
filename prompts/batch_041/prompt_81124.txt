Question. In this unit we treated Keynes's probability as a logical relation between a hypothesis and a body of evidence, rather than as a subjective degree of belief, and we noted his view that probabilities need not be numerically measurable or even mutually comparable. We also distinguished probability from the weight of the argument, and observed that additional relevant evidence can increase the weight of an argument while leaving its probability unchanged or even lowering it. Two claims are often made about the relation between Keynes's early theory of probability and his later stress on uncertainty: (i) that the later stress is simply the early theory carried over to economic decision-making; and (ii) that the later stress represents a retreat from the logical theory towards a subjective account of probability. Explain the distinction between probability and weight, and use it to assess claims (i) and (ii).

Response. Keynes's central claim in the Treatise on Probability is that probability is not a matter of how strongly anyone happens to believe a proposition. It is a logical relation between the hypothesis and the evidence available for it: given that evidence, the hypothesis stands in a determinate relation of probability to it, and that relation is the same for anyone who grasps the evidence. This is why his position is standardly called a logical theory, and it is why he can hold that some probabilities cannot be expressed as numbers and that some pairs of probabilities cannot even be ranked against one another.

The weight of the argument is a distinct dimension of evidential support. Where probability concerns how likely the hypothesis is on the evidence, weight concerns how much relevant evidence there is, that is, how complete our information is. The two can vary independently: as we saw, new relevant evidence can raise the weight of an argument without raising its probability, and a conclusion resting on a single reliable observation can have high probability but very little weight. The weight is best understood as a second-order probability, the probability that one's first-order probability is correct, and this is what makes it a genuinely separate dimension of support rather than another name for confidence.

Claim (i) is largely correct. The later emphasis on uncertainty is continuous with the early theory. In the General Theory and in his subsequent reflections, Keynes insists that in many economic settings we cannot assign probabilities at all: the future rate of interest, the outbreak of war, the course of a technology decades hence. These are the limiting cases of the early framework, in which the weight of the argument is so low that the probability, though in principle determinate, is too slight to guide action. The later doctrine is therefore a natural extension of the early one rather than a new departure.

Claim (ii), by contrast, is mistaken. The later work is not a retreat to subjectivism. Keynes introduced the now-standard distinction between measurable risk and unmeasurable uncertainty, and it is the second of these that he is invoking; he never concedes that probability is merely a degree of belief, and his practical advice, namely reliance on convention, on the confidence of entrepreneurs, and on animal spirits, presupposes that in these cases there is no determinate probability to be had, so that action must be taken on other grounds.

In short, claim (i) is largely right and claim (ii) is wrong.
