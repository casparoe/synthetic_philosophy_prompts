For years I have watched the doing/allowing distinction used as a load-bearing beam in two quite different conversations — effective-altruist arguments about omissive harm, where failing to give is said to be letting people die and therefore not much better than killing them, and bioethical arguments about how physicians may end lives — and I have never seen anyone check whether it is the same beam in both. I want the second case examined properly: not alongside the theory, but against it.

The episode is the American law of death and dying at the end of the twentieth century. In 1990, in Cruzan, the Supreme Court held that a competent patient has a liberty interest in refusing unwanted medical treatment. On a single day in June 1997 it handed down two unanimous decisions, Washington v. Glucksberg and Vacco v. Quill, upholding state bans on physician-assisted suicide. The resulting asymmetry is what I want explained. A physician may disconnect a ventilator from a patient who cannot breathe without it, knowing the patient will die once it is removed, and may not prescribe a lethal dose to that same patient at that patient's equally settled and repeated request. I am not asking whether the asymmetry is defensible. I am asking what draws the line, and whether the doing/allowing distinction is what draws it.

Structure the answer as a mapping rather than a survey. First, enumerate the distinct principles that could be doing the work here: a causal principle, on which doing means initiating a causal chain that terminates in death and allowing means declining to interrupt one; an intentional principle, on which the two are separated by what the agent aims at rather than by what he causes; a deontic principle, on which doing means violating a duty not to harm and allowing means failing a duty to aid; and a baseline principle, on which allowing simply means that the pre-existing course of events continues unaltered. State what each principle predicts about the ventilator case before you evaluate it.

Then say where the case breaks each principle. The claim I want tested rather than confirmed is that the baseline principle is the one the law is groping toward, and that the hospital destroys it, because inside an intensive care unit there is no default course of events to leave alone — the patient is alive only because a great many interventions are continuously underway, so that letting nature take its course is itself an intervention that someone must decide upon and someone must perform. If that is right, the distinction is not functioning as a constraint on causing harm at all; it is a device for assigning authorship of a death, and the real puzzle becomes why the law lets the patient's autonomous refusal transfer authorship to the physician in the withdrawal case but not in the prescription case. Note in this connection that the majority opinion in Quill reaches for intent rather than causation, and say what that concession costs the causal reading.

Finally, run the inference the other way. What does this episode establish about the doing/allowing distinction — that it is untenable, that it is sound but tracking something other than causation, such as agency or professional role or the social meaning of the act, or that it was never meant to carry this weight and the law is doing something else under the same name? Where the distinction is silent, say so, rather than making it speak.
