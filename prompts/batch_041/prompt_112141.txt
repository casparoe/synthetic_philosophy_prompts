I am a doctoral candidate in legal and political philosophy, and I am preparing an application for a one-year research fellowship that requires a short proposal of roughly two pages. Please take the role of an experienced supervisor in international criminal law and legal theory — someone fluent in both the jurisprudence and the philosophy, and who will insist that I state a question precisely rather than gesture at one. Before you draft anything, think the problem through carefully on your own: brainstorm several ways the idea below could be framed, decide which framing yields a question that is genuinely open yet answerable within a year, and then build the proposal around that framing rather than the most familiar one.

My half-formed idea concerns the charge sheets at Nuremberg and Tokyo and the old "victors' justice" objection. I have come to suspect that most of the debate runs together three complaints that are usually treated as a single one: first, that the conduct charged was not prohibited by any rule in force when it was done; second, that even where some rule arguably applied, the defendants had no fair warning that it would be enforced against them personally; and third, that the tribunals applied a standard of culpability — whether for waging aggressive war, for membership in a criminal organization, or for obedience to superior orders — that no legal system would have recognized in advance. My tentative hypothesis is that the standard defenses of the tribunals, whatever their form, answer the first complaint tolerably well and the second and third much less well, and that the Tokyo proceedings make this gap more visible than Nuremberg does, because the crime against peace carried the weight of the prosecution there and because the legitimacy of the bench was contested in a different way. I would like the proposal to turn this into one sharp question — something like whether the victors'-justice objection, properly analyzed, is an objection to the legality of the norms or to the fairness of their application, and whether the tribunals could have satisfied the second and third complaints without abandoning the first.

Please write the proposal so that it contains: a precise statement of the question such that it has a determinate answer; an account of why the existing positions leave it open rather than settling it; a description of the method — what I would actually do month by month, and why a conceptual and comparative approach to the judgments and the underlying doctrines is the right one rather than a straightforward historical or doctrinal survey; an honest statement of what a successful result would look like and what would count as the project failing; and a realistic scope for the time available, including which parts I should be prepared to cut. Write it in a formal, polished register suitable for a fellowship committee. I will add the citations and bibliography myself, so please do not include references, reading lists, footnotes, or any specific citations — refer to positions and arguments by description, and do not attribute any view to a named author or work.
