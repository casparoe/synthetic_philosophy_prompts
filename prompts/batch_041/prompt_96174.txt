I'm a novelist about two-thirds through a book, and one of my main characters is a mid-level State Department person who was in the room for the Libya decisions in 2011 — not the principals, the person who drafts the options memos and has to sit with the consequences. I need to get her head right, so I want to think through the 2011 Libya intervention using the R2P checklist, and I want the analysis to actually bite instead of turning into a morality play.

Here's the episode as I understand it, and I'm happy to be told where I've got it wrong. Qaddafi's forces were closing on Benghazi in March 2011, he was making threats about what would happen to the people there, and the Security Council passed Resolution 1973 — ten votes in favor, none against, five abstentions (Brazil, China, Germany, India, Russia) — authorizing member states to take all necessary measures to protect civilians, excluding a foreign occupation force. NATO ran the air campaign, and within months the mandate was being read as license to remove Qaddafi, and the aftermath was what it was. Kosovo 1999 is the other case my character keeps flashing back to, since that's the one where the intervention happened without a Security Council mandate.

What I want from you: take the precautionary criteria that got attached to the responsibility to protect — just cause, right intention, last resort, proportional means, reasonable prospects — and run Libya through them one at a time, with the understanding that these came out of a 2001 commission report and then got narrowed and partly displaced by the 2005 World Summit language that limits the whole thing to four mass atrocity crimes (genocide, war crimes, ethnic cleansing, crimes against humanity). I want to know what each criterion would have to mean to give a determinate answer in March 2011, and where it just doesn't. Last resort against whom, and by when? Proportional means measured against what baseline — deaths prevented, or the counterfactual political order? Right intention whose intention, when the coalition had several and they weren't identical? And reasonable prospects judged ex ante from what was actually knowable, not ex post from how it turned out.

Then flip it: what does Libya do to the criteria? Did the case expose a flaw in the checklist, or did it expose that the checklist was never meant to be a decision procedure and everyone misused it? The Brazilian proposal from late 2011 — responsibility while protecting — is interesting to me because it tried to add sequencing and accountability rather than new criteria, and I want to know whether that actually fixes anything or just relocates the discretion to a different set of people.

Finally, be honest about the limits. What can a theory built for one kind of case (Kosovo, Rwanda, Srebrenica) not tell us about a case where the intervention succeeds militarily and then the state comes apart anyway? And where does the theory have nothing to say that a novelist would want to know — the texture of the decision, the way a person in the room experiences the gap between the criteria on the page and the cable she actually has to write? I want alot of that gap.

No need for citations, I'll handle those myself. I mostly want the mapping to be disciplined and the places where it breaks to be named out loud.
