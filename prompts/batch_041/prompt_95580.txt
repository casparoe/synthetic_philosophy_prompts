I am a PhD student in philosophy, and I have spent the term trying to reconstruct Augustine's position on lying from a set of case verdicts that my seminar compiled. The cases and his verdicts, as best we can make out, are these: a man who denies Christ under threat of death, believing the denial false, lies, and lies gravely; a man who says a road is safe because he genuinely believes it is safe does not lie, even if the road is in fact dangerous; a man who says a road is dangerous because he wants to keep it for himself, believing it is safe, lies; a man who says a road is dangerous, believing it is dangerous, but as an obvious joke, does not lie; a man who says a road is safe, believing it is dangerous, in order to save a traveler from a murderer, lies, but this is the least grave kind of lie; a man who says a road is safe, believing it is dangerous, in order to lead a traveler into a trap, lies, and lies gravely; a man who says a doctrine is true, believing it false, in the course of religious teaching, lies, and this is the most grave kind of lie; and a man who says nothing when asked about the road, knowing it is dangerous, does not lie. There is also a pair of cases that seem to fix the criterion: a man who says a road is safe, believing it is dangerous, lies even if the road happens to be safe; and a man who says a road is dangerous, believing it is safe, does not lie even if the road happens to be dangerous. So the truth-value of the words seems irrelevant; what matters is whether the speaker's words match his belief.

Now here is a case that has divided our seminar. A man knows a road is dangerous. He also knows that he has a reputation as a compulsive liar, and that the traveler he is speaking to will disbelieve anything he says. He wants the traveler to believe the road is safe, so that the traveler will take it and fall into a trap. So he says that the road is dangerous. He says what he believes, and what is true, but he intends the traveler to conclude the opposite, and thereby to form a false belief. Would Augustine call this a lie? Is he running a rule on which a lie requires a mismatch between belief and utterance, so that this is a deception but not a lie? Or a rule on which any intent to deceive is sufficient, so that this is a lie? I am less interested in the verdict itself than in the rule he seems to be running, the other rules that fit his verdicts just as well, and which further case would best tell those rules apart.
