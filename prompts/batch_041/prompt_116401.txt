ok so I've been working through Leibniz's Theodicy (the old Open Court translation by Huggard, the one where he's mainly going after Bayle) and there's a stretch early in Part I that I keep bouncing off, and I want to make sure I'm not just reciting the Sunday-school version of it back to myself. I don't have the book with me so this is my own paraphrase, apologies if I've mangled it:

God has, Leibniz says, an antecedent will — a will that all men be saved, that no one suffer, that every possible good be realized. Considered on its own this will is real and sincere. But God also has a consequent will, which is the will he actually has once everything is taken into account, and that's the one that picks out the best of all possible worlds. The antecedent will doesn't always take effect, and this isn't a failure or a frustration, because the antecedent will was never a decree — it's a tendency, or what the Scholastics called a velleity (I'm fairly sure Leibniz uses that term, tell me if not). So God sincerely wills that all be saved, in the antecedent sense, and sincerely wills a world in which not all are saved, in the consequent sense, and there's supposed to be no contradiction because the two are wills of different kinds. Evil isn't willed for its own sake; it's permitted, and permitted only as a precondition of greater goods — I think he has a Latin phrase for this, condition of something or other — or as a consequence that comes along with the best order.

Here's where I get stuck. If the antecedent will is never defeated in the sense that matters — if it's not that God wanted X and couldn't get it, but rather that God never wanted X in the operative sense — then in what sense is it a will at all? It starts to look like a description of God's benevolence rather than an act of will, more like God approves of the good as such than God wants this to happen. And then the sentence does much less work than it sounds like it's doing. Alternatively, if the antecedent will is a real wanting, then it does get overridden, and now God has a want that never gets satisfied, which sounds like it costs him something, and Leibniz can't have God paying a price.

Can you walk me through this? Three things I'd like out of it:

1. What the antecedent/consequent distinction is actually doing in the argument, versus what it looks like it's doing on a first read. Is it doing anything besides letting him say God loves everyone and not everyone is saved in one breath without contradiction?

2. Whether that distinction is doing the same job as the permitted-evil move, or a different job. My hunch is they're two different patches over two different holes, and that the second is bearing more of the load, but I can't articulate why.

3. What happens to the whole picture if the ordering of possible worlds is only partial — some worlds better in some respects and worse in others, no fact about which is best overall. Leibniz seems to need a total order for the best to pick out exactly one world and for sufficient reason to force God's hand. If the order is partial, does he have a tie-break that isn't just God picked, which gives up the argument, or the best is whatever God picked, which is circular? I've been thinking about this because of how badly these orderings behave in population ethics, but maybe that's an anachronism and I should knock it off. Related: Leibniz wants the reason for God's choice to incline the will without necessitating it. If it inclines, God could've chosen a non-best world and then the best isn't doing the selecting; if it necessitates, freedom is gone. I suspect moral necessity is a technical term I'm reading as a euphemism.
