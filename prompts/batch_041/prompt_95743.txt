I need help with an argument my reading group constructed, because it now looks as though it refutes itself and I cannot tell whether that is a reductio of the argument or a reductio of us. The subject is the causal exclusion argument. The version we built runs as follows.

(1) If a property F is realized by a distinct physical property P, and P is by itself sufficient for an effect E, then F is not a cause of E.
(2) A mental property M is realized by, but not identical with, its physical realizer P.
(3) Hence M is not a cause of E.

That much has the familiar shape, and we would like to keep (1) through (3). What troubles us is that the schema in (1) never mentions the mental; it appears to apply to any realized property. So:

(4) P itself — say, this neuron's firing — is realized by a further physical property Q, and no description of P appears in the vocabulary of fundamental physics.
(5) By (1), P is not a cause of E unless P is identical with Q.
(6) P is not identical with Q — the same non-identity we granted in (2), now one level down.
(7) So P is not a cause of E either.
(8) But the conclusion the exclusion argument wants — that the physical realizer, not the mental state, is what causes the behavior — requires P to be a cause.
(9) Therefore the exclusion argument, if sound, is unsound.

Tell me which step to reject and what the rejection costs. I would rather not surrender (1), since it is the engine of the whole argument, but I also cannot see how to keep it without letting it eat its own tail. If the repair is to restrict (1) to the mental case, state the restriction precisely and say whether it can be given without circularity.
