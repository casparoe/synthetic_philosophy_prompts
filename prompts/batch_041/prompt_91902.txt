I teach a second-year interdisciplinary seminar on well-being and public policy, and we spend two weeks on national happiness rankings — specifically the annual World Happiness Report and the policy enthusiasm it has generated. I have one 90-minute session, about twenty-four students, movable chairs, a whiteboard, and no need for laptops. What I want out of that session is not a lecture on the index's methodology but an experience of three things in sequence: that a nation's happiness score is a constructed artifact rather than a reading off of some natural quantity; that the artifact behaves in ways that make the underlying idea of happiness slippery; and that once the artifact is published as a ranking, it acquires an incentive life of its own. I would like the activity to be arranged so the students arrive at the uncomfortable conclusion themselves — that a country could climb the table without anyone's life going any better — rather than hearing me assert it. Please do not name the relevant law of metric distortion, or the report's own technical vocabulary, until the debrief.

Concretely, I want the mechanics to embody a few features of the real instrument, and I would rather you build the game around these than around a generic points-and-rankings scheme. First, the measured quantity should be a self-report in which people place their own life on a scale whose endpoints are the best possible life and the worst possible life as they imagine them — so that the score depends on the comparison class a respondent is carrying around, and a uniform improvement across an entire population can leave the number flat. Second, the scoring should separate the thing being measured from the things used to explain it: in the real report the ranking rests on a single life-evaluation number, while income, social support, healthy life expectancy, freedom, generosity, and perceptions of corruption are used only to account for differences between countries against a deliberately miserable benchmark nation, with a large unexplained residual left over. Third, I want at least one available move that raises the reported number while leaving the simulated citizens' circumstances untouched, and I want that move to be tempting enough that some team takes it. Ideally two teams end up near the top by opposite routes — one through genuine improvement, one through managing appearances — and the room has to argue about whether the ranking can tell them apart.

Please give me a runnable design: the setup and materials, the roles or team structure, the budget and scoring rules in enough detail that I can run it from your description, a minute-by-minute timeline for the 90 minutes, and the debrief. The debrief should map specific moments in the game onto the corresponding pieces of the philosophical and methodological problem — the difference between evaluating a life and experiencing it, the anchoring problem in self-reports, the difference between a correlate and a cause, and the question of whether a state has reason to optimize a published index at all — and it should leave the two answers to that last question genuinely live rather than settling it. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this and how to handle it, and be honest with me about which feature of the real debate the game cannot reproduce and that I will therefore have to supply myself.
