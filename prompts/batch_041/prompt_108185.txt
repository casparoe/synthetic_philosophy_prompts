I am a novelist, and the book I am writing turns on a designer in 1930s Vienna who works at a museum of social and economic information, building a picture language: standardised figures, repeated rather than enlarged so that quantities can be counted instead of estimated, arranged on charts meant to be legible to people who share no common verbal language. Her mentor is Otto Neurath, by then a philosopher of science as much as a maker of exhibitions, and he maintains that the visual medium can be made relatively stable and autonomous with respect to interpretation and cultural reference — that a chart of this kind states facts about a society rather than merely decorating them. My character believes him, and I need to know precisely where the belief breaks.

So please explain the conceptual difficulty that underlies the whole enterprise, and explain it the way she would meet it at the drawing board rather than the way a seminar would. Take three things a language must be able to do and ask, for each, whether a purely pictorial notation can do it in its own right or can only borrow the capacity from words: first, to say that something is not the case — including the awkward case in which the quantity to be shown is a quantity of harm that was prevented, a count of events that never occurred; second, to say something general or comparative rather than particular, as when one distribution is declared more dispersed than another and the very notion of dispersion has already been settled somewhere off the page; third, to mark the difference between an assertion and a depiction, between a claim about these particular people and a claim about the type of person the pictogram stands for. Then tell me which of the three is a genuine limit of the medium and which is only a limit of a particular design, and what the difference between those two verdicts would mean for a writer who has never quite believed that showing and telling are two ways of doing the same thing.
