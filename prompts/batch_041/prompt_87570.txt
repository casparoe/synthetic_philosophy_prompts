I am a professor of philosophy, and for the past nine years I have taught in a college-in-prison program at a maximum-security state facility. This June I have been asked to deliver the commencement address at our graduation, and I would like you to write it for me.

The room will be mixed, and the mix is the difficulty. Some fifty graduates will be in caps and gowns, several of them men I have taught for four years; their mothers, partners, and children will be seated behind them. Along one wall will be the warden and a row of uniformed corrections officers — most of whom have been decent to me, and all of whom work inside an institution whose official purpose is not education. In the front row will be our donors, two administrators from the state department of corrections, and a state senator who has twice voted against funding prison education and who has argued on the floor that such programs are indulgences that people in prison have not earned.

Three weeks ago the administration also informed us that next year's funding depends on our reporting measurable reductions in recidivism, and the department's public materials now describe our program as a correctional intervention. Several graduates have told me, quietly, that this is the part they cannot stand — that a degree they worked for is being described as a treatment they received.

I want a speech that is honest about what is actually happening in that room and worth listening to for every part of it. That means it has to do real work with the distinction between punishment and rehabilitation rather than gesture at it. In particular:

It cannot say, however warmly, that these graduates were broken and are now repaired. It also cannot say that the degree compensates them for the years taken from them, or that the ceremony closes an account.

It cannot pretend this is a Norwegian prison. I have read enough about Halden to know what the alternative looks like — the thought that the loss of liberty is itself the punishment, and that everything else about prison life should be made as close to ordinary life as possible, so that the distance between inside and outside is as small as it can be. I do not want to borrow that language in order to flatter an institution that does not practice it.

It should say something true and useful about the morning after the ceremony, when they leave with a record, with restrictions on where they may live and work, and with fewer doors open than the diploma suggests.

One idea I would like you to build on, if you can make it fit: a colleague in criminology tells me that what best predicts whether someone stops offending is not a program's completion rate but a change in how the person narrates his own life — the point at which he begins to tell the story of himself as someone with a future rather than someone with a record. I find this both hopeful and uncomfortable, since it suggests that the useful thing we did may not be the thing we are able to measure.

Write the address for me to deliver. It should run about eight to ten minutes aloud, should speak to the graduates directly rather than about them, and should be something I could say in front of the senator without lying and in front of the graduates without condescending to them. No citations and no academic apparatus; this is spoken.
