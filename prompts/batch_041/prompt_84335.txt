I want you to act as a philosopher of engineering who works on AI safety — someone with real sympathy for the practice of building and evaluating systems, and equal impatience with doom rhetoric and with the assumption that every hard problem is just a software bug — and to help me stress-test a position I hold.

Call it the continuity thesis. In my formulation it has three parts. First, the alignment problems of systems we can actually build from current methods — large-scale pretraining, reinforcement learning from human or AI feedback, scaffolding and tool use, evaluation harnesses — are continuous with the ordinary problems of complex engineered systems: distribution shift, divergence between the proxy we optimize and the target we care about, measurement error in the training signal, and specification gaming. Second, these are all failure kinds we already have names for and track records of mitigating, so the correct response is better evaluation, better data, and better instrumentation rather than a new theory. Third, and this is the part I want tested hardest: define a failure mode as capability-coupled if its rate or severity grows with the system's capability, and capability-uncoupled if it stays roughly flat or shrinks as capability and tooling improve together. The continuity thesis predicts that the failures we actually observe are overwhelmingly capability-uncoupled, or at worst coupled in degree but not in kind.

What I want from you is a map of the thesis's exposure: evidence, arguments, formal results, or plausible developments that would count against it, with an honest ranking of how decisive each would be.

Specifically:

1. Which observations we already have bear on the thesis, and which way? Be concrete about what the evidence would look like if the thesis were false, and whether anything like that has already been seen.
2. Which candidate defeaters are decisive versus merely awkward? Distinguish "the thesis is false" from "the thesis needs qualification," and say which qualifications are cheap and which would hollow it out.
3. What would a defender of the continuity thesis concede as counting against them? Not only what critics say — I want the honest concession list.
4. Does the thesis predict anything its rival does not — the rival being the view that alignment is a qualitatively new problem rather than a harder version of an old one? If not, say so plainly, and tell me what the tightest falsifiable version of my thesis would be.
5. I would personally be most moved by a demonstration that some failure mode is capability-coupled in kind: that scaling up produces a failure whose explanation cannot be given in the vocabulary I listed. Tell me whether anything like that is already on the table or plausibly coming, and why.

No citations, no reading lists, no references to literature — argue from the concepts themselves, and if you lean on a result, state in your own words what it says.
