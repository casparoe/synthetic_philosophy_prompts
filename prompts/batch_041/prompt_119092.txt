I'm in the lower sixth, taking Maths, Further Maths, Economics and Philosophy, and I've somehow ended up as the person who has to do the twenty-minute slot at philosophy club next week on why we should maximise expected utility. I thought this would be a doddle, because I already knew the Pascal's wager sort of argument and the one about how if you're risk-averse over a long series of bets you'll lose money to someone who isn't, but then I went down a rabbit hole on the bus home and now I'm less sure what the actual argument is even meant to show. The version I keep coming across is the money pump: if my preferences go round in a circle, or if there are two things I simply can't rank against each other, then someone can offer me a run of trades that I'll accept one at a time and end up worse off than I started, sometimes back at the beginning but minus a tenner. Fine, that's a clever trick, but why does it make my preferences irrational, rather than just making me bad at dealing with that one person? I can see that I'd be an idiot to keep saying yes, but I don't see why the moral is "you must have transitive and complete preferences" rather than "don't stand there accepting trades all afternoon". And I've got a feeling the two halfs of that aren't the same thing at all — the loop case and the can't-compare case feel like completely different problems, and I'm fairly sure I've read somewhere that completeness is the axiom people actually fight about, while transitivity is treated as almost beyond dispute. I also can't work out what the maths theorem is doing in all this. I've seen it stated as "if your preferences obey these four axioms, they can be represented as maximising expected utility", which sounds like it's about how you'd go about measuring something, not about how you ought to behave, and I can't tell whether that's the same argument as the money pump or a totally different one wearing the same clothes.

So what I'd really like is an explanation of the conceptual issue underneath all of it: what the coherence argument is actually claiming, what it does and doesn't manage to establish, and where the real philosophical action is. Specifically, I want to get my head round whether the money pump is supposed to bite on a single choice or only on a sequence of them, and whether an agent who simply refuses to trade, or who binds himself in advance not to be pumped, escapes the argument or hasn't really answered it; whether the case against intransitivity and the case against incompleteness are genuinely the same argument, because they don't look it to me; and whether "your preferences can be represented by a utility function" is a requirement of rationality at all, or just a modelling convenience that we've quietly started treating as a norm. If there's a standard objection that the whole thing only works against someone who already values money in a particular way, or that it assumes you can't change your mind halfway through, I'd like to hear it, and I'd like to know which parts of the argument are still standing once you take those objections seriously.
