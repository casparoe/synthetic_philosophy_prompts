I've been reporting on the language-and-sexism fight for the better part of a year now -- off and on, for a feature that's already twice the length my editor signed off on -- and the thing thats driving me up the wall is that nobody in the story has moved a millimeter in all that time. I know both camps cold by now and they still just talk past each other.

Camp one: words are the plumbing of thought. If the generic he and the word man are the unmarked forms, then male is the default case and everyone else is the special case, and that shows up in who gets pictured for a job, who gets read as a doctor, who gets seen at all. Their ace is Hofstadter's Person Paper, where he runs the exact argument people make about gender but with race swapped in, and the racial version reads as monstrous to readers who'd shrugged at the gendered one for decades. If you laugh at one and shrug at the other, they say, you've just shown me which default you've stopped noticing.

Camp two: this is a distraction dressed up as politics. Words are downstream of power, not upstream of it; renaming a chairman doesn't move a nickel; and anyway the studies that supposedly show wording changes attitudes are soft -- priming has had a rough decade, the effects are small, the replications are a mess. Fixing the word is what institutions do instead of fixing anything else.

And then there's a third thing I keep bumping into, mostly from people who'd rather not be counted in either camp: the analogy is the problem. Race and sex aren't parallel, so the Person Paper doesn't prove what camp one thinks it proves. Calling the generic he sexist is a category error -- a words meaning is one thing, its statistical defaulting is another, defaults are cancellable, so where exactly is the harm?

My hypothesis, and please attack it: I don't think this is mainly an empirical dispute. I think the two sides are answering different questions under one headline. (a) Does the defaulting actually change minds? That's empirical, with messy evidence. (b) Does using a term that makes one group the neutral case do a wrong to the other group whether or not it changes any minds? That's normative and doesn't care about effect sizes. The stall is camp two insisting camp one needs (a), while camp one thinks (b) was the whole point.

So diagnose it for me. Verbal, substantive, two questions under one name, or bedrock intuitions about whether a harm has to be measurable to count as one? Is there a hidden empirical question doing the real work, and if so what study would settle it -- or is the honest answer that the empirical question is beside the point? And what would count as progress here, if anything. Dont send me a reading list, I need the diagnosis and the reasons for it, and I'd rather you be blunt about which sides framing is doing more work than it can cash out.
