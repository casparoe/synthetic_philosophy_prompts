I'm putting together an episode of the little podcast I do about the history of economic ideas, and next week's guest is a historian of 18th-century thought who's spent years arguing that Bishop Berkeley's The Querist is a real work of monetary theory and not just a few hundred rhetorical questions about Irish poverty. He's an invented composite, so no worries about putting words in a real person's mouth. I want eight to ten questions that actually press on the content of the position, and for each one the follow-up I should have ready depending on how he answers.

Stuff I want covered, roughly one question apiece:

- The "ticket or counter" thing. Berkeley asks "Whether money is to be considered as having an intrinsic value, or as being a commodity, a standard, a measure, or a pledge, as is variously suggested by writers? And whether the true idea of money, as such, be not altogether that of a ticket or counter?" I want to know whether that's genuinely a credit theory of money or just an awkward way of saying that velocity matters, and how he reconciles it with Berkeley's own insistence that land and labour are the source of wealth ("Whether the four elements, and man's labour therein, be not the true source of wealth?"). Have the follow-up ready for whichever way he leans.

- The passage where the denominations outlive the metal: "Whether the denominations being retained, although the bullion were gone, things might not nevertheless be rated, bought, and sold, industry promoted, and a circulation of commerce maintained?" Is that fiat money avant la lettre, and what does he do with a quantity theorist who says none of it works without a constraint on issue?

- The national bank scheme Berkeley kept putting in and then pulling out — his own preface to the collected edition says he omitted "particularly of those relating to the sketch or plan of a national bank, which it may be time enough to take again in hand when the public shall seem disposed to make use of such an expedient." Why did the circulating credit matter more to him than the coin, and why did he keep shelving it?

- Whether any of this connects to the immaterialism at all, or whether that's a connection scholars have retrofitted and Berkeley himself would have found bizarre. Push him on what the connection is supposed to be.

- The paternalism, which I find the most interesting bit: "Whether the creating of wants be not the likeliest way to produce industry in a people? And whether, if our peasants were accustomed to eat beef and wear shoes, they would not be more industrious?" A clergyman proposing to manufacture desires in the poor — set that against his 1721 jeremiad about luxury and stock-jobbing. I want him to say whether that's a contradiction or a development, and I want the follow-up for each answer.

- And something on the line about the "cynical content in dirt and beggary," where he blames Irish poverty partly on the poor themselves. Make him either defend it or give it up.

For every question, give me the reply he's most likely to give and the follow-up that reply earns. Then close with a short note, for me, on which of these claims about Berkeley's economics are actually settled among people who work on him and which are genuinely contested — if it's disputed whether The Querist is a systematic doctrine or occasional pamphleteering, or whether the proto-chartalist reading is anachronistic, tell me that instead of letting the guest's line stand in for the consensus. No citations or reading lists please, I just want the questions plus that settled-vs-contested note.
