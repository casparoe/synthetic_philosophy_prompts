I'm a fifth-year phil sci PhD, and one day a week I sit in our research office, which means I've somehow become the person everyone hands their well-meaning proposal for fixing research incentives to — stop counting journal names in hiring, mandate preregistration, hand out badges, switch a journal to registered reports, replace peer review with prediction markets, you get the idea. They all have the same shape: someone has read about Moloch and multipolar traps, concluded that the reason everyone keeps doing the locally rational thing that makes the collective worse off is that nobody has designed the right pledge yet, and now wants a committee to endorse it. My honest suspicion is that most of these either preach to the choir or just relocate the race instead of ending it, but I've got nothing better than vibes to argue with, and I'm tired of relitigating this case by case with people who are more confident than me. So what I want is a reusable audit procedure — a short ordered set of tests I can run on a proposal in twenty minutes at my desk, without doing any actual modelling, that tells me whether the situation it's aimed at is really a multipolar trap, which kind, and whether the proposal dissolves it, relocates it, or feeds it. Please give me the questions in the order I should ask them, what counts as a sufficient answer to each one, where the procedure just stops and returns a no, and where it has to hand the decision back to a human because the remaining question is about values rather than structure. It has to be usable by someone whose game theory is one grad seminar, it can't require me to know other institutions' private payoffs, and it should end in a small set of verdict labels I can write on a one-pager — something like escape, relocation, deepener, not-a-trap, needs-a-value-call. I also want you to push back on my own crude typology before you build on it: I currently think there are four shapes, (a) the plain race, where defecting is individually rational and everyone ends up worse off, (b) the pure-waste arms race, where the prize is fixed and therefore all the extra spending is deadweight, (c) the doorman trap, where cooperation is available but only with some credible exclusion or punishment mechanism, and (d) the self-erasing trap, where competing consumes the very resource being competed over. (d) is the one I'm least sure about and might just be a redescription of (a); tell me if these carve at the joints, if they're not mutually exclusive, or if there's a fifth I'm missing.

Then run the procedure on three cases and show me the full transcript of the tests for each. First, my own proposal: a departmental pledge not to use journal-level metrics in hiring and promotion decisions, which is the one I've been advocating for two years and which I suspect your procedure is going to be rude about — if it comes out as a relocation or a deepener I want you to say so plainly and explain exactly which test it failed, because I need to know before I take it to the working group. Second, a journal that moves its whole pipeline to registered reports. Third, a funder that allocates half its budget by lottery among proposals that clear a quality threshold, keeping the other half merit-ranked. For the lottery case I especially want to know whether the procedure can even see the thing that makes lotteries attractive to me, which is that they make the race unwinnable rather than better-policed.

Two more things. Be explicit about what the procedure can't do: I don't want it pretending to settle whether the competition is worth keeping at all (some of it probably is — I'd rather have labs racing on a real problem than not), it can't handle the case where the reform changes what people want rather than just what they can get, and it obviously can't distinguish a trap held up by real structure from one held up only by everyone's expectation that everyone else will keep playing. And note where I should stop trusting it and just make a judgement call, since I'll be presenting this to a working group of PIs, one economist who will say this is game theory with the math removed, and one dean who signs off on anything that touches hiring, so it has to survive being publically called hand-wavy.
