I'm a PhD student in early modern philosophy preparing a seminar on Berkeley's master argument. I want to give students a tier list, not of the familiar objections, but of five ways of tightening the argument's central inference: the move from the claim that one cannot conceive a sensible object without perceiving it to the conclusion that no sensible object can exist unperceived by any mind. Please rank the following five reconstructions from most to least strengthening of that inference — some may weaken it — and add a sentence or two for each explaining what it gains and what it costs. Treat the list as my own sketches, not as established positions.

1. The phenomenological reading: conceiving an object is having a quasi-perceptual experience of it; quasi-perceiving is not perceiving, so the premise about conceiving does not by itself yield the conclusion.
2. The self-referential reading: conceiving an unperceived object involves representing it as unperceived while also representing oneself as not perceiving it; the second-order component is itself a kind of perceiving, so the contradiction survives.
3. The dispositional reading: conceiving is exercising a disposition to perceive under certain conditions; the disposition can be unmanifested, so an object can be conceived as unperceived without being perceived.
4. The modal reading: to conceive an unperceived object is to conceive of a possible world in which it is unperceived; the conceiver need not inhabit that world, so no contradiction follows.
5. The indexical reading: to conceive an object as unperceived is only to conceive it as unperceived by me here and now, not as unperceived by every mind; Berkeley's conclusion requires the stronger reading.

Finally, say which reading, if any, deserves a tier of its own, and whether the master argument is better treated as a proof or as a diagnosis of a confusion.
