My reading group has been stuck on consequentialist cluelessness for like two months now and I honestly can't tell whether we're disagreeing or just talking past each other, so what I want is a diagnosis rather than another lap around the track.

The setup nobody disputes: if you try to judge an action by its consequences, the causal chain wanders off into the far future and into indirect effects that are chaotic and path-dependent, so any expected value you compute is either fabricated or wildly sensitive to guesses you've got no business making. The fight is over what that shows. One of us says it's devastating -- consequentialism is supposed to guide action by its consequences and can't, so it either can't guide at all or quietly turns into some other theory. Another says that's a magic trick: you never needed precise numbers, only rough comparisons, and anyway the same cluelessness afflicts anyone trying to predict the downstream effects of following any rule, so it's not a special problem for consequentialism. A third keeps insisting it's a problem for act-consequentialism as a decision procedure but not as a criterion of rightness. Nobody budges. And the case that keeps splitting us is tiny and stupid: should I spend an hour a week volunteering, where I can see the good it does, or donate the equivalent money, which might fund something with enormous long-run effects or none at all. Half the group says the money wins because of the tail possibilities; half says that's exactly the reasoning cluelessness makes worthless.

Here's the thing I keep circling, and I want you to either vindicate it or kill it. I suspect we're conflating two different things under one word. Call them sign-cluelessness -- you can't even tell whether the net effect is good or bad -- and size-cluelessness -- you're confident of the sign but have no idea of the magnitude. The people who say it's fatal seem to be worried about sign; the people who shrug seem to be worried about size; and if that's right, then we're not even disagreeing about the same claim.

So diagnose it properly. Is this a substantive disagreement or a verbal one, or two different questions wearing one name? Is there a hidden empirical claim doing the work -- whether the unpredictability is in-principle or merely in-practice, or whether long-run effects have a sign anyone can trust? Is it really a decision-theoretic fight about what rationality demands under uncertainty, dressed up as metaethics? Or is it bedrock: one side thinks a moral theory has to be usable by an ordinary person, the other thinks that's simply not a requirement on a criterion of rightness?

Dont just lay the options out and let me choose. Take a position, defend it against the strongest thing the other side would say, and tell me what would actually move this -- a distinction, a formal result about the relevant distributions, a case that seperates the camps -- or argue that nothing will and say why.
