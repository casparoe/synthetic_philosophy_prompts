I teach a course on technology and society, and a colleague in the engineering faculty advances the following argument whenever the regulation of some new technology comes up. Technology, he says, is a species rather than a project. Inventions are the mutations; markets, militaries and users supply the selection pressure; documentation, patents and standards are the inheritance; obsolete devices are the extinct lineages; and the whole thing drifts on without anyone at the helm. On this picture there is no inventor in the Darwinian sense of the word — no one is steering — and the shape of what we end up with is settled by the environment that selects it, not by anybody's intentions. He notes in passing that the eye has evolved independently many times over, which he takes to show that once a problem is posed some solutions are simply inevitable. His favourite illustration is the smartphone: not designed from first principles but jury-rigged from whatever components and conventions happened to be lying around, much as the vertebrate eye is jury-rigged from whatever the light-sensitive cell happened to be. From all this he draws the practical conclusion that trying to ban or heavily regulate a maturing technology is like legislating against evolution — you may slow the local case, but the lineage gets there in the end.

I suspect the analogy is carrying the conclusion rather than supporting it, but I want a proper audit rather than a debunking. Set out the correspondence explicitly — what plays the role of the gene, the mutation, the selection pressure, the niche, the species boundary, the fossil record — then locate where the mapping breaks. Separate the disanalogies that actually matter for his conclusion from the ones that are merely decorative, tell me what the analogy quietly imports that its target lacks, and say whether his fatalism survives once the disanalogies are on the table. Pay particular attention to the point about convergent evolution: say whether it can underwrite any defensible determinism about artefacts, and if so how much weaker that claim is than the one he needs. If the evolutionary picture can be repaired, or if a different analogy does the same work better, say so and say why.

Finally, tell me which single consideration would be most likely to move you to the opposite verdict, and where in your own reasoning you take the greatest risk of being wrong. Write in British English.
