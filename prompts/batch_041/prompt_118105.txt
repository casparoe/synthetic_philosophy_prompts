I run a monthly "theory lunch" for the engineering team I work on: twelve people, mixed seniority, most of whom have never read any philosophy and would tune out instantly if I framed it that way. I get one seventy-five-minute session, one room, a projector, index cards, and a whiteboard. What I actually want out of it is to change how they argue in incident postmortems, so treat the session as a rehearsal for those arguments.

The intuition I'm chasing comes in two halves, and I'd like the activity to make the team feel both, not hear them described.

First: the word "cause" may not pick out one fact about the world that a room full of people is failing to converge on. On one view I've run into, "cause" is really a three-place relation — this event, that outcome, and the contrast against which the outcome is being picked out. So "the deploy caused the outage" is shorthand for "the deploy rather than the config change caused the outage rather than a slow degradation," and if you silently swap the contrast you can get people who agree about every physical fact to disagree about what "the cause" was. Second: whether we say someone caused a harm by doing nothing — by not acting, not escalating, not replying — seems to track not a difference in the causal structure but whether we were holding that person to a norm they failed. The same omission reads as "let it happen" in one frame and "caused it" in another.

Design me one runnable activity that makes a group of non-philosophers run into both of these from the inside. Requirements:

- The scenarios should be recognizable to a software team — deploys, retries, a config change, someone not paging, a monitor that was never set up — but the structure, not the software detail, has to be doing the work. Avoid the tired trolley-and-drowning-child material; they'll have seen it.
- The rules and materials must arrange things so that the team produces inconsistent attributions themselves — flips in what they call "the cause" across scenarios that differ only in the contrast that was implicitly in play, or only in whether an actor was under a norm — before anyone names what happened. The reveal is the team's own voting record, not my explanation.
- Specify the roles, the materials, the sequence of rounds, a scoring or stake scheme that needs no real money, and where exactly I plant the manipulation so it stays invisible on the first pass.
- Give me the debrief: the specific moments in the activity to point back to, and how each maps onto the two ideas above. If either idea has a standard name in the literature, name it in the debrief only — I'd rather the team meet it first as something that happened to them.
- Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time — where a group will resist, game the rules, or converge too fast — and what to do in the moment.
- Be honest about what the activity cannot reproduce. I suspect it can show that our causal talk shifts with contrast and with norms, but cannot by itself settle whether there's a further fact of the matter about causation underneath. If that's the right limit, say so plainly and tell me what I have to supply in words that the activity can't.

Give me one committed design rather than a shortlist: if you see two plausible structures, choose one, commit to it, and tell me why you set the other aside. And commit to a position on what the activity does and doesn't establish about how "cause" works — I'd rather have a definite claim I can push back on than a survey of the options.
