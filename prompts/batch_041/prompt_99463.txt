I retired a few years ago from a long career in marine invertebrate physiology — thirty-odd years on feeding currents and ciliary pumps, mostly in sea slugs and their kin — and since then I've been working my way through philosophy of biology, mainly by arguing with a reading group at the local university. Next month I'm recording an episode of the tiny podcast I run (just me, a microphone and a dodgy Zoom link; audience of maybe two hundred, mostly other ex-scientists) and my guest is a philosopher who has spent her career defending the view that when we say what a trait is *for* we're really only giving a causal-role or capacity analysis — locating the trait within a system's working, no evolutionary history required. I come at this from the other side; the reason I drifted into philosophy at all was that I couldn't stomach the thought that the function of a structure has nothing to do with how it got there. So I want questions that actually dig, not the worn-out heart-pumps-blood material. I'd like at least one question each on how her account handles malfunction (the normativity problem — a heart that simply can't pump, a pump that's broken rather than just idle), on the 'which capacity?' problem (any trait has an endless list of causal roles; what on earth makes one of them *the* function rather than an incidental side-effect), and on traits that have never been selected. For that last one, here's a case I keep chewing on from my own corner of the world, and I'd rather the questions turn on it than on Swampman: a slug population I used to sample got washed into a new estuary, and a mucus gland that in the ancestral population was purely part of locomotion now happens to entangle a local parasite and thereby keeps the slugs clear of it. No selection has had time to act — it's a happy accident of the new setting, and the trait is just doing what it always did. Has that gland got the anti-parasite function yet, or not, and does her answer change if the lineage sticks around for ten thousand years? I want the questions to press exactly there, where I think her view either has to say something counterintuitive or quietly import history through the back door.

For each question, give me the follow-up I'll actually need, keyed to the answer she's most likely to give — she has a habit of dissolving indeterminacy worries by saying functions are relative to whatever analysis you choose, so I want to be ready for that move and for the 'you're just describing two different projects' deflection, without letting her off the hook. Eight to ten questions in total, ordered so the pressure builds rather than scattering.

Lay it out as a table with four columns: Question; What it presses on (name the specific objection or puzzle, in a few words); Anticipated reply (the answer she's most likely to give, in her voice); Follow-up (my next move, given that reply). British English throughout, please.
