I keep a notes file of my own moral reactions, in the spirit of those long forum posts where someone lists their intuitions about a topic and asks what theory they add up to, and the section I cannot get closed is the one on who is entitled to blame whom. I notice that I withdraw the sting from some instances of blame and not others, and my reading group has stopped conceding that my withdrawals are principled. So here are my verdicts, as flat as I can put them. A colleague who is late every single day blames me for being ten minutes late: the blame does not land at all. A colleague who was late every day for years, but has been punctual for a decade and apologised for the old pattern, blames me for the same thing: that lands, and I cannot square it with the previous case. A stranger who overheard me promise to be somewhere and watched me break the promise blames me: does not land either, but for what feels like a different reason, since she has no business in it. A friend who has owed me money for two years and has never repaid it blames me for cancelling on her at short notice: does not land. A friend who blames me for something I genuinely did to her, but only because she wants to win an argument: lands, and the blame is contemptible for reasons that feel orthogonal to standing, which makes me suspect these are two separate axes. A colleague who kept me on the phone for forty minutes and then blames me for being late to dinner: no standing whatsoever, and I want to know whether that is the same failure as the first case or a third kind. And a friend who has done things much worse than what she is blaming me for, though not this thing, blames me for this thing: lands, and I notice her record does not enter into my reaction at all.

The principle I would write down, if forced, is a reciprocity one: you may blame someone for a wrong only if you would accept the same blame in the same situation. That saves the punctual colleague and the reformed one, and it condemns the hypocrite, but it seems to get the stranger wrong, since she has nothing to be hypocritical about, and it says nothing at all about the friend who kept me on the phone. I am also unsure whether standing is all-or-nothing or admits of degrees, whether it can be lent, whether it survives a collective decision to stop relitigating an old wrong, and whether the whole apparatus is specific to morality at all — the senior engineer who writes the worst code in the repository and dresses down a junior for a style violation seems to me to have no standing, though style is not a moral matter, and I cannot tell whether that is the same phenomenon or merely an analogy to it.

Give me exactly five numbered items, each a sentence or two, with no introduction and no conclusion. First: the principle, or the small set of candidates if one will not do, stated precisely enough that I could apply it to a case you have not seen, with the clause each of my verdicts motivates and the case that motivates each clause, plus a verdict on whether standing comes in degrees. Second: the verdict of mine that the best of these principles cannot accommodate, and what I lose by giving it up. Third: your verdict on a victim who asks a friend to deliver the blame on her behalf. Fourth: your verdict on blaming one's own fifteen-year-old self for a cruelty that no one else witnessed. Fifth: your verdict on a family or a community that has agreed to stop relitigating an old wrong, and a member who raises it anyway.
