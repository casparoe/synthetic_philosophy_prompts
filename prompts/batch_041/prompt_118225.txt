In a thread last week about whether my lab should adopt a slower but more interpretable safety-training pipeline, a colleague ended the discussion by saying that the alignment tax is a myth — that anything we call a safety cost is really just alignment work we have not yet learned to do, and that once we learn to do it, safer models will simply be better models. By the alignment tax he meant the capability cost of making a system safe and honest. When I objected that even a small such cost compounds when competitors do not pay it, he replied that we cannot be the only lab paying it, so the point is moot.

I would like the phrase itself taken apart. Set out the distinct precise claims that someone asserting that an alignment tax exists — or denying that it does — could be making, as a menu of readings rather than one defended interpretation. I expect the readings to run from near-trivial to fairly radical, and I want to know which are which.

Axes I suspect matter, though you should add or drop them as you see fit:

- what is being taxed: the level of capability attainable at a fixed date, the rate at which capability improves, the cost of one particular method, or a lab's standing relative to others;
- the baseline against which the tax is measured: the same lab without the safety method, a later version of the same lab, or a rival's trajectory;
- whether tax is even the right metaphor: a tax is a transfer, with a payer, a collector, and a revenue, whereas a pure cost has none of these, and a deadweight loss is neither;
- who pays and who collects: the lab, its users, the public, or people who do not exist yet;
- the time profile: a one-time engineering cost that research amortizes away, or a structural cost that grows as capability grows;
- whether the denial is an empirical claim about current methods, a bet about methods not yet invented, or a definitional claim — if safety training improves instruction-following, the tax is negative and the phrase is empty;
- whether the claim concerns absolute capability or relative position, which is where the competitiveness constraint belongs: a lab that slows down alone loses the race to labs that do not, so the tax may be paid in market share rather than in capability.

For each reading, say whether it is a platitude, a substantive and defensible claim, or false; which of them would actually support my colleague's dismissal; and which one people who use the phrase in practice usually have in mind. Where a reading turns on an empirical question rather than a conceptual one, say what would have to be measured to settle it and how the answer might differ at larger scales of capability. Flag any reading that looks distinct but collapses into another. I do not need sources; I need the claims themselves separated.
