Ok, weird thought experiment. Hannah Arendt doesnt die in 1975, she lives long enough to watch the drone wars and then all the recent chatter about letting algorithms build the target lists. Whats her take?

Concretely: does she look at some guy in a trailer in Nevada, killing somebody on the other side of the planet in the morning and then driving home to coach his kids soccer team, and go yep, thats the banality of evil again, just with better wifi? Or does she say the analogy breaks down, and if so, where exactly?

My instinct is that her diagnosis was never ordinary people are secretly monsters. It was about thoughtlessness, someone who could think and wouldnt, plus a refusal to let the guy hide behind being a little cog who only followed orders. So what happens when the machine is the one choosing, when a model ranks people by predicted threat and the human is basically there to click yes, and nobody in the chain can really be said to have done the killing? People describe that as a responsibility gap. Does it make her analysis obsolete, or does it make it worse? If nobody is thinking and nobody is choosing, is it still banal, and is there even an evil there in her sense of the word?

Second part, if you have room. She cared alot about the courtroom, the idea that a person has to stand up in public and answer for what they did, as an individual, instead of dissolving into generalities about systems. So would she want drone operators in the dock? Or would she think a trial is the wrong instrument when the deed is smeared across a chain of operators, analysts, software and policy? And what would she make of international courts and the doctrine of command responsibility as a way of sticking a person back at the end of that chain?

Argue it from her actual commitments rather than just chanting banality of evil at me, and be explicit about which parts are your extrapolation vs. things she really argued.
