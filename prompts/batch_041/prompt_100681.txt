I work on reinforcement learning; I build and train agents for a living. I am fluent in the formalism of Markov decision processes, policies, value functions and the Bellman equations, discounting, stationary versus non-stationary reward, and the difference between an agent's objective and the behaviour it actually ends up exhibiting. My philosophy of action, by contrast, is patchy. I have been reading about money pumps and diachronic rationality: arguments to the effect that an agent whose preferences run in a cycle, or whose preferences shift over time in a predictable way, can be led by a sequence of individually acceptable trades back to where it started, minus a fee; and the various replies to this, ranging from pre-commitment by an agent that foresees its own future changes, to the thought that one may simply adopt a plan and treat the plan as settling what to do later. I should like this restated in the vocabulary I actually use.

Please do the following.

First, translate the money-pump argument and the surrounding debate as faithfully as you can into the language of MDPs, policies, reward functions and value functions. Tell me what a preference reversal amounts to in that language, what a commitment device is, and what corresponds to the contrast between an agent that foresees its own future preference change and one that does not.

Second, test the translation on two concrete cases: an agent with a preference cycle (A preferred to B, B to C, C back to A) facing an opponent who offers to swap bundles for a small fee; and a case in which each of a long series of individually attractive steps leads somewhere the agent would have refused at the outset, where the difficulty is time rather than a cycle. For each, say what the MDP formulation would have to look like — in particular, what must be non-standard about the reward function or the objective for the pump to be representable at all — and whether the requirement not to be pumpable comes down to the requirement that the agent's choices be representable by some value function.

Third, give me the residue report. Which claims translate cleanly, which translate only with distortion, and which have no counterpart in my vocabulary at all? I am especially interested in whether the normative element survives: reinforcement learning tells me what is optimal relative to a given objective, but not that any objective is the one an agent ought to have, and I suspect the money-pump argument is doing something my framework cannot express.

Finally, tell me whether the residue is a defect of my framework or evidence that the philosophers' disagreement is thinner than it looks. Keep the translation honest — do not tell me that the agent is simply maximising a well-defined reward when that is precisely what the argument is meant to call into question. Please write in British English.
