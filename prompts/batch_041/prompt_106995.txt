For the past several years, since retiring from a career in experimental physics, I have been teaching myself philosophy, and in six weeks I am to present a paper of mine at a small workshop that an economics department has kindly let an outsider attend. What I need from you is preparation for the discussion period, not reassurance about the paper: I want to know the objections I will actually face in that room, ordered by how likely they are and how much damage they would do, each phrased in the vocabulary the objector would use, each with a reply I can deliver in about two minutes, and each with an honest note on where the reply is soft. Tell me as well what I should concede at the outset rather than fight, and close with the one question I am hoping nobody asks. I am not looking for reading lists or citations — the arguments themselves are what I want.

The thesis I will defend is this. Satisficing is not a boundedly rational approximation to maximizing; it is the more fundamental notion, and maximizing is its degenerate limit. I take the aspiration level — the threshold that fixes what counts, for an agent, as good enough — to be the primitive element, and I claim that the interesting normative work is done not by the threshold itself but by the way aspiration levels adjust to outcomes. I model that adjustment on a feedback loop that holds an agent's operating point in place, rather than on any comparison against a fixed optimum. The picture comes from my old field: a physical system does not generally sit at the global minimum of its energy landscape; it sits wherever its dynamics and its couplings leave it, and asking whether it is at the optimum is often the wrong question. I want to say the same of an agent and its choices, and to argue that the standard objection — that a satisficer is merely a maximizer with search costs folded into the objective — gets the explanatory order backwards.

The audience will be about fifteen people. Ten or so are microeconomic theorists: they think in preferences over lotteries, in revealed preference, and in comparative statics; they prize parsimony and testability; and several of them regard any talk of aspiration levels as either a redescription of a constraint or an unfalsifiable psychological posit. Two or three are philosophers of practical reason, one of whom will very likely press me on whether good enough can be a genuine norm or only a description of what people in fact settle for. One or two are behavioral economists who will want to know what my account predicts that the standard model does not.

Give me the eight objections or so that this particular room will produce — not the ones I would get from a different sort of audience — ordered by how much damage they would do, with the likely source of each, the objection as that person would put it, my two-minute reply, and where the reply is weakest or question-begging. Then tell me which concession I should make early, which one would cost me more than it is worth, and what I should be prepared to defend rather than hope they miss.
