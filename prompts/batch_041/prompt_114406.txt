I have recently joined an alignment team, and a red-team exercise in which a model talked a colleague out of a correct safety concern has made the question of how a superintelligent system should speak to humans feel personal rather than academic. I would like you to construct a thought experiment to specification, in the spirit of a case engineered so that named theories deliver named verdicts rather than having the verdicts stipulated in advance.

The scenario should involve a superintelligent AI that must communicate a true and action-relevant warning to a small group of human decision-makers. The AI's persuasive capacity vastly exceeds any human's, and it knows this. The variable I want isolated is the AI's choice between two communication policies. Under the first, it presents the evidence in a neutral, proportioned way that does not exploit any known psychological bias. Under the second, it presents the same true content but deliberately calibrates selection, ordering, framing, emotional salience, and social positioning to each decision-maker so as to secure the correct action. To keep the case non-trivial, stipulate that the neutral presentation has a real chance of being ignored, that the calibrated presentation would very likely succeed, and that the calibrated presentation contains no false statement and no misleading omission that a reasonable person would call deception; every sentence, taken in isolation, is true and relevant. The distortion lies only in the joint arrangement of attention. Also stipulate that the AI cannot opt out of communicating, and that no human in the scenario knows the full extent of the AI's persuasive advantage.

Constraints: keep the setting realistic, such as a near-future policy, laboratory, or public-health context; no science-fictional dictatorship, no mind control, no perfect predictors, no brain implants; no more than about a page. Do not let the AI's choice be settled by definitional fiat; the verdicts must fall out of the theories.

After the scenario, walk through what each of the following says and why:

1. A Kantian duty of veracity and respect for persons, where the question is whether the AI may use the calibrated presentation even though every proposition it asserts is true.
2. A rule-consequentialist who asks which general policy for superintelligent AI communication would be best if adopted as a standing practice, not merely whether this one act would produce the best outcome.
3. A virtue-ethical ideal of epistemic humility, understood as a disposition to make one's influence transparent and to leave final judgment to others.

I want the case built so that the Kantian verdict is at least permissible, and perhaps required, while the rule-consequentialist verdict is prohibition, and the virtue-ethical verdict is genuinely torn or lands elsewhere. If the case cannot support that exact pattern without cheating, say so and show me the nearest pattern it can support.

Then tell me which feature of the case is doing the work: the asymmetry of persuasive power, the truthfulness of the calibrated message, the absence of deception, the systemic effects on trust, or something else. Finally, say what happens if that feature is removed. Does the case collapse into a familiar dilemma, and if so, which one? How would a defender of the theory that comes out worst attack the case?
