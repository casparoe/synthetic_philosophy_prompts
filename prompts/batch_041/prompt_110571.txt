Some friends and I have a long-running argument that flares up every time someone says Gen Z in a meeting, and I'd like to settle it with an actual case rather than another round of Boomer-versus-Millennial shouting. I'm sitting in on a seminar on social kinds this term and the prof keeps waving off generations as nothing but labels, which bugs me, because the labelling story and the shared-experience story both seem to predict something, they just predict different things. So build me a thought experiment to order.

The spec: two cohorts in the same city, or the same firm, matched on everything people normally point to when they call a generation real -- they came of age through the same formative shock, the same media environment, the same schools and the same rotten labour market. The only difference is that one of them acquires a name for itself that its members actually take up: they call themselves by it, they organise around it, employers and advertisers address them by it. The other one never does. Keep it realistic and short -- one paragraph, no invented islands, no time travel, no society where I have to stipulate how anyone feels, and the labelling has to emerge out of ordinary stuff (a magazine, a student union, a hit record, a hiring freeze) rather than me just announcing that it happened. What I need out of it: which cohort counts as a genuine generation under (a) the natural-kind-ish view, where a kind is real if there's some mechanism keeping its members' properties clustered and co-varying; (b) the looping/dynamic-nominalist view, where the classifying is itself part of the mechanism, so the label feeds back and makes the kind; and (c) the hard-nosed demographer's line that generation is just the age-period-cohort muddle, since cohort, period and age are three numbers and any two fix the third, so you can never separate a generational effect from an age effect or a period effect in the first place. I want (a) and (b) landing on opposite sides, and I want to see the mechanism rather than be handed the verdict. Then tell me which feature is doing the load-bearing work, what the case collapses into if I delete it, and how someone who thinks the view that comes off worst is actually right would attack the setup -- is there a cheap way to say the case stipulates its own conclusion? Last thing, and it's the bit I actually care about: say where your reasoning here is most likely to be wrong and what would change your mind about whether generations are real kinds at all. If the honest answer is that one of those three views isn't so much false as unfalsifiable, I'd rather you said so than papered over it.
