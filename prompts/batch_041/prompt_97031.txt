So I've been reading rationalist blogs and lurking in the LessWrong-ish Discords for like ten years now, and I keep getting into this same fight with a friend of mine who works on evals, and I want to see it actually played out on paper instead of me just gesturing at it in a group chat.

Heres the fight. I think "the alignment problem" is basically one problem wearing a trenchcoat: we're going to build systems that are smarter than us, we don't know how to point them at what we actually want, and everything else (specification, generalization, oversight, governance) is either a sub-case of that or downstream of it. My friend thinks that's a motte-and-bailey and that the phrase is doing at least four or five completely different jobs — like, the "how do we even write down what we want" problem, the "the thing we trained isn't the thing we deploy" problem, the "it's fine on the test distribution and unhinged off it" problem, and the "even a perfectly aligned system doesn't help if three labs race each other" problem — and that these don't share solutions, so calling it one problem makes people think solving one buys you the others.

What I want from you: write me a dialogue between two people who hold those two views. Roughly 900–1300 words. Both of them should be sharp — I don't want the "it's one problem" person to be a wide-eyed singularity guy and the "it's several problems" person to be a smug deflationist, or vice versa. I want each of them to land real hits on the other. Make them use concrete cases to fight over rather than just trading definitions — e.g. whether a given line of work (scalable oversight, mechanistic interpretability, RLHF-style fine-tuning, evals, control) is "solving the alignment problem" or "solving one of the problems people call alignment," and whether progress on one should update you on the others. Let them genuinely concede points where the other one is right; a dialogue where nobody ever moves is boring and also fake.

Also, at the end, add a short separate note (not in the dialogue's voice) that flags which parts of this are actually settled among people who work on it and which parts are live, unresolved disagreements where smart people land in different places. I don't want a confident wrap-up that quietly treats one camp's framing as the obviously correct one — if it's contested, say it's contested and say roughly who's on which side without turning it into a reading list or citing stuff you can't actually check. And dont invent any specific quotes or attribute exact positions to named researchers; keep it at the level of framings and arguments.
