I am a novelist, about two-thirds through a book whose entire spine is a long quarrel — four siblings on an island province, arguing across thirty years about whether their home should leave the mainland federation — and I have reached the stage where I can write each character's speeches beautifully but can no longer tell which of them is right. Over dinner last week a friend who teaches constitutional law told me that my problem is that I hold a doctrine without knowing it, and I've been unable to stop testing that claim. What I actually believe about secession, set out case by case, does not look like a doctrine; it looks like a heap of verdicts I inherited without noticing. So here they are, as honestly as I can state them, and what I want is the principle, if there is one, that generates them — and, where no single principle can, an honest account of which of my verdicts I should surrender rather than a survey of what other people have written on the subject. Scotland's independence referendum, negotiated with the central government and won by a clear majority: legitimate, and I find the economic arguments against it barely relevant. Catalonia's declaration of independence after a referendum the central state had declared unlawful: not legitimate, even granting that a majority there wanted out — and I notice I hold this quite firmly while being unable to say why the central state's objection should outweigh the voters'. A referendum won by fifty percent plus one, anywhere, on the question of dissolving a state: not enough; I want a supermajority, or a second confirming vote after a cooling-off period, and I cannot tell whether that is a principle or just nerves. Now the invented ones. A region whose central government has suspended its legislature and jailed its leaders, leaving no lawful route to a vote at all: legitimate to go, and the absence of any clean majority vote does not trouble me in the least. A rich port city that pays far more into the treasury than it receives, voting cleanly to leave, with no distinct language, no separate history of statehood, and no grievance beyond the ledger: not legitimate — a fiscal complaint is not a nation. An archipelago that joined the federation voluntarily a generation ago under a treaty containing an explicit right of exit: legitimate, plainly, and the treaty clause seems to do all the work. A region leaving in order to make itself homogeneous, stripping or expelling the minority within it that would rather stay: not legitimate, whatever the size of the majority. A newly independent state that immediately refuses its own sub-region the vote it just used: hypocritical, and I want to say the right must be self-applying. A federation that wishes to expel a region against its will: impermissible, even though I have just said the region may leave — I am fairly sure the asymmetry is real and not sentimentality. A region annexed by force within living memory: legitimate to leave, and here the injustice, not the demography, carries the weight. The tentative principle I keep reaching for is something like this: a group may secede if it is a distinct people, if it has either negotiated a lawful route to a vote or been left no lawful route at all, and if the new state extends the same right to its own minorities — but I can already see that this clause-stack is doing suspicious work, and that a cleaner principle might cost me Scotland or the annexed region.

What I would like from you is a principle, or a small set of rival principles, stated precisely enough that I can see where each one bites: for every clause, the case that forces it; the verdicts it delivers on cases I have not listed, especially the ones I suspect will embarrass it — secession to escape a debt, a region leaving a democracy to join a non-democracy or the reverse, a group that is internally divided about leaving, a region that wants to depart and take disputed territory or water with it, and the expulsion case I mentioned; and then the part I actually need, which is a frank statement of which of my verdicts the best principle cannot accommodate and why I should abandon that verdict rather than complicate the principle to keep it. I am not asking for a reading list or for anyone's authority; I want the shape of the thing, so that when my four siblings finally sit down at the table I will know which of them has been arguing badly and, more to the point, whether the book I am writing has been taking a side I never chose.
