# Prompt

Analyze the imageless-thought controversy of the early 1900s as a case study in the reliability of introspection. The facts I'm working from, so that you don't have to rely on any particular secondary account: the Würzburg laboratory associated with Oswald Külpe reported that subjects could judge and compare meanings without any accompanying sensory image, while Edward Titchener's Cornell laboratory insisted that careful introspection always turned up some image, however faint, and that the Würzburg observers were simply untrained or reporting carelessly. Each side treated the other's results as an artifact of bad introspective method, and the dispute was never settled on introspective grounds — it faded as behaviorism and, later, experimental cognitive psychology made the question of what is "given" to introspection look methodologically suspect.

I want the analysis to run in both directions. First, treat the controversy through the lens of a theory of introspection: the idea that introspection is not a form of inner observation that delivers raw data but rather a kind of inference or interpretation, so that what a subject "reports" is already shaped by the categories and expectations the observer brings. Does that account for why two well-trained laboratories could sincerely report incompatible things while both believing they were simply reading off the contents of experience? Where does the theory overreach — does it imply that no introspective report can ever be evidence, and if so, is that too strong, or does the controversy actually support it? Distinguish the position that introspection is unreliable in this domain from the stronger position that it is not a genuine source of evidence at all, and say which the episode supports.

Second, let the case test the theory. The controversy is often narrated as a simple failure of introspection that discredited the method wholesale. But is that the right reading? Consider the possibility that the two laboratories were asking genuinely different questions — the Würzburg subjects may have been reporting on the content of thought while Titchener's subjects were reporting on the phenomenology of their own observing, and the "contradiction" may be a comparison of incommensurable reports rather than a disagreement about a single fact. If so, what does that do to the claim that introspection is inference-laden? Does it weaken it (the two sides weren't really introspecting the same thing) or strengthen it (the choice of what to introspect was itself theory-driven)? 

Finally, tell me what you take the residual lesson to be for present-day reliance on introspective reports — in, say, the study of consciousness or the measurement of subjective experience — and whether the episode is better read as an argument that introspection needs external calibration, or as an argument that the whole project of treating introspective reports as data was misconceived.
