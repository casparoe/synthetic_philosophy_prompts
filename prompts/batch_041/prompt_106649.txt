You're a career archivist — twenty-odd years processing manuscript collections, you've written more finding aids than you can count — and you also know the critical lit on archives and power inside out. I want you to write a satirical finding aid: the dry, standardized document a repositry posts online to describe a collection, except this collection is invented, and the document is quietly a horror show. Deadpan all the way through. No winking, no "isn't this ironic" nudges — the conventions of the form should do the work.

Before you start writing, brainstorm for a few lines: what does a "neutral" descriptive apparatus actually do to the people it describes? Who gets a full name, who gets aggregated into a category, whose labor becomes "miscellaneous," what even counts as a record?

Here's the setup. The collection is the papers of the Hollis family, a 19th-century merchant-and-mill family from a made-up New England town, held at the (also made-up) Kestrel College archives. Give it a collection number, a title, a date range, an extent in linear feet. Include the usual sections: a biographical/historical note, a scope-and-content note, an arrangement note, access restrictions, controlled access terms / subject headings, and a processing information note. That's the spine — but the finding aid's silences and category choices are the whole point. The biography should celebrate the patriarch; the enslaved people, the servants, and the daughters should show up only the way the form makes them show up. Let the subject headings indict themselves. Put one line in the processing note that tells the reader exactly how much of the collection was never catalogued, in the flattest possible tone.

Bake in at least one sincere-sounding gesture toward "reparative" or "community-inclusive" description that, on inspection, changes nothing — the kind of sentence an institution writes when it wants credit for the problem it's still creating. And somewhere include one detail a real archivist will recognize as the actual, mundane, budget-driven crime: a choice made to save processing hours, not out of malice, that ends up deciding who gets remembered.

Around 700–900 words. Keep the section headers. It should be funny to someone who's processed a collection and still readable to someone who's never set foot in an archive, and it should never once state its own moral. Don't invent citations or point to real repositories — the document stands on its own. Take your time and get the form right; the satire lives in the gap between the neutral template and what it's describing.
