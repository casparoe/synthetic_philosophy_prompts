I'm preparing a session for a graduate reading group on epistemic utility theory, and I want to build a case for something my group keeps waving away: the accuracy-dominance argument for probabilism is usually presented as though it followed straight from accuracy being truth-directed, when on the face of it something stronger about the scoring rule is doing the real work. Please construct the case for me, in enough detail that it could be worked through at a whiteboard. It is fine to dress the rule up as a reward scheme an actual agent faces — a forecasting contest, say — so long as the mathematics is left explicit.

Here is the machinery I want you to use. Fix a finite partition of the possible worlds: two or three cells, your choice. A credence function assigns a real number to each cell, with no requirement that the numbers lie in [0, 1] or sum to 1. A scoring rule assigns a real number to each credence function at each world, higher being better; call the rule truth-directed if at every world a credence function that is certain of the truth attains the maximum score available at that world, and call it proper if for every probability function p, announcing p maximizes expected score when the expectation is taken using p itself; call it strictly proper if that maximizer is always unique. What I want is a credence function that blatantly violates the probability axioms — a credence above 1, or a negative credence, not a marginal case — which is nevertheless not strictly dominated, in the sense that no alternative credence function scores at least as well as it at every world and strictly better at some world; and I want the reason the dominance argument stalls to be precisely that your scoring rule is proper without being strictly proper. Constraints: give the rule in closed form, keep it truth-directed, and keep everything finite — no limits, no infinite partitions, no exotic mathematics.

Then walk through the following, in order. (1) Tabulate the deviant function's score at each world alongside the score of the probabilistic function the dominance argument would ordinarily point to, and show from the table exactly which inequality fails; say also whether any alternative weakly dominates the deviant function, and if so, what that tells us about how much the argument still delivers. (2) Identify the feature of your scoring rule that is doing the work, and say whether the failure is a failure of truth-directedness or of strict propriety. (3) Remove that feature by replacing your rule with the Brier score — minus the sum, over cells, of the squared difference between your credence in a cell and that cell's truth value, where a cell's truth value at a world is 1 if the world lies in it and 0 otherwise — and show that the deviant function is then strictly dominated, naming the function that dominates it. (4) Say whether the expected-accuracy route to probabilism can pick up the slack in your case, and why or why not. (5) Finally, play defense: give the strongest reply available to someone who thinks the dominance argument survives your case, whether by insisting that epistemic utility must be strictly proper, by settling for weak dominance, or by arguing that a merely proper rule is not a measure of accuracy at all, and say whether that reply leaves the argument sound or merely narrows its scope.
