For most of my career I was a field geologist, and since retiring I have been trying to teach myself moral philosophy. I have become interested in a family of views that give priority to the relief of suffering, and in particular in what I take to be a fault line within them. Some people hold that pains and pleasures are commensurable: that there is a single scale on which any amount of pleasure can, in principle, be traded against any amount of pain, even if the exchange rate is very unfavorable. Others hold a lexical threshold view: that once suffering passes a certain severity, no quantity of mild pleasure can compensate for it, so that the comparison is not merely unfavorable but blocked. I want a dialogue between two able defenders of these positions.

Please write a dialogue between (A) a lexical threshold negative utilitarian and (B) a commensurabilist. Both should be competent, and neither should be a strawman. They should respond to each other's actual points rather than talk past each other, and each should at some point state the strongest version of the other's view before attacking it. Do not have them cite books or authors. I want the argument to stand on its own, and neither character should appeal to literature I would have to look up.

I would like the exchange to be organized around a case I have been turning over, which I call the partition problem. Suppose a device can either inflict a single continuous episode of agony on one person, or divide the same total nociceptive intensity into a very large number of very brief fragments and distribute them across many people, each fragment being too mild to cross the lexical threshold on its own. The commensurabilist can then say: if the fragments are each sub-threshold, they are commensurable with pleasure, and enough mild pleasure can outweigh them. The lexicalist must say either that the threshold attaches to the whole pattern rather than to the fragments, or that the division changes the moral character of the suffering. I want the dialogue to test both replies, and I want the characters to press the partition problem until it is clear what each view must claim about the relation between momentary intensity and overall severity.

I also want them to consider a distinction I have not seen put sharply enough: between a threshold that is a feature of the psychology of the sufferer and a threshold that is a feature of the normative structure of reasons. If the threshold is psychological, it looks like a contingent fact about how much we can bear; if it is normative, it looks like a discontinuity in the space of reasons, and it is unclear whether such discontinuities are intelligible. Let the characters disagree about which kind of threshold their view needs, and let them explore whether a lexical threshold is better understood as a discontinuity in the exchange rate or as a discontinuity in the ranking itself. I would rather the dialogue be in the weeds than broad: let them spend time on the exact form of the threshold and the exact sense in which pleasures and pains are supposed to be commensurable.

The dialogue should not end in a draw. Have one of them land an objection that the other cannot entirely absorb, and have the other either concede a specific point or say exactly what they must give up to keep their position. I want to finish with a definite sense of which view survives the exchange better, and why. Please take a side in the dialogue itself rather than laying out the options for me to choose from. I have no formal training in philosophy, so I may be reinventing wheels; but I want the case treated seriously, and I want the characters to argue as if the answer mattered.
