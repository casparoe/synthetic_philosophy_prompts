I'm a third-year philosophy student, and the seminar I'm taking on material constitution has just reached the special composition question — the question of when some things add up to a further thing. Rather than argue from a theory down to the cases, I have been trying to work backwards from what I actually think about particular cases, and I have not been able to make my verdicts consistent. Here they are, stated as plainly as I can manage.

1. The legs, seat and back of a chair, screwed together and standing in a room: there is one object there, the chair.
2. Those same parts, unscrewed and strewn across the floor: there is no object composed of them, only the parts.
3. A heap of gravel tipped out of a truck: no object, just the stones, even though they rest on one another.
4. A boulder: one object. I am aware I cannot say what separates it from the gravel heap except that its parts are fused rather than stacked, and I am not sure that is a difference in kind.
5. A cat: one object.
6. A Portuguese man o' war, which is not a single animal but a siphonophore — a colony of specialised zooids physically joined into one body, unable to survive apart, that swims and stings as a unit: one object. I am confident about this one.
7. A lichen — a fungus and an alga or cyanobacterium growing as a single body, neither of which can occupy that habitat alone: one object.
8. A biofilm — a sheet of bacteria of several species embedded in a matrix they secrete, exchanging signals and nutrients, with internal channels and a division of labour: no object, just a great many bacteria. This sits badly next to 6 and 7.
9. An ant colony, with a queen, castes, and a nest whose temperature the workers hold steady: no object. This sits badly next to 6.
10. A candle flame: a definite boundary, self-maintaining for hours, its molecules constantly replaced: no object. I cannot say why it fails where the cat succeeds.
11. A tornado: no object, with the same worry.
12. A human being, mitochondria and gut microbiome included: one object — even though by my verdicts that is a colony plus its symbionts, and I have just called one colony an object and another not.
13. A heap of a thousand grains, with one grain removed at a time: no object at any point, and I cannot locate a line at which one appears.
14. The chair's parts, still on the floor, when someone is due to reassemble them in five minutes: no object. This is meant to rule out any view on which the arrangement being imminent does the work.
15. Two electrons in a singlet state, which I am told have no individual state at all, only a joint one: still two things, not one.

The principle I have been trying out is this: there is a composite object just in case its parts are held in a configuration they would not otherwise occupy by a single causal process, where that configuration is either maintained from within by the process or fixed by bonds between the parts. That gets the chair, the boulder, the cat, the man o' war and the lichen. It seems to make objects out of the flame, the tornado, the biofilm and the ant colony, all of which I deny, and out of the two electrons, which I also deny. And it gives no answer at all to 13.

So: is there a principle that yields my verdicts, or something close to them? If the price of consistency is that one or two of the verdicts has to go, I would like to know which, and what the repaired principle looks like. I would also like to know what it implies for cases I have not listed — a glacier, a termite mound considered together with the colony that maintains it, and two people in the middle of a conversation, each of whose next remark is caused by the other's. I care less about which answer is true than about whether my verdicts can be made to hang together; if they cannot, I want to know which one is doing the damage.
