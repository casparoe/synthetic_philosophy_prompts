I am preparing a seminar on Nietzsche's late writings about diet, climate, and regimen — the texts in which he treats physiology as the real subject of philosophy and offers his own eating and drinking habits as evidence for his thought — and I want to close it with Kafka's "A Hunger Artist." Read the story through that material, and set aside the existentialist reading it usually receives. Treat Nietzsche's late self-writing and Kafka's story as specimens of the same ancient genre, the dietetic text, which prescribes a way of living rather than arguing for a thesis, and ask what each regimen produces. The hunger artist fasts as a spectacle and is held to his impresario's forty-day limit, which he resents; when the fashion passes he is dropped, and he fasts on without limit until he dies, explaining at the end that he fasted only because he never found food he liked. A well-fed panther is put in his cage. Is his fast an instance of the ascetic ideal as Nietzsche describes it, the will that turns against life, or a case his account cannot classify — a self-denial that is not a denial of life, because it is the only appetite the man has? What does the story's social physiology, the impresario's rule and the crowd's boredom, do to Nietzsche's claim that a thinker's regimen can be read off his philosophy? End by saying what Kafka sees about fasting, health, and the body that Nietzsche's dietetics, for all its attention to them, misses.
