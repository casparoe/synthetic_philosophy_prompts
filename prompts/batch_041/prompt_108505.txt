Here is my opening. I write software, and we like to say that all models are wrong but some are useful, so I expected to agree with you. I'll grant the easy cases: I don't think a model of a fluid is refuted by treating the fluid as having no viscosity, or a model of a planet by treating the planet as a point. My problem is a different kind of false assumption — not a simplification you could in principle undo, but one that is doing the real work. Suppose a model of a market predicts prices well only because it assumes all traders are identical and perfectly rational, when the actual behavior comes from traders being different and their errors cancelling out. That model is a stopped clock: right by coincidence, and it will fail the moment I change the setting so the errors stop cancelling. There the falseness of the assumption is exactly what should make me distrust the prediction — which is what your view denies. So, without ever appealing to whether a model's assumptions are true, how do you tell the useful falsehood from the lucky one?
