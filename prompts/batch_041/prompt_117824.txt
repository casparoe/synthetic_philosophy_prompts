I have now heard the maxim that death is a process, not an event three times in the same hospital ethics committee, most recently from a transplant surgeon who used it to brush aside a challenge to a protocol that declares death after a few minutes of asystole and then restores circulation to the donor's organs while deliberately keeping blood from reaching the brain. His thought appeared to be that once one grants that death is a process, the moment we fix on is a policy choice, and there is nothing further to argue about. I doubt the maxim can carry that much weight, but I cannot locate the point at which the load exceeds the beam.

Set out the distinct precise theses that the sentence could be expressing, and give each a verdict. The list should contain at least one reading that is a platitude about the dying process, one that is a substantive but defensible metaphysical claim about the state of being dead, and one that is simply false; there may be others, including some that are true but irrelevant to the surgeon's purpose. For each reading, state what it commits you to, what it rules out, and whether it would actually license the inference he drew — that the boundary between the living and the dead is stipulated rather than discovered. Attend in particular to what each reading implies about the difference between a cessation of function that cannot be reversed and one that merely will not be reversed because everyone has agreed to stop trying, and about whether the neurological and the circulatory criteria for death stand or fall together under any of the readings. Do not tell me which reading is correct. Give me the menu, with verdicts, and identify which entry the surgeon was most likely reaching for.
