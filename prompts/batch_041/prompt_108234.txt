I cover science and ideas for a general-interest monthly, and the magazine has asked me to run a two-hour evening seminar for a mixed group — four working scientists (a physical chemist, two evolutionary biologists, a paleoanthropologist), half a dozen of our own reporters, and a few subscribers who won places in a draw — on whether the sciences carve nature at its joints. I have taught this kind of thing before and I know the room's starting positions: roughly half will assume that 'natural kind' just means whatever categories a mature science has settled on, and the other half will suspect the whole question is linguistic housekeeping that gets in the way of the science. What I want from you is not the explanation but the plan for it: the order in which the pieces have to come in and why each depends on the one before it, from whatever minimum of apparatus they need before the phrase 'joints of nature' can mean anything to them, through the point at which an essentialist picture of kinds — a kind fixed by an underlying nature that is discovered a posteriori, in the Kripke–Putnam style — becomes genuinely attractive, to the point where it starts to come apart, whether on a cluster-and-mechanism picture (a kind as a family of properties held together by an underlying process) or on the more deflationary view that which kinds there are depends on what we are trying to do. I don't want the session to end in a tidy settlement; I want the room to end up inside the disagreement, able to say what actually turns on it.

The single case that carries the whole two hours matters more than anything else, so please choose it and defend the choice: say what it has to have and what it must lack, and be explicit about why the stock examples are the wrong ones here — water and gold are too easily made to look like the easy case for essentialism, and 'whales are fish' is a joke about labels rather than a case about kinds. Two candidates I have been circling and cannot decide between: the Anthropocene, which the stratigraphers' formal body declined to ratify as a unit even though geologists, and everyone else, go on using the term; and something from the biomedical side, where contested categories get revised under empirical pressure and where the costs of getting a category wrong are paid by patients rather than by philosophers. If neither is right, say so and offer better. I also want the misconception map: which wrong ideas they will walk in with and which ones the session itself will plant. My own guess is that the likeliest casualties are the inference from 'this kind isn't natural' to 'this category is arbitrary,' the reading of the cluster view as a disguised anti-realism, the slide from 'species aren't kinds' to 'species don't exist,' and the assumption that the joint-carving metaphor promises one correct taxonomy — and for each of these I want the precise moment in the sequence where I should head it off. Finish with the one question I should put to the room at the end that would tell me whether the idea landed rather than whether they enjoyed the evening, and with what I should openly say I am not going to explain at this level, and why not.
