I am taking a graduate seminar on naturalistic approaches to religion, and we have been reading Hume's Natural History of Religion alongside contemporary cognitive and evolutionary accounts. I am having trouble with the opening of Hume's argument, especially the following passage:

IT appears to me, that if we consider the improvement of human society, from rude beginnings to a state of greater perfection, polytheism or idolatry was, and necessarily must have been, the first and most antient religion of mankind. This opinion I shall endeavour to confirm by the following arguments.

'Tis a matter of fact uncontestable, that about 1700 years ago all mankind were idolaters. The doubtful and sceptical principles of a few philosophers, or the theism, and that too not entirely pure, of one or two nations, form no objection worth regarding. Behold then the clear testimony of history. The farther we mount up into antiquity, the more do we find mankind plunged into idolatry. No marks, no symptoms of any more perfect religion. The most antient records of human race still present us with polytheism as the popular and established system. The north, the south, the east, the west, give their unanimous testimony to the same fact. What can be opposed to so full an evidence?

Our instructor suggested that Hume is not primarily making a documented historical claim but is writing in the eighteenth-century genre of conjectural history—a philosophical reconstruction of how a practice might have developed from ruder beginnings—and that the real target is the psychological priority of polytheism over theism. Even granting that, I cannot see how the appeal to universal testimony and the idea of a natural progress of human thought is supposed to support the conclusion that polytheism was necessarily first. It looks like an empirical generalization, but the word necessarily makes it sound like a conceptual or psychological claim. I would like help understanding what kind of argument Hume is making, how the historical and psychological strands are meant to fit together, and what work the claim about the improvement of human society is doing.

Please be careful to distinguish what is relatively settled in the scholarly literature on Hume from what is genuinely contested, and do not present a contested reading as the consensus. I am not asking for a bibliography or reading list; I want a clear reconstruction of the argument and its possible weaknesses. If there are multiple defensible readings, lay them out and say what each would have to assume.
