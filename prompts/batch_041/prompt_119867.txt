I'm a backend engineer with no formal training in philosophy, and I sit on the reliability review board at the payments company where I work. That board has to sign off on any change to the way our services coordinate during an incident, and at Thursday's meeting we vote on a proposal that has split us. I'd like you to draft the memo I'll circulate beforehand; keep it under two pages.

The trigger was a regional outage last month. Our two automated failover systems — one run by my team, one by the platform team — both sat idle for most of an hour because each was waiting for the other to confirm it was safe to act. Neither system had malfunctioned. Each was doing exactly what it was designed to do.

Three proposals are now on the table. The first, from a staff engineer, would require an explicit mutual-acknowledgment handshake before any coordinated action: each side confirms its readiness, then confirms that it has seen the other side's confirmation, and so on, until both sides act with certainty about what the other will do. The second, from the platform team, is simpler: designate a single service as the sole decision-maker for any incident, and require everyone else to stand down and defer to it. The third comes from a researcher we brought in for a second opinion. She argues that the first proposal cannot deliver what it promises, because with any finite number of acknowledgments you never get past some fixed level of nested knowledge — I know that you know that I know, and so on — and no protocol can manufacture certainty over a channel that can always drop the last message. She adds that a system designed to behave one way when the parties are nearly certain and another way when they are certain can flip between two completely different behaviors on the strength of a single lost packet, which makes it nearly impossible to test. Her own suggestion is the reverse of the staff engineer's: design each side to act on a high but not total probability that the other will act too, accepting a small chance that both act at once, on the grounds that near-certainty and certainty are not two points on one scale but different regimes.

Two findings from the postmortem matter here. First, during the outage nobody was sure whether the other team had seen the designation they were both waiting on, or whether the other team knew that they had seen it. Second, both teams had a reason to prefer that the other one act first, and both knew that about each other; the researcher says this is what makes the deadlock worse rather than better, and that the same feature of the situation is what the platform team's simpler rule would have to solve.

Please draft the memo for Thursday. It should recommend one concrete course of action, including what we should tell the two teams to build and by what date, and it should explain in plain language, for a board of engineers and one lawyer, what the mutual-acknowledgment proposal can and cannot achieve. Give the strongest honest statement of the case for whichever option you end up rejecting before you explain why you reject it. The researcher mentioned that there is a standard name for the underlying puzzle; if naming it helps the board, do so, but assume nobody has read the literature.
