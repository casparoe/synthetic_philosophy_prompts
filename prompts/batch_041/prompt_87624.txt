I'm teaching care ethics this term and want to end with a session on Ishiguro's Never Let Me Go — the fact that the Hailsham kids are literally called carers and donors is almost too on the nose, and I dont want a lazy allegory reading of it. Five points, one or two sentences each, no intro and no wrap-up.

1. Start with Noddings' one-caring and cared-for: is Kathy really the one-caring in her sense, with engrossment and motivational displacement, and if so who completes the relation by receiving? She's attuned to everyone's needs constantly, but I want to know whether any of her care is actually received, or whether the novel is built so that it never is.

2. Then use Tronto's four phases — attentiveness, responsibility, competence, responsiveness — as a scorecard on Kathy and on Hailsham as an institution. Where does her competence actually fail, and is her attentiveness really hers, or is it a trained habit of noticing only what the place taught her to notice?

3. The old complaint that care ethics is a slave morality, an ethic that flatters the powerful because it makes the exploited devoted — does the novel confirm that or refute it? Hailsham raises people whose capacity to care is the resource being harvested, and I want a verdict on whether that's just plot machinery or an argument about the theory.

4. Pick one scene where care looks like it's serving the carer as much as the cared-for — Kathy staying a carer longer than she has to, or the deference she keeps giving Ruth — and say what it shows about whether care needs reciprocity, and whether the novel treats unreturned care as tragic or as simply how things go.

5. End on where the novel resists the reading: what's done to them is structural, and no amount of good caring between them touches it. Does that mean care ethics is incomplete without justice, or does the novel show something care ethics sees that justice talk can't?
