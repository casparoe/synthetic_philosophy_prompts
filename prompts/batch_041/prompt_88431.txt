My friend has an arguement about many-worlds and probability that I can't get out of, and I'm pretty sure it proves way too much. Tidied up:

(1) If many-worlds is right, then after a measurement there's a future version of me for each outcome, and all of them are equally me.
(2) Credence is about what's going to happen to me.
(3) So if every outcome happens to some version of me, there's no fact about which one I get; what happens to me is just all of it. So my credence in any single outcome is 1, or else it's undefined -- never 1/2, never the amplitude-squared.
(4) So the Born numbers can't be my degrees of belief.
(5) And if they're not degrees of belief they're not probabilities; the decision-theoretic derivations are just dressing up my preferences over branches and calling the result a probability.

He calls (2)+(3) the no-fact-no-credence principle.

(6) But (2)+(3) never mention physics. Take the case where my body gets copied into two rooms and the original is destroyed -- there's no fact about which of the two is me, in the same sense. The principle says my credence that I'm in room A is 1 or undefined. That's obviously wrong; it's 1/2.
(7) So either the principle is false, and then his argument against the Born rule collapses with it, or it's true, and it takes ordinary self-location credences down too.
(8) And if the copy case is what shows credences still work, the thing doing the work there is symmetry -- the two of us are on a par. Fine, but branches aren't on a par, they're weighted by amplitude. If symmetry is what gives me 1/2 in the copy case, then by the same symmetry I'd give every branch the same credence, which is branch counting, not the Born rule. So the rescue seems to prove too much as well.

I'd like to keep (1) and (4) if there's any way to. Which step do I give up, why did it look fine, and what does each option cost me? Also say what would change your mind, and where your own reasoning is most likely to be wrong.
