For the better part of a year I have been reporting on the politics of constitutional amendment, and I keep running into a structural puzzle that I do not have the legal-philosophical equipment to resolve. Many written constitutions now contain provisions that purport to place certain other provisions beyond the reach of the ordinary amendment process — a short list of entrenched subjects, sometimes the republican form of government or human dignity, sometimes the amendment clause itself — so that a change which would otherwise command the requisite parliamentary majority is simply not available. In several countries the highest court has been willing to invalidate an amendment on just these grounds, reasoning that the amending power is itself a limited power and that there is an unwritten basic structure the amendment process may not be used to destroy; in others the same result is reached by requiring a referendum or a supermajority that no ordinary coalition can assemble. I notice the same self-referential move in a much lighter setting — the parlor game in which changing the rules is itself one of the legal moves, so that a player may legislate a rule governing how rules are legislated — and it seems to me that the two cases raise one and the same question, which is why I am putting it to you rather than to a constitutional lawyer.

What I want to know is what the practice presupposes. Does a self-entrenching clause commit its authors to a source of legal authority that is not itself part of the legal system — a constituent power of the people standing behind the constituted government, or a rule of recognition that the system's own rules cannot amend — or can the whole apparatus be reconstructed out of the system's ordinary materials? Which rival pictures of law does the practice quietly rule out: that a legal order is simply whatever its own procedures generate, that sovereignty is indivisible, that no rule can bind its own revision? And is the practice even coherent, or does every self-entrenching clause either collapse into an ordinary rule that the system remains free to revise by the very means that made it, or else rest on a premise that is moral or political rather than legal? I would like you to reconstruct the implicit theory as sympathetically as you can, then tell me how much of it the practice strictly requires as opposed to merely sitting comfortably with, and where the practice is underdetermined, in the sense that several quite different theories of legal authority would license exactly the same institutional arrangements.

There is a further wrinkle that bothers me. Entrenchment protects a text, but the same courts that will not let a legislature touch the text are perfectly willing to reinterpret it, generation by generation, and treat their own earlier readings as revisable in a way the words are not, so I am no longer sure what the practice actually conserves — and if the answer is that it conserves nothing but a rhetorical focal point, I would like to know what follows for the courts that invoke it. I would also like to know what would have to change, in the way such judgments are written and reasoned, if one gave up the presupposed view and adopted instead a frankly procedural account on which anything produced by the prescribed procedure is valid law. Please write this out in continuous prose — no headings, no numbered points, no bold text, no tables — the way you would explain it in a long email to a reporter who is out of their depth. And please be careful to separate the parts of this that are settled among people who work on constitutional theory from the parts that are genuinely contested, rather than presenting a contested reading as though it were the consensus.
