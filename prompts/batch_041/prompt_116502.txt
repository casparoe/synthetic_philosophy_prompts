I am a fourth-year PhD student in the philosophy of biology, writing a dissertation on how variance-component estimates from the human behavioral sciences are interpreted once they leave the journals that produce them. I am co-teaching a graduate seminar on quantitative reasoning in the human sciences, and I need a teaching text in dialogue form. Please write it.

The dialogue should be between two researchers who are equally competent and who disagree about what a heritability estimate tells us. The first is a quantitative geneticist who fits twin, family, and genomic models to large cohorts and defends the standard interpretation of her estimates. The second is a developmental scientist who has herself run behavior-genetic designs and has concluded that the parameter is systematically misleading in both scientific and public use. Neither may be a caricature. The critic has to know the algebra and the model assumptions from the inside; the geneticist has to be able to state, without evasion, what her numbers do not establish. They should answer each other's actual formulations rather than positions neither of them holds. Each should concede something real and land at least one point the other cannot fully absorb, and the disagreement should be sharper at the end than at the beginning — not dissolved into agreement, and not resolved by declaring a winner.

Anchor the exchange in a stipulated result, so that it cannot be settled by appeal to any existing literature. Suppose a large, pre-registered study, using a single instrument in a single population, reports that a composite of children's early reading ability has a heritability of roughly 0.7 in the most advantaged third of families and roughly 0.3 in the least advantaged third; that the shared-environment component is correspondingly larger in the disadvantaged third; and that the mean score is lower there. Let the two of them argue about what follows. What may be inferred about individual children? Is it defensible to say that the trait is more genetic in one group than in the other? What does the pattern imply about the prospects for intervention, and about where one should look for environmental causes? Is the difference between the two estimates a fact about the world or a fact about the environments that happened to be sampled? And does any of this bear on whether such estimates should be cited in policy settings at all?

Have them also contest a distinction I have been trying to work with in my own chapters — between a claim about the sources of variation in a trait within a population and a claim about the sources of the trait in an individual — and let them disagree about whether that distinction does real work or merely deflects criticism.

Two constraints on the argument. First, neither character may rest on the familiar disclaimer that high heritability does not imply immutability; whatever they say must go beyond it. Second, the dispute must stay inside the population: neither character should drift into claims about differences between populations. No literature review, no citations, no appeals to named studies or authors — the reasoning has to stand on the stipulated numbers and on general principles alone. Aim for about 1,500 words.
