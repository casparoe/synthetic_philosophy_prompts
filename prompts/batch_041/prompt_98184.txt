Write a satirical internal handbook — a caseworker's manual — issued by a fictional agency, the Directorate of Statutory Continuity of the Republic of Vellavia, in the years following the collapse of an authoritarian regime (the "Karstian Union"). The Directorate must decide, statute by statute, which laws of the old regime survive the transition as valid law and which were void from the outset. Write the manual as the Directorate actually wrote it: numbered sections, definitions, a decision procedure, worked cases, an appeals clause. Around 900 words. Keep it deadpan throughout; no authorial wink, no joke flagged as a joke. A reader who knows nothing of jurisprudence should find it readable, and a reader who knows the Hart–Fuller debate should recognize that whoever wrote it understands the positions and should find the satire fair rather than clumsy.

The humor must come from real features of the debate, not from invented absurdity. In particular, the manual should quietly take a stand on: whether the validity of a statute is one question and its moral quality another, and what the Directorate does with the gap between them; the rule of recognition, and what happens when the old regime's officials and the new Republic's officials disagree about which rules count as the rules of recognition; the open texture of legal language and the difference between a statute's settled core and its contested penumbra; and the several ways a legal system can fail to be a legal system at all (generality, promulgation, prospectivity, clarity, non-contradiction, possibility of compliance, constancy, and congruence between official action and declared rule). Invent the Directorate's own apparatus for operationalizing all this — a named test, a scored checklist, a triage between "core," "penumbra," and "not law at all" — and let the apparatus be the satire: the reader should see the moment where a moral question is dressed up as a form-filling exercise.

Include at least three worked cases. One should be a statute of unobjectionable content that nonetheless fails on formal grounds (e.g., never properly promulgated). One should be an informer case: a private citizen denounced a neighbor under an old statute that the new Republic now regards as gravely unjust, and the Directorate must say whether there was a law to obey at all. One should be a statute so unjust that the manual has to reach for a threshold of intolerable injustice — and should be honest, in its own deadpan way, that the threshold is not derivable from the rest of the manual.

Close with a short author's note (four to six sentences), separate from the manual, stating which side of the debate the piece presupposes, where it takes liberties with the ideas, and which reading it leans on that a specialist might contest. Take a definite position: the satire should land on a side rather than hover above both, and the note should give your reasons for the choices the brief left open.
