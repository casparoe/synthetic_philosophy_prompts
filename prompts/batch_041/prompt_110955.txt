I am a doctoral candidate in formal epistemology, and I am preparing to interview, for a departmental podcast, a decision theorist who has spent much of her career defending the significance of the non-negativity result for the expected value of information — the result, commonly credited to I. J. Good, that an expected-utility maximizer's ex ante expected utility cannot be lowered by the prospect of receiving costless information, since she can always ignore it and act as she would have acted anyway. She uses this result to argue that apparent cases of rational information aversion (patients who decline genetic tests, people who avoid learning their risk of illness, agents who prefer not to learn the outcome of a choice they have already made) are always either cases where the information is costly, or cases where the information itself enters the utility function. Please draft eight to ten interview questions that press her on the assumptions the result rests on rather than inviting her to restate it. I would like the questions to address, at minimum:

- the role of the "you can always ignore it" argument, and whether the real source of the result's non-negativity is the option to condition on the information rather than the option to ignore it;
- what happens when the agent's action set is endogenous to the information — that is, when learning the answer to a question also reveals options that were not previously available;
- whether the result survives when the agent is not a single time-slice but a sequence of selves with potentially divergent preferences, so that the ex ante self cannot guarantee that the informed future self will honor the plan the ex ante self would have chosen;
- the case of belief-dependent utility, where the agent cares not only about the consequences of her action but about what she believes, so that the information is not merely a means to better action;
- whether the result should be read as a theorem about the value of information or merely as a theorem about the value of the option to obtain information;
- what the result implies, if anything, for the rationality of information aversion in cases where the information is costless and belief-independent.

For each question, please include a follow-up keyed to the answer a defender of the result is most likely to give — that is, a follow-up that makes the standard defensive move harder to use. Please write the questions in British English. Close with a single line beginning "Verdict:" naming the one question you think is most likely to force a concession from her.
