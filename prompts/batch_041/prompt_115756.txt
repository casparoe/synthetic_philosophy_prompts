Next month I'm moderating a seventy-five-minute workshop for about a dozen early-career science journalists — most with a physics or biology background, none with any Sanskrit — on Schrödinger's late claim, developed in his philosophical essays under the influence of the Upanishads, that there is only one mind and that the apparent plurality of consciousness is an illusion. I don't want the explanation itself; I want the plan for delivering it.

Begin with what has to be in place before the claim can even be stated. My worry is that the audience will hear a numerical thesis and immediately test it against facts — I can't feel your toothache, we don't share memories, we die separately — without ever asking what principle is supposed to individuate one mind from another, or whether consciousness supplies such a principle at all. Tell me what has to come first, what depends on what, and where the claim must be split before it can be assessed. I take it there are at least three things people run together here — that there is numerically one subject, that experiences occur without an owner, and that all experiences are merely similar in content — and that only the first is really his.

Then the carrying example. I want one case or image to do the whole job, and I want to know what it must have and what it must lack: it cannot trade on shared information or causal connection between minds, and it cannot make the oneness rest on the fact that we are all made of the same atoms or energy, since that is trivially true and yields no unity of subjects. Say why the obvious candidates fail on those constraints, or how they can be repaired.

Give me the misconception map in the order the misconceptions will actually surface. I am expecting quantum mysticism — that his physics motivated the Vedanta, or that entanglement supports it — solipsism, telepathy and shared-memory readings, and the journalists' reflex that any claim of this shape is unfalsifiable and therefore not worth covering. Tell me exactly where in the sequence to head each one off, plus the ones I have not anticipated.

Finish with a check for understanding that can tell whether they have grasped the dependency structure rather than memorised a doctrine — ideally something where they must say which objection the thesis can concede and which it must deny, and why. And tell me what to leave out at this level, and to say so out loud. No reading list; this is about what I say, in what order, and what I refuse.
