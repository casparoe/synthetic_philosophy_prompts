I sit on the small grants panel of a municipal heritage foundation, and our chair — a retired professor of intellectual history, whom I will call R. — has for two decades decided every application by asking which of the three modes of history that Nietzsche distinguishes, the monumental, the antiquarian, and the critical, a proposal embodies. Her standing maxim is that history is worth having only insofar as it serves life, and she treats the three modes as three ways of serving it, or of failing to. I am a junior member, and I have never been able to reconstruct the rule she is actually applying. That matters now, because we vote next month on a proposal I care about.

Her record, as far as I can piece it together from the minutes, with the reason she gave on each occasion:

- A richly illustrated centenary volume on the town's founding families, printed at the foundation's expense and delivered free to every household. Funded. "It gives people something to live up to."
- A complete digitisation and print catalogue of the parish registers, wills, and gravestones, with no interpretive essay and no launch. Funded. "It keeps faith with what was here."
- An exhibition on the town's wartime role in a deportation, closing with a pledge wall and a school programme that every pupil now passes through. Funded. "It turns a wound into resolve."
- A memorial column designed to sink a few centimetres each year, so that within a generation only its tip will show, commemorating the same deportation, with no ceremony and no accompanying programme. Funded. "Memory should have to be carried, not visited."
- A revisionist monograph for an academic press, arguing that the town's founding legends are fabrications, with nothing addressed to the public at all. Declined. "It only takes apart; it leaves nothing to live by."
- The revival of a harvest festival abandoned eighty years ago, reconstructed from the parish records and staged again. Funded. "It gives the town back a rhythm."

The application before us comes from a local historian and a small press. They propose a book and a plain website documenting how the town's founding families built their fortunes on the enclosure of the common land and on a workhouse whose inmates died at a rate the town never acknowledged, and how the town then spent a century congratulating itself on their philanthropy. There is to be no monument, no ceremony, no plaque, no school programme, no pledge, and no recommendation about what the town should do next — only the record, made as public and as hard to ignore as possible. The applicant's covering sentence is: "The town cannot act honestly about its present until it has stopped lying to itself about its past; the clearing-away is the work."

What I want is not so much the vote as the reasoning behind it. What rule or rules fit the chair's record so far? What does each of them predict about this application? Which do you think she is really running, and why? And if her record genuinely leaves the matter open, what kind of proposal — one we might plausibly see in a later cycle — would most cleanly force the question and separate her real rule from the others?
