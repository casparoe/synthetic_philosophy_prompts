I am a doctoral student in political theory, preparing for a workshop at which a philosopher and an anthropologist will both be present, and I want to get my own position on corruption straight before I am made to defend it. What I need from you is not a verdict on whether these claims are true but an audit of whether they can be held together. Here is roughly where I have landed. (1) The most defensible general definition of corruption is the misuse of entrusted power for personal advantage. (2) Whether a given use of power counts as misuse, however, has to be settled by reference to the norms and expectations of the political community whose power it is, since there is no neutral standpoint from which an outsider can declare a practice abusive. (3) The gift — the transfer that creates and sustains a relation of mutual obligation — is a normal and often indispensable element of political life; a gift that is given openly, reciprocated over time, and not tied to any particular decision ought not to be called corruption. (4) Clientelism, the exchange of material benefits for political support, is not corruption either; where parties are weak and formal accountability institutions are captured, it is a rough substitute for representation, since it leaves politicians answerable to clients who can withdraw their support. (5) A colleague insists on distinguishing vertical accountability (a client's power to punish a patron) from horizontal accountability (an auditor's or a court's power to punish an official), and says that clientelism reliably delivers the first while systematically corroding the second; I cannot tell whether this distinction sharpens the problem or merely redescribes it. (6) For all that, some acts are corrupt whatever the local norms say: when an official diverts money earmarked for a clinic into a private account and then conceals the diversion, no appeal to the community's own standards can redeem it. (7) Anti-corruption conditionality attached to international loans is a form of external governance that treats the borrowing country's political order as a problem to be disciplined, and its universalism is not innocent.

I have the nagging sense that I am having it both ways somewhere in this list, but I cannot see where, and I am not willing to abandon any of it on a whim. So please treat the set as the object of inquiry and give me an audit rather than a lecture. Which pairs are genuinely in tension, and how severe is each tension relative to the others? Which apparent conflicts dissolve once the commitments are stated more carefully, and what exactly does the dissolving? For every genuine tension, tell me the cheapest revision that would remove it and what that revision would cost me elsewhere in the set — and then, if a single revision would clean up more than one tension at once, say which one it is and what I would be giving up by making it. Please rank the tensions by severity instead of presenting them all as equally bad, and be explicit about which of my commitments is doing the work that forces the conflict, since I suspect I am treating one of them as though it could be had in both a strong and a weak form at the same time.
