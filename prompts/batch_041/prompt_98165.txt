I write a column on science and ideas, and I'm drafting a piece on why expert panels sometimes endorse a package of claims that no single member holds. I want to build it around a case I've constructed: a five-member board reviewing a new drug, where each member is asked whether the drug is effective, whether it is safe, and whether to approve it, given the board's fixed rule that a drug is approved just when it is both effective and safe. Every member answers consistently — no one calls the drug effective and safe and then votes against approval — yet the majorities line up so that the board as a whole finds the drug effective, finds it safe, and declines to approve it. I'm told this is the discursive dilemma, but I need more than the label. Please explain the conceptual issue: what exactly goes wrong, why it is not dissolved by the fact that each voter is individually consistent, why the two natural repairs — aggregating the premises and then applying the rule, versus aggregating the verdict directly — can come apart and what is genuinely at stake in choosing between them, and whether the trouble is peculiar to majority rule or would persist under a supermajority threshold. Open with the crux in one sentence and keep the whole explanation under 600 words. No citations; I need the argument, not a reading list.
