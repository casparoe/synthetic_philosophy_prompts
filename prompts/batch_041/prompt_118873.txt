Neurath is remembered in this course for two things, the boat and the pictures. Take the pictures. What did Neurath think a picture language was for, and what did he think it could do that ordinary language couldn't? Where did he think its limits were? Here's what the student turned in:

Neurath ran the Social and Economic Museum in Vienna in the 1920s, and the museum existed to get social and economic facts across to ordinary working people who had no training in reading tables and statistics. Out of that came the picture language he called Isotype. The rules were strict. You show a quantity by repeating the same little symbol over and over, never by drawing one symbol bigger, because a symbol drawn twice as tall looks four times as big and lies to the eye. You keep a key so the reader knows what one symbol stands for. You drop perspective and any detail that doesn't carry information, since decoration competes with the message. And a whole team sat between the statistician and the finished chart — the person whose job was translating the data into the picture was called the transformer. So it wasn't illustration, it was a way of making the data legible.

His complaint about ordinary language was that words carry associations and slide around, and that alot of what passed for philosophy was just words dressed up as argument. He wanted something a person could look at and just get, without being told what it meant first, and that's also why the method was meant to travel, the same chart readable in Vienna or London or Chicago. This is where the boat comes in: we're sailors who have to rebuild the ship while we're already out on the water, so we can never start from scratch.

But I think he wanted the picture language to be more than a teaching tool. Since he was after the unity of science, he thought Isotype could eventually become the common language the unified science would be written in — the neutral, unambiguous medium all the verbal disputes of the philosophers could finally be translated into, and in that sense the firm ground the rest of knowledge could rest on. That fits his physicalism too, because the pictures would just record the observable states of affairs, the protocol sentences, and those were the certain starting points that everything else gets built up from.

So for Neurath the pictures and the philosophy are really one project: clean up the language and you've cleaned up the science.
