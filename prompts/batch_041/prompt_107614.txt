I chair a scientific advisory panel that must sign off, roughly monthly, on quantitative models used to support regulatory decisions — tax-incidence forecasts, integrated assessment models, projections of targeted public-health interventions. Panel members are mostly not modellers, we have about two hours per model, and our sign-off is what lets an agency act. Reviewers repeatedly invoke the idea — sometimes labelled the F-twist — that a model's assumptions need not be realistic provided its predictions are good enough; I find this discharges some objections and licenses worse ones. Please write a decision procedure my panel can run assumption by assumption to decide whether an unrealistic assumption is acceptable for the intended use: the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer, when the assumption obliges us to restrict the model's scope rather than license extrapolation, and where the procedure stops and hands the decision to the panel as a value judgment. Build it around a distinction I want to preserve: an assumption can be read as a negligibility assumption (approximately true, the neglected factor is small), as a domain restriction (the model makes no claim where the assumption fails), or as a heuristic device (known false but useful for reaching a testable result) — and these readings license different uses of the same model. Then run it on three cases: a representative-agent assumption in a model used to forecast the distributional effects of a tax; a homogeneous-mixing assumption in a model used to project the effect of an intervention aimed at a small subpopulation; and a forward-looking rational-expectations assumption in a model used to forecast the effects of a novel policy rule. Include at least one case where the procedure rules against an assumption I would prefer to wave through, and mark plainly which steps are technical and which are judgment wearing technical clothes.
