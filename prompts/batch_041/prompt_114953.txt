I am a fourth-year PhD student in philosophy of cognitive science, and I have been asked to circulate a short position statement ahead of a workshop on the metaphysics of the mental. Before I send it round, I want an audit of the package: I suspect that at least two of the things I say cannot be held together, and I would rather find out from you than from the discussion. Here is what I am committed to.

1. Pattern realism, with degrees. A pattern is real to the extent that it earns its keep in prediction, that is, to the extent that it compresses the data better than its rivals do. I take that to be a claim about the world and not merely about our convenience, and I take the mental, the biological and the social to be on the same footing here. I also think reality so understood is graded, so that some patterns are more real than others, rather than all-or-nothing.

2. Three stances, with the physical as the ground floor. Nothing outruns the physical facts; the physical stance is in principle complete. Natural selection, for instance, is a physical process that produces design with no intentions anywhere in the picture.

3. The intentional stance just is the optimality assumption. To treat a system intentionally is to attribute to it the beliefs and desires it ought to have given its situation, and then to predict what it is rational for it to do.

4. Anti-fictionalism about the mental. Belief-desire generalizations are real, testable generalizations; when cognitive science confirms one of them, we have learned something about the world rather than something about our habits of description.

5. Predictive supremacy, for us, of the intentional stance. For creatures like us predicting creatures like us, the intentional stance delivers far more information per unit of cost than the design stance or the physical stance does.

6. The design stance is derivative. In the order of understanding, function is downstream of purpose: to see something as designed to do something is already to see it as aimed at something, and function-talk inherits its normative force from purposes.

What I want is a discrimination, not a list. Which of these genuinely conflict with which, and which pairs only look as though they do? Please rank the real tensions by severity instead of treating them as equally bad, and for each one say what would have to give and what the cheapest repair would cost me elsewhere in the package. If it turns out that one commitment is quietly doing all the work, or that something has to go outright, say so plainly.
