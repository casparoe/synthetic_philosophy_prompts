I write about science and ideas for a general-readership magazine, and for the past two years I have also run a monthly methods workshop: roughly eighteen people, mostly early-career researchers with a scattering of working journalists, ninety minutes, no reading circulated in advance. The next session falls to me, and I want it to be about the problem I keep running into in my own reporting — how anyone gets from a heap of objects to a confident claim about what the people who left them believed. I would much rather the group collide with the difficulty than hear me describe it, so what I need is a runnable activity, not a lecture with a group exercise bolted on.

Let me put the intellectual content on the table, since I don't want the activity to depend on anyone's prior coursework. Three things, which I take to be the real machinery here. First, what I have been calling the frozen-moment assumption: reading an assemblage as though it were a photograph of a single instant, when in fact almost every deposit is a palimpsest laid down over years or centuries, so that objects found together need not have been used together, or by the same people, or for the same purpose. Second, formation processes: what survives is filtered twice, once by natural decay and once by deliberate human action — clearing, reuse, curation, discard, scavenging — so the record is a biased sample of what once existed, and the bias is structured rather than random. Third, and this is the one I care about most, the asymmetry between doing and believing: an object can testify to what someone did, but belief leaves no distinctive trace of its own, only material consequences that are equally consistent with other causes. I want participants to discover the third point themselves, and to discover that the first two make it worse rather than better.

Please design the activity around these, subject to the following. The participants should not need any archaeological training, so keep the objects mundane and the sites invented; nothing should turn on recognizing a real culture, period, or artifact type. The activity must be arranged so the group runs into the problem through its own choices — the rules, materials, roles, or scoring should do the work, not my closing remarks. And nothing should turn on my supplying a "correct" answer, because there isn't one.

I want the deliverable to include: a materials list that is cheap and printable, with the actual content of the object and context sheets sketched out rather than gestured at; a role structure and a scoring scheme that creates real stakes without money; a timeline broken down to the minute across the ninety minutes; and a debrief that maps specific moments from the run onto each of the three ideas above, including the moments where the group will feel most confident and least entitled to be. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle it when it does — I would rather hear about the failure modes now than discover them in front of eighteen people. Finally, be honest about which of the three ideas the activity cannot reproduce in a room and which therefore has to be supplied by me, in words, after the fact.
