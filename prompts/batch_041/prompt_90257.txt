In 1999 the British Post Office began rolling out an accounting system called Horizon, built by Fujitsu, to the subpostmasters who ran its local branches — small, often rural, owner-operated post offices. Over the next sixteen years the system repeatedly reported shortfalls that the subpostmasters could neither explain nor reproduce. Hundreds were prosecuted for theft, fraud and false accounting, most of them by the Post Office itself, which acted as a private prosecutor and therefore as investigator, complaining victim and prosecutor at once. More than nine hundred were convicted, over two hundred were imprisoned, and many more lost their businesses, homes and savings by paying sums they did not owe. Throughout, the Post Office insisted that Horizon was reliable and, in criminal and civil proceedings, did not disclose what it knew about the system's faults. Only after a group action brought by 555 subpostmasters did a High Court judge rule, in 2019, that Horizon contained bugs, errors and defects. Convictions began to be quashed the following year, and in 2024 Parliament passed a statute overturning the remaining convictions in England, Wales and Northern Ireland. Treat the facts as I have set them out and work from those.

Analyse this episode through the theory of epistemic injustice, and then turn the analysis around and ask what the episode does to the theory.

Begin with the mapping, and be disciplined about it. In Fricker's terms, who is the speaker, who is the hearer, and what precisely is the credibility judgment being made? Is the wrong here testimonial injustice in Fricker's strict sense — which requires identity prejudice on the hearer's part — or is it better described as hermeneutical injustice, as Kristie Dotson's testimonial smothering or contributory injustice, or as Gaile Pohlhaus's willful hermeneutical ignorance? Note that a hearer who already possesses the relevant interpretive resource and declines to take it up is a different case from a speaker who lacks the resource altogether; say which you take the subpostmasters to be, and why. Consider also the peculiar role of the machine. Much of the credibility in this story was extended not to a person but to a system's output, and one of the parties doing the believing was an institution rather than a face-to-face interlocutor, with the criminal courts as a further hearer downstream. Does that fit the framework, or does it require something the framework lacks? Where the episode is ambiguous between two of the categories, say so rather than choosing for the sake of tidiness.

Then reverse the direction. What does this episode expose about the theory's limits or blind spots — about identity prejudice as the trigger condition, about remedies framed as the individual virtues of hearers rather than as institutional or legal repairs, and about what counts as epistemic reparation when the remedy arrives as an Act of Parliament rather than as an acknowledgment by the wrongdoer? Be explicit about what the theory cannot say here rather than stretching it to cover the case.

Close with a verdict: is the Horizon case an instance of epistemic injustice as the literature understands it, an extension the literature ought to absorb, or a case that reveals the theory to have been built on a narrower class of wrongs than it claims?
