I have been arguing with a friend about where a person is and what makes them the same person over time, beginning from that thought experiment — Daniel Dennett's, I believe — in which a brain is removed and kept alive in a vat while the person's body goes on walking and talking elsewhere. Her verdicts on the cases we have worked through seem to me consistent: the person whose brain sits in the vat is in the vat, and the body is a puppet on a radio link; a whole brain moved into a body grown from the same person's cells is that person, no doubt about it; an exact duplicate made while the original is still alive is a stranger, and the duplicate's survival would be no comfort to the original; a body driven by a computer that reproduces the person's memories and mannerisms after the brain has been destroyed is not the person but a recording of them; and someone who loses all her memories and her entire personality after a head injury, with brain and body intact, is still the same person, only badly damaged. Now she must rule on a case I have put to her: over two years her brain is rebuilt one neuron at a time, each neuron replaced by a silicon part doing exactly what it did, with no interruption to her consciousness, until nothing biological remains — and she is awake, lucid, and remembers the whole process. What will she say? I care less about the verdict than about the rule she is running, whether it is the only rule that fits everything she has said, the other rule that fits her record just as well, what each predicts here, which you would bet on and why, and the single further case I should put to her that would separate them.
