A friend and I have been going around in circles about Galen Strawson's basic argument against moral responsibility, and I've decided I'd rather see it than keep arguing about it, so: write me a short story. Here's the version of the arguement we keep chewing on, so you don't need to go dig up the original — (i) you do what you do, in any situation, because of the way you are; (ii) to be ultimately responsible for what you do, you'd have to be ultimately responsible for the way you are, at least in certain crucial mental respects; (iii) but you can't be ultimately responsible for the way you are, since you didn't pick your genes, your upbringing, the language and the century you landed in; (iv) so you're never ultimately responsible for what you do. The obvious escape hatch doesn't help either: sure, maybe you can change the way you are by an act of will, but that act of will comes out of a self you didn't author either, so the regress just backs up one step and then stops nowhere. What I want is a story that lives inside a world that has actually taken step (iv) on board, instead of one that just talks about it.

Setting seed, bend it however you need: a mid-sized coastal town that, over about two generations, has rebuilt its institutions around the conclusion. There's no court that assigns desert anymore — the building where the courthouse was now houses something called the Office of Continuation, which quarantines and repairs rather than punishes, and whose staff talk about forward-looking measures the way we talk about the weather. Schoolkids learn a little civic rhyme about how nobody built their own self. There are resentment counsellors, whose entire practice is helping people put down grudges, including grudges against themselves, and a town holiday whose mood nobody can quite explain. Your protagonist should be someone with an old wound: a woman whose brother was killed by a drunk driver, say, or the last judge who used to hand down sentences before the reform and now does intake at the Office. Give her a scene with someone — a neighbour, a son, a stranger at a bus stop — who insists, plainly and with no philosophy at all, that she should still be blamed, or that he should still blame himself, and let the town's entire vocabulary fail in the face of it.

Around 1500 words, third person, close on her. No lectures and no essayistic narrator explaining the argument: a reader who's never heard of Strawson should feel the weight of it through what people do and say, and a reader who has should recognise each move — the regress, the demand that you'd have to be the cause of yourself, the gap between the argument being airtight and blame surviving anyway. Don't cheat by smuggling in a contra-causal will or a soul, and don't let anyone win the argument in the last paragraph; I want to close the file unsure whether the town is an advance or a loss. One quick thing at the top if you don't mind: I have a vague sense that Strawson himself doesn't think the argument gives us any way to simply stop blaming and resenting each other — tell me in a sentence if I'm mangling him, then get on with the story.
