For some years I have been caught in the same unresolved argument, in my department and, so far as I can tell, in the literature: what, if anything, Gödel's incompleteness theorems show about minds. The anti-mechanist line (the one filed under Lucas and Penrose) is that for any formal system whose consistency I accept, I can see the truth of its Gödel sentence, which the system itself cannot prove, and so I am not that system. The mechanist reply is that this requires me to be consistent and to know that I am, which is at best a fallible empirical claim about me, and that the theorem only excludes a fixed system, whereas a mind might be modelled as an open-ended sequence of systems, each extending the last with fresh reflection principles. Everyone knows all of this; nobody moves.

My hypothesis, offered as a hypothesis, is that this is not a disagreement about the theorems but about idealisation and about what seeing and knowing are doing in the argument. The anti-mechanist needs an idealised reasoner: consistent, and whose knowledge is closed under the relevant inferences. The mechanist's best model of mathematical practice is a finite, fallible, self-extending one, and where the anti-mechanist sees a mind grasping the truth of a Gödel sentence, the mechanist sees a new principle being adopted. On that reading the two sides are answering different questions under one heading.

So I want a diagnosis, not a verdict. Is this verbal, a hidden empirical claim about human consistency, two questions under one name, or a disagreement about whether that idealisation is legitimate in a claim about human minds at all? Does it matter that the argument seems able to conclude only something about mathematics, namely that there are arithmetic propositions no one could ever decide, rather than anything about minds? And given your diagnosis, what would count as progress here: a formal result, a distinction, an empirical question, or nothing at all? Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give the reasoning; no preamble.
