You are a philosopher of history with expertise in historiography and the philosophy of the human sciences, and I need you to draft one essay question for the final exam of an upper-level seminar on historical interpretation. Assume the exam is 90 minutes, closed-book, and that neither you nor the students can look anything up: the question and answer must stand on argument alone, with no reliance on names, dates, titles, or citations.

The question must not be answerable by reciting the familiar positions on presentism — that the past should be understood on its own terms, that all history is written from the present, that anachronism is a professional sin. Build it instead around the following crux, which I want candidates to have to confront explicitly: escaping the standards of one's own time is not one question but at least four, namely (i) which topics and questions are worth asking, (ii) how to describe past agents' beliefs, categories, and reasons, (iii) how to evaluate past conduct, and (iv) what counts as evidence and good explanation. The argument that presentism is inescapable is strong for (i) and (iv) — and note that (iv) is a case where we should not want to escape our standards, since a historicism that required adopting past actors' evidential standards would make history impossible — but it is routinely slid across to (ii) and (iii), where it does not follow. The question should make that slide visible and force a candidate to say which sense of presentism is doing the work in a dispute.

Write the question so that it presents a short concrete scenario: a historian describes some past practice in evaluative terms, a reviewer charges presentism, and the historian replies that all history is written from the present. The candidate must adjudicate that dispute and say what a defensible position looks like, including what it costs.

Deliver three things. First, the question as it would appear on the exam paper. Second, a model answer of roughly 700 words. Third, a marking scheme worth 25 marks, distributed over five must-hit points, with a one-line note for each on how candidates typically lose that mark, and a closing note on what separates an A answer from a B answer. Before you draft anything, think the crux through and list the plausible student errors you expect, then write in a formal register.
