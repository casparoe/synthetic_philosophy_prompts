For a philosophy symposium at my high school I have been asked to imagine a thinker from the past confronting a controversy that arose only after his death, and to defend the reconstruction rather than simply invent it. I have drawn John Stuart Mill, who died in 1873, and the topic of climate policy. I would like you to produce the reconstruction, in a disciplined way: every predicted verdict should be tied to an argument he actually made, and you should mark clearly where you are extrapolating rather than reporting.

The questions I want his positions on are these.

First, the choice of instrument. He is shown a carbon fee that starts at a posted rate per ton and rises automatically each year, with all the revenue returned to households as an equal per-person dividend, together with a tariff on imports from countries that do not tax carbon. Alongside it stand two rivals: a hard cap on total emissions with tradable permits, and a system of personal carbon allowances in which each citizen receives a declining annual ration that may be bought and sold. Would he back the fee, and would he care whether the permits under the alternative were auctioned to polluters or handed to them for free? I have the impression that he worried about owners pocketing gains in land value that they did nothing to produce, and I want to know whether that instinct transfers to the scarcity value of the remaining carbon budget, or whether I am stretching the analogy.

Second, degrowth. Mill is known for welcoming the prospect of an economy in which capital and population stop growing, for thinking that an endless scramble to get ahead is a poor ideal of life, and for believing that unending growth would spoil the natural world. Would that make him a supporter of a deliberate modern programme of shrinking throughput to stay within ecological limits? My hesitation is that he also seems to have thought more production was still the priority for countries whose inhabitants were poor, which suggests he might endorse degrowth for rich countries and growth for poor ones. Is that division his, or mine?

Third, the harm principle and vanishing contributions. On his account, coercion is justified only to prevent harm to others. An individual's emissions cause no identifiable harm to anyone in particular, and most of the damage falls on strangers and on people not yet born. Would he classify that as harm at all? And if he would not, what would he do about climate change, given that he also allowed people to be compelled to shoulder their share of public burdens such as defence and jury service? I would like to know whether he has a principled route from the harm principle to climate regulation, or whether he has to add something to it.

Fourth, the dividend and the meaning of work. If the fee is returned as an unconditional payment to every person, and if automation is at the same time removing many jobs, does his worry about a life organised around commercial striving argue for the dividend, as a release from drudgery, or against it, as a subsidy for idleness? I cannot tell which way his own premises point, and I suspect he could not either.

For each question, give me the verdict you predict, the argument of his that you are leaning on (describe the argument itself; do not bother with editions, page numbers, or a reading list, since I have no way to check them), how confident you are, and what would have to be true for the opposite verdict to be the better prediction. If two of his commitments pull in opposite directions on one of these questions, say so instead of forcing a single answer. Finish by ranking the four predictions from most to least secure, and name the one a specialist on him would be most likely to say you got wrong, with your reason.
