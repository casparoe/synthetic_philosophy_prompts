I edit a series of country-culture guides -- the kind companies hand to staff who are about to move abroad for a year. Half my authors think the whole idea of national character is bunk and half think you can't write the book without it, and meanwhile every manuscript is stuffed with sentences like 'in Japan the group comes before the individual, so a junior colleague won't contradict the boss in a meeting,' 'Brazilians are warm and physical, expect hugs,' and 'Nordic people are reserved with strangers but obsessively egalitarian.' At the moment it's vibes: whoever's drafting decides, and whatever survives the copyedit goes to print.

I want a procedure my editors can run on a sentence before it goes to print: the questions to ask in order, what counts as a good enough answer to each, where the procedure stops with a no, and where it hands the call to a person. My own instinct is that the line to draw is between claims about what people do and claims about what they're like inside -- behaviour allowed, character not -- and I'd like it built around that, but I don't trust it and I'd rather you tell me where it breaks. It also has to cope with the sentence that's true as a statistic about a country and still a rotten sentence to print, and with the sentence that quietly turns into a claim about the individual sitting across from you. Keep in mind it has to be usable in a couple of minutes by someone with no social science training, and that anything contested goes to our editorial director, who will ask me why.

Then run it on all three sentences above, including one where it tells me to cut something I like, and finish by saying what the procedure can't do -- I'd rather have a procedure with a known hole in it then one that pretends.
