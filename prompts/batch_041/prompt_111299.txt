Since retiring from a career in experimental condensed-matter physics I have spent several years reading in the philosophy of science, and I have agreed to lead a single ninety-minute session on reduction and emergence for a reading group. The group is about a dozen people — mostly graduate students in philosophy and in the history of science, with two colleagues from a biology department. They have the general scientific literacy of an educated adult, no physics beyond school, and they arrive already fluent in the word "emergence" as a term of praise and already suspicious that the whole debate is a verbal one. I do not want you to explain reduction and emergence to me; I want a plan for how I should explain it to them.

Give me the order in which the ideas must come and the reason for that order — what has to be in place before what, and which of the distinctions I care about cannot be drawn until an earlier one is secure. The distinctions I want them to leave with are these: the reduction of one theory to another, the reduction of a phenomenon to its parts or mechanisms, and the ontological claim that there is nothing but the parts; and, on the other side, the difference between a property's being unpredictable from the micro-description in practice and its being inexplicable from it in principle. I want them to see why the first kind of emergence is uncontroversial and the second is where the fighting is, and why a property can be "nothing over and above" its base and still not be derivable from it in any usable sense.

I intend to build the session around the glass transition as the carrying example: a liquid cooled below its melting point without crystallising grows steadily more viscous, its relaxation time lengthens by many orders of magnitude over a modest temperature range, and it becomes rigid — yet with no change of symmetry and no long-range order, and with the temperature of the change depending on how fast one cools. Whether an underlying thermodynamic transition exists or only a kinetic slowing is unresolved. I can talk about this system for an hour without notes. Tell me what the example must have and what it must not have if it is to carry the distinction rather than smother it, and tell me whether the obvious candidate — temperature and the kinetic theory of gases — would serve better or worse as the spine, and why.

Then the misconceptions. I expect them to equate reduction with explanation, to treat emergence as mere practical intractability, to assume that supervenience settles the question, and to suppose that strong emergence is either a truism or a refuge for vitalism. Tell me which of these the carrying example itself will generate or cure, which they bring with them, and exactly where in the sequence I should head each one off. I have my own bias — as an experimentalist I suspect strong emergence is mostly an honest name for our ignorance — so advise me on where, if anywhere, to declare it.

Finish with the omissions: what I should refuse to cover at this level and say that I am refusing. And give me one question to put to the room at the end that would show whether they have grasped the distinction or merely memorised the positions.
