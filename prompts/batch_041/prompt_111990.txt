I've been reading a fair bit of that psych literature on 'tight' and 'loose' cultures — the stuff where some societies have strong norms and a low tolerance for deviance and others are the opposite — and I keep feeling that when people argue about how much rule-following a society should demand, they slide between about four different things without noticing. Can you lay out the conceptual issue properly? What I want seperated is: how strong the norm itself is (how much deviation it actually prohibits), how severe the sanction is, how likely the sanction is to actually land, and how many domains of life the norms cover at all. It seems to me you can have a society that's ferociously strict about one narrow thing and completely indifferent to everything else, or one with mild norms that are enforced with total reliability, and calling both of them 'tight' hides the thing that actually matters. I'd also like to know which of those four dimensions the empirical measures are really tracking, and where the slippage does the most damage.

Then I'd like you to take a view on the normative question, because I think the four-way distinction changes the answer. Here's the argument I keep half-forming and cant finish: the benefits of tightness are immediate and legible — you can watch coordination working, you can see the mess when it breaks down — whereas the benefits of looseness are diffuse and delayed, showing up as innovations and adaptations that nobody can trace back to the person who was allowed to be weird in 1970. If that's right, then even a society reasoning perfectly well will systematically over-tighten, because the feedback for tightening arrives fast and the feedback for loosening arrives slow and unattributed. Does that argument actually work? And does it survive the obvious reply, which is that some costs of looseness are also slow and diffuse — erosion of trust, inability to coordinate on anything, commons problems — so the asymmetry isn't obviously pointing in one direction? I want to know whether the asymmetry claim is doing real work or whether it collapses into 'people notice visible things more than invisible ones', which isn't about tightness at all.

One more thing I'd like you to address, if it belongs here: whether 'demanding rule-following' is even the right unit of analysis. My suspicion is that what people actually mind isn't the number of rules but whether the rules are legible to them — whether you can know in advance what's required — so a society with many strict but knowable rules might be less oppressive than one with few vague ones applied unpredictably. Is that a real distinction or am I just redescribing the same thing in nicer words?

Please be explicit about how confident you are in each claim you make, and single out the one point you're least sure of.
