I'm in a course on early modern philosophy and a friend in my study group has been trying to write out the cogito as an actual argument, and it keeps ending somewhere neither of us wants to go. His version goes like this. (1) While I'm thinking, I can't be wrong that I exist — the act of doubting it proves the doubter. (2) So whenever thinking is going on, something exists, namely the I that thinks. (3) I can doubt that my body exists, demon scenario and all, without doubting that I, the thinker, exist. (4) If I can doubt that A exists while I can't doubt that B exists, then A and B are not the same thing; if they were one thing, a doubt couldn't land on one and sail past the other. (5) So the thinking I is not a body, which is exactly what Descartes wanted. Then he just runs (4) again: (6) I can doubt that the I which was thinking yesterday exists — my memories could all be implants, I might have popped into existence five minutes ago complete with a past — while I can't doubt that the I thinking right now exists. (7) So the I that thinks now is not the same thing as the I that thought yesterday. (8) So there's no persisting self at all, only a string of momentary thinkers, and the very step that buys the mind/body split also buys the no-self view. His line is that you can't keep the first and quietly drop the second, and I can't see where he's cheating. (I tried the obvious objection — this is just the Superman/Clark Kent thing, doubting is opaque — and he says no, because there the doubt is about which properties the thing has, whereas here it's about whether the thing exists at all, and existence isn't a property you can fail to spot, so a doubt about existence can't be a mere fact about me.)

(1) is the one thing in the pile I'd defend to the death; it's the only claim the demon can't seem to touch. So which step do I actually give up, and what does giving it up cost? Is it (2) — does thinking even need a thinker, or does the bare fact of thinking already say everything the cogito licenses? Is it (4) — is a doubt-based identity test just broken, and if so what precisely is the mistake, given that he's already answered the obvious version of that charge? Or is (4) fine and the real trouble is (6), so I get to keep the mind/body conclusion but have to live with (8)? I dont want a survey of everything that's wobbly; I want the single move to make, why the bad step looked so innocent, and an honest bill. If I drop that step, what does the mind/body conclusion have left to stand on — and if I keep it and swallow (8), what else falls with it: blame, the afterlife, the I that was supposed to be the subject of all these thoughts in the first place?
