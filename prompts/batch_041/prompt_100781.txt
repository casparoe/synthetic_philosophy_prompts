A grad-student friend of mine tried to explain "the problem of old evidence" to me, and I want to know how much of it to believe. Here's the gist of what he said:

"Bayesian confirmation theory says evidence confirms a hypothesis when the hypothesis makes that evidence more probable — you conditionalize on the evidence and your credence in the hypothesis goes up. The problem of old evidence is that our best evidence is usually stuff we already knew long before the theory showed up. The classic case is general relativity and the weird precession of Mercury's perihelion: astronomers had been scratching their heads over that anomaly for decades before Einstein. But because we already knew about it, its probability is 1, and conditionalizing on something that already has probability 1 leaves your credence in the hypothesis exactly where it was. So a strict Bayesian is stuck saying the perihelion data did nothing to confirm general relativity — which is nuts, since that anomaly was one of the main reasons phisicists took the theory seriously. That's why old evidence is a fatal counterexample to Bayesian confirmation theory. Bayesians try to wriggle out by saying you should use the probability you gave the evidence back before you learned it, but that's no good: you can't remember your old credences, and even if you could, then everything you already know would be confirming every theory you'll ever cook up, which makes confirmation trivial. A couple of them even claim it isn't really a problem about old evidence at all but about new theories, which sounds to me like pure damage control."

I have a hunch he's overselling, but I don't know the area well enough to locate it. Can you go through this claim by claim and tell me what a Bayesian philosopher of science would nod along to, what's a fair worry stated way too strongly, and what's just mistaken? In particular: is the real problem that the evidence is old, or is it something else about it? Is his "you can't remember your old credences" aimed at the fix that's actually being proposed, or at a strawman? Does "Bayesian confirmation theory is dead" follow from whichever parts of the summary are accurate? And is there a genuine distinction between an "old evidence" problem and a "new theories" problem, or is that a dodge? I'd also like to know what a Bayesian would say actually did the confirming when the perihelion data bore on general relativity, if not plain conditionalization.
