I sit on the formation council of a small contemplative religious community, and on Friday we vote on whether to admit a twenty-four-year-old applicant to the year of candidacy that normally ends in a lifetime vow. She argues — well — from premises she admits she cannot have: she says no one can know what this life is like, or what it would make of her, without entering it, and that she cannot enter it experimentally without our already shaping the judgment that will decide. Our rule requires that a vocation be chosen freely and knowingly; our own record is that about a third leave before final vows; and her mother, who left us years ago, writes that formation is a slow capture rather than a choice. This looks to me like the structure philosophers call a transformative decision — one whose value can be learned only by making it, and whose maker may not be the person who later evaluates it.

Please draft the memo I will circulate before the vote. Recommend a concrete decision — admit, admit with named safeguards, or defer — with a time frame and the reasons for it, and set out the strongest case for the outcome you reject as its defenders would put it. Do not settle the matter by citing our rule or by treating autonomy as a trump. What I need from the memo is a defensible account of what could count as consent to a decision whose outcome cannot be evaluated in advance.
