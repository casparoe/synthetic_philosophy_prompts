I teach a graduate seminar on the epistemology of mathematics, and I want to use a dispute that arose at a recent workshop between two colleagues of mine. I have set the exchange out below as faithfully as I can, in numbered moves. I would like you to adjudicate it: not to declare a winner in the abstract, but to assess each move in turn, say what each party gets right and wrong, identify what both are missing, and locate the real crux of their disagreement.

The topic is Benacerraf's dilemma. Colleague A defends the view that the dilemma is genuine and unresolved; Colleague B holds that it is a false dilemma resting on a defective theory of knowledge. Their exchange ran as follows.

(1) A: Mathematical statements have objective truth values, and those values are fixed by abstract objects — numbers, sets — that occupy no place in space or time and enter into no causal relations. But if knowing that p requires some causal or counterfactual connection between the knower and the fact that p, then we cannot know anything about such objects. So realism about mathematical truth and a causal theory of knowledge cannot both be true; and since we plainly do have mathematical knowledge, one of the two must give.

(2) B: What you have is not a dilemma but a proof that the causal requirement is false. We claim knowledge of the distant past, of other minds, and of electrons, none of which we have any causal contact with — the past is gone, other minds are sealed, electrons are too small to touch. In each case we reach the item by inference to the best explanation. So the causal theory is the weak link; drop it and the dilemma dissolves.

(3) A: The comparison fails, and it fails exactly where it matters. In the past, other-minds, and electron cases, the unobservable item is posited because it causally explains something we do observe — traces, behaviour, tracks in a cloud chamber. The explanatory work is causal all the way down. Abstract objects, by your own account, cause nothing. So inference to the best explanation delivers electrons but not numbers; the pattern does not transfer.

(4) B: You are assuming that explanation must be causal. It need not be. Mathematics earns its place by rendering our best empirical theories simpler, more unifying, and more predictively powerful; that is explanatory even if numbers push nothing around. So the inferential route is open after all, and it is the same route we already use elsewhere.

(5) A: Then the route is too permissive. If making a theory simpler and more unified licenses belief in numbers, it equally licenses belief in whatever else simplifies and unifies — sets, or possibilia, or properties, or one cosmic mind. With no causal anchor you have no principled way to say which of several mutually incompatible abstract ontologies is the true one. You have traded the problem of contact for the problem of selection.

(6) B: Selection is a general problem, not a mathematical one; we face underdetermination everywhere and settle it with the same theoretical virtues. And your move proves too much: if it worked, it would show we cannot know anything about the unobservable world at all. If that is the cost of your anchor, so much the worse for the anchor.

(7) A: It does not prove too much, because in the unobservable-but-causal cases the anchor does the selecting: we believe in the entities the causal explanation requires and no others. Numbers are supposed to be required for truth, not for causal explanation; that is precisely why the anchor will not stretch to cover them.

Please go through moves (1)–(7) in order. For each, say whether it is clearly mistaken, defensible, or clearly right, and give a short justification — including, where a move is defensible but incomplete, what would be needed to complete it. Then state where the two of them are merely talking past each other and where they have a real disagreement; say what the real crux is; and say what, if anything, both are missing. Finally, say who has the stronger overall position as the exchange stands.

Be explicit about your own confidence: tell me which argument or consideration would change your verdict, and identify the point in your reasoning that is most likely to fail.

End your response with a final line of the form Verdict: <short phrase>.
