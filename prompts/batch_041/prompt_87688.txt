Two colleagues of mine, both of whom work on the foundations of quantum mechanics, have fallen into a dispute about a proposed model for a Bell-type experiment, and I should like an adjudication of their exchange, move by move, rather than a general account of Bell's theorem.

The model they are discussing is this. A source emits pairs of particles. Each particle carries a finite ledger: for every possible setting on its own wing, the ledger specifies the outcome that particle would produce. The pair of ledgers is drawn from a joint distribution fixed at the source. That distribution is stipulated to depend on the pair of settings that will subsequently be chosen on the two wings, but it does so symmetrically and without permitting either wing to gain any information about the other wing's setting. The model reproduces the quantum-mechanical correlations exactly. There is no influence propagating from one wing to the other, and no possibility of signalling.

Colleague A maintains that the model is local. Each particle's outcome, he says, depends only on its own ledger and its own setting; the source simply supplies a correlated pair of ledgers, and correlation is not causation. Colleague B maintains that the model is not local. The distribution of ledgers depends on both settings, she says, so the source's state is not independent of the distant setting, and that is precisely what the locality condition in Bell's theorem forbids. A responds that 'local' ought to mean 'no superluminal signalling', and the model does not permit signalling, so it is local. B replies that no-signalling is a consequence of locality, not its definition, and that a model can be no-signalling yet nonlocal. A then objects that if locality is defined as the condition that the joint outcome distribution factorises given the hidden state, Bell's theorem becomes a tautology: one has simply defined 'local' as the class of models that cannot reproduce the correlations, and then announced that non-factorisable models reproduce them. B answers that factorisability is not a definition but a substantive physical assumption, and that a model violating it is a genuine discovery about the world.

I should like you to adjudicate each of these six moves in turn. For each, say whether it is clearly right, clearly mistaken, or partly right, and justify your rating briefly. Is A correct that the model is local? Is B correct that it is not? Is A's claim about the relationship between no-signalling and locality sound? Is A's tautology charge fair, or does it misconstrue the role of factorisability? What, if anything, is each party missing? Finally, if the dispute turns on a choice of definition, say what hangs on each choice and whether there is a fact of the matter. Please write your adjudication in British English.
