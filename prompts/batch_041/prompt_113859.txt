I'm a freelance science journalist — I do a long-ish ideas column for a weekend magazine, mostly philosophy of mind and AI, and I've fallen down a rabbit hole reading Turing on learning machines. What's grabbed me isn't the version everyone trots out. It's the tension between his two routes to a mind: you can let evolution do it, or you can educate — and education is faster, except it needs a teacher who already has the mind you're trying to produce, so the quick route looks like it presupposes the very thing it's meant to explain. Then there's the smaller snag underneath, which is that he talks about training a machine with rewards and punishments, but a machine doesn't mind anything, so reward can only mean something like whatever makes that response more likely next time — and once you've said that, I can't see what's left of the difference between educating a machine and just breeding it very fast. Same with the blank notebook: a blank notebook is never blank, it's already ruled, it has margins. The same thought keeps occuring to me about the sense organs too — you can't teach English to something that can't see or hear, so the syllabus seems to include building the body first. Anyway.

For the closer I'd like a poem. Two numbered sections, 14-16 lines each, 32 lines maximum. Section I should be in strict rhyming couplets with a fairly tight metre: the teacher's voice, drilling the child machine, patient, a bit schoolmasterly, confident the syllabus is known — and let the rhymes start straining before the break, as if the form itself were getting impatient. Section II breaks into loose, unrhymed, longer lines; same person, or the machine, or a voice you can't place, I don't mind, but the metre should go slack as the confidence goes. The last line of II should be the last line of I with one word changed, and that single substitution should carry the poem's whole argument, so pick it carefully. Please don't name Turing, don't lift his phrasing or put anything in quotation marks, and skip the technical jargon — I want someone who's read his child-machine material to feel the ideas land, and everyone else to just read a poem about teaching something that may not be able to mind.

Things I'd like felt in it rather than spelled out: that education and evolution might be one trick at two speeds; that you can't hand out rewards to something that wants nothing; that a decent education probably means giving the machine a body with senses first; and that the teacher here has never done it before either, so the pupil is being taught by someone who is guessing. Don't resolve it — I'd rather it end still unsure whether the thing in front of it is a pupil or a very fast breed of animal. And it has to work as a poem, not a versified essay with a formal gimmick bolted on.
