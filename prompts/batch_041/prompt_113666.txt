After my father was declared dead in a neuro-ICU last spring — the neurologist said "brain death," the transplant coordinator said "circulatory death," and my mother and I spent the week discovering that the two of us meant different things by the same word — I volunteered to run a session for our hospital's ethics consultation group, which is about twenty people: intensivists, two chaplains, a couple of nurses, and three people from the philosophy department. I want the session built around a decision tree that sorts participants by the view they actually hold, not the view they think they hold, because I have now watched a room full of thoughtful people agree on the words "he's dead" and disagree about everything underneath.

Please write that tree. Each internal node should be a concrete case, stated in a sentence or two, that a participant can answer without knowing which position it is going to land them in — no node should simply ask "do you accept the whole-brain criterion?" or "is death a biological fact or a social construction?" The leaves should be named positions, each with one sentence describing its most awkward commitment, and I want at least these to show up somewhere in the tree: the whole-brain criterion, the brainstem criterion as it is used in the UK, the higher-brain or cortical criterion, the circulatory or cardiopulmonary criterion as it operates in donation after circulatory death, the integration or "organism as a whole" view associated with Bernat, the position that brain death is not death because the organism is still integrated by other means, the view that the death of the organism and the death of the person can come apart, the view that death is a legal or moral status we assign rather than a fact we discover, the view that the dead-donor rule should simply be abandoned and the question of death is a distraction, and the view that death is a process with no determinate moment. I realize that last list is ten items and some of them may collapse into the same leaf — if so, say which and why rather than forcing them apart.

Build the tree from the logical space rather than from the names: start from the commitments a participant can actually avow, let the questions fall out of those commitments, and only then attach the labels. Where a branch leads to a combination nobody occupies, keep the branch and diagnose it — is it empty because the view is incoherent, because it is coherent but repulsive, or because it is merely unexplored? I especially want the tree to handle three things I kept tripping over during that week: whether "irreversible" is a fact about the body or a fact about what we are willing to do (the DCD patient's heart could in principle be restarted; the hands-off interval is a protocol, not a law of nature), whether a patient with a destroyed cortex and an intact brainstem counts as dead, and whether the ventilator that keeps a brain-dead patient's body warm is doing the integrating work that used to make the body one organism. A node that turns on any of those three should be in the tree.

Please also tell me, in a short section after the tree, which pairs of leaves the tree genuinely cannot separate — the brainstem and whole-brain criteria are the pair I expect to be a problem, but I want your honest list — and which single node you would drop if I needed the tree to fit on one side of a page. Finish with a facilitator's note of no more than a paragraph on where I should expect the discussion to get stuck, given that half the room will be clinicians who use these criteria professionally and half will be philosophers who do not.
