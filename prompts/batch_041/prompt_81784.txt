I'm a novelist — second book, about a small sect that starts in a mill town and eats itself over about forty years — and I've somehow agreed to run a 90-minute session at the writers' residency I'm at in July. Fourteen of us, all adults, mostly fiction, two poets, one guy who writes naval history and will be skeptical of anything that smells like a workshop game. What I want is an activity where they feel in their own hands that religious ideas catch on the way they do largely because of how ordinary memory works, not because they're true and not because someone's forcing them. I want them to catch themselves doing it, and I'd rather they never hear a single piece of jargon until the debrief.

Here's my rough idea, please build it out properly, and tell me where it's wrong.

The engine is a retelling chain. Three chains of four or five, seated so each person only ever hears the previous person. I need you to write the seed text: 250-300 words, and it has to contain a spread of items — some ordinary but concrete (a name, a specific object, a number), some that break exactly one thing about how a thing is supposed to work (a man in two rooms at once, a statue that hears you but can't move, a river that remembers), a couple that break three or four things at once (invisible, talking, immortal, judgmental, and also it flies), and one or two things that are flatly mundane but phrased oddly. Don't label any of them, don't tell the participants which is which, and please don't make the weird ones the most vivid in the prose — I want the survival differences to come out of the concepts, not out of my adjectives.

Before we start: everyone privately writes down which items they think will survive to the last person and which will die, and I collect the sheets. Small prize for whoever gets it most right, no money involved. This is the part that makes them care about the outcome.

Rules: person one reads the seed for two minutes, then it's gone. Person one tells person two, no notes, ninety seconds. Down the line, each telling capped the same. The last person writes their version down and reads it aloud. While it's running I'm walking the room with my item list, ticking off what's still there at each link, so at the end I've got a survival table on the flipchart and their prediction sheets to compare it against.

One wrinkle I want your help with: attribution. My hunch is that the same story survives differently depending on who it's said to be by, so I'd like a version where half the chains get a seed presented as a translation of a fragment someone found in a house clearance, and half get it presented as my own draft from last Tuesday. But with fourteen people I only get three chains, so that comparison is unbalanced — tell me straight whether it's worth running at this size or whether I should just report it as an anecdote, and give me the two-condition version for a bigger room (say twenty-four) if I ever get one.

The debrief is where I need the most help. I want the item-by-item survival mapped onto the idea: the single-violation stuff tends to hang on best, the four-violation monster dies early because there's nothing to hook it onto, and the mundane-but-odd stuff does better than anyone predicts. Then the harder turn: if memorability were all it took, why is nobody praying to a cartoon mouse? Whatever we've just demonstrated is about what survives being passed along, not about what people organise their lives around, and the gap between the two is the actual content of the session. Be blunt with me about that gap and tell me how to say it without deflating them, because the honest version is that the activity reproduces the stickiness and the facilitator has to supply motivation, existential stakes, costly commitment, ritual, social scaffolding, and centuries instead of twenty minutes. I also want a short frank note on the byproduct-versus-adaptation argument, and which side of it this activity quietly assumes.

Also: what predictably goes wrong the first time? My guesses are that someone in every chain embellishes on purpose to be funny, that the last person writes a summary instead of a retelling, and that one chain dissolves into giggling by link three. Tell me how to head those off, and which of them actually wreck the data.

Give me a handout — the item list for me to score against, a scoring sheet, and a minute-by-minute timeline — plus a version that still works if two people don't turn up. And if the whole thing runs fast, I want a fifteen-minute backup: some short passage where people have to mark what the deity in it knows at three marked moments, so they can notice themselves handing it a human sort of attention. No bibliographies, no reading lists, I can't use them.
