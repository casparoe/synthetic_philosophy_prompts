So my flatmate and me were up til about 2am arguing over this thought experiment he'd found in some metaphysics reader, and I still think he's wrong but I couldn't put my finger on why, which is annoying. Heres the setup as he told it: a universe where the only things that exist are two spheres, perfect duplicates of each other — same size, same material, same everything down to the last scratch — sitting some fixed distance apart in otherwise empty space. Nothing else exists at all. He says obviously there are two things there, and I said ok, but if theres not one single fact about that world that distinguishes them, what exactly makes it two things instead of one? He said "their positions," and I said the whole world is symmetric, so assigning a position to each one is just re-describing the same facts in two orders, you're not adding anything. He said "well each one is identical to itself and not identical to the other," and I said thats cheating, of course thats true, but it assumes the very thing we're trying to explain — your not allowed to hand a thing its own identity as one of its properties, or nothing is ever a puzzle.

So before I go read any more of this stuff I want the spec first. Write me the desiderata for a theory of what makes one particular thing distinct from another — what any theory of individuation has to deliver. And I mean the whole list, with the case or consideration behind each one:

- the cases it has to get right, at minimum the duplicate spheres, but also: a thing that persists through change when none of its properties stay the same, whats supposed to make it the same thing at two times rather than two things; whats supposed to make it the same thing across possible worlds, if anything; the fact that in physics you apparently cant say which electron is which and swapping two of them isnt a different arrangement; and counting, since a cloud is a thousand overlapping lumps of droplets and each of them seems to be a perfectly good candidate for being the cloud, so a theory that tells me what makes a thing one thing better also tell me how many things there are;
- the distinctions it cant collapse — numerical sameness vs qualitative sameness; there being no difference vs there being a difference nothing could ever in principle detect; and the one I care about most, whatever rule keeps "this thing is the one at position A" style properties from being counted as differences, because if properties like that are allowed in, the question answers itself and every theory comes out trivially true;
- anything it has to stay consistent with, including whether it gets to just take space, time or position for granted as a free resource.
