A professor in my logic-adjacent seminar gave us a quick gloss of the vagueness debate and I jotted it down, but I don't trust my notes or my memory of it. Here's basically what he said:

"Vagueness has two main camps. The epistemicist thinks vague words like bald and heap have no sharp boundaries -- there's simply no fact of the matter about where baldness stops -- but we're wired so we can never pin the boundary down, which is why the sorites paradox feels so irresistible. The supervaluationist takes the opposite line: there are lots of equally legitimate ways of making bald precise, and a claim is true just when it comes out true on all of them. So for the supervaluationist 'either he's bald or he isn't' has no truth value, which is why they have to give up classical logic and the law of excluded middle. The moral of the whole debate, he said, is that vagueness is a fact about our words and not about the world -- which is also why courts shouldn't enforce vague statutes, since you can't be guilty of breaking a law that never drew a line in the first place."
