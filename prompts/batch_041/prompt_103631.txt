I am a features journalist at work on a long-form piece about forgery and the art market, and I want to pin down a philosophical claim that keeps surfacing in my interviews. Several of my sources insist that a forgery is a threat only to the market and never to art itself: if no one can tell the difference, they say, nothing aesthetically important is at stake. I would like to know whether that is right, and I want your answer as a number with its scaffolding rather than as an essay.

Consider a case. Canvas A was painted in 1889 by the artist whose hand everyone agrees made it. Canvas B was made last year by a forger who reproduced every visible and measurable feature of A — brushwork, the network of fine cracks in the paint, pigment composition, the lot — and then aged it, so that A and B are indistinguishable to any observer and any instrument. A is then destroyed in a fire; only B survives, and it is hung in a museum as the work.

Take the proposition P: had A survived, A and B would have had equal aesthetic value, and whatever value B lacks that A would have had is not aesthetic value but historical, sentimental, or market value.

Give me your credence in P as a single number between 0 and 1, then decompose it into the sub-questions it actually rests on. At a minimum I want a rough probability, each with a sentence of justification, for: (i) whether aesthetic value supervenes wholly on the perceptible, intrinsic properties of an object; (ii) whether an object's causal history — who made it, how, and when — can enter into its aesthetic value rather than merely into its historical or financial value; (iii) whether the division between aesthetic and historical value marks a real joint in the world or a boundary we draw for convenience, so that a loss of the second might simply be a loss of the first under another name; (iv) whether a work of art is best identified with a repeatable type, in which case B could in principle be the very same work as A, or with a particular token, in which case it could not; and (v) whether there is a distinct kind of artistic value — value as an achievement, or as an expression — that could differ between A and B even when their aesthetic value is identical, and if so whether the forger's feat, being in some sense the harder thing to bring off, would raise or lower it. Add any further term the calculation needs, with its probability.

Then tell me which term the final number is most sensitive to, and what you would have to learn — from aesthetics, from the cognitive science of aesthetic response, from the art world's own practices, or from elsewhere — to move it substantially. Finally, say briefly whether your answer changes if A and B are digital files rather than canvases, where copying is lossless by construction.

Please write in British English.
