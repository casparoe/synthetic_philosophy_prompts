I've got a philosopher coming on my podcast next week who defends a hard-line Augustinian view of lying: lying is always wrong, no exceptions, not to save a life, not to catch a heretic, not to be polite. He's written a book on it and he's good at restating the view, so I want questions that actually test it. Can you draft 8 to 10 questions, each with a follow-up keyed to the answer he's most likely to recieve? I want alot of these to be in the weeds, and I definately don't want the obvious ones. I want at least one question on each of these:

- The silence vs. lie distinction. He says you can conceal the truth by staying silent but you can't lie. Press him on whether that's stable. What about a true statement that you know will mislead? What about saying "I won't tell you" in a tone that you know will make the pursuer think the fugitive isn't there? Is that a lie? If not, why not, if the intention is to deceive? If it is, then his definition is broader than he says.

- The lying to God problem. Augustine says Ananias and Sapphira lied to the Holy Spirit. But on the intention-to-deceive definition, you can't intend to deceive God. So either lying doesn't require the intention to deceive, or lying to God is impossible. Press him on which he wants to give up. Follow-up: if he says lying doesn't require the intention to deceive, then what about jokes and fiction? If he says lying to God is impossible, then what was the sin of Ananias?

- The biblical cases. Rahab lies to the soldiers, the Hebrew midwives lie to Pharaoh, Jacob lies to Isaac. He'll say either they didn't really lie, or they lied and it was a sin that God used for good. Press him on Rahab: if she lied, why does Scripture commend her faith? If she didn't lie, what makes her false statement not a lie? Follow-up: if he says she wasn't obliged to tell the truth to the soldiers, then isn't he saying the duty to truth is context-dependent, which is what he denies?

- The Priscillianist case. Augustine forbids Catholics from pretending to be Priscillianists to infiltrate them. Press him on modern analogues: undercover cops, espionage, a therapist who feigns agreement, a doctor who withholds a prognosis. He'll say you can do these things without lying, by not asserting. Press him on what counts as asserting. If a cop says "I'm a drug buyer" as part of a role-play, is that an assertion? If not, why can't the Catholic infiltrator say "I'm a Priscillianist" as a role-play? If it is an assertion, then undercover work is lying.

- The officious lie and the hierarchy. Augustine ranks lies into kinds, with the officious lie -- the lie that helps someone and harms no one -- as the least grave. He says it's still a sin, but a venial one. Press him on what makes it a sin at all if it's venial. If it's a sin because it's a misuse of speech, then the misuse is slight. If it's a sin because it's against the virtue of truth, then the virtue is absolute. Follow-up: if he says it's a venial sin, ask him whether a person who tells an officious lie to save a life should feel guilty. If he says no, then is it really a sin? If he says yes, then is he saying the person should feel guilty for doing the right thing?

- The mental reservation problem. The Priscillianists said you can hold the truth in your heart and speak a falsehood to outsiders. Augustine condemns this. But later Catholic moral theology developed mental reservation and equivocation -- saying something true in a literal sense while intending it to be understood in a false sense, or adding a silent qualification in your head. That looks like the same move. Press him on whether mental reservation is a lie. If it's not a lie, then what's the difference from the Priscillianist doctrine? If it is a lie, then he's condemning a long-standing Catholic practice. Follow-up: if he says mental reservation is not a lie because the words are true in a certain sense, ask him whether that's just a verbal trick. If he says it's a lie, ask him whether he's comfortable saying that.

- The God sends a lying spirit case. In 1 Kings 22, God sends a lying spirit to deceive Ahab. Press him on how God can do that if lying is always wrong. He'll say God permits it, doesn't do it. Press him on whether that's a distinction without a difference. If God can use a lie for a good end, why can't we? Follow-up: if he says God's ways are not ours, ask him whether that makes the absolute prohibition a rule for us but not for God. If so, is the rule grounded in the nature of lying or in our epistemic limits?

- The fiction problem. Augustine says jokes aren't lies because there's no intention to deceive. But fiction also has no intention to deceive, yet it's a false signification. So is fiction a lie? If not, then the false signification condition isn't sufficient. If it is, then he's committed to saying novelists are liars. Follow-up: if he says fiction is not a lie because it's not an assertion, ask him what makes something an assertion. If he says it's the intention to be taken as true, then a lie is a false assertion, not just a false signification. Then the double heart is not the essence of lying.

- The coded signal problem. Suppose a prisoner of war says "The weather is nice" to signal to a compatriot that the coast is clear, knowing the guard will hear it as a true statement. Is that a lie? He'll say no, because the prisoner believes the weather is nice, or doesn't care. Press him on a case where the coded signal is false: the prisoner says "The weather is nice" when it's raining, to signal something. Is that a lie? If not, why not? If yes, then the intention to deceive the guard is not necessary. Follow-up: if he says it's a lie because it's a false assertion, then what about the case where the guard knows the code? Then it's not a false assertion to the guard. So the same words can be a lie or not depending on who knows what. Is that plausible?

- The virtue vs. duty question. Is truthfulness a virtue that can be exercised in degrees and overridden by other virtues, or is it an absolute duty? He'll say it's a virtue, but an absolute one. Press him on what that means. If it's a virtue, it's a mean between extremes, and it can be overridden. If it's an absolute duty, it's not a virtue in the Aristotelian sense. Follow-up: if he says it's a virtue, ask him what the extreme of too much truthfulness would be. If he says there's no such extreme, then it's not a virtue in the mean sense. If he says there is, then lying might sometimes be the virtuous mean.
