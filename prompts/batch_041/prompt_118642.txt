I retired two years ago from a career in cell biology and have since been reading philosophy in a fairly undisciplined way, mostly metaphysics; I have reached the stage where I can follow an argument but not yet where I trust my judgment about how one should be introduced, which is why I am asking for a plan rather than an answer. In three weeks I am to lead a two-hour session at the lifelong-learning institute attached to my old university. The twelve participants are adults with no formal philosophy, several of them retired scientists like myself, and the topic is what philosophers call the special composition question: under what conditions do some things compose a further thing? Please plan the teaching and do not explain the topic to me. What I need is the order in which the components must be introduced, with the dependencies made explicit — in particular, what has to be in place before the question can even be put in a way that strikes the audience as a genuine question about the world rather than a quibble about the word "thing" — together with the point in that sequence at which each predictable misunderstanding should be intercepted. I have my own guesses about the menu of answers (everything always composes; nothing ever does; only some privileged class does, perhaps only living things; or composition is brute and unsystematic, with no informative principle to be had), but I suspect that presenting this menu early, as a taxonomy to be memorized, would rob the session of the very thing I want it to produce, namely the felt pressure of the question. Tell me whether that suspicion is right and, if it is, what should occupy the space instead. I should add that I want no reading list; the group will read nothing in advance, and anything that would require them to consult a text is of no use to me.

The choice of a carrying example is where I am least confident, and I would like you to reason it through rather than simply supply one. My instinct was to build the whole session on a case from my own field: the Portuguese man o' war, or a cellular slime mold that spends most of its life as independent amoebae and then aggregates into something that moves, feeds, and reproduces as a single entity. My thought was that a biologist's audience will feel the pull of the question in their bones when the example is biological. I now suspect this misfires, precisely because biologists have a settled professional habit of responding to such puzzles by stipulating a definition of "organism" and carrying on, which is the very move I want them to experience as an evasion rather than a solution. Advise me: should the example be biological, artefactual (a chair, a heap of sand), or deliberately outlandish, such as the top half of one animal joined to the bottom half of another? And state what the example must have and what it must lack if it is to make composition look like a real feature of the world rather than a puzzle about our words.

Beyond the verbal-dispute reaction, I expect at least two further misconceptions and want to know where in the sequence each should be headed off: the conviction that the natural sciences will eventually settle the matter empirically, and the assumption that anyone who denies that ordinary objects compose anything is denying that ordinary objects exist. I would also like the sequence to leave the audience able to see that "it depends, and there is no sharp line" is itself a substantive and contested position rather than a comfortable truce. Finally, give me one closing question I can put to the room that will distinguish a participant who can recite the menu of answers from one who has understood why the question is hard, and tell me what I should openly refuse to cover at this level — the technical mereological machinery, for instance — so that I can say so out loud rather than leave the audience with the impression that I have merely run out of time.
