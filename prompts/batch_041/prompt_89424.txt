I'm a science-and-ideas reporter, and I'm working on a long feature about whether it makes sense to say a chatbot believes anything. For background I asked a philosopher I'd interviewed for the piece — a public-facing tech-ethics guy, not a Dennett specialist, but confident and quotable — to send me a plain-English paragraph on the intentional stance that I could crib from, and here's what he sent: "Dennett's big idea is that there are three stances you can take toward anything you're trying to predict. The physical stance: work out the physics. The design stance: work out what the thing was built to do. And the intentional stance: treat it as a rational agent and work out what it would do given what it wants and what it believes. The catch is that a stance is just a stance, a posture you adopt, like deciding to give a colleague the benefit of the doubt — so when you say the chatbot believes Paris is in France, you're not describing anything inside the chatbot, you're describing your own convenient way of talking. That's what he means by calling beliefs useful fictions. He does also want to say that for systems complicated enough the beliefs are perfectly real, which has always struck me as him having it both ways, though he'd probably say belief comes in degrees and there's no sharp line. Applied to AI, the moral is that the intentional stance is the top of the ladder, the one you move up to once a machine gets complicated enough; and since you predict a chatbot's answers about Paris much better by assuming it knows where Paris is than by tracing its weights — and you can attribute that one belief without fussing over the rest of them — his own test says it really does have beliefs about Paris. Which is also why he thinks the hard problem of consciousness is a non-starter: if there's nothing more to having a belief than the stance paying off, there's nothing more to having an experience either. Anyone waving the stochastic parrot line is just refusing to climb off the design stance out of sentimentality."
