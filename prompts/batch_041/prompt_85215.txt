I'm in a reading group working through G. E. Moore's Principia Ethica (1903), and we've gotten stuck on the later chapters, where Moore sets out his positive axiology: his claim that the greatest intrinsic goods are certain states of consciousness, especially the enjoyment of beautiful objects and the pleasures of personal affection, and his doctrine of organic unities, according to which the value of a whole need not equal the sum of the values of its parts. I want to test that axiology against a real episode rather than against the usual thought experiments, because the usual ones feel engineered to make Moore come out looking right.

The episode I have in mind is the toppling of the statue of Edward Colston in Bristol in June 2020, during the protests that followed the killing of George Floyd. Colston was a seventeenth- and eighteenth-century merchant whose fortune was bound up with the transatlantic slave trade; a crowd pulled his statue down, dragged it to the harbor, and threw it in. The statue was later recovered and put on display in a museum, and what should become of it — restoration, recontextualization, permanent museum display, or something else — remains a live public dispute. The episode is a tangle of aesthetic value, moral disvalue, public memory, and collective attachment, and I think it is a genuinely hard case for Moore rather than a convenient illustration of him.

Please analyze this episode through Moore's views on beauty and friendship. Adopt the role of a professional philosopher with expertise in early analytic ethics, and begin by brainstorming the relevant Moorean resources — the intrinsic value of beauty, the method of isolation, the organic unity doctrine, the priority of individual states of consciousness, and the place of personal affection in the good life — before you settle into the analysis. Then work through the episode in a disciplined way: What does Moore's theory say the statue is worth, and to whom? Does the method of isolation imply that the statue would retain its value in a world with no one to see it, and does that result survive contact with a statue whose aesthetic character is bound up with its representational content and its public setting? How does the organic unity doctrine handle the whole composed of the statue, its subject, its location, and its history — and does the museum display create a new whole with a different value? Does the episode expose a gap in Moore's account, namely that his highest goods are individual and apolitical, while this dispute is about collective memory and civic identity? Finally, tell me what the episode shows about Moore's axiology: does it confirm it, complicate it, or reveal a case it never anticipated? Be explicit about where Moore's framework is silent rather than forcing it to speak, and end with a defended verdict rather than a survey of options.
