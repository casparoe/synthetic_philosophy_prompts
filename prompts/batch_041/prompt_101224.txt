I have recently been made secretary of a small learned society — a few hundred members, mostly academics and archivists, with a nine-seat council elected each autumn — and the thankless job of reviewing how we run those elections has somehow landed on me. Our present rule is simple plurality for each seat, which produces results nobody can defend once the field grows past three or four candidates, and the membership is now split between those who want to move to a ranked method (instant-runoff, or a Condorcet count) and those who prefer approval voting. To help us think, a colleague circulated a page from a well-regarded popular economics book, and since it has already been quoted in our email thread as though it settled the matter, I want a proper audit of it before I put anything to the committee. My own background is thin — one term of microeconomics a decade ago, from which I retain only a vague memory that Arrow proved something dispiriting about democracy — so I am in no position to tell the accurate parts from the confident ones.

Here is the passage, reproduced as it appears:

'Gibbard and Satterthwaite showed in the 1970s that no voting system can be fair. Their result, the twin of Arrow's impossibility theorem, states that in any election with three or more candidates there will always be a voter who can do better by voting dishonestly than by voting her true preferences, so every democracy is exposed to strategic voting, and none of the familiar methods — first-past-the-post, the alternative vote, approval voting, the Borda count — escapes. The only way out is to let one person's ranking decide everything, which is why economists summarise the theorem by saying that the only strategy-proof voting rule is dictatorship. This is the deep reason why real elections are always gamed by rational voters, and why tinkering with the ballot — ranked versus approval, Condorcet versus plurality — cannot make a system manipulation-proof.'

I should like you to act as a social choice theorist — someone who works on mechanism design and the theory of voting, and who is used to explaining it to non-specialists. Please begin by setting out, in your own words, the precise hypotheses and the precise conclusion of the theorem the passage is invoking, because I suspect the rest of the audit will fall out of that. Then go through the passage clause by clause, and for each one tell me which of three verdicts applies: what a specialist would accept as stated; what they would wince at — roughly right but loose, misattributed, or standing in need of a caveat; and what is simply wrong. Where a clause is a conflation, say explicitly which two claims have been run together; where it states fairly something narrower than it appears to claim, say what the narrower true claim is. I should also like to know what a defender of the theorem would say in its favour against the passage — which parts of the popularisation are, in fact, standard glosses that specialists tolerate — and what the passage leaves out that changes the picture, especially concerning the theorem's scope (which kinds of rule it is about, and which it is silent on) and any escape routes it fails to mention.

Finally, and this is the point I actually care about for the committee: the passage's closing claim is that tinkering with the ballot cannot make a system manipulation-proof. Does that conclusion follow from anything true in the summary, and if it does, does it give us any reason to prefer one of our candidate methods over another, or to abandon redesign altogether? Please be explicit about what the theorem does and does not license as a practical conclusion, and about how much daylight there is between 'manipulable in principle' and 'manipulated in practice'. Think this through carefully rather than reaching for the textbook line, and write your answer in British English.
