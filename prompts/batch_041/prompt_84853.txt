My friend in our rational-choice reading group dropped this on us and now the whole group is stuck. I think the argument proves way too much, but I dont know which step is the bad one and everyone I ask gives a different answer. Here's the chain, cleaned up:

(1) If a counterparty can take money off you using only a sequence of trades you'd each accept at the moment you're offered it, then your preferences are irrational.

(2) Picture someone who's present-biased. On Monday she'd rather have $110 on Wednesday than $100 on Tuesday — the bigger, later sum wins when both are still in the future. But on Tuesday the $100 is now and the $110 is tomorrow, and she flips: she takes the $100. Nothing is weird about her preferences at any single moment. She's just more impatient about the near than the far, which is how basically every human is built.

(3) A counterparty who knows her discount curve can then charge her a small fee to pull a payment one day closer — $5 to move it from Wednesday to Tuesday, another $5 to move it from Tuesday to Monday — and she pays every time, because at each moment the sooner money is worth more to her than the fee. Run it long enough and she's handed over alot more than the payment was ever worth, and she never once accepted a trade that looked bad from where she was standing.

(4) So by (1) her preferences are irrational.

(5) But she's not special. Every actual person is present-biased to some degree, and anyone whose goals or tastes shift as they live can be strung along the same way, since a shifting preference is just a preference that reverses.

(6) So every actual person is irrational.

(7) But irrational is supposed to be a mark of defect, something that singles you out for criticism. A verdict that lands on every agent with identical force has stopped doing that job. So at least one step is off.

I'd really like to keep (1), because I need it for the clean case: the guy whose preferences run rock-paper-scissors over three sandwiches. I want to say he's irrational, and I want (1) to be the reason. So which step goes? If your fix is that she should just precommit, tell me what precommitment actually amounts to for an agent who can't see the whole future, and whether it even touches the taste-shift version in (5), which isn't about time at all. And if you keep (1) and drop something else, price it for me — what's left holding up the sandwich guy's irrationality? Please write your answer in American English.
