I have no academic training, but for the past couple of years I have been worrying at a question I cannot put down: when several things are arranged in some particular way, when, if ever, do they add up to one further thing that has them as its parts? I am told philosophers have a name for this question, but I care about the question rather than the name. Please build me a decision tree that works as a self-sorting quiz, so that I can find out which answer I actually hold and so that my friends and I can see where we genuinely part company.

Three things I want to insist on. First, every branching node should be a concrete case or a plain yes-or-no claim, phrased so that a person can answer it without already knowing any of the positions or their names; no node may ask me to choose a label, and no leaf may be reached by my announcing which answer I like the sound of. Second, the cases should do the work. Please include at least the following: two bricks resting on each other; two bricks at opposite ends of a room; a heap of sand I have just swept together, along with the fact that I could always take one more grain away; a cat; a model built from small bricks, both when it is glued and when the pieces are merely balanced; a wave, a cloud, or a flame; a crowd at a stadium or a queue at a till; a forest; a statue and the lump of clay it is made of; and a tool whose head and handle have each been replaced at different times. Third, some of the branches should turn on the questions I take to be the ones that actually divide people: whether the answer has to be a rule that could be stated in advance and applied by anyone, or may instead be a bare fact with no further explanation; whether every case must have a definite answer, or whether it is acceptable for some cases to have none, or for the answer to be vague; whether the answer depends on features of the things themselves or on facts about us, our concepts, our interests, our way of talking; and whether it matters that a heap is simply found while a model is deliberately made.

For each leaf, give the name of the position if it has one, or mark it unnamed if nobody defends it, and add one sentence on the commitment that position has the hardest time explaining. Where a combination of answers on the tree is occupied by no position at all, say so, and say whether you think it is empty because the combination is incoherent, because it is unattractive, or merely because nobody has bothered to explore it. Where two leaves cannot be pulled apart by any of the questions, flag them as a pair and tell me which further case would be needed to separate them. I would rather have a leaf that nobody occupies than a leaf that is really just a label.

Keep it to six to ten branching questions and no more than eight leaves, and gloss any term of art in plain words, since I will be handing this to people who, like me, have read no philosophy.
