You are an expert in game theory, rational choice, and the moral psychology of commitment. I am preparing materials for a graduate seminar on the dollar auction and escalation of commitment, and I want a satirical piece that will force students to separate the mechanics of the game from the rhetoric of persistence. Before drafting, brainstorm in a few lines the actual structure: the auction for a dollar in which both the highest and second-highest bids are paid; why a player should refuse to enter; why the second-highest bidder, once trapped, has a live incentive to raise; how the auctioneer profits from the escalation; and how sunk costs and the fear of being the sucker keep the bidding alive. Then write a deadpan internal policy memo of roughly 600 words from the fictional Office of Strategic Persistence at a mid-sized university, proposing that all interdepartmental budget disputes be resolved by a modified dollar auction. The memo should be written by a true believer who understands the game perfectly and treats escalation as a feature of serious institutions. Include the proposed rules, a pilot-program anecdote, a section arguing that the second-highest bidder is the real engine of institutional commitment, a new metric called the escalation-adjusted return on commitment, a risk-management paragraph that quietly concedes the scheme works only because people cannot stop, and a final recommendation. The satire should land the standard objections to the dollar auction and to escalation of commitment without ever stating them as objections; it should be funny to someone who knows the material and legible to someone who does not. Do not include citations, reading lists, or named authorities. After the memo, on its own line, write a single sentence beginning Summary: that states the piece's central ironic thesis.
