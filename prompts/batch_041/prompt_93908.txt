Okay so this has bugged me ever since I found out about it. In most places the cops are allowed to lie to you flat out during an interrogation. They can say your fingerprints turned up at the scene when they didn't, that your friend already confessed, that they've got you on video, whatever — all made up — and any confession you give because of it is still perfectly fine evidence against you. But if they got the exact same confession by threatening you, or by promising to let you go home if you talk, the whole thing gets thrown out. And I sort of get the stated reason: a coerced confession might be false so we can't trust it. But a confession you coughed up because they lied about the evidence is also pretty likely to be false, maybe even likelier. So something else has to be doing the work, and I want to know what.

What does this whole arrangement actually presuppose — about the state, about me, about what counts as coercion versus just trickery? Like, it seems to assume the state is allowed to do things to me that I'm absolutely not allowed to do back (if I lie to a cop thats obstruction, if they lie to me thats just technique). So does it presuppose the state isn't my moral equal, or that it is but has special permissions, or what? And it seems to draw a hard line between making someone talk and tricking them into talking, which only makes sense under some particular view of when a choice counts as free — I'd like that view spelled out and then told whether it's actually coherent, since a lot of false confessions come from exactly this kind of lie.

Then tell me what's strictly presupposed here versus what the practice would still allow under other theories. Could a deterrence theory, a rights theory, and a kind of paternalist theory all license the same rule, or does the rule secretly commit us to one? And if we swapped out the presupposed view — say we decided deception undermines voluntariness just as much as threats do — what would actually have to change: the exclusionary rule, the recording rules, letting the defense lie back, telling the jury the cops lied? Walk me through it like I'm a person whos puzzled, not a law student.
