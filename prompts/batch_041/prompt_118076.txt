I am a philosophy professor, and at our department's recent retreat two colleagues became entangled in an argument about whether the relation better than is transitive, a dispute I was asked to referee but could not resolve. The argument began when one colleague, A, introduced what she called the palette problem: a series of two hundred color swatches arranged so that each adjacent pair differs by a single imperceptible increment along one dimension, say saturation, while also differing by a slightly smaller imperceptible decrement along another, say hue purity. At each adjacent comparison the gain in saturation seems to outweigh the loss in hue purity, so each swatch seems marginally better than its predecessor as a paint sample; yet over the whole series the cumulative loss in hue purity becomes obvious while the cumulative gain in saturation yields diminishing returns, so the two-hundredth swatch is clearly worse than the first. (1) A concluded that better than is not transitive, since we have a chain of pairwise betters running from a clearly better item to a clearly worse one. (2) B replied that this only shows our pairwise judgments are unreliable at the margins, not that the relation itself is intransitive; transitivity, B said, is a conceptual constraint on any ordering, and if the two-hundredth really is worse than the first, then at least one adjacent judgment must be false. (3) A countered that B's response requires a sharp cutoff somewhere in the series, and no such cutoff is introspectively or experimentally detectable. (4) B said vagueness is not intransitivity; a vague cutoff is still a cutoff. (5) A then said that if B accepts a vague cutoff, B owes an account of how a relation can be transitive when its application is indeterminate, and that any such account will either collapse into the view that there is a sharp but unknowable cutoff or concede that the relation is not fully real. (6) B responded that this is a false dilemma and that the case actually reveals a distinction between better simpliciter—better, period—and better for a purpose, since the swatches are being evaluated for different purposes at different points along the series. (7) A said that concedes the point: if better than is purpose-relative, then it is not a single transitive relation.

Please adjudicate move by move. Rate each of the seven moves from clearly mistaken to clearly right, identify which claims are true, which are false, and which are ambiguous or underdescribed, and say what both colleagues are missing. I am not asking for a literature review, citations, or a summary of what famous philosophers have said; I want a direct philosophical analysis of the arguments as they were actually made, with attention to the distinction between the transitivity of a relation and the transitivity of our judgments about it, the possibility of vague thresholds, and the significance of purpose-relativity. Conclude by saying who is more right overall, and what both parties have overlooked.
