I'm a data engineer by trade and spend most of my working life in relational schemas and property graphs — foreign keys, join tables, the reification of n-ary relations, and the recurring argument over whether a relationship deserves to be an entity in its own right or is merely a constraint between entities. I've lately been reading about F. H. Bradley's regress and the puzzle about the unity of the proposition: the thought that if a relation is itself just one more item alongside the things it relates, then some further item is needed to relate the relation to its relata, and then something further to relate that item in turn, so that the attempt to explain how several items come to form a single unified content never terminates. The associated worry is that a proposition may turn out to be a mere heap or list of items rather than one thing capable of being true or false.

Please restate that problem, as faithfully as you can, in the vocabulary I actually use: tables and rows, primary and foreign keys, joins, reification, n-ary relations, schema constraints, and the closed-world assumption. I want the translation to be honest — do not quietly import assumptions from my world, such as the idea that a schema's constraints are simply stipulated by a designer who could have stipulated otherwise, and then present the result as Bradley's position. After the translation, give me the residue report: which parts of Bradley's problem map cleanly onto the database picture, which parts carry over only with distortion, and which parts have no counterpart in my vocabulary at all. I am especially interested in whether my instinct — that unity is just what a primary key plus a foreign key buys you, and that the regress is the familiar fact that you can always add another join table — is a genuine solution to the philosophical problem or an evasion of it, and if it is an evasion, exactly what it leaves out.
