I am an applied econometrician by training, and my working life is causal inference: designing randomized trials, defending identification assumptions in observational studies, writing pre-analysis plans, and refusing to estimate anything until someone has specified the estimand. My instincts are thoroughly those of the potential-outcomes framework. I want a defined treatment, a defined contrast, a population of units, and a credible assignment mechanism; where those are unavailable I am inclined to say that the question is not well posed and to move on. My knowledge of history, by contrast, is that of an interested amateur. I know the rough shape of the long-running debate over why the breakthrough to sustained industrial growth occurred first in Britain rather than in China, and I have a vague sense that some historians regard the question as malformed for reasons that sound to me like complaints about framing and presupposition rather than about identification. But I have no feel for the historiography and no stake in any of the proposed answers.

Please do two things.

First, translate the debate into my vocabulary as faithfully as you can. Not a summary written in my general style: an actual translation. Restate the question and the principal competing answers as claims about estimands, units, treatments, contrasts, assignment mechanisms, identification assumptions, mediators, moderators, and estimators. For each proposed explanation, tell me what the treatment would have to be, what the counterfactual comparison is, over what population it is supposed to hold, and which of the standard threats — confounding, selection, reverse causation, interference, the uniqueness of the unit, the fact that the outcome is realized only once — are the ones that actually bite.

Second, give me the residue report. Which claims survive the translation without loss? Which survive only with distortion, and precisely what has to be altered to make them fit? Which cannot be expressed in my vocabulary at all? I have one suspicion I would like tested: that the choice of contrast class is not a technical parameter but does a great deal of the explanatory work, so that why Britain rather than China and why Britain rather than Japan are different questions with different answers. Can my framework express that at all, and if it can, does the expression preserve the point or trivialize it? I have also been taught to distinguish sharply between the effect of a cause and the cause of an effect, and to regard the second as generally beyond reach; tell me whether that distinction is doing real work here, or whether it is a limitation of my training that the historians' question is designed to circumvent. And finally, the verdict I am actually after: when my framework rules that the question is ill posed, is that a substantive result about the question, or an artifact of a framework built for many exchangeable units and randomized assignment? Where the residue resists translation, tell me whether the fault lies with my framework or with the historians.
