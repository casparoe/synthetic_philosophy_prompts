I am a hospitalist, and last spring I had to choose between two treatment paths for my father: one whose odds were reported as a single number in a large registry, and one whose reported odds ranged widely across a small and inconsistent literature. I went for the single number almost reflexively, even though nothing I could find suggested the second path was worse on average, and I have been chewing on that decision ever since. What has surprised me, now that I have been reading up on ambiguity aversion and Ellsberg's problem, is that the two explanations of the phenomenon I keep running into are not two ways of saying the same thing. The first holds that when people face an option whose odds they do not know, they evaluate it by the worst odds their information permits and act as though those were the odds, so that only the floor matters. The second holds that what people cannot stand is the width of the range itself, the gap between the best and worst odds, so that a person may reject a wide range with a perfectly respectable floor in favor of a narrow range that sits lower, because the narrow one is the one that feels like knowledge; I have not seen a name attached to this second view, only descriptions of it. I would like to watch the two come apart in a clean, deliberately constructed case rather than in an anecdote about my father, and I would like the case to be one I could hand to a skeptical colleague.

So please construct the thought experiment. It should be a single realistic decision, with no urns, balls, dice, lottery tickets, or game-show framing, of a kind a person could genuinely face — a treatment, an insurance plan, a job, a purchase, a wager among friends — and modest stakes are entirely fine. There should be exactly three options, each paying the same prize on success and nothing otherwise, with the same stake and the same horizon, and the chooser should be given the range of possible odds for each option and nothing else, so that the shape of the range is the only thing distinguishing them. Please engineer the ranges so that three different choosers each land on a different option: one who goes by the middle of the range, one who goes by the floor, and one who goes by the width of the range. I want three distinct verdicts rather than two verdicts and a tie, so give the ranges as explicit numbers and make sure the arithmetic genuinely separates them, with the verdicts falling out of the numbers rather than being asserted; and keep the scenario itself to a single paragraph.

Then, please, in American English: state each option's range, and for each of the three choosers say which option is taken and why, with the numbers. Tell me which single structural feature of the case is doing the work of pulling the three verdicts apart, and what becomes of the case if that feature is removed — if, say, all three ranges were given the same floor, or all three the same width — and whether the three verdicts then collapse into one. Tell me what the case has to assume about how the chooser takes the odds to be distributed inside each range, whether every point in the range is equally likely or whether that is left open, and whether any of the three verdicts turns on that assumption or only on the endpoints. And finally, tell me how someone who holds the second view, the one about the width of the range, would attack this case as rigged, and whether the attack succeeds. No need to point me toward the literature; I want the case itself.
