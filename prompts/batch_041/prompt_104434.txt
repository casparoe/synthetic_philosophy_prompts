I have been reading a popular introduction to the philosophy of religion, and I have reached the chapter on Hume's argument against miracles. The author writes for a general audience and seems competent, but I cannot tell how much of his summary I should trust. He presents Hume's position as follows:

"Hume's argument is a proof that miracles are impossible. He defines a miracle as something that breaks a law of nature, and he points out that our evidence for a law of nature is uniform experience — we have seen the sun rise every day, we have never seen the dead come back to life. The only evidence we could ever have for a miracle is human testimony, and testimony is itself grounded in experience: we believe what people tell us because we have observed that people usually tell the truth. But since a law of nature is backed by all of our experience, and a miracle report is backed only by the testimony of a few witnesses, the scales can never tip in favor of the miracle. We should believe only in proportion to the evidence, and the evidence always favors the law. Hume adds that the testimony for miracles is especially weak in practice, since miracle reports tend to come from uneducated and remote peoples, from people with a prior religious commitment, and from rival religions whose claims cancel each other out. So Hume concludes that no miracle has ever been established and that no testimony could ever establish one — which amounts to the claim that miracles do not happen."

Something about this summary seems too neat to me. I know Hume is a major philosopher and I do not want to dismiss him, but I also suspect the author has made his argument either stronger or simpler than it really is. Could you go through the summary claim by claim and tell me what a careful reader of Hume would accept, what is a common simplification that gets the spirit right but blurs something important, and what Hume would actually deny or not have said? In particular, I would like to know whether the final conclusion — that miracles do not happen — is something Hume actually asserts or something the author has added on his behalf, and whether the bits about uneducated peoples and rival religions are the core of the argument or just side remarks. I am not looking for a bibliography or a reading list; I just want to understand the argument well enough to know whether this popular summary is fair.
