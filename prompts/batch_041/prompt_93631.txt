I am drafting a chapter on the ethics of humanitarian intervention and regime change, and I would like to use you as a foil. A colleague of mine — a political theorist whose positions I know well but whose governing principle I have never been able to pin down — has taken the following line on past cases.

- NATO's 1999 air campaign over Kosovo, launched without Security Council authorization in response to the Serbian offensive against the Kosovar Albanian population: justified.
- The 2003 invasion of Iraq, launched without Security Council authorization and openly aimed at replacing the government: unjustified.
- The 2011 intervention in Libya, authorized by the Security Council to protect civilians, in the course of which the intervening states pursued the removal of the government: a mistake on balance, with the mistake located in the pursuit of regime change rather than in the protection of civilians.
- The failure to intervene in Rwanda in 1994: a grave wrong.
- The deployment of Gulf Cooperation Council forces into Bahrain in 2011, at the request of the Bahraini monarchy and without Security Council authorization, to suppress an uprising: unjustified.
- The Australian-led international force in East Timor in 1999, mounted with Indonesian consent and with Security Council authorization after the violence that followed the independence referendum: justified.
- The British deployment to Sierra Leone in 2000, which began as an evacuation of foreign nationals and ended by shoring up a collapsing government and a failing United Nations mission: justified.
- NATO's belated air campaign over Bosnia in 1995, after three years of United Nations resolutions and after Srebrenica: justified, though far too late.

She attaches one qualification to the first item. She regards the precedent that Kosovo set as a genuine cost of the intervention — she has said that Russia's later appeals to the protection of threatened populations in Georgia and Ukraine are exactly the precedent she feared — and yet she still counts the campaign as justified.

Next week I intend to put the following case to her. A regional power invades a neighbouring state without Security Council authorization. Independent observers agree that the neighbouring state's government is carrying out a campaign of mass killing against a minority that shares the regional power's ethnicity. The intervention will halt the killing and, on the best estimates available, save on the order of two hundred thousand lives within a year. The minority has publicly and overwhelmingly asked for it. The rest of the neighbouring state's population, however, regards the intervention as a foreign invasion and opposes it. The intervening power's motives are frankly mixed: it intends to install a client government and to secure favourable terms for its own firms, and its leaders have said as much. The regional organization to which both states belong has endorsed the intervention by a narrow vote, but the intervening power dominates that organization. Nothing suggests the aftermath will be worse than the status quo; nothing suggests it will be a stable or liberal peace either.

What will she say, and on what ground? I am less interested in whether she is right than in the rule she is implicitly running. Set out the two or three rules that fit her record equally well, say what each predicts about my case and where they come apart, identify which of her past cases is the closest analogue and why, tell me which rule you would bet on and what makes you prefer it, and name the further case — one I could put to her the week after — that would separate the surviving candidates from one another.
