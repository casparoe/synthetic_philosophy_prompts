Last month, at a housewarming for a mutual friend, I spent most of the evening as the bystander in a long argument between my roommate Nadia, who is finishing a doctorate in atomic physics and thinks about quantum foundations more than she likes to admit, and Owen, a philosophy graduate student who enjoys pressing her on exactly this. The subject was the thought experiment usually called quantum suicide, and whether it licenses the thesis usually called quantum immortality. Both of them were careful and neither was being silly, and I came away genuinely unsure who had the better of it, so what I want is a referee rather than a general essay: the exchange itself adjudicated as it was actually conducted. I have a leaning, but I am deliberately not going to tell you what it is, because I do not want the verdict shaded toward the answer I already like.

Nadia's opening was this. In any theory in which a measurement outcome is not a single fact but a branch, both the chamber firing and the chamber not firing are real; she is present only in branches where she is still alive to notice anything, so she can never be on hand for her own death, and the thought experiment exposes the single world of a collapse theory as a fiction produced by mistaking one branch for the whole of what there is. Owen answered that the argument proves too little and too much at once. Too little, because the same first-person fact holds under collapse theories and in a purely classical world as well: he has never once experienced his own non-existence, and he does not conclude that he is immortal, so the survivor is simply the only one who can file a report, which makes the whole thing a selection effect rather than a test of many worlds. Too much, because the interesting content of quantum immortality is not that some successor of hers is always around but something stronger, and the weight of the branch she is asking us to look at is not constant: if each round of the experiment leaves her with a survival amplitude equal to the square root of (N−1)/N, then after n rounds the branch containing her has weight ((N−1)/N) to the n/2, which tends to zero, so the honest reading is that she becomes increasingly improbable rather than increasingly deathless. Nadia's comeback was that weight is a red herring: a small weight is not a small amount of existence, and in a no-collapse theory there is no fact of the matter about which branch is her, so there is no event called her death for her to be present at or absent from, and her future, understood as the collection of her successors, is never empty. Owen replied that this last move reduces the doctrine to a remark about the pronoun, namely that there is always some branch containing a successor, and that as soon as you ask what such a successor is like the doctrine either collapses into the trivial or runs into absurdity: it should tell him to expect to find himself among the survivors of every improbable thing that has already happened to him, and it should tell him that a man destroyed by a bomb is immortal too, so long as some branch of vanishing weight contains something continuous with him. Nadia did not have a ready answer to that, and I do not think either of them was persuaded.

What I would like is a point-by-point adjudication. For each of the moves above — Nadia's opening, each half of Owen's reply, her comeback, and his closing charge — say who has the better of it and why, rated on a scale running from clearly mistaken, through defensible but overstated, to clearly right, keeping the justification for each rating brief. Then tell me what both of them are missing, and which of the two is more nearly right overall. Please be explicit about how confident you are in each verdict, and single out the one point in your answer you are least sure of. Write in American English.
