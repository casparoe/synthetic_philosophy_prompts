I'm a philosophy professor, and I've somehow ended up as the unofficial referee in a long-running spat between two colleagues in my department — one a scientific realist, the other a semantic anti-realist who keeps banging on about the knowability principle (the idea that every truth is knowable, in the sense that for any true proposition p, it's possible that someone knows p). Last week they went at it again over coffee about Fitch's paradox, and I said I'd try to adjudicate, but I'm honestly torn, so I want your help. Here's the exchange as best I can reconstruct it. The realist opened: "The Fitch argument is a knockout. Take any truth that no one knows — say, that a particular grain of sand on some beach was never counted by anyone. The conjunction 'that grain was never counted and no one knows it was never counted' is a truth. If all truths are knowable, then it's possible that someone knows that conjunction. But if someone knows both conjuncts, then by factivity — knowledge implies truth — if they know no one knows the first conjunct, then no one knows it, including them, so they don't know it. Contradiction. So the knowability principle is false, and anti-realism is dead." The anti-realist shot back: "That's too quick. The argument only works because you chose a proposition that contains the knowledge operator. Of course 'p and nobody knows p' can't be known — its content ensures that. But anti-realism was never committed to the knowability of self-defeating propositions like that. The principle should be restricted to propositions that are in principle verifiable, and 'p and nobody knows p' is precisely not that. So the paradox doesn't touch the view; it just shows you formulated it naively." The realist: "But that's ad hoc. If you restrict knowability to truths that are in principle verifiable, you've just defined the restriction to exclude the counterexamples, and now the principle says nothing. Any truth that turns out not to be knowable gets classified as not 'in principle verifiable' by fiat. And the anti-realist's whole point was that truth isn't independent of knowability; you can't then say there are truths that are unknowable but still truths, because that's the realist picture." The anti-realist: "No, the restriction is principled: knowability applies to truths that can be verified in a way that doesn't destroy the truth being verified. The unknown truth is a truth, but the reason it's unknowable is that knowing it would make it false; that's a feature of its content, not a refutation of the general link between truth and possible knowledge. The anti-realist can say: for every truth that is not self-undermining in that way, it's knowable. That's not vacuous; it's a substantive thesis about the relation between truth and verification, and Fitch's proof actually helps us state it correctly." The realist: "But then you're conceding that there are truths that are unknowable. If truth is supposed to be epistemic, how can there be a truth that no possible epistemic agent could know? You're halfway to realism. And if you say the unknown truth is knowable in some other sense — say, knowable as an instance of a general fact — then you've changed the subject from knowing the proposition to knowing that it's true, which is what the paradox was about." The anti-realist: "I'm not conceding that truth is independent of all possible knowledge; I'm saying that the proposition 'p and nobody knows p' is not a counterexample because its knowability is blocked by its own content. The general truth 'there are unknown truths' might be knowable, even if the particular conjunction isn't. And anti-realism can accept that. The paradox shows the need for a more careful formulation, not the death of the view."
