I'm a first-year law student who wandered into a philosophy of economics reading group, and I keep tripping over the same collision between two things I'm taught as settled. On the one hand, in open-market securities-fraud cases I'm taught the reliance presumption: because the price of a listed security is presumed to impound all public information, an investor who bought during the class period counts as having relied on the defendant's misstatement even if she never read it, never heard of the fraud, and would have bought the stock anyway. On the other hand, I've been told — and I only know this as a piece of hallway gossip, so correct me if I've garbled it — that there is a result showing that if traders share a common prior and it's common knowledge among them that they're all rational, then a public announcement moves the price without generating any trade at all: at the new price, whoever wants to buy has to ask why the seller is willing to sell, the seller has to ask the mirror question, and the trade evaporates. But every payrolls Friday the tape shows enormous volume changing hands within seconds of a number that everyone saw at the same instant, and nobody acts as though the theorem had happened to them.

What I want from you is an excavation of what the reliance presumption actually presupposes about the epistemology of prices, and I'd like you to keep three readings apart rather than collapsing them: that a price is a warranted belief which every participant in the market thereby holds; that a price is a public signal whose accuracy the law simply stipulates, for reasons of administrability, without anyone's believing anything in particular; and that a price is nothing but the residue of whatever trades happened to clear, informative only when someone has gone to the trouble of modelling what a given price implies. Say which of these the doctrine strictly needs, which it merely travels comfortably with, and whether the doctrine can distinguish itself from its nearest rival at all. Then tell me whether one legal system can coherently hold all of the following at once: that a market price reliably distils dispersed public information, enough to ground a presumption of reliance; that markets are periodically gripped by panic or mania, so that regulators may halt trading, restrict short sales, or announce that prices have come unmoored from fundamentals; that insider trading is prohibited because it degrades the informational quality of prices; and that everyone from the financial press to the central bank's own staff routinely reads probabilities straight off prices — the market's odds of a rate rise, of a recession — as though a price just were a credence. Where those commitments pull apart, tell me which one is doing the real work and which is window-dressing, and who exactly the credence is supposed to belong to when the central bank reports what the market thinks. Finally, and this is the part I actually care about: describe what would have to change — in doctrine, in regulation, or in the underlying epistemology — if we dropped the picture of prices as aggregating dispersed private information and treated them instead as artefacts of clearing, and say honestly whether anything of practical importance would be lost.
