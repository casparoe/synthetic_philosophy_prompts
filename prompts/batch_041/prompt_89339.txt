I've got 14 students in an upper-level seminar on conditionals, 80 minutes, and I want them to feel the triviality results in their gut instead of watching me push symbols around the board. Here's the seed of an activity — build it out into something runnable.

It's a betting market, chips only, no real money. Half the room are bookmakers posting prices on contracts against a scenario I hand out (an urn, a deck of cards, whatever's easy to settle in the room). Contract one is the ordinary conditional bet: "B if A" — you pay the price, your stake comes back if A never happens, otherwise it pays off iff B. Everybody agrees that ones fair price is P(B|A); that part isn't interesting. Contract two is a bet on the sentence itself: pays 1 if "if A then B" is true, 0 if it's false. Here's the hinge — the bookmakers have to write the settlement rule for contract two themselves, including what it pays when A turns out false. Let them land on "true, vacuously, nothing got falsified" (or let them fight about it; either ruling gives the same shape of problem). Then traders buy and sell at the posted prices and we settle.

What I want them to hit: they'll price contract two at P(B|A) out of habit and bleed chips in the ¬A worlds. The fix is that its fair price is P(¬A) + P(A∧B), so the two contracts come apart by P(¬A)·P(¬B|A) — which is zero only if A is certain or B is certain given A. So you can't treat the conditional as a proposition with a truth value and also price it at the conditional probability; the only conditionals you can price that way are degenerate ones. Then run the other branch: contract two goes void when A is false, so the bookies are finally safe — until a trader asks for a price on a nested conditional ("if A then, if B then C") and there's nothing left to conditionalize on. That's the trap I want them sitting in.

The debrief should map the two rounds onto the real results: the ¬A payoff gap is why nothing can have conditional probability as its probability, and the nesting mess is the seed of Lewis's collapse proof against any semantics that keeps the equation across a language closed under the conditional. Be honest with me about what the activity can't do — it stages the intuition, it doesn't derive the theorem — so tell me what I have to supply at the end and where I'd be faking it if I didn't.

Also tell me what predictably goes wrong the first time I run it. I'm expecting at least one bookie to refuse to settle at all and insist the conditional is neither true nor false when A fails, which is either the best or the worst thing that can happen to the session.
