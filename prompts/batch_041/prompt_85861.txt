For the past several years my main hobby has been reading the kind of blogs and forums where infinite-population puzzles, decision-theoretic paradoxes, and their cousins get litigated in the comments; I also TA a seminar on decision theory and ethics, and the exam question below is one I wrote myself for the unit on infinite populations. I am about to return the papers and would like a careful second opinion on a single student's answer, because it is fluent and I would rather not let fluency do the grading for me.

The question was this:

Treat a "world" as a collection of persons, each with a welfare level, which we may assume to be an integer. For finite worlds the total-welfare rule says: the world with the larger sum of welfare is better. Unless a question says otherwise, assume that the same persons exist in the worlds being compared.

(a) World A contains infinitely many persons, each at welfare 5. World B contains infinitely many persons, each at welfare 6. Explain why the total-welfare rule fails to rank A and B.

(b) Here is a proposed method of comparison, sometimes called cancellation. Line up the persons of the two worlds; cancel every person whose welfare is the same in both worlds; if the persons who remain (the "unmatched" persons) are finite in number on each side, compare the total welfare of the two remainders. Apply cancellation to World A and to World C, where C is just like A except that one person's welfare is 7 rather than 5.

(c) Let World D be just like World A except that D also contains one additional person, whose welfare is 1. Cancellation says that D is better than A. Many people find this verdict absurd. State the objection and assess it.

(d) A classmate objects as follows: "An infinite set can be put into one-to-one correspondence with a proper subset of itself, so adding a person to an infinite world cannot change how good it is. Therefore D and A are equally good." Evaluate this reasoning.

The student's answer:

"(a) The total-welfare rule fails because each world has infinitely many members, so each total is an infinite series that diverges: 5 added infinitely many times, and 6 added infinitely many times, are both infinite. Both totals are countably infinite, since each is a sum over a countable collection of persons, so neither total exceeds the other. Infinity is not greater than infinity, so the rule cannot say that B is better than A. It follows that there is no fact of the matter about which of the two is better, and that A and B are equally good. (A different rule, such as average welfare, might give a verdict, but that would be a different rule.)

(b) We cancel the two infinite populations, since every person has welfare 5 in A and all but one person has welfare 5 in C. What remains is a single person at welfare 5 on A's side and a single person at welfare 7 on C's side. Since 7 is greater than 5, C is better than A. This is the intuitively right verdict, and it shows that cancellation can compare worlds that the plain sum rule cannot.

(c) The objection runs as follows. D is just A with one extra person, at welfare 1. Cancellation matches all of A's persons with themselves and leaves the extra person unmatched, so it declares D better than A. But the extra person's welfare is finite, whereas the world's total is already infinite, and adding a finite quantity to infinity leaves it infinite. A finite addition cannot make an infinite total any larger, so the extra person makes no difference, and D is no better than A. I think the objection succeeds.

(d) The classmate's appeal to one-to-one correspondence is beside the point. Hilbert's Hotel shows that an infinite set can be matched one-to-one with a proper subset of itself, so adding a person does not change the number of persons; but the number of persons is not what makes a world good — their welfare is. Even so, the classmate's conclusion is right, though the argument is not: as I said in (c), a finite addition cannot enlarge an infinite total, so A and D are equally good."
