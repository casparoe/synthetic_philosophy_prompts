I'm taking a course on ethics and public life and I've ended up with a practical assignment I actually care about. Next term I'm doing a placement with a small NGO that runs water projects in four countries, and my supervisor there asked me to write the gift-and-hospitality rule for field staff, since right now it's just "no bribes, use your judgement" and everyone hates it. Our field staff deal with offers constantly and the stakes are real — we get audited by a big funder, and my supervisor has to sign the rule off before it goes out.

The hard part is that most of what gets offered is just ordinary social life. A village chief gives us a goat when the well is finished. A supplier offers to fly one of our engineers out to see the factory. A district officer invites two of our staff to his daughter's wedding. And customs always wants a "processing fee" to release the medical supplies.

Can you turn the philosophy on corruption into a procedure my staff could actually run? A few things it has to do. It should give the questions to ask, in order, and say where the procedure stops with a no. It needs to take seriously that people mean at least three different things by corruption — abusing a public office for private gain, an agent quietly serving a third party's interest instead of the person they're acting for, and acting against the public interest even when nobody privately gains — because these pick out different cases, and the goat only looks obviously bad on one of them. It shouldn't pretend a blanket no-gifts norm is culturally neutral: I want it to separate what's just local colour from what genuinely travels, and to say which is which out loud instead of smuggling in a Western standard. And it should handle the fact that bribery is often not one bad apple's choice but a stable equilibrium, where everyone expects everyone else to pay, so the individual who refuses just loses — that changes what the rule can fairly demand of a person.

Then run it on those four cases, tell me which of the answers will make my supervisor unhappy, and flag where the procedure has to hand the decision back to a human because it's a value call rather than a fact, since I have to admit that in the write-up.
