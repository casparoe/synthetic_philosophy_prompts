I am preparing a seminar session that pairs Ridley Scott's The Last Duel with the Jain doctrine of anekāntavāda, and I want a reading of the film that treats the doctrine as philosophy rather than as a slogan about everyone having their own truth.

The film's architecture is a gift to this pairing. It narrates one sequence of events three times — first as Jean de Carrouges experiences and remembers it, then as Jacques Le Gris does, then as Marguerite does — and the later chapters quietly revise details of the earlier ones. The audience is nudged to treat Marguerite's chapter as the one that finally gets it right: it comes last, and it is the only chapter that shows the assault as she experienced it rather than as a seduction or as a slight to a husband's honor. Meanwhile the plot is driven toward a judicial duel, an institution that promises to settle a contested proposition with a single unqualified verdict, and that will burn Marguerite as a perjurer if her husband loses.

Now the Jain apparatus, which I would like used with some precision. Anekāntavāda holds that reality is many-sided and that no unqualified assertion captures it. Nayavāda treats our judgments as nayas, partial standpoints that are true within their scope but not of the whole; a naya becomes a nayābhāsa, a false or fallacious standpoint, when it is asserted without qualification as the whole truth. Syādvāda, or conditioned predication, prefixes assertions with syāt — in some respect — and its seven modes run through being, non-being, both, and the inexpressible, with avaktavya marking what cannot be jointly stated in a single predication. And on the Jain account only kevala-jñāna, the omniscient cognition of a perfected being, is non-perspectival; everyone else's knowledge is partial and provisional. I stress that anekāntavāda is not ordinary relativism: it is committed to one complex reality and to a disciplined logic of qualified predication, and I will not accept a reading that turns it into the platitude that all accounts are equally valid.

Here is what I want from the reading. First, does the film's three-part structure actually illustrate nayavāda, or does it undermine it? Are the three chapters rival answers to one question — did Jacques rape Marguerite — or answers to different questions, so that the apparent contradiction dissolves? If they are rivals, which are nayas and which are nayābhāsa, and what in the film lets us tell the difference without simply preferring the standpoint we already like? Second, does Marguerite's chapter function as a kevala-like standpoint, a view from nowhere that finally sees the whole? If so, is that a legitimate use of the film's form or a smuggled absolutism, and does Jain philosophy have the resources to resist it? Third, the inexpressible: Jean's mother reveals that she too was raped and never told anyone, a standpoint that never enters the record and has no place in the available language of honor; and Marguerite's account has to be translated into the legal vocabulary of her world before it can be heard at all. Is that a case for avaktavya, or a case of a naya being suppressed rather than expressed? Fourth, the duel. What does it mean, in Jain terms, to settle a many-sided matter by ordeal, and how does that sit with the tradition's association of non-one-sidedness with ahiṃsā, non-violence?

End with what the film adds that the doctrine alone does not. If the film shows something anekāntavāda misses — perhaps that some standpoints are not merely partial but interested, or that the powerful get to have their partial standpoints treated as complete while the powerless get one hearing and a threat of execution — say so plainly, and say whether the doctrine can absorb the objection or has to be amended. Where the film resists the Jain reading, I would rather hear that than a forced fit. Assume I know the film and the basic outlines of Jain philosophy; I need the argument, not summary.
