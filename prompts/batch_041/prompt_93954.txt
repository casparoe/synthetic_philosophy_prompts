I run the behavioral design team at a state benefits agency. We write the letters and texts that try to get people to claim benefits they are owed, attend screenings, and enroll in a retirement savings plan: roughly a dozen new messages a month, each going to hundreds of thousands of households, and every one of them is doing some kind of persuasion — the order of the information, the example we choose, the deadline we print, what goes on the envelope. After a reporter's questions last month, half my team believes we have been manipulating people for years, and the other half believes that if we stopped we would be leaving money and medicine unclaimed. I need a decision procedure we can actually run on each message before it ships. It should be grounded in the distinction between manipulation and legitimate persuasion itself rather than in a reading list, be usable in about twenty minutes by a designer who has never read any philosophy, and be explicit about what counts as a sufficient answer at each step, where it stops with a no, and where it hands the decision to the general counsel or the communications director because the question is one of values rather than of fact. Our current working rule is that a message is manipulative if it conceals something or makes people feel bad, and I think that rule is at once too strict and too loose; the procedure should replace it. Apply the procedure to three messages now in front of us: an accurate social-norm line telling people that most households in their county have already claimed this credit, where we deliberately chose the comparison group that tests best; a real but unrepresentative and moving personal testimonial, chosen because a plainer one tested worse; and a default that pre-enrolls people in a savings plan they may leave at any time. One of these should come out as a no that my team will hate. Finish by telling me what the procedure cannot do and where it will misfire in the hands of a designer who wants a yes. Write it as continuous prose, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables.
