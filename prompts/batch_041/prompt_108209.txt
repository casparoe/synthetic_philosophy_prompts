I have been reading a general-audience book on decision theory, and its treatment of risk-weighted expected utility has left me uneasy. The author's summary runs roughly as follows. Expected utility theory holds that a rational agent cares only about the final outcomes of a gamble and their probabilities, and so it must treat any preference that turns on how the risk is spread as a mistake. Buchak's risk-weighted alternative responds that what people actually care about is the risk itself, and captures this with a risk function that reweights the probabilities of the several outcomes, much as Kahneman and Tversky's prospect theory reweights probabilities and builds in loss aversion. Because expected utility theory cannot represent such attitudes, and because the Allais paradox shows that ordinary people violate it, Buchak concludes that expected utility theory is refuted as an account of rational choice, and that risk-averse agents who decline its axioms are nevertheless fully rational. The theory is therefore best read as a descriptive account of how people in fact choose under risk, and its upshot is that the old ideal of the rational maximizer should be retired.

I do not know this literature well enough to adjudicate, but the passage seems to be doing several things at once, and I cannot tell which of them are the theory's own claims. Please audit the summary for me: which parts would a defender of risk-weighted expected utility accept, which are simplifications that blur distinctions the theory is careful to keep apart, which are misattributions to the theory or to its target, and whether the concluding lesson survives once the corrections are made. Where the author's framing quietly substitutes one theory for another, name the substitution and the dimension along which the two differ.
