I'm prepping a session on material constitution and keep tieing myself in knots over the statue and the clay. I don't want a survey of the literature, I want the conceptual issue itself laid out. Everyone agrees the statue and the lump are made of the same stuff, arranged the same way, in the same place, at the same time, so whatever the disagreement is about, it isn't the material facts. Explain what it is about.

Two things in particular. First, the argument that gets trotted out to show they must be two things: the lump survives being squashed, the statue doesn't, so by Leibniz's Law they're distinct. Why do plenty of people think that move is too quick — what does someone who says they're one thing say is wrong with it? Second, suppose we grant they're distinct. What's supposed to make them distinct, given they've got the same parts in the same arrangement? If the answer is just that they have different persistence conditions, hasn't the problem been restated rather than explained?

Then try it on a case I made up: someone takes an unmodified lump of clay, puts it on a plinth in a gallery, and exhibits it as a sculpture; the lump had been sitting in the studio for a week before that. Squashing it now destroys the sculpture but not the lump, and the sculpture seems to have come into being with no physical change at all. Does that break the one-thing view, or can the one-thing person say the sculpture isn't a thing at all, or that it was there all along? And finally, does any of this matter outside the seminar room, or is it a fight about how we talk rather than about what there is.
