I have a question about the strategy-stealing argument that I suspect I have generalized too far, and I would like you to set me straight.

As I understand it: to prove that the first player wins, you suppose for contradiction that the second player has a winning strategy, then let the first player make an arbitrary opening move and thereafter "steal" that strategy, treating the extra piece as a harmless spare. Since an extra move can only ever help, the first player wins, and the supposition is refuted. From this I draw two conclusions: that any finite, symmetric, two-player game of perfect information must be a first-player win, and that the argument actually delivers the first player's winning strategy.

Two things now puzzle me. First, chess and Go are finite, symmetric, two-player games of perfect information, and the only asymmetry between the players is who moves first; so why has the argument not been used to prove that White — or Black — wins? Second, even in the games where the argument is textbook, such as Chomp and Hex, the first player's winning strategy is still not known in general; yet if the argument establishes that such a strategy exists, why can we not simply read it off?

I am fairly sure I am conflating something, but I cannot see what. Tell me where the reasoning breaks down, what the argument actually establishes in the cases where it works, and precisely why the cases that worry me fall outside its reach. Please be explicit about how confident you are in each claim you make, and flag the single point in your answer about which you are least sure.
