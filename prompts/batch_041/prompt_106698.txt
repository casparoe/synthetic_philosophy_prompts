So here's a thing that's always bugged me and I can never get a straight answer on.

Regulators treat tiny risks as a huge deal once you add them up. A chemical comes along that raises any single person's chance of getting cancer by some microscopic amount, like one in a million, and if a few million people are exposed, the agency will restrict it or ban it — on the reasoning that a few million times one in a million is a real, countable number of cancers. Okay, sure.

But then the same exposed person walks into court and the judge says: you can't show you actually got sick, you can only show your risk ticked up a hair. No injury, no case, come back when you've got a tumor. And that's not one cranky judge, that's basically the standard rule — a bare increase in risk usually isn't a harm you can sue over.

So the state is telling me, out of one side of its mouth, that shaving a hair off each of a million people's life expectancy is worth regulating, and out of the other, that shaving a hair off *my* life expectancy isn't something I've suffered. What gives?

What view of harm and of value does each practice presuppose? Is the regulator quietly committed to harms being addable across people in a way that doesn't care whose they are — a total accounting, not a per-person one — while the court is committed to the opposite, that only what lands on this particular person counts and a small bump doesn't count? Or can you explain both without any deep disagreement underneath — say the court is just hung up on proving causation, or scared of a flood of suits, or making a call about who should pay rather than about what harm is?

Related: does it matter that the regulatory harm is statistical and nobody's identified, while the courtroom wants a victim with a name? And if we took the aggregation side seriously and let people sue over raised risk, paid them in proportion to the risk, ran it as a class — what would actually have to change, and would that be coherent or would it break something else in the law?

Also the courts have this old line about not concerning themselves with trifles, yet the regulators spend their whole day concerning themselves with trifles. Same tiny increments. Opposite instincts.

I'm not after cites or a reading list, just tell me what each side is really assuming, which assumptions are load-bearing vs. just convenient, and where the practices are underdetermined enough that several theories would license them.
