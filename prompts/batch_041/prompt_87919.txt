I teach a graduate seminar on decision theory, and the same objection comes up every year, most recently from a student who is otherwise very good: that Newcomb's problem is really a puzzle about predictors, and that once you strip away the predictor, the simulation, or the backwards causation, what is left is an ordinary problem of dominance in which causal decision theory is plainly correct. I would rather answer that objection with a case than with an argument, so please construct a decision problem to the following specifications. First, nothing in the story predicts, knows, simulates, or is informed about the agent's choice: no predictor, no being, no copy, no backwards causation, no exotic physics. Second, the agent's act and the state of the world that fixes the payoff must nevertheless be strongly correlated, correlated enough that evidential decision theory and causal decision theory recommend different acts (the link need not make the act certain; a robust probabilistic correlation will do). Third, that correlation must be fully explained by a shared upstream cause of both the act and the state, a cause the agent understands and can describe. Fourth, the state must be settled after the act, so that the causalist cannot fall back on the claim that the payoff is already fixed by the time one chooses. Fifth, the case should be realistic, something a person could actually face, and stated in a single paragraph of no more than 150 words.

Then do the work of showing that the construction meets the specification. For each of the two theories, state the act it recommends and give the reasoning that produces that recommendation, being explicit about the conditional probabilities the evidentialist is relying on and about the counterfactual the causalist is relying on. Identify the single feature of the case that makes the theories come apart, and say whether that same feature is what does the work in the original Newcomb problem, that is, whether the predictor's reliability is doing anything beyond generating a correlation between act and state.

Next, describe in two or three sentences a small modification in which the agent acquires a way of decoupling its act from the shared cause, and say what each theory recommends there. I am particularly interested in whether the modification exposes something awkward in the evidentialist's advice: whether it can tell the agent to perform a certain act because that act is evidence of a favourable state without the recommendation undermining the correlation it depends on. Finally, tell me how a causalist would attack the case as a case of deliberation at all rather than a case of prediction, and whether the attack lands. Assume I know the standard positions and want the engineering rather than a survey; if the five constraints cannot all be satisfied at once, say which one fails and why, rather than quietly relaxing it.
