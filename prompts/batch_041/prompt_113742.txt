I teach a 300-level ethics seminar, 16 students, one 75-minute block, and I want to run something that makes the seperateness-of-persons objection to utilitarianism land as something they feel in the room rather than a bullet point they nod at. Below is the rough shape I have in mind. Develop it into a full runnable activity, or replace my mechanism with a better one if it does the same job.

Each student gets a strip of five index cards, face up, one card per stretch of their life, each with a small integer on it (can be negative) -- that's their well-being in that stretch. Their final score is the sum of their own five cards. Nobody's score gets pooled; the numbers on your strip are yours.

Phase A, solo, five minutes: I ask them to move 3 points out of their own period 2 and into their own period 4. Same total, different location in time. Everyone does it instantly, no complaint, no debate.

Phase B, pairs, secret ballot: I hand each pair a policy card that reads, take 3 from player A's period 2 and give 4 to player B's period 4. Net gain of 1 to the world. They vote whether to enact it. Key rule: their A/B assignment is drawn at random *after* the ballots are collected, so nobody is voting for their own pocket.

Phase C, same pair, another ballot: the identical numbers, but now as a within-life card -- take 3 from your own period 2, give 4 to your own period 4. Same total, same shape, same arithmetic. I expect a near-unanimous yes.

Phase D: I reveal that the Phase B card is exactly what a total-sum maximizer is committed to endorsing, and ask them, in small groups, to state the principle that separates B from C, and then to defend that principle against someone who says it's just a bias toward your own timeline.

What I want them to come away seeing: that they themselves aggregate freely across moments within a life and flinch at the same aggregation across lives, and that this asymmetry is the whole load-bearing wall of the objection -- not a slogan about respecting persons, but a real discontinuity in their own reasoning that they now have to either justify or give up.

Give me: materials and setup (how many cards, how the integers are chosen so the sums stay legible), the timeline for the 75 minutes, exact ballot wording for B and C, the reveal script for D kept short, and a debrief that maps each phase onto the actual structure of the objection -- the compensation-within-a-life move versus the compensation-that-lands-in-someone-else's-life move -- without just lecturing. You can name Rawls and Parfit in the debrief; I don't need citations.

Then tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle each thing. My own guesses: someone will say the B transfer is fine as long as A consents, so you'll need to close that off in the rules or tell me how to handle it in the room; and the random A/B draw will push a chunk of the room toward a maximin instinct, which I think is a different objection (Rawls's own, not the separateness one) and I want the debrief to keep those apart rather than let them blur.

Finally, be honest about what this activity cannot reproduce. My worry is that it dramatizes the *asymmetry in our intuitions* and never touches the metaphysical claim underneath -- that persons are genuinely distinct loci of experience in a way that moments within one life are not -- and that if anything it reinforces the boundary rather than letting a Parfit-style reductionist pressure it. If that's right, say so and tell me what the facilitator has to supply in words that the game can't do for them.

Also, when you write this up, tell me how confident you actually are that each piece will work the way I've described, and single out the one point you're least sure about.
