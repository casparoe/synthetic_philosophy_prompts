I'm part of a standing reading group on decision theory and practical rationality, and this term we've each been asked to circulate a short statement of our own position so that the others have something specific to attack. I've been putting mine off, because the more of it I get written down, the less it looks like one position. Here is the list as it stands.

(1) When someone says that intransitive or cyclic preferences are irrational, I take the real content of the charge to be that an agent with such preferences can be walked around a trading cycle, paying a small fee at each step, and end up with exactly what she began with and less money — a money pump. I don't think there is a separate axiom of transitivity doing any work; the exploitability is the whole story. (2) But I also think a pump only indicts an agent who cannot see it coming. An agent who anticipates the sequence can simply decline the first trade, and then nothing leaves her pocket. So the requirement I actually want is that an agent not be exploitable by a sequence of trades that she can foresee and cannot bring herself to refuse. (3) I take diachronic coherence requirements seriously in the credal case: if a bookie can guarantee himself a profit by exploiting the difference between my credences today and my credences tomorrow, betting with me now and again later, then something is wrong with me rather than merely with one of the two bets. (4) I am a resolute chooser about the toxin puzzle. If a billionaire credibly offers me a large sum to form, tonight, the intention to drink a foul but harmless poison in the morning, I take the money and form the intention, and in the morning I drink the poison, even though by then I would very much rather not and nothing has changed except the hour. (5) I think genuine parity is possible: two careers, or the interests of two people, can be related such that neither is better than the other and they are not exactly equally good either. (6) I don't believe in multiple selves. My preferences at twenty and my preferences at sixty are the preferences of one agent, and prudence is not a matter of bargaining with a future stranger. (7) Ulysses-style commitment devices — burning the boats, having a friend change the password, locking the door behind you — are rationally legitimate, and once I have made one I am required to honour it.

What I want is an audit rather than a summary. Which of these actually pull against one another, which only appear to, and how severe is each conflict? Where there is a real conflict, I want to know which item has to be weakened or given up, what the least costly retreat would be, and what that retreat costs me elsewhere on the list. Please rank the conflicts by how much damage they do rather than presenting them as a flat set of tensions, and say explicitly where an item turns out to be doing no work at all. I have a nagging sense that one of the seven does not really belong on the same list as the others, but I can't tell which one, and I would rather you told me than have me guess.
