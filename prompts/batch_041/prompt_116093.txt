I work as a software engineer on a team that builds evaluation suites for language models, and a conversation last week has been nagging at me. We were proposing a set of tests meant to measure whether a model holds false beliefs about its own capabilities — whether it takes itself to be able to do something it cannot, whether it endorses a claim that it will contradict under a different prompt framing, and so on. A colleague with a machine learning research background told me the project rests on a confusion, and offered an analogy he clearly regarded as decisive: attributing beliefs to a language model is like attributing a desire to win to a chess engine, or a belief that the room is warm to a thermostat. These are convenient ways of speaking, he said, and they let you predict what the system will do next, but nobody imagines there is a fact of the matter about what the engine wants or what the thermostat believes; there is a mechanism and there are outputs. So the sentence "the model believes X" is at best shorthand for a disposition to emit certain tokens, and an evaluation suite for false model beliefs is measuring nothing beyond token statistics.

I have no formal philosophy training and I cannot tell whether this is a knockdown argument or a sleight of hand, though my instinct is that the analogy is carrying more weight than he thinks it can. Could you lay out the correspondence explicitly — what is meant to map onto what, taking the engine's desire to win, the thermostat's belief about the temperature, and the model's belief that it can perform some task as the three relata — and then say which side of the analogy is actually doing the work of supporting his conclusion? After that, I would like to know where the mapping breaks in ways that matter for the use he is making of it, as distinct from breaks that are merely incidental. A few things strike me but I cannot assess them properly. The chess engine and the thermostat were built around a single explicit objective and a fixed input representation, whereas a language model is trained on next-token prediction and then steered by whatever prompt it receives, so what it wants or believes appears to depend on the framing it is handed. And unlike the thermostat, its dispositions to assert things shift across paraphrases of the same question, which is precisely the phenomenon our tests were meant to capture. I have also come across the phrase intentional stance in this connection — the idea that treating a system as having beliefs is a stance one adopts for predictive purposes, and that adopting it does not by itself settle whether the resulting ascriptions are literally true or merely convenient — and I would like to know whether my colleague's analogy is a fair statement of that view, a caricature of it, or something else altogether.

Finally, suppose the analogy only partly holds. Does his conclusion survive in weakened form, so that talk of model beliefs is imprecise but harmless, or does it collapse, so that such talk is straightforwardly confused? And if the chess engine is the wrong comparison, what would a better analogy be for the kind of thing a language model's dispositions actually are, and what would that better analogy license us to say about the evaluation suite we wanted to build?
