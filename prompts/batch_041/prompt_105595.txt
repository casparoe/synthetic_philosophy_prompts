Write a dialogue between two philosophers of biology, each fully competent and neither a strawman, on the following question: can a selection process yield genuine reasons where no reasoner exists, or is the notion of a free-floating rationale either empty or a covert form of mentalism?

Anchor the exchange in one concrete case. A larval flatworm infects a small ant, takes up residence in the ant's head, and induces it to climb to the tip of a grass blade and clamp on until dusk, which makes the ant likely to be eaten by the grazing livestock that the flatworm's life cycle requires next. The ant gains nothing and dies; the behavior serves the parasite's lineage, not the ant's.

The first speaker defends the view that there is a bona fide reason in play here — something on the order of climb, or the cycle stops — a consideration that is answerable to how the world actually is, that was shaped by selection, and that is nevertheless entertained by no one: neither the ant nor the flatworm represents anything of the kind. The second speaker holds that this is a projection. Where there is no representing state, there is at most a convenient way of describing the machinery; to call the thing a reason is either to add nothing to the causal facts or to smuggle in a mind that is not present.

Have them press each other on at least these four points. First, whose rationale it is, given that the trait and its fitness consequences belong to the parasite's lineage while the behavior belongs to the ant's nervous system. Second, whether a rationale that no one can be mistaken about — since no one holds it — can do the explanatory work that reasons are supposed to do. Third, what shifts if the parasite is replaced by a self-replicating molecule or by a learned behavior in an animal, and whether the first speaker can draw a principled line between reason and mere cause without borrowing a mind. Fourth, whether the disagreement is substantive or merely about how to talk.

Each speaker should concede ground where the other lands a real blow and should offer a revised formulation in response, rather than restating the opening claim.

Begin directly with the dialogue itself — no preface, no scene-setting beyond what the speakers say to one another, and no closing summary. No citations or references to the literature are needed; argue the substance.
