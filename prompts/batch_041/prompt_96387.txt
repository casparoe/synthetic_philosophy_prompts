Ok so this is a weird one but it's suddenly urgent. I got voluntold onto the bylaws committee for our co-op (48 units, we bought the building a few years back, dont ask) and the current bylaws are a trainwreck, so I've been reading up on self-amending rules and fell down a Nomic-shaped hole. Now my friend Dan, who's in law school, has half the committee convinced that the whole idea of an organization amending its own rules is incoherent, and I need to either refute him or admit he's right.

Here's his argument. Our draft has Clause 14: these bylaws may be amended by a two-thirds vote of the members present at a general meeting. I wanted to protect it, so we added Clause 15: Clause 14 may be amended only by a unanimous vote. Dan says fine, watch.

(1) A change to the bylaws is valid only if the procedure actually used is the procedure authorized by the bylaws that are in force.
(2) Which bylaws are in force at any moment is just whatever valid changes have produced up to that moment.
(3) Picture a meeting where a bare majority votes to replace Clause 14 with a new version letting the bylaws be amended by a bare majority.
(4) To say whether that vote worked, we need to know which Clause 14 was governing the meeting: the old two-thirds one, or the new majority one.
(5) If the old one was governing, the vote failed, since a majority isnt two-thirds, so the new Clause 14 never comes into force.
(6) If the new one was governing, the vote succeeded, so the new Clause 14 is in force.
(7) But which one was governing is exactly what the vote was supposed to settle, and whether the vote settled it depends on which one was governing.
(8) So there's no non-circular answer to whether the vote worked. A rule cannot author its own revision.
(9) So any organization whose rules govern how its rules get changed is running on a fiction, and the only fully coherent setup fixes the amendment procedure outside the system -- a founder, a court, a sovereign, or a revolution.

I want to keep (1) and (2); they read like they're just what "valid" means. And I obviously want to reject the conclusion, since we amend our bylaws all the time and the co-op would fall apart otherwise. So something has to give and I can't see what.

Dan also has a nastier variant: same setup, but the proposed amendment says, in so many words, that it is valid just in case it is valid under the rules as amended by itself. I told him thats obviously cheating but I couldn't say why without just asserting it.

Tell me: is the argument sound? If not, which step is doing the illegitimate work, why does that step look so obviously true, and what do I pay if I give it up? If you think it's actually sound, tell me which of (1), (2), or our everyday practice I should sacrifice and what that costs. And say whether whatever fixes the first argument also fixes Dan's nastier variant or whether thats a separate disease.

Give me the diagnosis as prose, then a short JSON object with exactly these keys: culprit_step (the number, or list of numbers, of the step(s) you'd reject, or null if you'd reject none); one_line_diagnosis (25 words max); why_it_looked_fine (why the guilty step seems self-evident); the_real_problem_or_cost (what's actually wrong with it, or what you pay to keep it); runner_up_target (if it's not the culprit, the step you'd attack next and why); self_amendment_verdict (one of: "coherent", "incoherent", "coherent only under stated conditions"); does_it_also_handle_variant_b (yes/no plus a sentence); and still_open (the part of this you think is genuinely unsettled).
