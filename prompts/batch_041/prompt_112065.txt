I'm a postdoc at a university center for ethics and computing, and I've been drafted to give the opening framing remarks -- eight to ten minutes, spoken, no slides -- at a two-day invitation-only workshop we're hosting next month. The room is about thirty people and splits three ways: alignment researchers from two labs, who hear the workshop's question and immediately reach for the social-choice toolkit, as if the task were to aggregate preferences, pick a rule, and mind the familiar impossibility results; political philosophers, who hear the word neutrality and assume the hard part was settled somewhere in the liberal tradition long ago; and a handful of funders and civil servants who need to leave with a clear sense of what the actual open question is. I have to give them shared vocabulary without pre-empting anyone's paper, and the organizers have promised there will be no house line -- which means the remarks have to pose the problem sharply while remaining genuinely uncommitted about how it comes out.

Here is what I want them to do. I have a concrete case I'd like to install as the running example for both days: a tutoring model deployed across a large, heterogeneous school district, which has to decide what to teach a nine-year-old about human origins, and about sex and gender, when the child's parents, the child herself, the school board, and the district's own curriculum guidelines do not agree. Every abstract question we care about -- whose values, aggregated how, constrained by what -- shows up in that one deployment, and I don't think the standard moves survive contact with it: there is no single principal, no exit, no amendment process, and no way for the model to be neutral that is not also a decision. My co-organizer keeps insisting that the whole debate is confused because it conflates two different things -- the values a system is optimized for and the values a system is accountable to, which can come apart in both directions -- and that once you separate them, the question of whose values an aligned system should serve turns out to be at least three questions wearing one coat. I half believe her, and I want the remarks to test that suspicion rather than assert it, which is itself a constraint: the case has to be left genuinely open, since settling it would commit us to a position on neutrality that the next two days are supposed to be arguing about.

Write the remarks. They should make the room a little uncomfortable in a productive way -- I don't want a tour of the standard menu of aggregation rules, and I don't want a survey of positions with names attached to them. I want the concrete case doing the real work, the distinctions that matter surfaced cleanly enough that the philosophers can't wave them off as engineering and the engineers can't wave them off as hand-waving, and an honest close about what two days of this can and cannot settle. It is spoken, so no citations, no reading list, and nothing that assumes anyone has the literature at their fingertips; keep it to what can actually be said out loud in ten minutes, and don't let the funders in the back feel lectured at.
