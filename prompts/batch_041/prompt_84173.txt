I spent thirty-odd years as a fisheries biologist — salmon for most of it, then a long stretch running a langoustine holding and export facility on the west coast — and since I packed it in I've been doing a part-time MA in philosophy, which has been more fun than I expected and also more irritating. The irritating bit is animal welfare. I keep reading the welfare literature and the aquaculture codes side by side, and I've got a half-formed idea I'd like you to turn into a proper two-page research proposal, for the little fellowship my old department runs for people coming in from other fields (one year, part-time, no teaching, so call it eight or nine months of actual work).

Here's the half-formed idea. When a code says an animal has to be stunned before killing, or mustn't be handled in ways that cause avoidable suffering, the operative notion is supposed to be the animal's experience — whether there's still anyone home to have one. But everything we can actually measure in a fish or a crustacean is responsiveness: reflexes, withdrawal, righting, all the stuff that stops when you chill them or dose them. In mammals those two things track each other closely enough that nobody loses sleep over it. In decapods, and I suspect in some fish, they come apart much more than the literature is willing to admit — you can get an animal that has stopped behaving like a creature with a point of view while something is still doing thoroughly unpleasant things in its nervous system, and the reverse. If that's right then the word unconsciousness is doing work in these codes that it can't actually do, and the precautionary principle gets waved about to cover the gap. I can't seperate the two strands in my head yet, and I'm honestly not sure wether this is one question or two.

What I want from the proposal: a single sharp question stated so it has a determinate answer (something like — is the notion of unconsciousness that slaughter and handling rules lean on even well-defined for decapods, and if it isn't, what should the rule be instead?), an account of why the existing positions in the welfare literature don't settle it, and then the method — what I'd actually do, in what order, given that I'm a philosopher now and not doing lab work. Be honest in it that this is conceptual rather than empirical, and say why that's the right way in rather than yet another review of the behavioural evidence, which I've read and which I don't think gets you anywhere on this. Say what a real result would look like as opposed to a tidy restatement of what everyone already thinks, and what would count as the project failing. And say something about scope: if it's too big for a year part-time, put in the proposal what I should cut.

No references or reading list, please — I'll fill those in myself and I'd rather you didn't invent any. British spelling throughout, it's going to a UK funder.
