I would like help with an argument that a colleague of mine has been defending, and that I am confident proves too much, though I cannot put my finger on where it goes wrong. Here it is, as she states it. (1) To complete a sequence of actions is to perform each of them and then to be finished. (2) If a sequence of actions has no last member, then no action is the one whose completion finishes the sequence, so the sequence is never finished. (3) Now consider a switch flipped at the half-minute mark, again at three-quarters, again at seven-eighths, and in general at 1 − 2^−n minutes, so that the flips have no last member. (4) Hence the flipping is never finished, and the position of the switch at the one-minute mark is not settled by the description. (5) Nor is it settled by the history, since the history up to that moment consists of exactly those flips and every flip is followed by another. (6) A state that neither the description nor the history settles is either uncaused or impossible, and she takes uncaused events to have no place in a physical theory. (7) Therefore the switch case is impossible; and since nothing in the reasoning depended on switches, no physical description calling for infinitely many operations within a finite time — what she calls a supertask — can be coherent.

The conclusion strikes me as far too strong. A ball released above the ground and bouncing to half its previous height each time, with each bounce taking less time than the one before, makes infinitely many bounces within a finite time and then lies at rest; its height and its speed both tend to zero, and if its trajectory is required to be continuous, its state at the limit is fixed, at the ground and at rest. That seems to me a perfectly respectable kinematic description, and my colleague's reasoning would condemn it along with the switch. At the same time I do not want to give up the sense that the switch's position at the one-minute mark really is unsettled: there is no last flip, the sequence of positions has no limit, and the description supplies nothing else to consult. A friend suggests that any answer one wanted — up, down, or neither — could be secured by stipulating a rule, as one might stipulate that the switch is up at the one-minute mark just in case the number of flips is even; he says this shows the description to be merely incomplete rather than the process impossible, and I cannot tell whether that saves the argument or surrenders it.

Tell me which of the numbered steps I should give up, why it looked so compelling, and what giving it up costs. If I have to choose, I would rather keep the bouncing ball and abandon the claim that completion requires a final act, but I want to know what else falls with that claim — in particular, whether I can still say that the switch case is defective, and in what sense, once I have allowed that infinitely many operations can be completed at a time containing no last one. Please answer in continuous prose, without headings, bulleted or numbered lists, bold text, or tables; write it as you would a long letter, though you may of course refer to the steps by their numbers inside a sentence.
