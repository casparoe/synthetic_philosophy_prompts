You are a philosopher of language and metaethics serving as the instructor of record for an upper-level undergraduate course, and you are grading one student's final exam. Your task is to evaluate that student's answer against the question and criteria below, at the standard of a rigorous course. Before you assign anything, work the question yourself: sketch what a fully correct answer would contain, identify the subtle points at which a superficially plausible answer can go wrong, and only then turn to the student's response. When you assess the answer, proceed point by point. For each element of the question—(a) the statement of the problem, the distinction it demands, and the explanation of the threat; (b) the evaluation of the proposal—say explicitly whether the student has it right, has it wrong, or has left it underdeveloped, and support each judgment with a specific reason drawn from the text of the answer. The question on the exam:

"(a) Expressivists hold that a moral sentence such as 'Lying is wrong' does not state a fact but expresses the speaker's non-cognitive attitude, for example disapproval. The Frege–Geach problem is usually introduced as a difficulty for this view. State the problem as precisely as you can, distinguishing the difficulty about unasserted (embedded) occurrences of moral sentences from the separate difficulty about negation, and explain why the problem threatens expressivism.

(b) Now consider the following proposal: 'The meaning of a moral sentence is given by the pattern of inferences it licenses. "Lying is wrong" means what it does because of which sentences it commits one to and which it rules out. Because a sentence licenses the same inferences whether or not it is asserted, expressivism faces no special problem with unasserted contexts.' Does this proposal solve the Frege–Geach problem? Explain."

General grading criteria: accuracy in stating the problem and in drawing the distinction the question demands; precision in explaining why the problem is a problem; a fair and well-argued assessment of the proposal in (b), including whether and how it meets the difficulty; and overall clarity and coherence of the argument.

The student's answer:

"Expressivism says that calling something wrong is expressing disapproval rather than describing a fact. The Frege–Geach problem is that this cannot handle moral words in unasserted contexts. Take the argument: If lying is wrong, then getting your little brother to lie is wrong; lying is wrong; therefore getting your little brother to lie is wrong. This is plainly valid by modus ponens. But in the first premise 'lying is wrong' is not asserted; it is only supposed. Since nothing is asserted, nothing is expressed—there is no attitude on display. So the expressivist must say that 'wrong' in the first premise does not mean what it means in the second, and an argument cannot be valid if its words change meaning between the premises and the conclusion. Hence expressivism cannot even explain the validity of this simple argument.

This is the problem about unasserted contexts. It should be separated from the problem about negation. The negation problem asks what 'lying is not wrong' means. It cannot be the mere absence of disapproval, because that is not a way of thinking about lying at all; and if it is disapproval of disapproval, the expressivist seems to be describing an attitude instead of expressing one.

The proposal in (b) is a promising response. If the meaning of a moral sentence is fixed by the inferences it licenses, then 'lying is wrong' licenses the same inferences in the first premise as in the second, so it carries the same meaning in both, and the modus ponens argument comes out valid. Inferential role is not something that switches off when a sentence is not asserted, so the unasserted occurrence is no longer a special case. This is essentially Blackburn's quasi-realist strategy: moral sentences come out true or false, with truth-conditions of the ordinary descriptive kind, and that is exactly what the expressivist needs in order to explain validity.

The only thing the proposal leaves out is a reason why these inferences rather than others are the ones that fix the meaning. This can be supplied by the attitudes we actually have: we disapprove of lying, and we are disposed to disapprove of getting our little brother to lie whenever we disapprove of lying. Since this expressivist addition completes the picture, the proposal does answer the Frege–Geach problem."

Grade the answer.
