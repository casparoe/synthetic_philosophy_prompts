I'm teaching an upper-level seminar this term called "Minds, Machines, and Freedom," and I'm building the take-home final. I want one essay question on sphexishness, and I'm already dreading the answers I'll get, because most of them will just retell the digger-wasp story and then announce "so the wasp is a mindless automaton and we are not." You probably know the story — the wasp provisions her burrow with a paralyzed cricket, and if you nudge the cricket a few inches while she's inside inspecting the burrow, she comes back out, drags it back to the threshold, and runs the entire routine over from the top, as many times as you care to move it. That's a recitation, not an answer. What I actually want them to wrestle with is whether sphexishness is a fact about an organism or a fact about a level of description, and whether the two can come apart. So I want to seed the question with a scenario of my own rather than let them coast on the wasp.

Here's the setup I want to give them. Invent a creature — call it the "two-storey" creature — that is genuinely Popperian about which routine to run: it can mentally simulate several candidate courses of action, weigh them against each other, pick one, and revise its policy when the outcomes disappoint. But each of the routines it can select is itself as rigid as the wasp's: once it commits, a small perturbation that's irrelevant to the goal sends it back to the start of that subroutine, and it runs the same sequence again, indefinitely, without updating. Suppose it's also Gregorian, in the sense that it plans with the help of culturally inherited tools and notations. Is this creature sphexish? At which level? Does being Popperian at the top redeem it, or does the rigidity of the parts infect the whole? (For the record, in case anyone in the room has forgotten: on the ladder I gave them, Darwinian creatures are fixed by selection, Skinnerian ones learn by reinforcement within a lifetime, Popperian ones let hypotheses die in their stead instead of dying themselves, and Gregorian ones offload thinking onto external tools — language, notation, instruments.) I want the essay to do four things: (1) say what work the word "sphexish" is actually doing — is it a claim about mechanism, about insensitivity to a particular kind of change, or about how an observer would predict the thing — and defend one reading against the others; (2) apply the four-grade ladder to the two-storey creature and say whether it breaks the ladder or just straddles two rungs; (3) answer whether a creature can be sphexish "all the way up," i.e. sphexish about its own rules for revising its rules, and what that would mean; and (4) close by saying whether any of this should worry someone who thinks human freedom consists in being a Popperian or Gregorian creature, or whether the whole worry is a category error. Sixty minutes, about 800 words, 25 marks.

Please write me the question as I'd actually hand it to the students, then a model answer of maybe 600-700 words, and then a marking scheme that separates an A from a B by naming the specific moves a top answer has to make and the specific mistakes that should cost marks — things like conflating sphexishness with determinism, or assuming that Popperian simulation buys you flexibility when the simulator's option-set is itself fixed. And keep it all self-contained: don't lean on quotations or citations, since they can't look anything up and I don't want them guessing at who said what. The point of the question is the argument, not the sources.
