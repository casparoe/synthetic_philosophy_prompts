I've been reading up on philosophy of medicine for a seminar and I've come down on the naturalist side of the health/disease debate — the picture (I think it's basically Boorse's) where whether somthing counts as a disease is a purely descriptive, value-free matter: a part of you is diseased when it's doing its job less well than is statistically typical for your reference class (species, age, sex, etc.), where the "job" is whatever the part contributes to survival and reproduction, and health is just statistically normal functioning. So on this view "disease" is a biological fact, and calling something a disease isn't (or isn't just) a way of saying we dislike it — the valuing comes later, when we decide whether to treat it. The normativists who insist disease is inescapably value-laden seem to me to be overcomplicating things. But I want to stress-test this, so: what would count against naturalism? Give me the real defeaters, ranked from most to least decisive, not just the textbook "but what about X" stuff.

Here's the case that's been bugging me, and I think it bites deeper than the usual objections. Take reading. As far as I know nobody thinks the capacity to read was shaped by natural selection — it's too recent, and it's parasitic on visual and linguistic systems that were. Yet we talk about dyslexia as a disorder, and we'd say a kid who can't decode words the way her peers can has something wrong with her that's worth diagnosing and treating. But if "disease" means statistically subnormal functioning of a part relative to its job, and the job of a mechanism is fixed by what it was selected for, then reading has no job, so there's nothing for it to fail at, so dyslexia comes out not-a-disease — which seems plainly wrong. I don't think you can patch this by saying the job is just "whatever the part typically does in the population," because then the reference-class problem comes back worse. And that problem is lurking anyway: imagine a town where a pollutant means basically everyone's thyroid runs low from birth — is that a town full of diseased people, or a town of people whose thyroids are statistically normal for that town? The naturalist owes me a principled, non-arbitrary account of the "right" reference class and environment, and I suspect any such account smuggles in a value judgment about which comparisons matter.

So walk me through it. Rank the objections from fatal to merely awkward, tell me which ones a committed naturalist would themselves have to admit actually count against the view (versus which they can dismiss as changing the subject), and be honset about whether the view can be patched each time without collapsing into the normativist position in disguise — or into something so hedged that it stops predicting anything and can't be falsified at all. If you think it ends up well-insulated, say that straight, and tell me whether that's a genuine problem for a view like this or just the price of any general theory. American English, please.
