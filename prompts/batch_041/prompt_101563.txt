Godel's incompleteness theorems show that in any formal system rich enough to describe arithmetic there are true statements the system cannot prove, and that such a system can never prove its own consistency. The upshot is that mathematics can never be completed — there is a permanent limit to what logic can achieve. And since any computer, and by extension any theory we can write down precisely enough to count as a formal system, is just such a thing, there will always be truths that outrun our best machines and our best science. That is why Godel gets invoked so often to show that the human mind cannot be a computer: we can see that the undecidable sentence is true even though the machine cannot prove it. The whole business rests on self-reference, the same trick that lies behind the liar paradox — the sentence more or less says of itself that it is not provable.
