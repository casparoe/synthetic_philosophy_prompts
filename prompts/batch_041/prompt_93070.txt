I'm putting together the reading packet for the first meeting of a seminar on the ethics of killing, and I've decided to open with a piece of fiction rather than with an argument, because the difficulty I want my students to feel is easier to dramatize than to state. The difficulty is this. Nearly everyone in the room accepts that there is a constraint against killing — that you must not kill an innocent person even to save five, and that you must not kill even to head off a great many killings of the same kind. But when I ask them why, they reach for a justification that seems to eat the constraint from the inside: they say that killing is a terrible thing, that a world with fewer killings in it is better than a world with more. If that is the reason, though, the constraint begins to look perverse. It appears to rank the one killing I might do above the many I might prevent — as though the cleanness of my own hands outweighed the lives of strangers — and there is something distasteful about a morality that treats my not being a killer as more urgent than other people's not being killed. Yet when the constraint is dropped, what remains is a permission to use people as instruments whenever the arithmetic is favorable, which is precisely the conclusion we recoiled from in the first place. I want a story that makes a reader feel both of these pressures at once instead of quietly siding with one.

Please write a short story, of roughly a thousand to fifteen hundred words, built to do that. I'm partial to the following seed, though you may adapt it: a near-future city in which licensed intermediaries will perform, for a fee, the acts their clients would rather not perform themselves — and in which the law, the insurers, and the neighbors' gossip all treat the client as having merely purchased a service. Your protagonist has spent her life holding that some acts are not to be done, even to prevent worse ones; she is now the only person positioned to stop a massacre, and the only lever within reach is to commission a single killing. Keep three things visible as the story moves: why she will not do it with her own hands, and whether that refusal survives contact with what she is about to arrange; whether commissioning the act is a way of escaping the constraint or merely a more expensive way of violating it; and the fact that the people she may fail to save are particular people with names and habits, not a number. Let the story turn on the moment when she must choose among doing it herself, having it done, and letting it happen, and let at least one character state the uncomfortable thought that her scruples are costing other people their lives — while someone else insists that this is exactly what a conscience is for.

I don't want the piece to resolve the puzzle for me; an ending that leaves a reader genuinely unsure which of the two answers is the worse one would be ideal. And please resist the temptation to have anyone stand up and explain the dilemma in a speech — if a character articulates it, let another character answer with the horn they've left out, so that the argument stays alive in the scene rather than being settled by the narrator.
