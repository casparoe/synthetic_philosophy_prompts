The prof is strict about written comments beacuse students can appeal their grades, so I need to be able to say exactly what I'm docking and why, part by part. The question was out of 20, split 8 for (a), 6 for (b), 6 for (c). Background, in case it matters: the lecture defined the equation as the claim that for every credence function P and any sentences X, Y of the language with P(X) > 0, P(X→Y) = P(Y|X), where → is a two-place indicative conditional operator; it was presented as a thesis about all credence functions, and the students were told to assume → is a genuine sentence-forming operator, so that X→Y has a truth value at each world and can be embedded and supposed like any other sentence. They had a lecture on the triviality results and read a couple of papers, so the answer is supposed to engage with the standard responses rather than reinvent the wheel. Here's the question:

(a) Reconstruct the argument that the equation cannot hold in general. You may illustrate with the following credence function on a language with two atomic sentences A and B: P(A∧B) = 0.1, P(A∧¬B) = 0.2, P(¬A∧B) = 0.3, P(¬A∧¬B) = 0.4. (b) State precisely what the resulting triviality is. (c) Does the argument show that indicative conditionals have no truth conditions? Explain what you take the upshot to be, and mention at least the main alternatives.

And here's the script:

The argument is a reductio on the equation, i.e. on the claim that the probability of A→B is the conditional probability P(B|A). Fix any credence function P and take sentences A and B with P(A), P(B), P(A∧B) and P(A∧¬B) all positive, and consider the conditional A→B. By the probability calculus, P(A→B) = P(A→B|B)·P(B) + P(A→B|¬B)·P(¬B). Now P(A→B|B) is the probability of the conditional given B, and the conditional probability of A→B on B is just P(B|A∧B), which is 1, so P(A→B|B) = 1; the same reasoning gives P(A→B|¬B) = P(B|A∧¬B) = 0. So P(A→B) = 1·P(B) + 0·P(¬B) = P(B). Combining that with the equation, P(B|A) = P(B) for arbitrary A and B, which is to say that every sentence is probabilistically independent of every other sentence. Taking B = A gives P(A|A) = 1 = P(A), so any sentence with positive probability has probability 1: the only credence functions that satisfy the equation at all are the degenerate ones that assign every sentence 0 or 1. That's the triviality. It's easy to see on the numbers: P(B) = 0.4, but P(B|A) = 0.1/0.3 = 1/3, so if the equation held then P(A→B) would have to be both 0.4 and 1/3, which is impossible — the equation fails here, and by the argument above it fails for every non-degenerate credence function. Note that the two steps in the middle used nothing beyond the ordinary definition of conditional probability and the equation itself, so nothing special is being assumed about how conditionals behave when they're embedded or supposed, and the result is correspondingly general. For (c): the proof started from exactly the assumption that A→B is a sentence with a truth value and a probability, and it derived an absurdity, so that assumption is what has to go. Any truth-conditional account of indicative conditionals is therefore untenable. The live options are the ones that stop treating conditionals as having truth conditions at all — reading 'if A then B' as expressing a high conditional credence, or a conditional assertion, so that its 'probability' is really a degree of assertability or acceptance, and the equation survives as a thesis about that — or else a semantics on which conditionals are simply not in the business of being true or false. It's tempting to think you could keep the truth conditions and just deny the equation, but the equation isn't an optional extra sitting on top of the truth-conditional story; it's what it means to say that the probability of the conditional is the conditional probability, and if you drop it there's nothing left for the truth conditions to do.
