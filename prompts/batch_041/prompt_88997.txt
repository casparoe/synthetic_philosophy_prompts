For a political theory seminar I'm taking this term, I've been reading around the problem of dirty hands, and I've reached the point where I want to step back and ask what an account of it would even have to accomplish before I can judge any of the candidates on offer. So please write me the desiderata: the full specification that any adequate theory of dirty hands must meet. I want the requirements themselves, each paired with the case or consideration that motivates it, rather than a survey of who has said what — the purpose is a seminar paper that has to survive cross-examination from both the Kantian and the utilitarian in the room, so the standard of adequacy matters more to me than any particular verdict.

The theory has to earn its keep on cases like these: a leader who must authorize the torture of a suspect to stop a bomb that will otherwise kill many; a legislator who lies, or breaks a promise to a colleague, to secure a just and necessary law; a wartime leader who orders the bombing of a city knowing it will kill civilians; and — the case I care most about — the person who refuses to get their hands dirty, lets a great harm occur, and is admired for their integrity. It also has to survive the mirror-image worry that the "necessity" in these stories is often manufactured after the fact to license what the actor wanted to do anyway, and to say something about the victims: what is owed to the person who is sacrificed, and whether their consent could ever matter. I'd like the list to distinguish dirty hands from ordinary moral tragedy where no one is at fault, from a merely hard choice among permissible options, and from the case where an actor does the right thing and only happens to feel bad about it; it should also keep separate the agent who is dirtied and the institution that authorizes or profits from the act, and treat the guilt as a genuine moral remainder rather than a cost to be weighed.

Then the part I most want: flag which of these requirements probably cannot be satisfied together, and say why — my suspicion is that the demand that the act be genuinely wrong and the demand that it nevertheless be the right thing to do are pulling against each other, and that whatever reconciles them will also decide whether the theory collapses into consequentialism or into an all-purpose excuse. A sentence or two on how the leading positions (the strict prohibition view, the consequentialist view, and the "tragic remainder" views) fare against the finished list is welcome, but keep the weight on the criteria. A table is fine if it helps. End with a single line beginning "Verdict:" that states, in one sentence, whether the list is jointly satisfiable and which requirement does the most to rule out the popular accounts.
