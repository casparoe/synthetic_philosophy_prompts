I spent most of last night arguing with two friends—one a committed degrowth theorist, the other an environmental lawyer who thinks regulation and industrial policy should lead—about whether carbon pricing can bear the weight of rapid decarbonization. I came away defending a hybrid I have not seen defended in quite this form, and I want to know what would count against it. Call it price-plus-ratchet. It has four moving parts: a universal carbon fee levied at extraction or import with a border adjustment; an automatic escalator tied not to territorial emissions but to a rolling five-year average of consumption-based emissions; a hard cumulative cap on covered emissions, so the fee is one instrument among others rather than the sole driver; and a per-capita dividend plus a development carve-out for countries below a threshold. It contains no degrowth target, no technology bans, and no sectoral industrial policy beyond the border adjustment and the carve-out. My degrowth friend says the escalator will be gamed by offshoring and that the cap merely relocates the growth assumption; my lawyer friend says the border adjustment will be captured or struck down and that the dividend will be politically fragile. I want to know which of their objections has teeth and which are question-begging.

What would count against price-plus-ratchet? I want the defeaters sorted by how decisive they would be: empirical results about leakage, rebound, elasticity, revenue recycling, and the green paradox; normative objections about historical responsibility, distributive justice, and whether consumption-based accounting unfairly penalizes net importers; institutional objections about regulatory capture, the interaction between the fee and the cumulative cap, and the legal durability of the border adjustment; and strategic objections from degrowth, namely that any pricing scheme presupposes the growth it is supposed to discipline. For each defeater, say whether a committed defender would concede it as a genuine cost or dismiss it as an external criticism, and whether it would be fatal, merely awkward, or absorbable by a minor amendment. Tell me what this hybrid predicts that a pure carbon tax and a pure degrowth program do not, and where those predictions could fail. If the position turns out to be insulated against every possible result, say so plainly and say whether that is a vice for a policy proposal of this kind.

I do not need citations, a bibliography, or a reading list; I need the map of exposure. If some of these defeaters are already visible in existing carbon-pricing experiments, say which ones and why they matter, but reason from the mechanism rather than from names. I am especially interested in whether the consumption-based escalator creates a perverse incentive to offshore dirty production and then import, and whether the hard cumulative cap saves the view from that problem or only relocates it. Finish with a short ranking: which single consideration would most likely make me abandon the view, and which I should treat as a cost I can live with.
