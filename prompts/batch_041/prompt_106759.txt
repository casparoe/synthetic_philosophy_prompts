I write about science and ideas for a general-readership magazine, and I am at work on a long piece about the recurring quarrel over whether culture can be a cause — whether the fact that a group of people holds a certain set of values can explain why they act, organize their economies, or build their institutions the way they do. The Weberian Protestant-ethic thesis is the canonical instance, and I do not want the piece to spend its energy relitigating that one case. What I want is to be able to state, on the page, what any thesis of that shape would have to deliver before it earns the word explanation. So give me the desiderata.

Please write the requirements list for a theory that treats a culture's values as a cause of some social or economic outcome: the phenomena it has to account for, the cases it must get right, the distinctions it cannot afford to collapse, and the findings from neighboring disciplines it must be consistent with — each requirement paired with the consideration that motivates it, and a note on which are non-negotiable and which are merely preferences of a particular camp.

Two things I would especially like you to press on. First, a distinction I keep groping toward and cannot get clean: a theory that explains an outcome by the content of a value people hold, versus one that explains it by the fact that the value is widely shared or socially enforced, versus one that explains it by the value operating not as a motive in anyone's head but as a filter that selects among practices already available. Tell me whether these are three genuinely different explanatory claims, whether they impose different requirements, and whether any of the familiar accounts quietly trades on the ambiguity. Second, the demand that such a theory be able to distinguish the case in which the value causes the outcome from the case in which both the value and the outcome are downstream of something else — an institutional arrangement, a legal regime, a pattern of material incentives — and whether there is any requirement on the list that does the most to rule out the popular accounts. If some subset of the requirements cannot be jointly satisfied, that is the result I most want to see, so say so plainly.

I would also like a short note on how the main families of answer — the ones that treat values as exogenous causes, the ones that treat culture as itself an equilibrium of repeated interaction, and the ones that deny that values explain anything that institutions cannot — fare against the list. Keep that part brief; the criteria are where I need the weight, since the scorecard leans on my knowing the contenders and the criteria are what I will actually use.

Finally, separate for me what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who work on this. If a requirement looks like common ground but is in fact disputed, or if something I have taken for granted is a live disagreement, flag it rather than smoothing it into a consensus.
