Not sure this is the sort of thing you take questions on, but here goes. I retired a few years back from field biology — I spent most of my working life doing population surveys of beetles across dry savanna, which is about as far from moral philosophy as you can get — and since then I've been sitting in on a seminar on transitional justice run by a couple of law people and a historian at the local university. Every few weeks we do a moot amnesty hearing: one of us plays the amnesty board of an invented commission (we model it loosely on real ones but change every name and detail), and we're handed the board's past rulings plus a fresh case, and we have to decide the fresh case the way that board would, using only its own record. This week it's my turn and I'm stuck, so I'm taking a shortcut and asking you.

Here's the board's record as we've got it. A teenage conscript who beat detainees at a checkpoint, testified fully and named his sergeant: amnesty granted. A captain who ran a small prison and ordered the beatings, testified fully and named his colonel and the minister above him — the victims' families objected and the board granted it anyway. An interior minister who signed the detention decrees, testified but refused to name the council members who directed him, and kept invoking state necessity: denied and referred for prosecution. A colonel who ordered a village massacre, then destroyed the records and gave shifting, evasive testimony: denied. A military doctor who falsified the death certificates of prisoners who died under torture, testified fully and named his commander: granted. Now the case I actually have to decide. A woman who was a mid-ranking intelligence officer in charge of an interrogation unit where torture was routine. She has testified fully and — this is the odd part — she named not only her own chain of command but the foreign intelligence service that was paying and directing her unit. Two things muddy it, though: while she was running the unit she also pocketed money and jewelry taken off detainees, so some of what she did looks like ordinary theft dressed up as political work; and several of the victims' families have announced they'll refuse any amnesty for her.

I suspect the board is running one of a few different rules and I definately can't tell which. What would it do with her, and why — and more than the verdict, which rule do you think it's really been following, what are the other rules its past rulings fit just as well, and what single new case would tell those rules apart? I keep flip-flopping between "she told them everything, so she gets amnesty like the conscript and the captain" and "she ran the unit and she was lining her own pockets, so she goes the way of the minister and the colonel."
