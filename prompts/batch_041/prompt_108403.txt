I am presenting a paper at a comparative genocide workshop next month. My thesis is that atrocity comparison is neither impossible nor obscene if it is reconstructed as a partial ordering rather than a ranking. On my view, which I call the dominance test, atrocity A is worse than atrocity B only when A imposes strictly greater constraint on agency along every dimension on an independently justified list—duration, scope, reversibility, targeting of group identity, and destruction of the conditions for collective self-determination—and when A does not merely add a new dimension of harm. Comparisons that are not domination relations are not failures of nerve or acts of sacrilege; they are indeterminate. The audience is mostly historians of mass violence, several of whom work on colonial atrocities and on Holocaust memory, plus two analytic ethicists who work on value commensurability and a political theorist who advises a reparations commission. Prepare my Q&A sheet: the objections I will actually get from this room, ordered by how likely and how damaging they are, phrased the way each faction would phrase them, each with a two-minute reply and a note on where the reply is weakest. Tell me what to concede up front, what to hold, and which single reply has to land for the paper to survive. Include the objection I am hoping nobody asks. I do not need a defense of the thesis; I need the questions and what to say back.
