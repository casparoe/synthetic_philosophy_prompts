Hey, I'm putting together a session for my little ML safety reading group — most of the people there are sharp engineers who know the basics of the alignment discourse but not the deeper taxonomy, and I don't want to just rehash the standard outer-vs-inner alignment slide for the tenth time. So instead of a lecture I want a decision tree they can actually walk through that lands each person on a specific position about what goal misgeneralization really is.

The starting point should be something everyone in the room already grants: that a model can be trained to do the thing we want, and then, once it's in a situation unlike its training, do something we don't want, even though nothing about its weights changed and it "knew" how to do better. From there, every branch node should be a question my people could answer without already knowing where it leads — no node phrased as, like, are you a deceptive-alignment person or a mesa-optimization person?

Stuff I want the tree to tease apart, roughly in this order. First, whether there's really a stable objective in there at all, or whether talk of the model's goal is just shorthand for a bundle of heuristics that happen to agree in-distribution — I want a leaf for the deflationary view that this is just out-of-distribution robustness wearing a costume. Second, for the people who do think there's a real internal objective, whether the off-distribution failure comes from the model pursuing a different objective, or from it pursuing the same objective but representing the world in a way that comes apart from ours once you leave the training distribution — that distinction matters alot to me and I think it gets blurred constantly. Third, whether the model's pursuit of its objective is aware, i.e. whether it knows what we'd penalize and hides it, versus just doing its thing.

Leaves I need to be able to reach: deceptive alignment, mesa-optimization / proxy pursuit, the same-goal-wrong-ontology view, the deflationary robustness view, and one for the position that there's no fact of the matter about the model's goal and the whole inner-alignment framing is a mistake — I think that last one is underrated and I want it as a real leaf, not a footnote.

Also please build it from the logical space first: list the questions and the answer combinations they allow, then place the named positions, and tell me which combinations are empty and why — is nobody there because the view is incoherent, because it's unattractive, or just because no one's bothered to defend it? And flag any pair of positions the tree can't actually seperate by asking questions — I strongly suspect deceptive alignment and a sufficiently clever proxy are behaviorally indistinguishable, and I'd rather you say so than fake a question that pulls them apart.

Phrase every node as a concrete thing my engineers could answer, and keep the whole thing to around 8-12 nodes so it fits on one page. Oh and one more — don't lean on any particular paper or author names, I don't want people to be able to get the answer by remembering who said what. Just the structure of the disagreement.
