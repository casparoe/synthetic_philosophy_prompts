Take the Black Death — the pandemic that reached Europe in 1347 and is commonly estimated to have killed between a third and a half of the population within a few years — and analyse it as a case of a non-human cause acting as an historical actor. I want conceptual analysis, not epidemiology or historiography, and I want it organised around a distinction I should like you to adopt and then test to destruction.

The distinction is between three roles a non-human cause can occupy in an episode. As a trigger, it sets off a sequence that differently constituted human actors could have absorbed or deflected. As an amplifier, it intensifies a conflict or vulnerability that was already structurally present, without determining how that conflict is resolved. As an author, it fixes the outcome more or less independently of what the human actors do. Much of the writing that treats climate, disease, and catastrophe as historical actors, I suspect, has evidence for the first two roles and a vocabulary appropriate only to the third.

(i) Assign the plague to a role, and answer the obvious objection that a pathogen which kills a third of a population is plainly an author.

(ii) Test the assignment against the divergence between Western and Eastern Europe. In the West, the labour shortage is standardly said to have strengthened the peasantry and to have contributed to the erosion of serfdom, notwithstanding the efforts of landlords and legislatures to hold wages down. In the East, the aftermath of the same shortage is standardly said to have run the other way, towards the imposition or intensification of serfdom. If one non-human cause, acting on broadly comparable populations, issues in opposite institutional outcomes, what becomes of the claim that it acted?

(iii) Say what this shows about the determinist impulse — the inclination to treat non-human causes as determinants of historical outcomes. Is the divergence a refutation, a boundary condition, or a case the theory never anticipated?

(iv) Finally, the question underneath: when historians say that a plague or a drought did something, is the agency-talk a dispensable metaphor, or does the explanatory work require it? Take a position, and identify a historical cause that fits none of the three roles.
