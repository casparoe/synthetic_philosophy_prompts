For the last several weeks I have been sitting in on a reading group on self-knowledge, and one of the regulars keeps pressing a thesis that I find at once seductive and slippery. His claim is that our own mental lives are luminous: whatever state you are actually in — believing that it is raining, being in pain, wanting another cup of coffee — you are thereby in a position to know that you are in it, without observation, inference, or evidence. He adds that this knowledge iterates: if you know something, you are also in a position to know that you know it, and to know that you know that you know, and so on; he calls this the iteration principle, or KK. When pressed for an argument he does not give one; he offers a picture. A room can be lit, he says, and a lit room is one in which you can see what is in it. But consider the light itself: it needs no second lamp in order to be seen; in showing the room it shows itself. Consciousness, he says, is exactly like that — the very act that puts your pains, beliefs, and desires on display also puts itself on display, so that there can be no gap in which you are in a state without being in a position to know it.

What I want from you is an audit of the analogy rather than of the thesis, since the thesis is doing no work beyond the picture. Lay out the correspondence explicitly — what is supposed to play the role of the lamp, the room, the objects in it, the light, the eye that sees, and the seeing itself — and then tell me where the mapping breaks down. I am especially interested in which disanalogies matter for the work he wants the picture to do and which are merely decorative: whether a light can be mistaken about the room it illuminates; whether seeing admits of an appearance/reality gap that being in a state does not; and what becomes of the picture when the illumination is not even but graded. I once put to him a case in which a state shades off gradually into its neighbours, so that any light faithful to it would have to be dimmer near the edges, and the picture seemed to fail in a way he could not repair. Finally, tell me which features of the source he is quietly importing into the target that the target may simply not possess, and whether the conclusion he is after — luminosity, together with the iteration of knowledge — survives once the disanalogies are on the table. If the lamp is the wrong picture, say what a better one would be.
