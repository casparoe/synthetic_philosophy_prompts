Bit of an odd one, but: I'm in a small left political group that's basically tearing itself apart right now, and everyone keeps reaching for the French Revolution — we're at 1792, this is the Terror, the revolution devours its children, etc. We've already expelled two people and I have a sinking feeling the next one is me. Which sent me off reading the historiography on why the Revolution happened and why it went the way it did, and I've come out more confused than I went in.

The fight I can't get past: the social/class reading (bourgeois revolution, a rising class against a feudal order), the revisionists (no bourgeois class actually seizing power, the revolutionaries were lawyers and officeholders, politics doing alot of independent work), and the newer contingency line (nothing was inevitable — accident, faction, fear, the king's failed flight, the war). It feels like this was never resolved, just abandoned: the field declared the social reading dead and moved to culture and discourse, but the underlying questions never got settled. Was that because the questions were bad, or just because everyone got bored and found new toys?

So diagnose it. Is it a still-open factual dispute (was there a capitalist bourgeoisie? was there a feudal reaction? was the Terror driven by war and emergency or by the revolutionaries own ideology of virtue and regeneration?), two sides answering different questions under one heading — one about why the Revolution happened, one about who made it and what they wanted — or a verbal quarrel about what class means, when one side uses it as a structural relation and the other as what people called themselves? Or is it bedrock politics, where bourgeois-progress versus proto-totalitarian-slide is doing the real work and the evidence is downstream? My working theory is the different-questions one, but I don't trust it.

And the devouring bit, which is what I actually need for my group: is that a real mechanism — radicals outbidding each other, emergency powers ratcheting, yesterday's patriot becoming today's traitor — or just a topos people repeat, or survivors hindsight? If it's a mechanism, what evidence would show it, and does the French case actually have it?

Tell me what kind of disagreement this is, and what would count as progress — a distinction, a piece of evidence, or an argument that it's ill-posed and I should stop looking.
