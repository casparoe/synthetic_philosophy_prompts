I teach a twelve-person upper-level seminar in philosophy of mind, and I have one ninety-minute session to spend on the idea Dennett labels competence without comprehension: the inversion Darwin performs on design and Turing performs on thinking, in which a mindless process yields results that look as though comprehension must have been behind them. I want the students to run into this rather than be told it. Design a two-part session in which they are themselves the mechanism, with no lecture to open. In the first part they produce something that looks designed, by blind variation under a selection rule I fix in advance; in the second, each of them holds only a local instruction while the group as a whole answers questions that no individual in it understands. Give me the rules, materials, roles, timings, and the pass criterion for each round, and arrange things so that I do not name Darwin, Turing, or the inversion until the debrief. The debrief should lead them to see that the two halves are the same move, and to locate exactly where comprehension was smuggled back in at the moment the system appeared to be working. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it — I expect them to coordinate in whispers and to peek at the target — and how to convert that into evidence rather than merely police it. Be candid, finally, about what the activity cannot reproduce: which part of the Darwinian story and which part of the Turingian story the rules leave out, and what I will have to supply myself.
