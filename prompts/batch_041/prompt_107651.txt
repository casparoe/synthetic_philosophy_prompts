I host a small philosophy podcast, and next week's guest is a philosopher whose new book defends a position I will call dilemmic realism, though she may use a different label; for the purposes of the interview, treat her as an unnamed defender of the view rather than as a particular living person. Her central claim is that genuine moral dilemmas are possible: there are situations in which an agent ought to do one thing and ought to do another, but the two things cannot both be done. She denies that this generates a contradiction because she rejects the agglomeration principle, the inference from the claim that one ought to do A and the claim that one ought to do B to the claim that one ought to do both A and B. She also insists that the agent may be blameworthy no matter which option she takes, even though she could not have done otherwise, and that the phenomenon of moral residue—guilt, regret, the sense of having done wrong even when one did the best available thing—is a genuine normative fact rather than merely a feeling. She thinks this view is compatible with moral realism, but she is not a pluralist in the familiar sense; she argues that the conflict can arise even within a single value, when that value makes incompatible demands on a single life. I want the interview to press her on the structure of the view rather than let her restate it.

Please draft eight to ten interview questions, each with a follow-up keyed to the answer she is most likely to give, and write the whole response as continuous prose, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables—the way one would write an email or a letter. I want at least one question on how she can reject agglomeration without giving up the ordinary logic of obligation, at least one on the principle that ought implies can and whether her view makes the agent irrational by definition, at least one on the difference between guilt and regret in her account of moral residue and whether residue can generate reasons for compensation, and at least one on what evidence or argument could count against the existence of genuine dilemmas. Please also include questions on whether her view collapses into the claim that everything is permitted, on how she distinguishes a genuine dilemma from a merely hard choice, and on whether her view can allow that one horn of a dilemma is less wrong than the other. For each question, say in a sentence how confident you are that it will elicit a substantive answer rather than a restatement of her position, and at the very end say how confident you are that the set as a whole gets at the live issues; single out the one question you are least confident about and explain why. Avoid generic questions about how she became interested in philosophy or what she hopes readers will take away.
