I teach the decision-theory module in a PhD-level seminar on rationality, and I'm assembling the take-home portion of this year's final. My recurring frustration is that the Ellsberg material gets answered by reciting ambiguity aversion and gesturing at the sure-thing principle, so I cannot tell from a script whether the candidate could reconstruct why the two-urn case is supposed to embarrass subjective expected utility at all, or whether they understand what the various repairs to that framework actually surrender. I want a single question that forces the reconstruction rather than the label, so please draft the question itself, a model answer of roughly 700 words, and a marking scheme worth 25 points that separates an A from a B answer by naming the specific moves each must contain and the specific errors that should cost marks. No citations are needed; I will attach the reading myself, and I would rather the standard be stated in terms of arguments than in terms of who said what.

Here is the material I want the question to work with, including one twist of my own that should be folded in. Part (a) should ask the candidate to take the two-urn setup — Urn A holding fifty red and fifty black balls with the composition known, Urn B holding a hundred balls of unknown composition — and show that for an agent maximizing subjective expected utility, the comparison between betting on red from A and betting on red from B turns only on whether the prior over B's composition gives red a predictive probability above, below, or equal to one half, so that no prior whatsoever can make the agent strictly prefer the known urn for both colours at once. From this they should draw the moral about the popular claim that Ellsberg's subjects simply hold a different prior rather than a non-SEU preference. Part (b) should present a variant and ask the candidate to adjudicate it: a colleague proposes to de-ambiguate the unknown urn by having its composition set by a fair coin — heads yielding sixty red and forty black, tails forty red and sixty black — and predicts that once the composition is generated by a known chance device, the preference for the known urn will vanish. The candidate should say whether the prediction is correct and what its holding or failing would reveal about whether the preference is best read as aversion to compound risk, aversion to a particular source of uncertainty, or something about the agent's epistemic position relative to others. Part (c) should ask for the smallest set of changes to the expected-utility apparatus that would accommodate the preference, with the candidate naming at least two inequivalent repairs and identifying the axiom or assumption each one sacrifices, so that it becomes clear that relaxing expected utility is not a single move.

The model answer needs to be precise enough to serve as the standard, so please do not let it slide over the difference between the two-urn case and the single-urn three-colour case, nor over the distinction between ambiguity aversion as a property of preferences over acts and ambiguity aversion as a property of the agent's second-order beliefs. The marking scheme should be explicit about which moves are load-bearing — the ones a merely competent B answer can omit while still passing — and which are the moves that mark the difference between a genuine reconstruction and a rehearsal of the received summary, and it should include, for each must-hit point, a one-line note on how candidates typically get that point wrong.
