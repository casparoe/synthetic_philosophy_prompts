I'm a retired field biologist — thirty-five years counting insects and arguing about sampling methods — and since I retired I've been reading alot of philosophy. Last weekend two friends got into it over dinner and I couldn't referee. I'm hoping you can.

Ellen started it. She said an ant colony is a mind. Not like a metaphor, actually a mind. Her case: the colony chooses nest sites, allocates tasks, remembers routes through pheromone trails, and adjusts when conditions change. It integrates information and acts on it. If you think minds are just systems that do that kind of work, then substrate doesn't matter, and a colony is as good a candidate as a brain.

Raj said no. He said all you've got is ants following local rules and bumping into each other. There's no unified subject there, no place where it's like something to be the colony. A mind needs a point of view, and a colony doesn't have one. It's a useful model, maybe, but calling it a mind is a category mistake.

Ellen came back: a point of view can be distributed. What matters is that the colony-level system has states that carry information about the world and guide action. It can even be wrong about the world — ants follow a false trail to an empty food source, the colony wastes foragers, that's a mistake. If you say only brains can have minds, that's carbon chauvinism.

Raj said fine, then a traffic jam has a mind, and so does the stock market. Your criterion is too liberal. Also, colony memory is just pheromones in the environment, not inside the colony. If external memory counts, then my laptop plus the internet has a mind.

Ellen said maybe mindedness comes in degrees, and drawing the line at the skull or the skin is arbitrary. Aunt Hillary isn't one ant and isn't a ghost; she's the organization. She added something I hadn't thought about: a parasitic fungus can hijack ants and make them do things that are bad for the colony. If the colony can be deceived or manipulated like that, doesn't that mean it has something like a false belief?

Raj said no, that's just individual ants getting hijacked, like a brain parasite in a human. It doesn't show a colony belief; it shows components being messed with. And a false trail is just a chemical gradient. Calling it a false belief is a metaphor, and you're sliding from function to phenomonology without paying the cost.

I threw in one more thing, because I'd read about it: a colony can get split by a fallen tree into two foraging groups that still share the same nest and queen, and each group can learn a seperate route. Ellen said that's either two minds or one mind with dissociated subsystems. Raj said it's just two groups of ants. Neither would budge, and I honestly don't know who's right.

Can you go through their exchange move by move and say who is right about each specific point — not just who wins overall? Rate each move from clearly mistaken to clearly right and briefly justify each rating. Also tell me what both of them are missing, if anything. Please say how confident you are in what you claim, and single out the point you're least sure about. American English, please.
