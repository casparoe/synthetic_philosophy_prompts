I teach an upper-level undergraduate seminar on collective harm and individual responsibility, and I am setting the final examination. Please act as an experienced examiner in moral philosophy and draft a single essay question for that paper, together with a model answer and a marking scheme. Think the crux through carefully before you write: the question is worth little unless it separates candidates who understand the problem from candidates who can only summarise the debate.

The seminar has covered the objection that an individual's contribution to a large-scale harm — one vote in a national election, one purchase of factory-farmed meat, one person's annual emissions — makes no difference to whether the harm occurs, and so cannot ground an individual obligation. The candidates can recite that objection and the stock replies to it. The examination is closed-book and candidates are not expected to cite anything, so the question must be answerable by reasoning from the case below and from ordinary knowledge of the three examples. Put no citations, quotations, reading lists, or names of authors or works anywhere in your output; where a familiar case or argument would help, describe it in your own words.

I want the question to turn on a distinction and a case that we built in the last week of term and that the candidates have not met in print.

The distinction. "Makes no difference" may mean (i) that the outcome would have been the same had you acted otherwise; (ii) that your act does not change the probability of the outcome; or (iii) that your act does not increase the amount of harm. The three are not equivalent, and an objection that establishes one of them has not thereby established the others.

The case. A lake's fish die if and only if the total phosphorus runoff entering the lake in a season strictly exceeds 100 units; at or below that threshold the lake is clean and the fish survive, and above it the surplus runoff adds nothing, the harm being all or nothing. In arrangement A, 1,000 farms each discharge 0.5 units. In arrangement B, 201 farms each discharge 0.5 units. In both arrangements the fish die, and in both they would have survived had no farm discharged anything. The only difference is that in B each farm's discharge is necessary for the kill — withdraw any one farm's 0.5 units and the total is exactly 100, at the threshold — whereas in A no farm's discharge is necessary. Same act, same threshold, same outcome.

Please write:

1. One essay question, answerable in 90 minutes and worth 25 marks, restating the case in a sentence or two so that candidates need not reconstruct the figures from memory. It must require candidates (a) to state the inefficacy objection precisely and to separate its causal component from its moral component; (b) to run the objection against each of the three examples and to explain why its force is not the same in all three; (c) to confront the A/B case and say what, if anything, it shows about whether being pivotal is morally relevant; and (d) to state and defend a principle connecting individual acts to collective harms that survives that case, or to argue that no such principle is needed. The question must not be answerable by summarising the literature, and it must be neutral between the verdicts a good answer might reach.

2. A model answer of roughly 800 words, which argues rather than reports, names no authors or works, and flags at least one move that is tempting but mistaken.

3. A marking scheme organised around five must-hit moves, with the marks each is worth (25 in total) and, for each, a one-line note on how candidates typically get it wrong. Award marks for the moves rather than for the conclusion, do not reward name-dropping, and say which of the five moves a solid B answer will typically omit.

Before you draft, work through the logical space for yourself: list the readings of "makes no difference," decide which of them the objection needs if it is to have any force, and check which of the three examples, if any, satisfies all of them at once.
