I am writing a paper on the principle of indifference with a colleague — a philosopher of probability — and I have been keeping a record of her verdicts on cases as we work through them, because her pattern of answers is turning out to be more informative than any single answer. Here is the record.

Bertrand's chord. Given the standard three methods, yielding 1/3, 1/2, and 1/4 for the probability that a random chord of a circle exceeds the side of the inscribed equilateral triangle, she says the question as posed has no answer, and that the three numbers are answers to three different questions. She adds that if you supplement the problem with a generating mechanism — the midpoint chosen uniformly over the disc, the chord drawn perpendicular to the radius through it — the answer is 1/4, and that having to name a mechanism is the whole difficulty.

The cube factory. Told only that a factory's cubes all fit inside a 1 cm cube, and asked for the probability that a randomly chosen one has volume below 1/8 cm³, she says there is no determinate answer: indifference over edge length gives 1/2, over volume 1/8, over surface area 1/4.

A die whose sixfold symmetry is guaranteed by how it is made: 1/6, no hesitation.

An urn holding 100 balls, each red or white, the mix unknown, one ball drawn uniformly: she says my credence that it is red should be 1/2, and that no other value is defensible.

A stick broken at a point chosen uniformly along its length: 1/2 that the longer piece is more than three quarters of the stick.

Two points chosen independently and uniformly on a unit segment: 1/4 that the three resulting pieces can form a triangle.

Next week I put this case to her. A sealed envelope contains a real number x. I am told that x was produced by one of two procedures, but not which: procedure A, x uniform on [0,1]; procedure B, x the square of a variable uniform on [0,1]. I am asked for the probability that x is less than 1/4.

Predict her verdict. I care less about the number than about the rule she is running, so please state that rule explicitly; say which other rules fit her entire record just as well and what each of them predicts about the envelope; tell me which verdict you would bet on and why; tell me whether the record is in fact jointly consistent, or whether some of these verdicts cannot be held together and which of them a careful version of her would have to give up; and name the case I should put to her next if I want to separate the rules that survive.
