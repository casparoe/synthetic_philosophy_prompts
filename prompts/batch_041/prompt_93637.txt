I'm a third-year PhD student in philosophy of mind, and I've been trying to use a historical episode as a test case for the problem of other minds — specifically the Clever Hans affair. Briefly: the horse exhibited in Germany in the first decade of the twentieth century appeared to do arithmetic and keep a calendar by tapping his hoof; a commission convened to check the claim found no trickery; Oskar Pfungst then showed that Hans answered correctly only when the questioner knew the answer and Hans could see him, and that the questioner's involuntary changes in posture and breathing were doing the work. Von Osten never accepted the result. I want a careful analysis of the episode through the lens of the other-minds problem, and I want you to be disciplined about which version of that problem you're using: the epistemological version (what justifies my belief that another creature has a mind — analogy, inference to the best explanation, or something else) and the conceptual or criteriological version (what makes mental-state ascription intelligible in the first place, and whether a purely behavioral criterion could ever be adequate). For each reading, say what the actors — the commission, Pfungst, von Osten — are actually facing, what the correct procedure is by the lights of that reading, and whether Pfungst's controls (isolating the horse from spectators, using blinders, using questioners ignorant of the answer) are the controls that reading would prescribe or merely good experimental hygiene that the theory comes along behind and blesses.

Then the harder half, which is what I really want: what the episode shows about the theory. My working hypothesis — tell me whether it survives — is that the other-minds problem is not one problem but a family, and that its difficulty varies inversely with the availability of a non-mental mechanism that would produce the same behavior. Hans was resolvable because such a mechanism was on the table; an ordinary human interlocutor is not, because the only mechanism available is the one we already attribute to ourselves. If that's right, what does it imply about the epistemology/conceptual divide — does the criteriological reading collapse into the epistemological one once you concede that criteria are always defeasible? And what does it imply about the comparative-psychology maxim that one should not posit a higher faculty where a lower one explains the behavior — does that maxim do genuine epistemic work here, or does it stipulate the answer in advance and then present it as parsimony?

One detail I keep returning to and can't place: Pfungst ran laboratory trials in which he played the horse and human subjects tapped out answers to his questions, and he reported them giving off the same involuntary cues. So a mind was present in the episode throughout — it simply wasn't in the horse. Say what, if anything, that symmetry shows about where the other-minds problem actually lives. And be explicit about where the theory goes silent: does anything here bear on whether Hans had any subjective life at all, as opposed to whether he could do arithmetic, or are those questions simply orthogonal? Finally, a short check rather than a treatise — if the Clever Hans template transfers to the current dispute over whether large language models understand anything, say how, and what the equivalent of the blinders test would be.
