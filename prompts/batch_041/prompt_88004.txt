I need help with something and it's probably going to be longer than it needs to be. I cover science and ideas for a magazine — I do a fair number of the here's-a-weird-argument-a-philosopher-told-me pieces — and a friend who teaches philosophy at a state school in Ohio walked me through this at a hotel bar after a conference and I have not been able to shake it. She's a Moore person. She thinks the two-hands thing is a real proof of an external world and that the skeptic who asks how you know you're not dreaming is just asking the same question over again in a louder voice. Fine, I'm sympathetic, I'd like her to be right. But then she said the price of that is a general principle, and that once you state the principle generally it starts handing out knowledge to anybody. I wrote the argument down on the back of a receipt, cleaned it up a bit, and now I can't tell where it goes wrong:

(1) Moore knows he has two hands, on the basis of how things look and feel to him, and he knows it without having first ruled out that he's dreaming or a handless brain in a vat. (2) He also knows that having two hands means he isn't a handless brain in a vat. (3) So he knows he isn't a handless brain in a vat — and he didn't have to do anything to get there that a vatted version of him couldn't have done just as well. (4) So whatever makes (1) knowledge has to be a completely general fact about experience: if it looks to you as though things are some way, you're in a position to believe things are that way, even though your evidence would have been exactly the same if they weren't. Nothing about the hands can be special, or (1) isn't knowledge and the proof is dead. (5) My neighbour in 4B is convinced the people upstairs are reading his mail and putting something in his food. He has a persistent experience as of being watched and interfered with, he can't tell his situation apart from that of someone who is merely paranoid, and no test would settle it either way. (6) By (4) he's in a position to believe the people upstairs are reading his mail. (7) He obviously isn't; that's not something anyone is in a position to believe. (8) So either (4) is wrong, or (4) isn't saying the thing that separates Moore from my neighbour.

What I want is a diagnosis, not a tour of the literature — don't bother with a reading list, I'll pull sources myself. Tell me which step to give up, why it looked fine to me when I wrote it down, and what giving it up actually costs: if it's (4), how does (1) survive; if it's (7), then what is it about the neighbour's case that I'm mis-describing; if the honest answer is that he does get some justification and the thing that's wrong is only how much, say that too, but then tell me what that does to Moore. I'd rather not loose (1) or (2), so if the cheapest exit runs through one of them, I need to know what I'm buying. And I want you to be up front about how confident you are in the step you pick: tell me what would change your mind, which case or counterargument would move you to a different step, and where your own reasoning is most likely to go off the rails — because I've been burned before by a tidy answer that turned out to rest entirely on one analogy nobody had checked.
