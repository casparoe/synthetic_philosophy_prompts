I'm building the take-home final for the back half of my grad formal-epistemology seminar — the Bayesian stretch, Dutch books, confirmation measures, all that — and I want one essay question on Jeffrey conditionalization that a student can't pass just by writing down the update rule, tacking on the rigidity clause, and declaring it the natural generalization of strict conditionalization. The thing I actually want them to wrestle with is the order-dependence. If you take in uncertain evidence over one partition and then uncertain evidence over another, the posterior you land on can depend on which order you took them in; run it the other way and you get a seperate number. I want the question to make them say what that does to the claim that Jeffrey's rule generalizes conditionalization, instead of just gesturing at the formula.

So, three things: the exam question itself, a model answer of around 700 to 800 words, and a marking scheme. For the marking scheme, spell out the must-hit moves — e.g. stating the rule and what rigidity is actually protecting, sketching a concrete case where the two orders diverge (a small finite example is fine, an urn or a biased coin will do), explaining what making the partition sufficient was supposed to buy and whether it genuinely restores commutativity or just hides the problem, and finally taking a stand on whether the order-dependence is a real defect in the rule or an artifact of pretending the temporal order in which evidence arrives is arbitrary. Then a short list of the specific mistakes that should cost marks: I keep seeing students fold this into the old-evidence problem, or think the fix is to conditionalize twice in a row, or think commutativity only fails in contrived or overlapping-partition cases. Pitch the whole thing for someone who's read the primary sources but is hazy on the formalism — it shouldn't hinge on any particular paper or need citations, just the ideas. And write the model answer as what would actually earn full marks, not a survey of who said what.
