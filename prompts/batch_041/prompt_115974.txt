I have become convinced of the occasionalist position, in the form usually attributed to al-Ghazālī: that no created thing possesses any genuine causal power; that what we call causes are merely the occasions on which a single continuous agent produces what follows; and that the tie between a so-called cause and its so-called effect is not necessary in any sense that would make its denial contradictory. I do not want the stock objections rehearsed — that this is Hume with an idle deity appended, that it makes science impossible. Grant for the sake of argument that those can be answered. What I want is a candid map of the position's exposure: what would actually count against it, ranked from fatal to merely awkward.

Work through the following, and for each say whether it is a real cost the occasionalist must concede or something the view can absorb without damage.

First, the problem of evil in the view's own idiom. If God is the sole agent of every event, then no creature ever really does anything, and the distinction between causing and permitting appears to have been erased. Does that make God the author of each cruelty as much as of each kindness? If so, is that fatal to any theistically motivated version of the view, or can the occasionalist give a principled account on which a creature's act remains its own?

Second, whether "habit" can do the work asked of it. If the world's regularity rests on divine habit rather than necessity, then habit supplies no reason to expect the next case to resemble the last; and if the reason offered is that God is constant or truthful, that looks like presupposing the very reliability in question. Is this a genuine circularity at the heart of the view, or only an unfinished chapter? Relatedly, does the view really eliminate dispositions, or does it merely relocate them into the divine will, so that what was denied to creatures is quietly reasserted of God?

Third, whether the causal efficacy of reasons survives. If no creature's states produce anything, then my premises do not produce my conclusions, and the argument I am now giving for occasionalism cannot be what causes anyone to believe it. Is that a self-undermining consequence, or does it rest on an assumption the occasionalist is free to reject?

Fourth, whether quantum indeterminacy is a friend. Some have treated the failure of determinism at the micro-level as support for the view, since it suggests no lower-level law is doing the work. I suspect that is an equivocation — indeterminism about which outcome occurs is not the same as the absence of a power to produce it — but I want you to adjudicate it.

Finish with the question I care most about: is there anything that would disconfirm the view, or is it compatible with every possible observation? If the latter, say plainly whether that immunity is a defect or the honest consequence of a metaphysical rather than an empirical thesis, and tell me which single defeater should move me most.

Commit to a definite ranking and a definite verdict rather than laying out options for me to choose among. Write in American English, in no more than 700 words.
