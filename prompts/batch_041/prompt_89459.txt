Consider the following scenario, which I have designed to isolate the disagreement between two principles of self-locating belief. Answer the questions in order, giving numbers and the reasoning that produces them rather than a survey of the literature.

An experimenter tosses a fair coin. If it lands heads, she creates exactly one inhabited chamber, and you are its sole occupant. If it lands tails, she creates one thousand inhabited chambers, causally sealed from one another, each occupied by a person who is a perfect duplicate of you: identical memories, identical total evidence, identical present experience. You awaken in a chamber and possess no information about which chamber you occupy, or how many chambers exist, beyond what I have just described.

The two principles are these. Self-Sampling (SSA): you should reason as if your evidence had been obtained by uniformly sampling one observer from all observers who exist according to the hypothesis in question, and then conditionalizing on that sampled observer having your evidence. Self-Indication (SIA): the posterior probability of a hypothesis is proportional to its prior multiplied by the number of observers it predicts who have your total evidence.

1. Under SSA alone, what credence should you assign to heads? Under SIA alone? Show the likelihood each principle assigns to your evidence under each hypothesis before combining.

2. Give your own all-things-considered credence that SIA, rather than SSA, delivers the correct answer in this scenario. Decompose it into named terms — for instance, the probability that SSA is the correct principle, that SIA is, that neither is because the question is ill-posed or admits no determinate answer (state what would make it so), and that some hybrid is correct, such as SIA governing whether you exist at all and SSA governing where among the existing observers you are located. Give a number and one line of justification for each term, show how the terms combine, and identify the term your final number is most sensitive to.

3. Now vary the case so as to reverse the verdicts. Suppose that in the tails world the thousand chambers hold a thousand mutually distinct people, none of whom shares your evidence, and that you are additionally present there as the sole person with your evidence; in the heads world you remain the sole person with your evidence. The number of observers with your evidence is therefore one under both hypotheses, while the total number of observers is one under heads and one thousand and one under tails. Recompute the heads credence under each principle. Does this variation tell in favor of either principle, and how far should it move the number you gave in answer to (2)?

4. Finally, let the tails world contain ten to the ninth duplicates of you rather than one thousand. State what each principle implies for your heads credence, then say whether SIA's verdict here — that you should be overwhelmingly confident in the hypothesis positing vastly more observers — is a reductio of SIA, a cost to be accepted, or neither, and why. Close by naming the single argument or consideration that would move your overall credence most, and in which direction.
