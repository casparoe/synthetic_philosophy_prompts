I'm a novelist midway through a book about a hospital administrator who has to ration a scarce drug during an outbreak, and the antagonist of the middle section is a young physician who accuses him, in open committee, of treating the patients as so many litres in a common tank. I want that accusation to be the strongest version of itself, and I want the administrator's answer to be more than a shrug about hard choices, because a reviewer who knows the literature will otherwise say I built a straw man on one side and a platitude on the other. Since my own philosophical education consists of one undergraduate survey I've largely forgotten, I asked a friend who is finishing a doctorate in philosophy to brief me on the objection she said the physician should be making. Here is what I wrote down from our conversation:

"The separateness of persons objection, which takes its name from Rawls's complaint that utilitarianism does not take seriously the distinction between persons, says that utilitarianism is incoherent, because there is no such thing as a collective subject of experience. Pleasure and pain occur only inside individual people, so there is nothing for the utilitarian's sum to be the happiness of; adding up the welfare of strangers is a category mistake, like averaging the colours of the cars in a car park. Rawls's version of the objection is that the utilitarian applies to a whole society the principle of rational choice for one person, as though a society were one person writ large. Nozick's version is that the doctrine licenses using some people as means to others' ends, which is why he insists that persons are separate and may not be sacrificed for the greater good. Because it exposes a contradiction at the heart of the theory, the objection is now generally taken to have refuted act utilitarianism, though rule utilitarianism escapes it, since a rule can be defended to each person who lives under it. The deeper lesson is that the theory puts our own lives and projects on the same scale as those of strangers, and this destroys personal integrity."

I don't know how much of this to trust, and I don't know where the seams are — she is an advocate, and advocates simplify. I am inclined to think there is something real in the objection, but the word "incoherent" makes me suspicious; it sounds like a verdict delivered before the argument, and my instinct is that a doctrine can be morally repugnant or morally blind without being self-contradictory. So please go through the passage claim by claim and tell me which parts a philosopher working on this material would simply accept, which are standard simplifications that a sympathetic reader would wince at but tolerate, and which are plainly wrong — and in each case say why. I would like to know especially whether the two versions she distinguishes really are two versions of one argument or two different arguments with different targets and different replies, and whether the closing claims about act versus rule utilitarianism and about personal integrity follow from anything true in the earlier sentences or are being smuggled in. Finally, since I need the physician's speech to be formidable, give me the version of the objection that a well-informed utilitarian would have to work hardest to answer, and tell me what that utilitarian's best reply is — I would rather know the strongest case against my administrator than have him win an argument nobody would actually make.
