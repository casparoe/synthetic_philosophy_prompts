I teach a final-year undergraduate seminar on Wittgenstein's later philosophy and I am drafting the final examination. One essay question is to be on the family-resemblance treatment of general terms, and I want it to be unusable by a candidate who has merely memorised the standard summary — that meaning is use, that many concepts have no common essence, that instances are linked by overlapping similarities, and that the point is to look rather than to assume a definition. The question should instead force candidates to take a stand on what kind of claim the family-resemblance account is, and to earn their marks by adjudicating a specific objection rather than by expounding a doctrine.

Please construct the question so that it obliges the candidate to do three things. First, to state precisely what the family-resemblance account asserts and denies, in terms that distinguish it from two positions it is routinely confused with: the view that a concept is governed by a disjunction of sufficient conditions, or by a weighted cluster of criteria with a threshold, so that some combination of features is necessary after all; and the view that a concept is applied by comparison with a stored exemplar or prototype. Second, to confront the objection that the appeal to similarity is too cheap to individuate concepts — that any arbitrary collection of things resembles one another in some respect, so resemblance cannot by itself distinguish a genuine concept from a gerrymandered class, and that the account risks circularity if the relevant similarities are specified as similarities in being instances of the very concept at issue. Third, to say what resources the later Wittgenstein can deploy in reply and whether they survive being used, in particular whether the appeal to a shared practice, or to the grammar of the word rather than to facts about its extension, is available to someone who wants the account to be a substantive description of how concepts work, or whether it converts the doctrine into a methodological injunction that makes no claim a critic could refute. To keep the question concrete, require the candidate to test the account against a case I supply in the question itself: a general term whose extension has widened by successive extension, so that the earliest and latest instances share no feature (a term that began as the name of a physical object and now also covers its digital counterpart will serve).

Write the essay question itself, in no more than 150 words; a model answer of about 650 words; and a marking scheme out of 30 marks. In the marking scheme, name the moves a first-class answer must make, the moves that separate it from a solid second-class answer, and the recurring errors that should cost marks — for example, treating the family-resemblance point as an empirical hypothesis in psychology that could be settled by experiment, or taking it to license the conclusion that anything goes in the application of a word. The model answer must be written without quotations, citations or page references, and must stand on argument alone, since candidates will have no access to the texts during the examination.
