I have been trying for a while now to sort out a question my friends and I keep circling: are the rules we live by discoveries about how things have to be, or inventions that could just as easily have gone the other way? Someone lent me a short popular book on how social rules arise, and the chapter on the philosopher David Lewis's theory of convention is the part I keep coming back to. The book summarizes him roughly like this: 'Lewis starts from the fact that some things only work if everyone does the same thing. Two cars meeting on a narrow road have to end up on the same side, and which side it is does not matter, so the rule is arbitrary. What makes our all keeping to the right a convention is not any law or agreement — nobody signed anything — but mutual expectation: everyone expects everyone else to keep right, everyone knows that everyone expects this, and so on. That expectation is what does the work; a convention is simply a regularity that is common knowledge and self-sustaining. The same machinery explains why people keep promises, pay their taxes and tell the truth even when nobody is watching. Lewis's most striking application is to language: the words of English are conventions in exactly this sense, no different in kind from driving on the right. Two things follow. Conventions are arbitrary, and by that we mean they have no rational foundation — there is no reason to keep left rather than right, no reason to speak English rather than French; we simply happen to have settled on one. And since they need no agreement and no law, they can arise and persist by themselves, so there is nothing mysterious about how a whole society comes to share a rule. The critics say this cannot be the whole story, because we feel bound by the rules of our own language in a way we do not feel bound to keep right, and once you admit that, the interesting part of the explanation is missing. The upshot is that morality is just one more convention of this kind — nothing more than a regularity we all happen to expect each other to follow.' The argument with my friends started because one of them said that if morality were a convention in this sense, then quarrelling about right and wrong would be like quarrelling about which side of the road to drive on, and none of us could say why the comparison felt wrong.

My instinct is that the final sentence is doing something the earlier sentences do not license, and that the complaint about feeling bound is a genuine difficulty rather than a knockout, but I cannot tell how much of my discomfort is simply that I do not want the conclusion to be true. I suspect the word arbitrary is carrying some technical weight I am not seeing, and that the promise-keeping and tax-paying examples may not be the same shape as the driving example, but I could not defend either thought. What I want is an audit rather than a verdict: which sentences the book gets right, which are honest simplifications, which are the book's own additions to Lewis rather than Lewis, and what someone who actually knows this material would wince at.
