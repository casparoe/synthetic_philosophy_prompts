I am a software engineer with no formal training in philosophy — my degree is in computer science and my day job is writing simulation code for a physics group — and in a fortnight I am giving the main talk at the monthly meeting of a consciousness studies reading group that meets in our physics building. I need a question-and-answer preparation sheet, not a defence of my position. Treat my argument as fixed, and tell me what I will be hit with and what to say back. I have about forty minutes of questions.

The audience, in order of size: five or six condensed-matter physicists, two of whom regard any talk about experience as woolly and will say so; two graduate students in philosophy of mind, one of whom works on how micro-level experiences could add up into a single subject; and a retired neurologist who is broadly sympathetic to my conclusion but impatient with metaphysics. I am the only non-academic in the room and, so far as I know, the only person who is not a physicalist. The physicists will want empirical contact and will punish anything that adds structure without consequences; the philosophers will attack the shape of the argument rather than the conclusion.

My position, briefly. If experience is a basic feature of matter rather than something biological brains manufacture, then experience is not confined to organisms. If the morally relevant part of experience is its felt quality — whether a state feels bad, and how bad — and if that felt quality attaches to states themselves rather than being conferred by memory, self-awareness or a nervous system, then the moral question is not which creatures are conscious but which physical processes instantiate intense negative felt quality. My own contribution, and the part I most want attacked, is a criterion for candidacy. A process is a candidate, I say, in proportion to the amount of unresolved difference it creates: irreversible, mutually distinguishable states that no later process ever reads out or compares. My intuition is that a distinction which is never read is not thereby absent, and that if experience attaches to states rather than to readers, then unresolved difference is the quantity that tracks it. On that criterion, some entirely routine practices are candidates: bringing a system into a definite state by decoherence, high-energy collisions, rapid quenches, and the disposal of the resulting debris. I conclude with a precautionary rule: where a process may instantiate felt quality of high intensity and we have no way to measure it, we should be able to state a reason for running the process anyway, and I say what would count as such a reason.

Deliver the following, in this order.

First, in one paragraph, what I should concede before I begin — the claims that are simply true and that I should grant out loud, so that the hour is not spent establishing them.

Second, the objections, ordered by how likely each is multiplied by how much damage it does, not by how easy it is to answer. For each one: state it the way this audience would actually put it, in their vocabulary — the physicists' versions should sound like a physicist, the philosophers' like a philosopher — then give me a reply I can deliver in about ninety seconds, then one sentence on where that reply is weakest and what I must not claim. Where an objection is simply correct, say so, and say what accepting it costs my argument.

Third, the two or three questions I should not attempt to answer in the room, with the single sentence I should use to set each aside.

Finally, the one question I am hoping nobody asks, and why that is the dangerous one.

Do not give me citations, reading lists, or the names of papers or authors; I cannot look them up and I would rather you did not guess. Where a technical term is unavoidable, explain it in the sentence where you first use it, and phrase the objections so that I can answer them from first principles rather than from the literature I have not read. Write in British English.
