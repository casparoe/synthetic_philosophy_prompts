I have been following a long argument about whether transformative AI should be reasoned about by historical analogy, and I keep meeting a move I cannot assess. Someone who wants to deflate the nuclear-weapons comparison will say that generals fight the last war: we reach for the most dramatic recent technological upheaval we can remember, model the coming one on it, and get it wrong. The line is used as a conversation-stopper, but I suspect it is doing several different jobs at once, and I want to know which of them it is actually entitled to.

Please treat the sentence as a claim and disambiguate it. Lay out the distinct precise theses it could be expressing, ordered from the trivial to the radical — for instance, readings on which it is a claim about institutional inertia, about the base rate of discontinuities in history, about the psychology of forecasters, about the logic of analogical reasoning itself, or merely a rhetorical hedge. For each reading, say whether it is a platitude, a substantive and defensible claim, or false, and what would count as evidence for or against it. Then say which reading the people who repeat the proverb probably have in mind.

Bring it back to the argument where I met it. Which of these readings would actually license using the proverb to dismiss the nuclear analogy? Does any of them also, by the same logic, undercut the printing-press and Industrial Revolution analogies — in which case the proverb cannot be used to pick a winner among them? And does any reading turn the proverb back on the person wielding it, since drawing on the printing press or the Industrial Revolution is itself a way of fighting the last war?

I would also like the readings tested against some specific episodes, since I think the proverb trades on how these are remembered. Consider (a) the pre-1914 faith in a short, offensive war, and the interwar expectation that the next war would be another static trench stalemate; (b) the decades after the Industrial Revolution began in Britain in which output per worker climbed while real wages for most workers barely moved; and (c) the long lag before factories were reorganized around electric power rather than merely swapping steam engines for motors. For each, say whether the episode supports the proverb and, if so, under which reading.

I want the result as a menu I can carry into the discussion rather than a single verdict — but end with one final line beginning "Verdict:" that names the reading, if any, that would actually justify the dismissive move, or states that none does.
