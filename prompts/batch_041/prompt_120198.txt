I teach a grad seminar on consequentialism (mixed MA/PhD) and I'm putting together the take-home final. I need one essay question on cluelessness, plus a model answer and a marking scheme. Two things I care about.

First, I don't want a question a student can pass by reciting the usual setup ("our actions have vast unknowable consequences, so consequentialism can't actually guide us") and then the usual expected-value reply ("just maximize expected value — cluelessness is just ordinary uncertainty"). They've all read that and can produce it in their sleep. The question has to force them past it.

Second, so the question has something concrete to bite on, build it around a position I'm defining right here (I've been workshopping it with a colleague; it's not from the literature, so treat it as brand new for the exam):

Prospective Consequentialism (PC): an act is right iff it maximizes expected value computed over the agent's "epistemically available" consequences — those she can assign credences to that are grounded either in observed frequencies or in a well-understood causal mechanism. Consequences that are merely conceivable (no mechanism, no track record) are excluded from the calculation entirely, rather than being given some tiny positive credence.

And the objection I want them to wrestle with, which I'm calling the gerrymandering worry: the availability line looks ad hoc — you can always redraw it to include or exclude exactly the consequences that would flip the verdict, so PC may just relocate the cluelessness out of the consequences and into the boundary.

Your job:

1. Write the exam question. It should be answerable in ~90 minutes of writing, pitched at students who know the basic cluelessness debate cold but have never seen PC. It should require them to (a) say what PC actually buys you over plain expected-value maximization, (b) decide whether the gerrymandering worry is fatal or answerable, and (c) reach a verdict instead of hedging.

2. Write a model answer (~600-800 words) that a strong student would produce. Make it actually take a side and defend it — don't let it just survey.

3. Write a marking scheme: five must-hit points, 4 marks each (20 total), where each point names the specific move a good answer has to make, plus one line per point on the characteristic way students blow it.

Before you write any of it, think the structure through first: what's the strongest version of PC, what's the strongest version of the gerrymandering worry, and where does the real crux actually lie. You're an experienced exam writer in normative ethics, so make the question discriminating — a B answer should be able to summarize the whole debate and still miss the crux.

One more thing: keep the model answer honest. If PC survives the objection, say so and let the marking scheme reward the survival argument; if it doesn't, let the model answer conclude that instead. Don't rig it either way.
