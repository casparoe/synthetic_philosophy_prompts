Retired chemist here — thirty-five years on heterogeneous catalysis, and since I hung up the lab coat I've been working through the philosophy of mind classics, slowly, with a lot of going back. I've got stuck on Turing's 1950 Mind paper, "Computing Machinery and Intelligence," specifically the last section where he turns to learning machines. I'm going partly from memory because the scan I'm working off is a terrible OCR job, so tell me if I'm mangling him: the gist is that instead of trying to write a program that reproduces what an adult human mind does, you should write one that reproduces what a childs mind does, and then educate it. Part of his motivation, if I've got this right, is an argument from economy — the initial state of the child machine has to be something you can actually specify, so it can't already contain everything the adult has, which means the adult competence must be mostly the product of upbringing. Then he says how the education would work: the teacher signals approval or disapproval, the machine is wired so those signals function as reward and punishment, and it adjusts. He also floats an alternative to hand-designing the child machine — start from something random and apply mutation and selection — but then drops it on the grounds that evolution is far too slow, and says the experimenter should instead use his own intelligence to shortcut the search.

What I can't get my head around is what work the child analogy is actually doing, and whether Turing is entitled to it. Two things bother me. First, there's a tension in the economy argument itself: if the child machine has to be simple enough that its initial state can be written down compactly, then nearly all the structure has to come from the education — but on his account the education is just a stream of approval and disapproval signals. Can a stream of reward and punishment really install the kind of structure an adult mind has, or is he quietly smuggling a great deal of innate machinery in under the label 'the machine's initial state' and just not counting it as information? Second, the evolutionary shortcut strikes me as suspicious as a piece of reasoning rather than engineering. He rejects random mutation plus selection because it's too slow, and says to use the experimenter's intelligence instead — but the experimenter's intelligence is itself a product of that same slow process, so it's not obvious why designing by hand is cheaper than evolving by selection. Is there a principled argument there, or is it a hunch he's dressing up? I'd like a close reading of the argument in that section: which premises are actually load-bearing, which of my two worries lands and which is just me being a chemist, and how Turing's position here relates to the later innateness-versus-learning fights I keep hearing about secondhand.
