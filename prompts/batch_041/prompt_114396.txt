I host a small podcast on the methodology of the social sciences, and next week's guest is an economist-turned-philosopher who has spent the better part of a decade defending a deliberately unorthodox reconstruction of Milton Friedman's 1953 thesis that the realism of a theory's assumptions is irrelevant to the theory's quality -- that what matters is only how well it predicts. Her reconstruction, which she calls the two-tier reading, holds that the thesis is defensible but only for a restricted class of assumptions: those she labels domain-specifying (assumptions whose job is to delimit the range of cases a model is meant to cover) as opposed to mechanism-positing (assumptions that assert something about the causal furniture of the world). Her diagnosis is that nearly all the familiar objections to Friedman are aimed at the second kind while Friedman was really talking about the first, and that this mismatch is why the debate has never resolved. I want the interview to be genuinely substantive rather than a rehash of the usual pro-and-con, so I need questions that press on the internal machinery of her position and not just on whether one likes or dislikes Friedman.

Please write me eight to ten interview questions in that spirit. I specifically want at least one question on what, on her view, actually makes an assumption unrealistic -- whether that is being false, being approximate, or being deliberately abstract -- and whether the two-tier reading can survive the awkward fact that a single assumption often does both jobs at once. I want another on Friedman's own stock illustrations (the expert billiard player and the leaves arranging themselves toward the sunlight) and whether those examples really support her thesis or instead a different thesis about as-if behavior; and another on the underdetermination worry: if assumptions are irrelevant to appraisal, how are we to choose between two models with equal predictive success but mutually contradictory domain-specifying assumptions? I would also like a question on whether her reading collapses into instrumentalism and whether that would be an objection at all, and a closing question on policy use -- if models are judged only by prediction, what constrains an economist from applying a model with false domain-specifying assumptions to a domain they were never meant to cover? For every question, add a follow-up that anticipates the answer she is most likely to give and pushes one step further.
