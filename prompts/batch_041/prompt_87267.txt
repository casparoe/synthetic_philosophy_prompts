I have never studied history formally, and what I know about the early modern period comes entirely from popular books and lectures I have followed on my own, so I may be missing something obvious. I keep running into the phrase "the Scientific Revolution" used as though it named an event that happened, in the way a war or an earthquake happens, with a beginning, a cause, and an end; and I also keep running into historians who say the whole category was manufactured after the fact by writers who came along centuries later and needed a tidy story in which Europe suddenly woke up. The same suspicion seems to attach to the Middle Ages and the Renaissance — the words themselves give away that somebody, looking back, decided where one age stopped and the next began. So I would like your actual credence in a proposition I have tried to state as precisely as I can: that the Scientific Revolution picks out a genuine, causally unified episode in European intellectual history — a real change in how people investigated the natural world, concentrated roughly between 1500 and 1700 — rather than a retrospective label that later writers imposed on a stretch of time for narrative convenience, with no single process underneath it. My own number is about 0.35; a friend who studied history at university puts hers at 0.8, and I would like to know whether we are disagreeing about the facts or about what "real" is even supposed to mean here.

Please give me a number and then the decomposition, not a survey of the debate. I want the sub-questions the number rests on, each with a rough probability and one line of justification, and then how you combine them: whether the documentary record actually shows a discontinuity in the rate and style of discovery compared with the two centuries before it, or only a smooth acceleration that we have learned to slice in a flattering place; whether whatever happened was one process or a bundle of loosely related developments in different fields and countries that look unified only in hindsight; whether the people living through it understood themselves as breaking with the past or as recovering something older; how much the answer depends on which activities you count as science in the first place — astronomy and mechanics versus chemistry, medicine, natural history, and the practical arts; and whether the question is even well posed, given that if every period label is a convenience, then the Scientific Revolution is no worse off than the Middle Ages, and my skepticism about the one should extend to all of them or to none. Then tell me which single sub-question the final number is most sensitive to, and what kind of evidence — the sort of thing a patient reader of primary sources could actually go and check, not a general change of scholarly fashion — would move you most.
