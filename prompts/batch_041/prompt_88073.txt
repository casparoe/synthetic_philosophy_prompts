I'm a law student writing a seminar paper that has gotten away from me, and I'd rather have your reasoning than a reading list, so please don't bother with references or citations. The paper is an attempt to say what Plato would have made of intellectual property, and I keep hitting the same objection from a classmate: that in his Athens nobody could own a text or an idea in any legal sense — a playwright had no way to stop a rival from staging his plays — so the question never arose for him and he could have had no view on it. Her stronger version is that he'd have been a pirate on principle, since if all learning is recollection of what the soul has already seen, then nobody originates anything and there is nothing for anyone to own. I don't accept either version, but I also don't want a paper that says the Forms belong to no one, therefore nothing is owned, and stops there. What I want is for you to put a modern intellectual-property regime in front of him and reason out, from his own commitments rather than from generalities about antiquity, what he would actually say.

Here is what is on the table, and you may assume he can be told all of it in plain terms. (a) A legal right, lasting a fixed term of years, that lets an author — or a publisher who has bought the author out — stop others from copying the exact words of a book, while the plot, the ideas, and the facts in it remain free for anyone to use. (b) The same kind of right granted to an inventor, but only in exchange for publishing a full account of how the invention works, so that others can build on it once the term expires. (c) Rights that a creator keeps even after selling the work, recognized in some countries and not others: to be named as its author, and to object when the work is altered or mutilated. (d) A public domain into which every protected work eventually falls, after which anyone may reprint or rebuild it. (e) A rule allowing a distinctive word or symbol to be monopolized indefinitely as a mark of trade, so that it can never be used by a competitor. (f) The live dispute over whether training a generative model on millions of copyrighted works is copying at all. For each, give me his verdict — accept, reject, or indifferent — and trace it back to something specific he holds: the theory of Forms, the objection that writing is a drug that cannot answer questions put to it, the claim that poets speak by divine possession rather than by knowledge, the arrangements for the guardian class (no private property, and stories censored by the city's founders), and the account of the soul's longing to beget in beauty so as to be remembered. Then do the two things I actually care about. First, the pressure test: identify where the modern regime would force him to give up or revise something he says elsewhere, and say how he would resolve the clash — in particular, whether the guardians' communism of property and family is a precedent for abolishing the ownership of ideas, or rather a sign that he would want the circulation of ideas kept under the control of whoever runs the city. Second, the residue report: which features have no counterpart in anything he thinks about at all, so that predicting his view requires importing assumptions he does not hold — and mark clearly which parts of your answer his texts constrain and which are your extrapolation. Finish with the one feature he would find not objectionable but simply unintelligible or absurd, and say what he would substitute for it. If the honest conclusion is that my classmate's anachronism objection wins and no prediction is possible, say so, and tell me what the defensible question underneath it would be.
