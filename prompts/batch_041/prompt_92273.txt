I am a third-year undergraduate in a seminar on the foundations of mathematics, and I am trying to write a short paper on a question my instructor regards as confused but which I cannot put down: what should our credence be that the basic axioms of mathematics are inconsistent? I would like your answer to be a number, with the reasoning exposed, and I would like the number stated before the decomposition so that I can see whether the reasoning is doing any work or merely decorating a prior.

Let me fix the target so that it has a truth value. Take the axioms of ZFC with classical first-order logic and the usual semantics, and let the claim be that the theory proves some sentence together with its negation: an outright contradiction, not a diffuse unease about set-theoretic truth, and not the observation that some axiom is inelegant or unmotivated. If you think the target is better split, give me separate credences for first-order Peano arithmetic and for ZFC. If you think credence in a mathematical proposition is degenerate — that there is a fact of the matter and no one can coherently be 0.7 sure of it — say so plainly, and then give me the number under the reading I actually care about: your subjective probability, given your present evidence, that a derivation of a contradiction exists.

Then decompose it. I want the sub-questions you believe the number genuinely rests on, a rough probability for each, one line of justification each, and an explicit statement of how they combine. The considerations I would put into the decomposition are these, though I would rather be told that my decomposition is wrong than have it filled in uncritically:

(a) The base rate of foundational failure. The most natural formal systems on offer have a habit of collapsing — naive comprehension, and Frege's logicist system when Russell's paradox arrived — and one could read the history as a sequence of systems that looked certain until they did not. Is that base rate informative about ZFC, or is it a selection artifact, since we remember the systems that failed and forget the ones that quietly worked?

(b) The meta-induction from the second incompleteness theorem. Every consistency proof available to us is relative to a theory we already trust: finitistic reasoning for weak systems, transfinite induction up to a certain ordinal for arithmetic, large-cardinal assumptions for set theory. The strength required keeps climbing and I cannot see a natural stopping point. Does that raise the probability of inconsistency, or is it neutral, on the ground that any inconsistency would have to be witnessed by a finite derivation and is therefore in principle discoverable from below?

(c) The evidential weight of the null result. ZFC has been used by a great many people for a long time without a contradiction surfacing, and I do not know how to convert that into a likelihood ratio. How much should the absence of a contradiction update me, given that the shortest derivation of one might be very long, or might be short and simply have gone unnoticed?

(d) Whether the question is well posed. On a pluralist or multiverse reading of set-theoretic truth, or in a paraconsistent setting where a contradiction does not trivialize a theory, is the claim even determinate — or determinate but harmless?

(e) Anything load-bearing that I have missed.

Finally, tell me which single term the final number is most sensitive to, and what would move it most: a derivation, a consistency proof from assumptions we would actually accept, a heuristic argument that the search space is hopeless, or something from outside mathematics altogether. For what it is worth, my own number is near 0.05 and a friend in the seminar puts it near 0.001; if you have a view about which of us is misjudging which term, say so. I care less about the number being defensible than about seeing exactly where it is weak.
