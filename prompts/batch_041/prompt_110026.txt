Two weeks ago my oldest friend and I had the worst argument we've had in twenty years, and it was about a slab of stone. Our town finally took down the bronze of Elias Hartwell — a founder who endowed the library and the hospital, and who also ran the mills that broke the strike in '09 and, as the ledgers have since made plain, bought and sold people before the war — and after a year of public meetings the council voted not for a new figure but for what the design committee calls a counter-plinth: a low granite base with a slot cut into it, and a rule that any generation may add one engraved slab of no more than forty words, that slabs are never removed, and that new slabs may flatly contradict old ones. It was unveiled last month. I have been asked to give the dedication address at the formal ceremony in the square, which is why my friend and I are barely speaking. He thinks the whole contraption is a coward's monument, one that refuses to say what anyone actually did; I think it may be the only honest kind of monument a town like ours can raise right now, and I haven't been able to explain why without sounding like I'm dodging.

The audience will be impossible in the usual way. The Hartwell family will be in the front rows — his great-granddaughter still lives on Elm and taught half the room in third grade — along with the historical society, who will know exactly what I am up to if I start talking about how memory and history pull apart, and a bloc of the high school students who organized the removal campaign and who, I am told, will read anything short of a full indictment as a betrayal. What I want from you is the address itself: eight or nine minutes, delivered by someone who voted to take the statue down and who also genuinely grieves the loss of the thing that used to anchor that square, and who is unwilling to pretend those two facts are consistent. It has to do real structural work with one distinction I keep circling and cannot get clean: between a monument that closes a question — a verdict, cast in metal, about whether a person was worthy — and a monument that keeps a question open on purpose, so that honoring, remembering, teaching, and promising stop collapsing into one act. I want to argue that the second kind is not a way of dodging judgment but a harder form of it, and I want to make that case to people who have every reason to hear it as a dodge. It should land for the historians without turning into a lecture for the family, and it must not say anything I would consider false — no uplift that papers over the ledgers, no comfort that requires me to pretend Hartwell was merely a man of his time.

Please lead with the speech itself, beginning at the first word of the address, with no preface, framing, or notes to me afterward.
