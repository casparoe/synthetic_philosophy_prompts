For the past year I have been running a monthly discussion evening: sixteen or so adults, schoolteachers, a nurse, a couple of retired engineers, people who read popular science and philosophy for pleasure but have never studied either formally. The next session is mine to lead, ninety minutes, and I want to spend it on a thought I keep returning to in Turing's writing on machine intelligence. Rather than trying to construct an adult mind directly, he suggests building something like a child machine and educating it: give it senses, train it with rewards and punishments, and let whatever intelligence it ends up with come out of the upbringing rather than out of the initial design. Half the time this strikes me as the only sane way to go about it. The other half it looks like a way of smuggling the entire problem into the word education, because somebody still has to decide what the child is like before the schooling starts, and somebody has to be the teacher.

I want the group to feel that difficulty in their hands rather than hear me assert it, so I would like the evening built as an activity rather than a talk. Design it. My own sketch, which you should improve on or replace if you can do better, is this. Four teams of four. Each team is issued a learner and has to get that learner to perform some small task — sort cards, reach a target on a board, produce a sequence — while being permitted to communicate with it only by handing over tokens: one colour meaning that was right, another meaning that was wrong. No speaking to the learner, no writing anything down for it, no demonstrating the answer. Each learner comes with a hidden disposition, and the dispositions differ across the four teams: one generalizes by similarity, one goes by position, one is relentlessly literal, one is sensitive to the order in which the tokens arrive. The teams are not told this. Each learner also has deliberately limited senses, and the limits differ too, so that what a learner can perceive is itself a design decision that somebody made before the teaching began. At the end there is a fifth round: each team must set its learner a problem of the same kind that it never trained on.

I want the rules, the materials, the sealed cards written out in full, the token economy (no real money), the timings for the ninety minutes, and what I say at each point, with the constraint that nothing is named until the debrief. The debrief should walk back through what actually happened and map each moment onto a piece of the Turing picture: which part of what they did was teaching and which part was design, why the same training produced four different learners, where their own tacit knowledge turned out to be the real bottleneck, and what the fifth round shows about a machine doing something of its own accord rather than something it was drilled into. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this — I would rather hear it now — and how to handle each failure without spoiling the discovery. Finally, be honest about the one feature of Turing's position that the activity cannot reproduce and that I will have to supply myself in the last ten minutes.
