I am in a reading group working through the decision theory in Hofstadter's Metamagical Themas, and a friend has constructed an argument that I can neither refute nor bring myself to accept. He begins from the Platonia dilemma: twenty people are told that whoever alone sends a telegram by noon will receive a billion dollars, that if more than one telegram arrives or none arrives nobody gets anything, and that they are forbidden to communicate. The superrational answer, he says, is for each of them to send with probability one in twenty, since that is the symmetric profile that maximises each person's expected payoff; on that profile each of the twenty expects something under twenty million dollars, and because the twenty randomisations are independent the chance that anybody at all wins is only about thirty-eight per cent, with no telegram or several being the likelier outcomes. He says this is already the interesting part: the recommended profile is much worse for everyone than a correlated one, such as a single designated sender chosen at random, which would be worth fifty million each in expectation. He then argues as follows.

(1) A strategy is superrational when it maximises expected payoff under the supposition that every agent in the same problem adopts the same strategy. (2) The Platonia dilemma is such a problem, and the strategy just described is the superrational one. (3) That recommendation nowhere rests on any causal influence of my rolling a die upon anyone else's rolling a die; the twenty randomisations are causally independent, and my choice cannot make another person wire or not wire. What it rests on is only the fact that the other nineteen are in the same problem and will therefore arrive at the same strategy. (4) So whenever I am in the same problem as others who reason as I do, I am entitled to act on the symmetric optimum, and doing so is not magical thinking but merely the recognition that the same problem solved by the same reasoning yields the same strategy. (5) Now consider a case where I am indeed in the same problem as someone who reasons as I do, but where my payoff does not depend on his actions at all: my exact duplicate sits in an adjacent room facing his own Platonia dilemma with his own nineteen strangers. By (4) I should treat my choice as evidence about his and act on the symmetric optimum of the enlarged situation, even though the enlarged situation is not a game and his telegram neither helps nor harms me. (6) Consider next a case where my payoff does depend on others but they are not all in my problem: a thousand of us are deciding whether to contribute to a public good, and I know that nine hundred reason exactly as I do while a hundred do not. By (4) I should contribute, since if all who reason as I do contribute the good is provided. The same argument tells each of the nine hundred to contribute; it also tells me to contribute when only three others reason as I do, and when I am the only one. (7) The principle therefore obliges me to treat every description under which I can be grouped with others as a coordination problem and to act on the best uniform rule for that group, however little my action matters and however badly it serves my ends. (8) No decision theory may demand that, so (1) as stated is false.

I cannot find the step that breaks, and I am not persuaded by the diagnosis offered in the group, which is that the trouble lies in the independence of the randomisations in (2); I do not see how a fact about dice could rescue (7). If I must choose, I would rather keep (3), and with it the thought that superrationality is not a causal hypothesis, than accept (7). Tell me which step I should reject, why it looked innocent, and what I pay for rejecting it. If the repair is a restriction on when agents count as being in the same problem, say what work that restriction does and whether it can be stated without circularity; if instead the damage is done earlier, say where. I would also like to know what remains of the Platonia dilemma's answer once the offending step is removed, and whether anything in the vicinity survives as a defensible decision rule. Please write in British English.
