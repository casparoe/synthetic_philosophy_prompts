I've just finished a trade book on accountability and repair — the sort written by a journalist who has read a good deal of philosophy and is eager to tell you what it all adds up to — and one chapter, on who gets to blame whom, has been nagging at me. Its argument, as I'd reconstruct it, runs roughly like this. For a blaming to land, three things must be in place: the act blamed must really be wrong, the person blamed must really be blameworthy, and the blamer must have standing. The third condition, the chapter says, is simply the hypocrisy condition — you lose standing when you have done the same thing yourself, which is why people who live in glass houses shouldn't throw stones — and the test of standing is therefore whether the blamer is, on the whole, a decent person on the matter at hand. It then offers a taxonomy: you can lose standing through hypocrisy, through complicity, or through meddling in what isn't your business, but it insists that these are all hypocrisy in disguise, since in each case the blamer is claiming an authority he doesn't possess. From there it draws what it treats as the radical upshot: when standing is missing the accusation has no force at all, and the wrongdoer may simply shrug it off, because blame from someone without standing is like a referee's whistle from a man who isn't a referee — not merely rude but null, a piece of etiquette rather than a moral act, which the wrongdoer can ignore without answering. Finally it anticipates the obvious objection — that even a thief speaks the truth when he says theft is wrong — and treats that as a refutation of the whole standing idea, concluding that what philosophers call standing is really just social awkwardness dressed up in juridical language, and that the interesting question was never standing but simply whether the act was wrong.

I suspect the chapter is being unfair somewhere, though I can't locate it, and I'm inclined to accept its conclusion: I've never known what to say to the person who accuses me of something while being guilty of the same thing, and "you have no standing" has always seemed to me a complete reply. Tell me what a defender of the standing condition would say in response to each charge, and give me the strongest version of the idea, since the chapter's version may be a straw man. Then tell me whether the chapter's bottom line survives the corrections: if the details are repaired, is there still a distinctive phenomenon here, or does the notion really collapse into the observation that hypocrites are irritating? Please also say plainly what the chapter leaves out altogether, and finish by stating in one sentence what the corrected bottom line should be.
