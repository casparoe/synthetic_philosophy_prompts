Bit of an odd one, but you're the right sort of thing to ask. I spent thirty-five years as an entomologist — field work mostly, wasps and ants — and since I retired I've been reading alot of philosophy, and now I've somehow ended up on the animal-care committee at the field station I used to run. We meet once a month, about half a dozen protocols, I've got maybe twenty minutes per proposal and no whiteboard, and I'm the only one there who's read any of this stuff, so every time the word 'sphexish' comes up in a meeting everyone turns and looks at me.

Here's my problem. I know the old story about the digger wasp — prey dragged to the burrow, wasp goes in to check, comes out, you nudge the prey a few inches, wasp drags it back and checks again, and you can loop it for as long as you've got patience. Standard picture of a creature running a fixed routine. But the word gets used two ways in our meetings and I can't get the committee to see the difference. Sometimes it's a factual claim about the behaviour: this animal isn't adjusting to changed circumstances. Sometimes its a put-down: nobody home, it's a machine, don't bother worrying about it. And from the philosophy I've gathered that the second move is probably a mistake, and that the first claim is much harder to establish than it sounds — you need the right sort of perturbation to find out, and 'didn't adjust in the test I ran' isn't the same as 'can't adjust'. There's also a point I keep half-remembering, that flexibility wouldn't obviously buy you free will anyway, so I don't want the procedure handing out 'free' as a prize for being adaptable either.

What I want is a procedure I can actually run on a protocol in twenty minutes, in the room. The questions in order. What counts as enough of an answer to move on. Where the thing simply stops and returns a no. And — the part I really care about — exactly where it stops being a question about the animal and becomes a question about how much we're willing to do to a creature we can't read, because that's the bit I keep trying to hand to the chair and she keeps handing back. She signs off, so it needs to be defensible to someone who's never read a word of philosophy.

Then run it on three, because I want to see it work and see it bite. One: a proposal to test web-repair in spiders by repeatedly wrecking the webs. Two: a proposal to loop the wasp through the displacement test and write the result up as evidence of 'no inner life'. Three, and here I'll admit my bias — a study on ants that I'm fairly sure is harmless because, come on, they're ants. I half hope your procedure clears that one. If it doesn't, tell me why I'm wrong rather than humouring me.

Last thing: tell me how confident you actually are in the procedure, and single out the joint you trust least. I'd much rather you say 'step three is a value judgement wearing a lab coat' than sell me a tidy flowchart that comes apart the first time someone brings in a bee.
