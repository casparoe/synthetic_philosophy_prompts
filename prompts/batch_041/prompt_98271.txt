Logical omniscience isn't a bug in our models, it's a bug in our words

In epistemic logic we model knowledge like this: an agent knows φ just in case φ is true in every world the agent considers possible. This immediately gives you logical omniscience. Every logical truth is true in every world, so the agent knows every logical truth. And if the agent knows φ and knows φ→ψ, then in every accessible world both φ and φ→ψ hold, so ψ holds, so the agent knows ψ. Closure under modus ponens falls right out. Same story for the K axiom, K(φ→ψ)→(Kφ→Kψ); it's valid because K is what logicians call a normal modal operator — it obeys the K axiom and the rule that all theorems are known — and normality is exactly what buys you the omniscience. People treat this as a scandal, and there are whole research programs — awareness models, where the agent is simply unaware of certain formulas, or impossible worlds, where the agent's accessible worlds can violate logic — devoted to defusing it.

I want to argue that the scandal is fake, or at least that it's a scandal about our vocabulary and not about the models. Here's my reasoning. Try to fix omniscience by saying the agent only knows what it has explicitly represented. Now "knowledge" is just a list of sentences the agent has stored, and there's no truth condition left. An agent could "know" something false. That seems wrong — knowledge is factive; you can't know a falsehood. So we keep the truth condition. Try instead to keep truth but drop closure. Then we owe an account of which consequences the agent does and doesn't know, and any such account is going to say something like: the agent knows whatever it can derive in n steps, or within some resource bound. But then the agent knows all the n-step consequences, including the ones it never bothers to derive. That's just omniscience again, only now relative to a bounded consequence relation instead of classical consequence. You haven't escaped omniscience; you've rescaled it.

So my conclusion is that "knows" is being used in two senses. There's a factive sense — success, truth, closure under consequence — and there's a psychological sense — what's actually in the agent's head, which isn't closed and arguably isn't even factive. The problem of logical omniscience is what happens when you try to make one word do both jobs. The awareness/impossible-worlds industry isn't confused exactly, but I think it's mostly building models of the psychological sense and then getting upset that they don't satisfy the axioms of the factive sense. Which, like, of course they don't.
