I've been sitting in on a small reading group this term — five of us, Tuesday nights, someone's kitchen, a whiteboard that never quite erases — and we've spent the last few weeks on higher-order evidence, i.e. the stuff you have that's about how reliable you are rather than about the thing you're trying to figure out. Last week Dana, who's the sharpest of us and knows it, put an argument up on the board and then just stood there looking pleased with herself, and I've been chewing on it alot since, because I think the conclusion is obviously false but every line looks fine to me and I can't find the seam. Here it is roughly as she wrote it:

(1) You should believe a proposition just in case your evidence supports it. (Boring, uncontroversial, the thing everyone nods at before the interesting part starts.)
(2) Suppose I've spent a long night on a hard question — say whether some argument in a paper I'm reading is actually valid — and I've ended up convinced that it is (call that claim p). The first-order evidence, the actual considerations about the argument, really does support p, and I can't point to anywhere my reasoning went wrong.
(3) So I should believe p. (from 1, 2)
(4) But I've also got a second pile of evidence, about me rather than about p: I know I'm fried and that this is a domain where I run overconfident; I know that people at least as careful as me who read the same paper don't all agree with me; and I know my track record of confident late-night verdicts is not great. Call this the reliability evidence.
(5) The reliability evidence is evidence too, and what it supports is the claim that my belief in p is irrational — that it's the sort of belief a well-calibrated person wouldn't be in.
(6) So by the same principle as (1), I should believe that my belief in p is irrational. (from 1, 5)
(7) So the fully rational state is to believe p and also to believe that believing p is irrational.
(8) But that's akrasia, and akrasia is supposed to be impossible: you can't be rational and be in that state, because if you really think a belief of yours is irrational you don't get to keep it.
(9) So either (1) is false, or the move at (5)/(6) is bad, or akrasia is possible after all.

Dana says (9) is a genuine trilemma and we have to pick a horn. I hate all three. Giving up (1) feels like giving up on belief answering to evidence at all. Giving up (5) seems to mean a fact about me — how tired I am, how often I've been wrong before — can't bear on what I ought to believe, which is weird given that higher-order evidence is supposed to matter. And saying akrasia is fine seems to drain "irrational" of any force, since the whole point of calling a belief irrational is that you can't rationally hang onto it. I'd rather keep (1) if I can, but I don't think I can afford the other two either, so I keep going in circles.

What I want from you: say which step I should give up, and then be straight with me about the price. If it's (1), tell me what the principle should be instead and whether the replacement still does the work (1) was doing at both levels. If it's (5) or (6), tell me exactly how the reliability evidence is supposed to bear on what I ought to believe, without turning it into evidence about p. If it's (8), tell me what akrasia actually is and why the state I'm calling incoherent isn't. And if the real problem is that I've framed the whole thing wrong and it isn't a trilemma at all, say that instead. No reading list please — I'm trying to get the shape of this, not cite it.
