I recently signed a pledge to give a significant fraction of my income to organizations that fight extreme poverty, and at a dinner last week two friends of mine, Daniel and Mira, argued about whether that kind of commitment is what morality actually requires. I did not take part, but I have been turning their exchange over in my mind, and I would like an impartial adjudication of it.

Daniel began by saying that consequentialism is not merely demanding but abrasive. It is demanding, he said, because it requires one to keep sacrificing until the marginal cost to oneself equals the marginal benefit to others; it is abrasive because it treats one’s own projects, relationships, and talents as instruments for producing good, so that any of them may be overridden whenever something else would produce slightly more. He offered a case: suppose one could save a stranger’s life only by permanently abandoning the pursuit that gives one’s life meaning. A consequentialist, he said, must tell one to abandon it, and that shows the theory leaves no room for the distinction between the morally required and the morally supererogatory.

Mira replied that this conflates a criterion of rightness with a decision procedure. Consequentialism can hold that the right act is the one that maximizes expected value while permitting agents to use heuristics, rules of thumb, and partial attachments to approximate that criterion. She added that the demandingness objection is often question-begging: it assumes that common-sense morality’s permission to favor oneself is a fixed constraint on any acceptable theory, whereas an impartialist will deny precisely that. She also said that if the objection proves anything, it proves too much, since any morality that takes impartiality seriously will sometimes demand that we sacrifice our projects.

Daniel responded that the decision-procedure reply is evasive. If one never actually uses the consequentialist criterion to decide what to do, he said, one is not living as a consequentialist; one is following rules and giving them a consequentialist label. He also said that the “proves too much” reply misses a distinction between demandingness and abrasiveness: a theory may demand that one give up a kidney without demanding that one treat one’s own life as a mere resource. He claimed that any acceptable morality must be one an agent can own from the inside, and that a theory which tells one to regard one’s deepest commitments as fungible cannot be owned by anyone with a life to live.

Mira answered that this is either an empirical claim about what people can psychologically sustain or a normative claim smuggled in as a constraint. If it is empirical, it may be true but irrelevant to what is right. If it is normative, it needs an argument, and the argument cannot be that the theory makes one feel bad. She also pointed out that Daniel’s case is underdescribed: if the pursuit really is less valuable than the life one could save, why should its meaning for the agent outweigh the stranger’s survival? She noted that consequentialists can accept agent-relative prerogatives as a concession to human psychology without treating them as fundamental.

Daniel replied that the case is not underdescribed; it is designed to isolate the value of a life project from its consequences. He said Mira’s reply assumes that the only reason to care about one’s own projects is the good they produce, which is precisely what is in dispute. He also said that the arbitrariness of any cost threshold cuts against consequentialism, not for it: if there is no principled point at which sacrifice stops, then the theory cannot be action-guiding in the way a moral theory must be. Mira said the threshold is not arbitrary if it is set by the criterion itself: sacrifice until further sacrifice would produce less good. Daniel said that is not a threshold an agent can live by.

Please adjudicate their exchange point by point rather than simply choosing a side. For each main move, say whether it is clearly mistaken, partly right, or clearly right, and explain why. Address at least: (1) whether the demandingness objection is question-begging; (2) whether the criterion/decision-procedure distinction saves consequentialism from Daniel’s reply; (3) whether the demandingness objection should be distinguished from an abrasiveness objection about treating one’s projects as fungible; (4) whether Daniel’s life-project case has force or is underdescribed; (5) whether the absence of a principled cost threshold is a problem for consequentialism or simply a consequence of the view; and (6) what, if anything, both parties are missing. End with a verdict about who is more right overall, and what a defensible consequentialist position would have to concede.
