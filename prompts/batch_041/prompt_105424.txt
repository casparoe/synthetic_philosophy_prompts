I teach a second-year moral philosophy seminar (16 students, 80 minutes, ordinary classroom with movable chairs) and the unit on promises is the one that never lands. They can all recite that you shouldn't break promises, but when I push on *why* the obligation is there they either say "because people rely on you" or "because society would fall apart," and neither of those gets at what I actually find puzzling. What I want is an activity where they run into the puzzle themselves instead of hearing me describe it. The puzzle, roughly: the moment you say "I promise," you seem to have changed your own normative situation in a way that doesn't obviously depend on whether anyone was counting on you, whether anyone finds out, whether keeping it still does anyone any good, or even on whether the other person still wants you to do it. Most explanations make the obligation come from something outside the promise itself -- expectations, reciprocity, the general usefulness of the institution -- and I want them to feel the bit that won't fit into any of those, and also feel how tempting it is to keep explaining it that way anyway.

So: design a runnable thing for that room. I'd like it built around making real commitments to each other with small non-monetary stakes (points, tokens, privileges, whatever works without cash), arranged so that in the first phase keeping your word is fully explained by reciprocity and by everyone watching -- and then, in later phases, one by one, you strip those supports away: a round where the choice is completely private and the other person will never learn what you did; a round where the promisee comes back and says they no longer care, or actively wishes you'd do the opposite; a round where the person you promised is out of the picture entirely. I want to see at which point they still feel the pull and at which point they stop, and I want them arguing about it. Give me the rules, materials, the payoff scheme, the sequence of phases, timing for 80 minutes, and roles if you need a facilitator or a couple of scripted players.

Then a debrief that maps what actually happened onto the underlying disagreement -- the family of views that treat the obligation as generated by a social practice and the mutual expectations it creates, versus the family that treats the promise as handing the promisee some kind of standing or authority over what you do, so that only they can let you off the hook -- and that flags the trap where someone keeps a promise only because breaking it would make them feel like a rat, which might be a different thing from the promise binding. Also tell me honestly what this setup can't reproduce and that I'll have to supply some other way (I suspect promises to people who are dead, and promises to yourself, are both going to be impossible to stage, but say what else). And what predictably goes wrong the first time I run it -- my guess is that at least one group turns the whole thing into a bargaining game and stops caring about the promises at all, but tell me what else to expect and how to handle it.
