I am a novelist, and once a year I teach a short elective to graduate students in a writing program on chance in fiction — on how a narrator manufactures the reader's sense that something happened at random, and why that sense is far easier to produce than to defend. The session I am writing to you about is the one I have never managed to get right. I want my students to walk into the failure of the principle of indifference, feel it in their hands, and only afterward learn that it has a name.

There are twelve of them, and I have a single ninety-minute block in a seminar room with movable tables. I would like the entire block to be an activity, with no opening lecture and nothing on a screen, followed by a debrief.

Let me state the idea plainly, because the activity has to be built to deliver it. Suppose we agree that a stick is to be divided into three pieces by two random breaks, and we ask for the probability that the three pieces could serve as the sides of a triangle. There is a natural way to make the breaks — choose two points on the stick independently and uniformly — and under it the long-run frequency of triangles is one value. But there is another procedure that is, by any ordinary standard, just as random: break the stick once at a uniformly chosen point, and then break the longer of the two resulting pieces at a uniformly chosen point. Neither procedure cheats, neither rests on a mistaken picture of the stick, and yet they do not yield the same frequency. The disagreement lives entirely in what a random break was supposed to mean, and no amount of care about the stick will dissolve it. There are further natural readings as well — for instance, breaking a coin-flipped one of the two pieces — and they do not agree with one another either.

What I want the students to feel, before anyone says the words principle of indifference, is the particular vertigo of holding two defensible answers to what had looked like a single question, and then the almost irresistible urge to declare one procedure the real meaning of random and dismiss the other. I want them to feel how strong that urge is and how little actually supports it.

So please design the ninety minutes around this. My instinct is small groups, each committed to one procedure and given enough dry spaghetti — I will buy several boxes — to estimate its own frequency by actually breaking sticks, with some stake that makes the group care about getting the number right: a bet, a scoring rule, a small prize, whatever you judge will work better than money. Then I want the groups to meet, compare their numbers, and have to explain the gap to one another. If you can arrange it, I would rather the groups generate the competing procedures themselves — asked cold to write down the fair way to divide a stick at random, with different groups writing down different things — than have me hand the recipes to them; but if that is too unreliable, assign the procedures and keep the reveal.

Give me the rules, the materials, the payoff or scoring scheme, a minute-by-minute shape of the block, and the debrief. In the debrief I want you to mark the precise moments in the activity where the indifference intuition surfaced and what it was promising, and to say plainly why the promise could not be kept. I also want an honest account of what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how I should handle it in the moment.

One last thing, which I care about more than the rest: tell me which part of this idea the activity cannot reproduce on its own — the piece I will have to supply myself, out loud, once the sticks are on the table — so that I am not quietly pretending the game has done work that only an argument can do.
