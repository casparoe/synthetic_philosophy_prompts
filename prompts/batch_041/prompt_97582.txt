For the past two quarters I have been the person at my company responsible for what our assistant products are permitted to say about themselves, and the part of the job I did not anticipate is that it has turned into a metaphysics seminar. The recurring question is which of a system's internal states, if any, we may describe in belief-talk — "believes," "thinks," "remembers," "expects" — in our documentation, in the interface, and in the incident reports we file when a system gets something wrong.

My most useful interlocutor has been a colleague on the review panel, a cognitive scientist who is willing to commit to verdicts on particular systems rather than discuss the topic in the abstract. I have been recording them. On his account: a household thermostat does not believe the room is warm, though it registers the temperature; a chess engine does not believe it has lost its queen, even when its evaluation registers the loss, because the position is handed to it and there is nothing in it that could be wrong; a honeybee's waggle dance carries information about the direction of nectar, but the bee believes nothing; a dog that runs to the door at the sound of keys believes someone is coming; a rat that takes a shortcut down a corridor it has never traversed believes the food is in a particular place; a learning thermostat that infers a household's occupancy pattern forms and revises internal predictions, but believes nothing; and a lookup-table chatbot that outputs a sentence about rain tomorrow believes nothing. His verdicts have been stable across these cases, and he has never once answered "it depends."

The system now in front of us is the one that prompted this whole exercise. It maintains a persistent, editable model of each user's circumstances — say, the user's travel schedule — populated from conversation and from a calendar feed. It sometimes holds an entry that is false; it corrects the entry when the user says the date is wrong; and it plans and acts on the entry in the meantime. Suppose it currently records a flight on a Wednesday when the flight is in fact on Tuesday. Before the correction, does he say it believed the flight was on Wednesday?

What I need from you: first, his likely verdict on that case and your confidence in it; second, the general rule, or the several general rules, that fit the verdicts he has already given — I want at least three candidates, including any that fit all his verdicts equally well, and for each one the verdict it predicts here; third, which of them you think he is actually running, and why that one over the others; fourth, the case beyond this one that would best separate the surviving rules, and what each predicts about it — and if some of those separations cannot be constructed at all, say which and why; and fifth, whether you think his track record genuinely underdetermines the new case, and if so, what he would be committing himself to with each verdict. I am less interested in a verdict about our system than in the shape of his rule, since it is the rule, not the verdict, that will end up in our documentation standard.
