I have been keeping a private ledger of my verdicts on decisions that turn on unlimited payoffs and vanishing probabilities, and I have reached the point where I can no longer tell whether the ledger expresses a principle or only a temperament. I would like you to settle the question. The cases and my verdicts are below, followed by the rule I have been using in the hope of keeping them in order.

The cases

1. Ordinary long shot. One chance in a million at ten million dollars; a dollar to play. I play without hesitation.

2. Doubling game. A fair coin is flipped until it lands tails. The payout is two dollars if tails comes on the first flip, four if on the second, eight if on the third, and so on without a cap. I would pay a few dollars to play. I would not stake my house on it, even though I accept the arithmetic that its expected value is unlimited.

3. The wager. I choose between living as though a certain God exists and living as though he does not. If he exists, the first way of life is rewarded without limit and the second is not; if he does not exist, the first costs me a finite amount of ordinary life. I find the argument genuinely compelling. I resist acting on it. I cannot say that it is a bad argument.

4. The threat. A stranger tells me that unless I give him a hundred dollars he will bring about suffering that exceeds any finite measure, and I have no way to check his claim. My honest estimate of the chance that he can do what he says is about one in a hundred billion. I do not give him the money, and I am not tempted to.

5. The mirror. My evidence supports, roughly equally, a being who rewards disbelief without limit and punishes belief without limit. I do not think this argument has any force at all, and I cannot say why it has less force than the argument in case 3, which I think has a great deal.

6. Many gods. Several mutually exclusive religions each promise an unlimited reward to their adherents and an unlimited punishment to everyone else, and no more than one can be satisfied. I do not feel merely undecided; I think the situation gives me no reason to move in any direction, and that this is a fact about the situation rather than a failure of nerve on my part.

7. Undefined probability. Same shape as case 3, except that no one, not even an ideal reasoner, has grounds for assigning any probability to the unlimited outcome; there is no small number that is the right one. I refuse to treat this as case 4. In case 4 I think the probability is small but real, and I refuse the offer anyway; here I refuse because there is no probability to work with, and I take that to be a different reason.

8. Sincerity. As in case 3, except that the unlimited reward is reserved for those who sincerely believe, and I cannot decide to believe something by deciding. I think this changes the case, though I cannot say whether it strengthens the wager or weakens it: the only thing I can do is try to bring the belief about, and whether I succeed is not up to me.

My tentative principle

(P) An option is available to me only if its outcomes have determinate probabilities. Among available options, any option carrying a nonzero probability of an unlimited loss is ruled out, whatever its upside, and this constraint outranks the pursuit of unlimited gain. Subject to that constraint, I maximize expected value in the ordinary way.

I offer (P) mostly so that you have something to break, and I already suspect that it breaks on case 4. I want to know whether the fault lies in the principle or in my verdict.

What I want

I want to know whether a single principle can accommodate my verdicts; whether (P) can be repaired or must be replaced; what the best principle says about cases I have not listed; and which of my verdicts I must give up. Treat the wager itself as the thing to be decided rather than as background: I want a verdict on whether it is a good argument, a bad one, or an argument that yields no verdict at all.

Structure your reply in six sections, in this order.

1. Summary — about 200 words. Restate my verdicts and (P) in your own terms, and flag any ambiguity in my cases that you had to resolve in order to proceed.

2. Candidate principles — about 600 words. State two or three rules precisely enough to apply mechanically, including (P) repaired if it can be. For each, name the verdict it was built to preserve and the verdict it sacrifices.

3. The test of (P) — about 400 words. Where it delivers my verdicts, where it fails, and whether each failure calls for a repair or for abandoning the principle.

4. Verdicts on cases I have not listed — about 400 words. Apply the principle you are prepared to defend to each of the following: (a) a determinate probability of one in ten to the eightieth of a finite good of ten to the eightieth units; (b) an unlimited payoff available only if a second person, facing the same problem, also takes it; (c) two options each of unlimited expected value and no further structure; (d) an unlimited payoff that accrues to me only if I perform an act I have already been performing unknowingly.

5. The verdicts I have to abandon — about 300 words. Name them, say what each costs me, and say which you would abandon if only one could be saved.

6. Bottom line — about 200 words. The principle you would endorse, what it implies for the wager, and the consideration that would make you change your answer.
