I host a small podcast on decision theory and ethics (a few hundred listeners, mostly grad students). Next month's guest is a philosopher who's worked on Harsanyi's aggregation theorem for years, and she's bringing a line I want to push on. Her reading is that the theorem is almost always read wrong: on her view it isn't a proof of utilitarianism at all — it's a proof that if you make the social ranking satisfy the expected-utility axioms, then the only way to satisfy even a weak Pareto condition is a weighted sum of individual von Neumann–Morgenstern utilities, where the weights just are the social observer's own credences about being each person. So the "how much does each person count" question turns out, for her, to be an epistemic question about a hypothetical gamble rather than a moral one — and she's happy to say social welfare is observer-relative. I think thats either trivially true or a reductio.

Draft me eight to ten interview questions. I want at least one each on:

- whether putting the expected-utility axioms on the social ranking is itself a substantive moral assumption (the old objection that it bakes in a "before the coin lands" conception of fairness), and why she thinks that isn't where the theorem is weakest;
- what actually happens to the weights once you drop the assumption that the observer is equally likely to be anybody — i.e., whether utilitarianism was ever inside the theorem or only ever came out of an extra impartiality premise;
- the antisocial-preferences problem (the sadist who gets utility from other people's misery), and whether Harsanyi's own fix of splitting personal preferences from moral ones does any real work or just relabels the difficulty;
- the case where two people's utilities can't be compared at all, and whether the theorem needs genuine interpersonal comparability or only the comparability of an observer's "extended" preferences across whoever she might turn out to be;
- and one on whether her observer-relative reading can tell an actual government anything, or whether it quietly makes the whole thing useless for policy.

For each of those, add a follow-up keyed to the answer she's most likely to give, so I'm not stuck when she says the obvious thing.

One more thing: when you hand me the questions, tell me which of the claims packed into them are settled versus genuinely contested among people who actually work on Harsanyi — I don't want to walk on stage and assert some contested reading as if it were the standard one.
