I write a weekly ideas column for a mid-sized paper, and right now I'm working on a piece about how philosophers of language actually settle cases, which is how I ended up sitting in on a graduate pragmatics seminar at 8:30 in the morning with a notebook on my knee — hence the patchiness of what follows. The professor is a Grice person, very old school, and every week she puts an utterance on the board and makes the class vote on whether it's a case of conversational implicature before she gives her own ruling. I've been keeping a list of her rulings because I want to see if I can call the next one before she does. So far: a landlord's reference for a prospective tenant that mentions only that the man has never set fire to any part of the building — she says the landlord conversationally implicates that he's a bad tenant, and that this is cancellable and not part of what was said. "Some of the reviewers liked the draft" — implicature that not all of them did, cancellable, not said. A speaker addressing an almost empty room is told "what a fantastic turnout" — she says the sarcasm is an implicature too, arrived at by flouting the maxim about truthfulness. But then: "she's a good novelist but she can't spell" — she says the contrast there is not a conversational implicature at all; it rides on the word "but", it doesn't survive cancellation, and it evaporates if you swap in "and" (she called that detachable, if I wrote it down right). And a theatre critic who writes that the understudy "delivered a sequence of sounds bearing a loose resemblance to the lines of the play" — she says that one IS a conversational implicature, namely that the girl was terrible, even though the same point could plainly be made in a way that didn't carry it, because the reader has to work it out from the flouted maxim about how you say a thing.

Next week she's saving this one for the end of class. A man has a houseguest who is driving him up the wall. He'd never say so, and he definately doesn't want the guest to twig that he's being steered out of the house, because then the guest would dig in out of spite and stay another fortnight. So over breakfast he remarks, quite truthfully, that there's a lovely little restaurant on the far side of town that does a superb lunch, counting on the guest to take the hint and spend the afternoon over there; if the guest asked him point blank whether he was hinting, he'd deny it, and he'd mean the denial, because he never wanted his nudging to be visible as nudging. Did he conversationally implicate that the guest should go out to lunch? What I want from you is her verdict, the rule she seems to actually be running, the other rules that fit her earlier rulings just as well, what each of those predicts about this case, and the one case I should put to her next if I want to split the rules that survive. One more thing, and it's the part I can't settle on my own: does the answer turn on whether the guest actually catches on, or only on whether the man intended him to catch on? I ask because I have 1,200 words to file and I'd rather not get this wrong in print.
