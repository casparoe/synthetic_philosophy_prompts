I'm a PhD student working on decision theory and multi-agent interaction, and I've been circling a family of cases that I find genuinely unsettling and would like to see dramatized rather than explained. I'd like you to write me a short story built around them.

Here is the machinery, stated plainly, so that the story can rest on it rather than on prior reading. In the ordinary causal picture, a threat works because the threatened party's choice bears on whether the threatened harm occurs: comply and it is withheld, refuse and it lands. There is a different structure — sometimes called an acausal threat — in which the harm is not caused by your choice at all. The threatener has already decided, or will decide, whether to harm you on the basis of a prediction of how you will respond; at that point your actual choice is only evidence about you, not a lever on the outcome. The usual reason to comply evaporates, since complying no longer does anything; but refusing does not help either, because the harm tracks the prediction rather than the act. The threatener, if it models you well, is exploiting the fact that your decision procedure is transparent to it.

The defense I want the story to take seriously is that you can sometimes protect yourself not by choosing differently in the moment but by being a different kind of agent in advance — by adopting what I've been calling, in my own notes, a surrogate goal: a goal you do not intrinsically have, taken up precisely because, in a world where other agents can predict your decision procedure, the goal you have is itself a form of deterrence. A threatener who predicts that you are pursuing the surrogate goal may find threatening you pointless or counterproductive, and so will never issue the threat. The cost is that you must now really act on a goal that isn't yours, including in cases where no threat ever materializes — and the goal has to be genuine rather than a bluff, or the prediction won't go through.

What I want from you is a short story — not an exposition of these ideas — that lives inside this problem. I'm less interested in a technothriller about superintelligence than in the human-scale texture of it: what it is like to reason this way about one's own motives, to wonder whether adopting a surrogate goal is prudent self-modification or a slow form of self-betrayal, and what happens when the other party is running the same play, each of you trying to be predicted as the kind of agent the other should not threaten. Let the story find its own setting and characters, and don't feel obliged to put the terms above on the page; what matters is that the central situation is a genuine instance of the structure, and that the ending takes a position — through what happens, not through a lecture — on whether the surrogate-goal move is a solution, a trap, or something in between.

Begin directly with the story. No preface, no framing remarks, and no explanation of the concept afterward.
