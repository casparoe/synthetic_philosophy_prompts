I have no training in philosophy; what follows comes from a few years of reading on my own and thinking about it in the evenings, so please correct my terminology where it is wrong.

The position I want to examine is one I have seen called tranquilism, and I understand it as follows. The only thing good in itself is the absence of craving and of suffering. Pleasure and satisfaction are not separate goods; a satisfied desire is the removal of a discomfort, so it is good, if at all, for what it takes away and never for what it adds. Everything else that people call good — knowledge, achievement, love, beauty — is good only instrumentally, insofar as it reduces craving or suffering.

I have two things I would like you to do with this.

First, a distinction of my own that I suspect matters and cannot get straight. Craving usually has an object: I want the coffee, I want the letter to arrive. But I have also noticed something I can only describe as a hum — a low background restlessness present even on days when there is nothing in particular that I want, an itch with no location, which quiets when I am absorbed in something and returns when the absorption ends. Is tranquilism a claim about object-directed cravings, about the hum, or about both? Can the two come apart, as in a person with no cravings for anything in particular but a loud hum, or a person with strong cravings and no hum? And if they can, which of them is the view really about?

Second, a comparison I keep circling. Set against tranquilism the view — I will call it the gripped-life view — that what makes a life worth living is being gripped by something: that desire is not a defect to be removed but the very thing that gives a life shape and weight, so that a life with the cravings removed would not be a serene version of a life but the absence of one. I would like you to compare the two views properly, not merely to adjudicate between them. In particular:

- If the absence of craving and suffering is the only intrinsic good, why is the best life not a dreamless sleep, or the life of someone in a coma? If the answer is that someone must be there to be undisturbed, has the view not admitted a second good — awareness, or presence — alongside the first?
- Wanting to be rid of craving is itself a craving. Does the view survive that, or must it be treated as a ladder to be thrown away after climbing?
- The gripped-life view says a satisfied desire is a good; tranquilism says it is only the cancellation of a bad. Is there any difference in how the two views would have me live? If not, is the quarrel only about how to describe the same life?
- Does tranquilism care whose craving is absent? If it does, something has been smuggled in that is not in the stated definition; if it does not, I cannot see how it distinguishes one person's tranquility from no one's.

Give me your best attempt at saying where these two views genuinely disagree, where they turn out to be saying the same thing in different words, and which of them you would hold, with your reasons. Also tell me plainly which of the formulations above you take to be confused, and why.
