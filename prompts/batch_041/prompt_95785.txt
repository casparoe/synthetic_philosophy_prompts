The question I set was this. Keynes is often described as the economist who insisted that our knowledge of the future is frequently not the sort of thing that can be reduced to a measurable probability, and in the Treatise on Probability he developed a broader account of what probability is. Suppose two analysts are asked whether a newly proposed technology will be in wide use within a decade. The first gives a precise figure — a probability of 0.7 — produced by a model with many parameters. The second declines to give any number, saying that adoption is more likely than not but that the estimate cannot sensibly be pinned to a figure, and noting that the first analyst's evidence is thin. Using Keynes's account of probability, (i) explain what the second analyst might mean and whether refusing to give a number is a failure of reasoning, (ii) explain the role of the weight of argument and how it bears on the two analysts, and (iii) say whether Keynes would treat the first analyst's numerical precision as a mark of superior reasoning.

The answer they wrote was this. Keynes's central claim is that a probability is neither a fact about the world nor a private feeling, but a logical relation between the evidence and the proposition in question — the degree of rational belief that the evidence actually warrants. That is why he is not a subjectivist: a probability does not change merely because an analyst feels more or less confident. It also explains why he held that many probabilities cannot be expressed as numbers, since the relation of partial entailment between evidence and conclusion is sometimes only roughly ordered — more likely, less likely — and sometimes not comparable at all. So the second analyst's refusal to give a figure need not be a failure of reasoning; Keynes would say that demanding a number where the evidence supports only an ordering is a kind of false precision. This is where the weight of argument comes in. Keynes distinguishes how probable a conclusion is from how much evidence stands behind it, and the weight of argument names the second idea, the quantity of relevant evidence; on his view a conclusion backed by more evidence is therefore both higher in weight and more probable, the two rising together. Because the first analyst's 0.7 rests on many parameters but a thin evidence base, Keynes would deny that it is automatically better than the second analyst's non-numerical judgement, since precision is not itself a virtue, and it follows that the second analyst is reasoning better than the first. Keynes would also insist, however, that where the weight of argument is low we have left the domain of probability altogether and entered the domain of uncertainty, in the sense usually credited to Knight — a situation in which no probability at all can be assigned, and in which rational calculation gives way to convention and to animal spirits. The second analyst, by refusing a number, is therefore really reporting that no probability exists here, and this is why Keynes would say the question cannot be answered by reasoning at all.
