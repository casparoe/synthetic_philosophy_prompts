Question 4 (25 pts). Marrow Creek, a town of about 4,000, has to pick between two water-treatment systems, A and B. One of them is genuinely better for the town's aging pipes, but nobody knows which. Before the town meeting, each resident independently gets a private lab result — a noisy but informative reading that points to A or to B. At the meeting residents speak one at a time in a fixed order; each announces out loud which system they favor, and everyone hears every earlier speaker. Residents care only about the town picking the better system; nobody cares about looking consistent, agreeing with their neighbors, or pleasing the mayor. Assume the two systems are equally likely to be better a priori, that each resident's reading is right with probability 2/3, and that the readings are independent of each other given which system is actually better.

(a) When does a cascade begin, and what does a resident who is in one do with their own lab result? Is that behavior rational?
(b) Does the meeting aggregate the town's information well? Explain how the outcome can be bad even though every speaker is doing the best they can with what they know.
(c) As more and more residents speak, does the town's chance of picking the better system keep improving? Explain why or why not.
(d) Halfway through, the county health department publishes an independent engineering report on the two systems. Does that change your answers to (a)–(c)? Why or why not?

And here's the student's answer, transcribed as is (their handwriting is rough in spots, so a word or two is my best guess):

(a) The first speaker just goes with her own reading, since she has nothing else to go on and the two systems start out equally likely. The second speaker has one previous announcement and her own reading, and since the readings are equally informative, her own reading is enough to break the tie, so she goes with her reading too. The cascade begins with the third speaker if the first two agreed: two independent readings pointing the same way outweigh a single contrary reading, so a resident whose lab result says B should still announce A if the first two said A. Everybody after that copies the first two. What's going on isn't people caving to social pressure — they can't see each other's lab results, only the announcements, so they're making an ordinary Bayesian inference from other people's behavior. So the behavior is rational, in the sense of being the best choice given what each speaker knows. It's just that most of them are throwing away their own evidence in the process.

(b) No, the meeting aggregates information badly, and the town can end up unanimously backing the worse system. Once the cascade starts, the private readings of everyone who hasn't spoken yet are basically wasted, and the whole decision rests on the first two readings that happened to agree. A planner who could collect all 4,000 readings and take the majority would be right almost for sure, whereas the sequential meeting is stuck with whatever the first couple of speakers happened to see. This is the same basic thing as the bandwagon effect and groupthink that the social psych people study, where people go along with the crowd and stop thinking for themselves.

(c) Yes, it keeps improving. Every additional speaker who joins the consensus is one more piece of evidence that the consensus is right, so a long run of agreement is a sign that the underlying evidence is strong. In the limit, with enough speakers, the chance the town ends up on the wrong system goes to zero, which is why the model isn't as pessimistic as it first looks.

(d) The report wouldn't change much. Once a cascade is going it's self-sustaining, because everyone who speaks after it starts is deciding on the same two readings, so for the report to flip the town it would have to outweigh the entire run of speakers before it, which by that point is alot of evidence. In practice cascades lock in and only something really dramatic breaks them. And since nobody in the model is being irrational, there's nothing for the report to correct.
