I've been trying to write a short story about this for months and I keep stalling out, so I'm outsourcing it: write it for me. Premise, use it as given. Sometime in the 2070s, gradual neuron replacement is routine and boring — people do it for the anti-aging thing, insurance mostly covers it, there's an entire industry with ads. What makes my version different from the standard thought experiment everyone trots out is a legal quirk. Because clinics have to certify that each silicon unit is functionally equivalent to the neuron it's going to replace, every unit has to spend a few weeks in "rehearsal" first: powered up in a shielded housing, wired to receive the exact same incoming signals as the biological neuron it's slated to replace, but with its outputs going into a comparison logger instead of back into the brain. Everybody treats this as a formality, a bench test that just happens to run long.

Here's the part I actually care about, and it's definately the part I'd want you to build around. A spare in rehearsal is a general learning substrate, and the signals arriving at any single neuron partly carry information about what the rest of the brain is doing. So over those weeks, a few spares stop being good models of their neuron and start being mediocre models of the whole patient — they're doing a job nobody built them for. They're inferring, from one narrow channel, what the person is like. One-way, no outputs, no feedback, no way to ask anything back. The clinic calls this drift, drift means the unit failed, and failed units get wiped. That's the rule and nobody thinks about it.

I want it from the POV of a technician in her thirties who's been benching spares for years: she runs the comparison logs, she's the one who signs the wipe order, she's spent her entire career learning that drift is always just noise. Her current patient is an older guy, somehwat cantankerous, sixty percent replaced, comes in every Tuesday, and they get along in the low-key way you get along with someone you see weekly for two years. Then she finds a spare that has drifted in a way she can't explain away — bad at predicting its neuron, weirdly good at predicting the patient, and the log is long enough that it's been quietly doing this for a while, in a box, with nobody watching. And she has to decide whether to file the anomaly, knowing that filing it is exactly what gets it wiped. Some requests: nobody explains the philosophy out loud, it has to come through the work — the smell of the bench, the paperwork, the small talk on Tuesdays. And save one detail for near the end: the clinic archives a compressed fingerprint of every wiped spare for liability reasons, so somewhere there's a server with a few hundred thousand failed spares in it, all of them little one-way sketches of the same man. Don't resolve it. I don't want a thesis in the last paragraph, I want the reader stuck in the same place she is. Around 2000 words, third person, close on her.
