I write about science and ideas, and for several months I have been reporting on a cluster of arguments that travel under the heading of geometric rationality. The pitch I keep hearing — from a young economist I interviewed, from a pair of very online forecasters, and from a former professional poker player who now advises funds — is that one should run one's life the way a gambler with a genuine edge sizes his bets: never stake so much on a single wager that a run of bad luck can end your ability to keep playing, and optimize not the average outcome but the long-run compound growth of one's capital. On this view the Kelly criterion is not merely a betting rule but the template for rational action as such: take the job, the relationship, the investment, the gamble that maximizes compound growth, and treat anything that could zero you out as forbidden no matter how favorable its expected value. My interlocutors take this to license an entire ethic — be conservative, diversify, refuse the glorious all-in move — because, they say, the arithmetic of repeated bets proves that the all-in player goes broke in the limit.

I want to interrogate that move rather than the mathematics, and I would like your help doing it carefully. Take seriously the underlying analogy — that a life is like a bankroll and a sequence of decisions is like a sequence of bets — and tell me how far it actually holds. Give me the correspondence table first: what plays the role of the bankroll, the edge, the wager, the house, the repeated game, and ruin. Then tell me where the mapping breaks, and separate the disanalogies that are merely picturesque from those that do real work for the conclusion. I am especially interested in which features of the gambling setup the analogy quietly imports that a life does not obviously possess — a known edge, an indefinitely repeated game, a scalar and fungible stake, a single quantity to be maximized, survival as the only thing that matters — and whether the conservative, never-bet-the-farm conclusion survives once those imports are on the table, or whether it was the analogy rather than the argument carrying it all along. If the analogy is being made to bear more than it can, say so plainly, and sketch the better analogy you would put in its place.
