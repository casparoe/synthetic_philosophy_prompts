I'm putting together the closing session of a reading group on moral demandingness — we've spent most of the term on how much morality can actually ask of us, and I want to end on a poem rather than yet another paper, because the whole discussion keeps collapsing into "so is it required or not?" and I'd rather have something that sits in the gap instead of resolving it. So: you're a poet who's also spent real time in moral philosophy, and I'd like a sestina on supererogation — the act that is good, and praiseworthy, and yet not owed. A bit of background so you're not just reaching for the obvious, since I know this is a topic you've seen a hundred times. The word comes out of the old theological idea of works of supererogation, from the parable where the Samaritan leaves the innkeeper money for the injured man and promises to cover whatever more gets spent — the whole point being the paying out *beyond* what the situation strictly required. Two things I want pulling against each other. First the paradox: if the act really is better than the alternative, why isn't it just required? And second, the thought that praise only makes sense where the act *wasn't* required — nobody congratulates you for not stealing — so the "not owed" bit seems to be doing the praising as much as the deed is. And please work in the mirror-image too, the suberogatory: the act that's neither wrong nor good, the small meanness you'd never actually be blamed for. The old distinction between the saint (who goes beyond in self-sacrifice) and the hero (who goes beyond in courage) might give you two figures to hang it on. No citations or reading lists needed — I only want the poem.

Form-wise: six six-line stanzas plus a three-line envoi, end-words rotating in the proper sestina order. Let me suggest the six — must, debt, more, spare, rest, gift — but swap any that fight you. Here's the trick I actually want you to pull off: the sestina's grid forces every line to be *placed*, nothing in it is optional, everything has to be accounted for in the rotation — which is exactly the pressure the supererogatory act resists. So let the envoi do the work: it has to gather the six words the way an envoi must, but the act itself should end up being the thing the six words can't quite hold, the surplus that won't fit the grid. The speaker can be the one who did the thing, addressing whoever keeps saying "you didn't have to" — and let that phrase mean three things at once (thanks, a way of refusing the gift, and a way of keeping the bar low for everyone else). Start by brainstorming a few candidate images and the two or three philosophical beats you want to hit, then write it. Make it accurate enough that someone who's read the literature would nod along, and good enough to read aloud at the end of the session.
