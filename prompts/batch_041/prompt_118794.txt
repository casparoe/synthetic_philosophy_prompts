I'm a student in a philosophy of language seminar this term, and we have just finished the unit on performative utterances and illocutionary force. For my final paper I would like to argue about speech act theory rather than summarize it, but I've come to think I can't fairly evaluate any of the competing accounts until I know what a theory of this subject is supposed to deliver in the first place. So before I start writing, I'd like you to give me that specification.

Set out the desiderata that any adequate theory of performative utterances — or, if you think the category is better widened, of illocutionary force generally — has to satisfy. Treat the intended domain as including informal and implicit acts, not just ceremonial formulas. For each requirement, state it in a sentence or two, give the case or consideration that motivates it, and say whether you take it to be non-negotiable or merely the preference of one camp. I care less about a complete survey of positions than about the criteria themselves, so put the weight there.

I am especially interested in how a theory is supposed to handle the following, though the list should not be limited to them: the difference between an act that fails to come off at all and one that comes off but is somehow defective; the fact that the distinction between performatives and ordinary statements seems to collapse once you notice that stating, warning, and promising are all things we do in speaking; the hereby test, including the awkwardness that it applies just as smoothly to explicit statements as to explicit promises; utterances on stage, in poems, in soliloquy, and in jokes, which appear to be parasites on ordinary use rather than instances of it; indirect acts, such as a question that functions as a request; the role of the hearer's uptake and recognition; acts carried out by gesture, by signature, or by a machine that has no intentions of its own; genuinely new institutional acts that no standing convention covers; and the line between what an act is and what it merely brings about.

Two things matter most to me. First, identify any pair of requirements that cannot be jointly satisfied, and say what a theory gives up on either horn. Second, tell me which single requirement does the most to rule out the most popular accounts. A short paragraph on how a thoroughgoing conventionalism (Searle), an account that grounds illocutionary force in intention and hearer uptake (Strawson), and a Gricean reduction of force to audience-directed intention fare against the list would help me see which requirements are doing the real work, but keep that part brief.
