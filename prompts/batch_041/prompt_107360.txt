I'm in a seminar on Augustine's ethics and we've just hit On Lying, and there's a passage I can't get my head around. It's the bit where he's trying to say what a lie actually is: "Wherefore, that man lies, who has one thing in his mind and utters another in words, or by signs of whatever kind. Whence also the heart of him who lies is said to be double; that is, there is a double thought: the one, of that thing which he either knows or thinks to be true and does not produce; the other, of that thing which he produces instead thereof, knowing or thinking it to be false. Whence it comes to pass, that he may say a false thing and yet not lie, if he thinks it to be so as he says although it be not so; and, that he may say a true thing, and yet lie, if he thinks it to be false and utters it for true, although in reality it be so as he utters it. For from the sense of his own mind, not from the verity or falsity of the things themselves, is he to be judged to lie or not to lie. Therefore he who utters a false thing for a true, which however he opines to be true, may be called erring and rash: but he is not rightly said to lie; because he has not a double heart when he utters it, neither does he wish to deceive, but is deceived." Then a bit later he says the fault of the liar is the desire of deceiving. I can follow the individual sentences, but I can't tell how they add up to one definition. Is the lie the double thought itself, or is it the intention to deceive? If the double thought is enough, why does he need the desire to deceive? And if the desire to deceive is what makes it a lie, then what's the point of the "double heart" language? I also get confused by "by signs of whatever kind" -- does that mean a nod, a gesture, a misleading silence, a forged document, a fake smile? Would he count any intentional misleading sign as a lie, even if no words are spoken? And what about the case where I say something true but believe it false -- he says that's a lie, which seems to make the truth or falsity of the content totally irrelevant, but then he still treats the person who says a false thing in good faith as not lying. I'd really like a plain-language reconstruction of what he's distinguishing here: lying vs. erring vs. deceiving, and which clause is doing the real definitional work. Also, if you can, point out where the passage is genuinely ambiguous or leaves a gap -- like whether he is committed to saying that all deception is lying, or whether he could allow a deceptive truth and still not call it a lie. I'm not looking for a bibliography or anything, just help reading the argument closely.
