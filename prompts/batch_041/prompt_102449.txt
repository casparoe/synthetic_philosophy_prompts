I've been chewing on this idea that two people with pretty different moral outlooks can both come out ahead by trading — like, I care a lot about animal welfare and only mildly about, I dunno, preserving historic buildings, and you're the reverse, so I make a concession on the thing I care about less to get what I want on the thing I care about more, and we both walk away better off than if we'd just dug in. It's basically comparative advantage but for values, and I'm pretty sure it's right in the easy cases. But I have a hunch it gets strange when you push on it, and I want a short story that pushes on it.

Setup, take it or leave it: a negotiation over a single settlement with, say, five disputed clauses (water rights, a burial ground, a development permit, some reparations payment, a language-in-schools thing) between two delegates — one's a hardline deontologist who thinks certain things are just off the table no matter the price, the other's a cheerful utilitarian who thinks everything has a price and that refusing to trade is itself a kind of moral failure because you're leaving value on the table. Give them a room, a deadline, and let the trades start happening across clauses, so the reader feels the pull of "come on, just trade, you're being wasteful" and the pull of "some things aren't currency." Then have the deontologist make a move the utilitarian can't just wave off — something like: the very act of putting a price on X changes your relationship to X, so the person who'd sell X for a good cause isn't really the person who values X, and the "gain" is partly an illusion because the thing being traded isn't the same thing anymore once it's tradable. I'd like the utilitarian to have a real comeback too, not just get dunked on. Around 1500 words, third person, and please don't tie it off with a neat bow — I'd rather it end on the disagreement still live, with both of them at least seeing why the other isn't just being stupid.
