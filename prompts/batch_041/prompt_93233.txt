Ok so my book club is doing The Fountainhead next month and we're already fighting about it alot — half of us think Roark is a hero and half think the book is a cringe-fest. I want to run it through two lenses that don't usually get put on this book. First, Jantelagen — the Scandinavian Law of Jante, the whole unwritten code that you shouldn't think you're anyone special, that sticking your head up just gets it cut off. Second, its supposed opposite, the American Dream, where the self-made individual is the one we're all meant to cheer for rather than drag down (tall poppy syndrome, crab-bucket stuff, all that).

What I'm after: is Ellsworth Toohey basically a Jante-enforcer in a nice suit — the critic who turns mediocrity into a virtue and greatness into a vice? And does Roark read as the answer to the tall poppy problem, or does Rand rig it so the levelers never get a fair argument? I want both sides taken seriously — where does the anti-tall-poppy ethic actually have a point that Rand just waves away (envy vs. a real complaint about arrogance, about luck, about what you owe the people who aren't exceptional), and where is it just ressentiment dressed up as humility?

And be honest about where the novel resists the lens too. Roark is basically superhuman, which maybe makes him a useless test case for whether we should celebrate the successful — a real person who's merely very good is a harder case. Is there a scene that dramatizes this tension better than either the Rand fans or the Jante crowd would admit?

Don't bother with citations, and don't quote the book at me, I don't have it in front of me — just the reading. End by saying whether the novel adds anything to the actual arguement that a straight essay couldn't, or if it just makes the position easier to swallow.
