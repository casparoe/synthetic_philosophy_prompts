I've been reporting on the uses and abuses of social choice theory in democratic design, and I've settled on a position I want put under pressure. Gibbard and Satterthwaite showed that any deterministic rule that is strategy-proof and onto and defined over an unrestricted domain of preferences must, once there are three or more alternatives, be dictatorial; the moral usually drawn is that strategy-proofness is a lost cause and we should simply design institutions to keep manipulation bounded. I think that moral is too quick. The hypothesis to attack is the unrestricted domain, not strategy-proofness itself: keep the choices binary and sequential, or frame the question so that preferences come out single-peaked, and the impossibility no longer bites — so the real task is to engineer the domain, not to surrender the requirement.

Tell me what would count against this. Which of the escapes I'm leaning on are genuine and which are modelers' fictions; what happens when the domain is only approximately restricted, or when the restriction is assumed rather than earned; whether engineering the domain is itself a species of agenda control that reinstates the very manipulation I claim to have avoided; and whether randomization, cardinal information, or the sheer computational difficulty of finding a profitable misreport buys me anything I haven't already spent. I'd also like you to say whether the position risks becoming unfalsifiable — if every failure is met with a further narrowing of the domain, has it asserted anything? Rank the objections from fatal to merely awkward, note which ones the position's own defenders should concede rather than leave to critics, and keep separate what is settled among people who work on this from what is genuinely contested; I don't want a contested view dressed up as the consensus.
