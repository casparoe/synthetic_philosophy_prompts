I am a high school student in my school's philosophy elective, and I am writing for help with a sentence my teacher uses to end arguments. Whenever a classmate or I ask whether some borderline case falls under one of our terms, she says that the line has to be drawn somewhere, and then moves on as if the question had been settled. Earlier in the term we worked through the puzzle about the heap of sand, in which removing a single grain never seems to make the difference between a heap and not a heap, and I have come to think this sentence is her way of declining that puzzle rather than answering it.

She has used it twice in the past month, and the two occasions do not look alike to me. The first was about our academic integrity policy: a classmate asked whether using an AI tool to correct the grammar of an essay counts as cheating, given that the policy forbids unauthorized assistance but says nothing about tools that leave every idea intact. She replied that the line has to be drawn somewhere. The second was in a discussion of a novel, when someone asked whether a character who had been lied to really understood what she was doing. She gave the same reply. In the first case it seems to me that somebody genuinely does have to draw a line, because a school rule has to be usable by people who disagree about its edges; in the second case it is strange to say that anyone is drawing anything, since the question looks like one about what a word means or about what was going on in a fictional mind, and not about a policy at all.

What I want is not an argument that my teacher is wrong to end discussion, but an account of what she could actually be claiming. Please lay out, as a list I can bring to her office hours, the distinct precise claims that this sentence could be making, ordered from the trivial to the radical. State each reading as a claim with a clear subject, and say of each whether it is about the words we use, about the world, about what we can know, or about what we are required to do. Then give a verdict on each: a platitude that nobody denies, a substantive claim worth defending, a claim that holds in some domains but not others, or simply false. For every reading, say what it predicts about my two cases, and especially whether it would make her reply an answer to the question that was actually asked rather than a change of subject. Please also say which reading the people who repeat this sentence most often have in mind when they repeat it.

Two further things. First, the sentence looks to me as though it hides an equivocation between the claim that a boundary exists and the claim that we are required to settle on one, and I would like to know whether that equivocation is real or whether the two claims come to the same thing once they are made precise. Second, if the line has to be drawn somewhere, I want to know where the line about where the line is gets drawn: if some cases plainly fall on one side and some plainly on the other, there would seem to be a first case on the unclear side, and it is itself unclear whether that case is unclear. Say whether this objection refutes all of the readings on your list or only some, and say what remains true of the sentence even once the objection is granted. Finally, two of my classmates draw opposite lessons from the sentence: one says it means that one of the borderline cases really is the first heap, but that we can never know which; the other says it means there is no fact of the matter and that our rules are conventions we impose. Each of them believes he is agreeing with her. Tell me which, if either, is right, and whether the one sentence can honestly carry both.

End your answer with a line beginning "Verdict:" that names the single reading which would genuinely license her reply in the plagiarism case, and states in a short phrase whether that reading survives scrutiny.
