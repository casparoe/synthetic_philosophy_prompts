I've spent more time than is healthy in the rationalist blogosphere — the Sequences, the interminable decision-theory threads, the recurring fights about whether determinism leaves room for anything worth calling agency — and I've noticed that the standard thought experiments in that milieu all assume a world that is either tidy or chaotic, never both at once. Here is the puzzle I can't shake. In a deterministic universe, but-for causation is either trivial or universal: every event is a but-for cause of every later event, since removing it removes everything downstream. Turn up the sensitivity and the picture flips. A breath moves a hurricane, so my act is a but-for cause with enormous reach; but some other perturbation — the butterfly, the delivery van's swerve — would have produced a hurricane anyway, so my act isn't necessary for the outcome in any robust sense. The act is neither necessary nor sufficient, and yet it is enormously consequential. I want that tension dramatized rather than argued, so write me a short story of roughly twelve hundred words that puts a reader inside a world which has taken it seriously.

Setting seed, in case it helps: a near-future jurisdiction has replaced ordinary tort causation with what the statute calls divergence liability. A defendant is liable in proportion to the sensitivity of the outcome to their act — the faster nearby trajectories diverge, the more the act counts — on the theory that in a system like this the only meaningful way to assign responsibility is to ask how much the world's future hinged on the nudge. Follow one case through the machinery: an adjuster or assessor, a hearing, an expert who has to put a number on how much a particular act mattered, and the moment somebody realizes that the doctrine rewards precisely the unpredictability that made foresight impossible in the first place. Third person, confined to one character's point of view, no lecture-hall exposition — the concepts should surface through what people do and through what they cannot say to each other.

Then, in a closing note of a paragraph or so, tell me which single feature of that setup is actually carrying the philosophical weight, and what the story would become if you removed it.
