I've got a work-in-progress talk in a couple weeks and I need Q&A prep, not a summary of the talk.

The talk is about Parfit's hitchhiker — you're stranded in the desert, a driver will only give you the lift if he believes you'll pay him once you're in town, and you and he both know that by the time you get there, paying is the last thing you'll have any reason to do. The standard spin is that this is the flagship case of rational irrationality: it's rational to now intend to pay, even though it'd be irrational to actually pay. I want to argue the standard spin is a con. The whole trick depends on the driver being a bit dim about decision theory. Make him sophisticated — he's read the same stuff, he knows your payoffs, he knows you know he knows — and the promise stops being credible on the intention reading, so the ride never happens. What actually gets you picked up isn't an intention, it's a type: you're the sort of person who pays, and thats a fact about your character that you can't just decide into existence at the roadside. So the case is really about the gap between choosing and being — a disposition can be rational to have even though every action it issues in is one you'd be irrational to perform, and crucially the disposition has to be in place before the moment of choice, which is why you can't bootstrap it. If that's right, then alot of what gets sold as rational irrationality is a mistake about where the rationality lives.

Here's who'll be in the room, and it's an awkward mix: three or four people who work on intentions and plans, who'll say I've just reinvented standing intentions and that my sophisticated driver is the banal point that intentions have to be public to do any work; two economists who do commitment devices and mechanism design, who'll say the whole thing is a cheap-talk / signaling game and that escrow, reputation and bonds dissolve the puzzle with no philosophy left over; a couple of Kantians, who'll say the case is really about the categorical imperative and that my opacity talk concedes their point by making the reason to pay a coordination benefit rather than a moral one; one formal epistemologist, who'll want to know whether my type story is just evidential decision theory wearing a trenchcoat, and what causal decision theory says about the driver's inference; and one or two moral-psychology types, who'll ask why a character trait gets to be called rational when none of its outputs are.

Give me, ordered by how likely each is and how much damage it'd do: the eight or so objections I'll actually get, each phrased the way that person would phrase it rather than the way I'd phrase it, each with a two-minute reply I could actually say out loud and a line on where the reply is weakest. Then tell me which one to concede up front and rebuild the paper around, which ones to just absorb, which one to hold the line on even though it'll look bad, and the question I'm secretly hoping nobody asks.
