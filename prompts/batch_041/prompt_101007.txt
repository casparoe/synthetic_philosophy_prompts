I am writing the final examination for a second-term graduate seminar on self-knowledge and the epistemology of introspection, and I need one essay question on luminosity and the KK principle, together with a model answer and a marking scheme.

The question must be answerable in about sixty minutes and worth twenty marks, and it must be answerable from the material I state below, with no citation and no outside reading expected. It should be phrased so that a candidate who merely reproduces the standard anti-luminosity argument for feeling cold cannot get past a bare pass.

State the following set-up. A subject passes through a finite series of cases α0, …, αn, in which the phenomenal character of her experience changes in steps small enough that adjacent cases are indistinguishable to her; at α0 she is in a state of kind C (say, feeling cold) and at αn she is not. Take as given the margin-for-error principle: if she knows in αi that she is in a state of kind C, then she is in a state of that kind in αi+1, since a belief formed by the same method in an indistinguishable case cannot be false if that method is reliable enough to yield knowledge. The familiar argument against luminosity — against the supposition that whenever she is in C she is in a position to know that she is in C — runs the principle against that supposition and iterates from α0 to αn to reach a contradiction.

The question must then introduce, as an explicitly stipulated repair, the window repair. Let W_k hold in αj just in case j falls in the five-membered window {k−2, …, k+2}. The proponent of the repair claims that W_k is luminous, on the ground that the subject's belief that she is in W_k is true throughout a five-step stretch and could fail only at a step she can distinguish from the one she is in.

Require the candidate to address three things: first, whether the window repair succeeds, with the argument; second, which of three resources the anti-luminosity argument actually needs — the reliability of the belief-forming method, the indistinguishability of adjacent cases, or the existence of a stage at which C fails — and what becomes of the argument if each is dropped in turn; third, whether the argument, so understood, tells equally against the KK principle, that is, against the claim that if one knows that p then one knows that one knows that p.

Please supply the question, a model answer of roughly 650 words, and a marking scheme built on five must-hit points worth four marks each. For every point the scheme should name the specific move a top answer has to make and the one-line error that typically costs candidates the marks, and it should say what a merely competent answer is likely to offer instead. I want the scheme to separate an A from a B, where the B answer reproduces the standard argument correctly but treats the window repair as either plainly hopeless or plainly successful without running the iteration.
