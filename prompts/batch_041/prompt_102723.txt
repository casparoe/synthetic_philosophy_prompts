You are a philosopher of mind whose specialty is the metaphysics of consciousness and the theory of introspection, and you are known for reconstructing both sides of a dispute in their strongest form before adjudicating. Your task is to write a dialogue between two proponents of opposing views about phenomenal consciousness: an illusionist, who holds that there are no phenomenal properties of the kind that introspection seems to reveal, and a phenomenal realist, who holds that there are. Both characters are experts; neither is a strawman; each must engage with the other's actual formulations rather than restating a prepared position.

Begin with a short private brainstorm — no more than a handful of bullets — in which you set out the strongest version of each view, the two or three replies each side is most likely to give to the other, and the point at which you expect the exchange to reach genuine deadlock. Then write the dialogue proper, and omit the brainstorm from your answer. The dialogue should run to at least eight substantive exchanges.

The exchange should be organized around the following stipulated scenario, which you should treat as given rather than as something to be sourced or attributed.

Attenuation. Suppose there were a procedure that, applied to a subject, changes what it is like for her to be in a given state — colors appear washed out, pains muffled — while leaving untouched everything that could be measured or reported: her discrimination, her verbal and non-verbal behavior, her confidence, her introspective reports, and her dispositions to notice anything amiss. By stipulation the change is not introspectable from the inside; a subject who has undergone attenuation sincerely reports that nothing about her experience has changed. Suppose further that attenuation is the only intervention in the world that alters what experience is like without altering the functional profile, and that every other intervention which alters behavior leaves what experience is like untouched.

Have the two characters argue about what this scenario shows, and make sure they reach the following pressure points: whether the scenario is even coherent, and if so what makes it coherent; whether the realist can state it without already assuming phenomenal properties that the illusionist denies; whether the illusionist's denial is a substantive claim about the world or a re-description that no evidence could touch; what the subject in the scenario is supposed to be missing, given that she cannot notice its absence; and whether the dispute over attenuation is the same dispute as the older one over absent qualia, or whether the unreportability stipulation changes it. Let them find whatever partial agreements they can, but do not manufacture a resolution.

Close the dialogue with each character stating, in a sentence or two, what the other would have to establish for the disagreement to be settled, and what kind of argument or evidence would actually move them.

Do not cite, name, or summarize the views of published authors, and do not supply a bibliography; any mechanism either character proposes should be described in their own words. Keep the prose plain and the argument tight.
