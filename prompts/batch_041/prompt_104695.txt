For the past three years I have run a small mutual-aid fund in my neighborhood. We collect a few thousand dollars a month and distribute it as grants, and I am the person who decides how it gets split. The money has to go somewhere, and it is never enough to cover everyone who applies, so every month I choose among them.

With most applicants the pattern is familiar and a little flat. A few hundred dollars helps enormously at first and then less and less; give them more and they improve, but the returns taper off the way you would expect. One applicant is different. Call her Rosa. Everything we give Rosa seems to compound. Her first grant paid a utility bill, and by her own account and by everyone who knows her she was transformed — more energetic, more generous, more alive. The next grant did more for her than the first, not less. When we doubled her grant last spring, the gain in how well her life was going was larger than the gain from the doubling before it. This has now held across nine consecutive grants, and I have watched closely enough to believe it is real rather than gratitude or good manners. As far as I can tell there is no ceiling in sight: every additional dollar we route to Rosa produces more well-being in her than the dollar before it.

Here is my problem. I am fairly confident that Rosa is the single most effective use of our money, and that this will stay true no matter how much we give her. The obvious inference is that we should give her everything, every month, and let the other applicants fend for themselves. Yet something in me recoils. The others are not freeloaders or grifters; several are genuinely badly off, and their lives would also improve with the money. I cannot shake the sense that it would be wrong to let one person's apparently limitless capacity for benefit swallow the entire fund.

Am I being sentimental, or is my reluctance tracking something real? I want you to tell me what is actually at stake here: what the argument for giving everything to Rosa genuinely commits me to; what a serious defender of that argument would say to my unease; whether there is a principled stopping point that is not just a prejudice against the person who benefits most; and whether the fact that the argument licenses this outcome is a defect in the argument or merely a defect in my stomach for it. If the honest answer is that I should give Rosa everything, say so plainly, and tell me what I am thereby accepting about morality. I am not asking which choice feels kinder — I am asking which one I can defend.
