I'm a philosophy professor — ancient philosophy, mostly, so I'm hopelessly out of my depth on anything post-Kant — and for a couple of years now I've been noticing something in my own life that I can't find a name for, and it's driving me a little nuts. Here's the pattern. When my old dissertation advisor was dying — this was right at the end — I promised him I'd finish the commentary we'd started together. Neither of us was ever going to finish it and we both knew that; it was more like a promise to keep the thing alive than a promise to produce it. He died about four months later. The book never happened and never will. And yet I still feel bound by the promise, and here's the part I can't parse: I don't feel bound to write the book. That's off the table and I don't feel guilty about it. What I feel bound by is something vaguer — not pretending it didn't happen. If someone asked me at a conference about that project I'd feel obliged to say "we started it" rather than "I had an idea once." And if I ever did write something in that area I'd feel obliged to put his name on it, even though he contributed maybe a paragraph. So the promise outlived the act it was a promise to perform, and it outlived the person I made it to, and yet it isn't dead — it's just migrated onto some other, fainter thing.

I've asked around and nothing quite fits. A colleague in ethics said "that's just a prima facie duty that got outweighed," but that can't be it, becuase there's nothing for it to be outweighed by — the book isn't bad to write, it's just impossible, and he's dead, so I can't wrong him by not writing it. Someone else said "moral residue," but residue is supposed to be what you carry for having gone ahead and done the wrong thing anyway, and I didn't do anything wrong. My brother-in-law, who reads a lot of pop psychology, said it's just grief wearing a costume, which is charming but doesn't explain why it shows up in the grammar of obligation — I owe, I'm bound — rather than the grammar of sadness, and I don't think I'm confused about the grammar, because I'd describe a much more mundane case the same way. I promised a friend I'd drive her to the airport; she got a ride from someone else and told me not to bother — fine, released, gone, no residue. But if she'd never said anything and I'd simply decided on my own that she didn't really need me, I'd feel I owed her an explanation even if she never found out and was never inconvenienced by it.

So what I'm after isn't mainly a label. I want someone to carve this up for me. Is there an established concept for the piece of a promise that survives the impossibility of its object, or the death of the promisee — or, better, the piece that survives as a standing claim on how I may treat the promise rather than on whether I perform it? If there's a name, what is it, and what does it cover that directed obligation, prima facie duty, and moral residue each miss? If there's no name, coin one, define it, and tell me where its edges are: does it attach only to promises to the dead, or to any promise whose object has become impossible, or to any commitment whatsoever once it's been made? And tell me what it is not — I don't want to be told it's just habit, or grief, or the social cost of being seen as unreliable, unless you can also show me why the obligation-grammar is a mistake. Bonus points if you can tell me whether the release asymmetry in the airport case is real or something I've talked myself into.
