I have been reading a popular history of the British Empire whose opening chapter offers a brisk account of Adam Smith on colonies. The author writes:

"Adam Smith was the first great critic of empire. In The Wealth of Nations he demonstrated that colonies were a pure loss to the mother country: the only people who gained were a handful of merchants and planters, while the public bore the costs of defending the colonies and of the monopoly that kept their trade in a few hands. Because the colonies were a drain, Smith concluded that Britain should let them go; he saw American independence coming and welcomed it as a deliverance from a bad bargain. His position is the ancestor of the modern anti-imperialist case — empires do not pay, and the rational policy is to trade with free nations rather than to rule subject ones."

I suspect this is too neat. I know Smith was writing in the 1770s, in the middle of the crisis with the American colonies, and that he had something to do with the idea that the colonies might be represented in Parliament; I also have a vague sense that he distinguished the monopoly from the colonies themselves. But I do not know how much of the author's summary is accurate and how much is a modern gloss. Please audit it claim by claim. In particular: (1) What exactly did Smith think was a loss, and to whom — the colonies, the monopoly, or the empire as a whole? (2) Is it fair to say he "concluded that Britain should let them go," or did he propose something else as preferable, and if so what? (3) Did he regard American independence as inevitable, and did he welcome it? (4) Does the author's final sentence — that Smith's position is the ancestor of the modern anti-imperialist case, that empires do not pay and free trade with independent nations is the rational policy — follow from what Smith actually argued, or does it overstate or distort it? Tell me which sentences a careful historian of economic thought would accept, which are common simplifications, and which are genuine distortions, and explain what turns on the distinctions. I would rather not be told merely that the summary is "broadly right"; I want to know where it goes wrong and why it matters.
