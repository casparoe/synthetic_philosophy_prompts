For eighteen months I have been trying to write the history of my grandmother, and I have been failing in a way that has changed what I think a methods course is for. She cleaned other people's houses for forty years. The register gives her a birth line and a burial line, a rent book gives her two addresses, and the only document in which she says anything at length is a statement she gave to the police about a neighbour. Everything else I know about her comes from my mother. I teach the third-year methods seminar — nineteen students, one ninety-minute session — and I want that session to leave them unable to do source-based history innocently again. Design it.

My own idea, which you should feel free to rebuild if it will not run, is a simulation set in a parish that is entirely invented and kept deliberately thin on period detail, so that nobody in the room, including me, can win by knowing more history than anyone else. Every participant begins as an inhabitant with a private sheet of ten things that happened to them that year. A smaller group are the recorders — parish clerk, tax assessor, magistrate's clerk, estate steward, whatever the archive needs — and each works from a rule sheet specifying which kinds of event they are obliged to write down. The inhabitants keep their sheets hidden; the recorders assemble the archive.

Then the historians: a group takes the archive and nothing else, and answers three questions about the year in the parish, with confidence ratings. My strong preference is that this first pass should go well — that they should produce something that reads like competent, satisfying, genuinely source-based history — because the point I want them to feel is not that bad history distorts the past but that rigorous and faithful history reproduces the structure of power that produced its sources. The failure has to arrive as a consequence of their diligence, not their carelessness.

What I want them to collide with, roughly in this order: that the archive is dense exactly where power is and empty everywhere else; that the best-documented ordinary person in the parish is the one the authorities had most reason to watch, so that visibility is a symptom of subjection rather than of significance; that one richly documented anomalous life is the best and most misleading thing they will ever be given, since the moment they generalise from it they are asserting a representativeness it cannot bear, and if they refuse to generalise they have no history at all; and that a recording rule introduced partway through the year — a new tax, a licensing scheme, a crackdown, your choice — can turn a person who was invisible in January into the best-documented person in the parish by December, which teaches them a great deal about the archive and nothing whatsoever about her.

Give me the whole thing runnable: what goes on each inhabitant's sheet and each recorder's rule sheet, the three questions for the historians, the sequence and timings within the ninety minutes, the moment the private sheets are revealed and what I say there (I would rather say almost nothing and let them do the arithmetic on what fraction of the year survived), and the debrief mapping each phase onto the difference between a history of states and elites and a history from below — including what the second kind of history has to do instead, since I do not want them leaving with the archive is a map of power as the takeaway. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle it in the room, and be candid about what the activity cannot reproduce and that I will have to supply myself. I would especially like to know whether it overstates the silence: real historians of ordinary people do find rich sources, and I do not want to teach my students a pessimism the evidence will not support.
