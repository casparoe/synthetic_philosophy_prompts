I'm a science-and-ideas columnist, and my paper has asked me to run a ninety-minute workshop for a dozen early-career reporters on a distinction I keep needing and keep failing to make in print: the difference between two ways a group can end up sealed off from the views of outsiders. In the first, the group simply has never been exposed to the other view — it lives in a town, a profession, or a feed where the opposing case never comes up — and contact with a well-informed outsider can move it. In the second, the group has heard the opposing case and has been given a standing reason to treat anyone making it as corrupt, paid, or malicious, so that each fresh encounter with the other side confirms the group's picture instead of correcting it. My suspicion, which is the reason for the workshop, is that most public commentary about polarization collapses these two into one, and that the standard remedy — more exposure, better facts, a patiently argued column — works on the first and can harden the second.

Don't write the explanation for me; plan how I should teach it. Tell me what has to be established first for the distinction even to land, and what depends on what — in particular, whether the room needs to accept something about how beliefs respond to evidence before the second case looks like anything more than ordinary stubbornness. Tell me what single example should carry the whole session and what it must have and lack; I had planned to build it around a flat-earth forum, but I can't decide whether that is the second case or a disguised version of the first, and I don't want to anchor the session on an example that blurs the line I am drawing. Name the misconceptions a room of reporters will bring — I am anticipating that they will hear the second case as simple irrationality, that they will assume the cure is more and better information, and that they will quietly file the whole thing under social media — and tell me at exactly what point in the sequence I should head each one off. Give me one question to put to the room at the end that would show whether they have the distinction rather than merely nodding along, and tell me what I should refuse to cover in ninety minutes, and say so out loud.
