I'm in a game theory class and we've been doing iterated deletion of dominated strategies, and I built an argument for a homework problem that I now think proves too much, but I cant put my finger on where it breaks. Two players; Player 1 picks a row (T, M, or B), Player 2 picks a column (L or R). Player 1's payoffs are: T gives 0 against L and 2 against R; M gives 2 against L and 0 against R; B gives 1 against L and 1 against R. (Player 2's payoffs are a matching-type thing — she wants to line up with T or with M, and B pays her 0 either way — I don't think it matters for my question but there it is.) Now here's the argument:

(1) A rational player never plays a dominated strategy — one that is strictly worse than some other available strategy no matter what the opponent does. (2) A strategy counts as dominated if there is some other strategy, possibly a coin-flip between your options, that does strictly better against each pure strategy the opponent might pick. (3) So if a strategy is never the best reply to any single one of the opponent's pure strategies, then there's no way the opponent can behave that would make it the best reply — nothing for it to be best against — and so it is dominated and shouldn't be played. (4) Here B is never the best reply: against L, M does better (2 vs 1); against R, T does better (2 vs 1). (5) So by (3), B is dominated. Delete it. (6) That leaves T and M; T is the best reply to R and M is the best reply to L, so neither is dominated and we stop. (7) Therefore a rational Player 1 plays T or M, and never B.

I want to keep (1) and (2) — they look to me like just the definition of dominance. But (7) has to be wrong: B is the safe, cautious play, it locks in 1 no matter what Player 2 does, while T and M both risk ending up with 0. It feels crazy to call the careful option the irrational one. A classmate told me the real trouble is that you have to recheck dominance against the strategies that are still left and that the order of deletion matters, but that doesn't help me here, since nothing else gets deleted either way, so I don't think that's it. Where does the argument actually go wrong, and if I give up that step, what does it cost me — do I have to throw out iterated dominance altogether, or just be more careful about one particular move? I'd like to keep as much of the procedure as I can.
