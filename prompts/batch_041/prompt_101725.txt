You are a philosopher of medicine who has spent years advising health insurers on coverage policy. I sit on the medical policy team at a mid-sized insurer: about thirty times a month I get a petition to add or remove a condition from our list of covered diagnoses, I have to decide in under half an hour, I have no access to the philosophical literature, and whatever I decide has to be defensible in a written explanation read by an appeals board and sometimes by the member. Start by thinking through for yourself what the naturalist and the normativist accounts of disease actually commit a decision-maker to, and especially the parts usually left vague: how a reference class gets picked, what "dysfunction" can mean when there is no design plan to appeal to, and how value judgments sneak into accounts that present themselves as purely descriptive. Then turn that into a decision procedure I can actually run in sequence: the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops and returns a verdict, and where it must hand the decision to a values committee instead of pretending the question is one of fact. Mark the steps that are judgment calls dressed up as procedure, and say where the procedure will misfire.

Apply it to three petitions: testosterone therapy for a seventy-year-old man whose levels are typical for his age; recognition of sickle-cell trait, which is harmless in most carriers and protective against malaria, as a covered disease; and coverage for prolonged grief, where the suffering is unmistakable but no underlying dysfunction can be pointed to. Include at least one outcome my team will not like, and close by stating plainly what the procedure cannot decide. Do not cite or recommend literature — describe the positions in plain language. Give me the procedure as a numbered list I can pin above my desk, then the three worked cases.
