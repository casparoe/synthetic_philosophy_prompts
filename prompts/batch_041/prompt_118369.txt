I spent the better part of four decades as an experimental physicist, and like most of my colleagues I absorbed Newton early and uncritically: the laws of motion, universal gravitation, the clockwork universe, and very little else. Retirement has given me the leisure to read around him, and the more I read, the less comfortable I become with the picture I grew up with. What finally prompted this letter was a chapter in a recent popular history of science — a book I had taken to be reliable — on Newton's religion. Let me set down its central claims as fairly as I can.

'The mature Newton was a theologian first and a natural philosopher second; he devoted far more of his pen to prophecy and church history than to motion and light. His private faith was anything but orthodox. He held that the doctrine of the Trinity was a fabrication of the fourth century, and that the pure, simple monotheism of the earliest Christians had been corrupted first by Athanasius and later by the Church of Rome. But the most arresting feature of his theology concerns God's relation to the world. Having once impressed his laws upon matter, Newton believed, God withdrew from the cosmos and left it to run on its own — which is to say that Newton was a deist before deism had a name. He was also scrupulous about keeping his two intellectual lives apart: the Principia is a work of mathematics and mechanics from which theology has been deliberately excluded, and Newton would have regarded any attempt to read religious conclusions out of his physics as a confusion of categories. His reticence about all this was prudential — publication in his own lifetime would have cost him his chair at Cambridge — and it was only in the twentieth century, when the bulk of his papers finally came to light, that the full extent of his heterodoxy became clear.'

I have learned to be suspicious of stories that fit together this neatly, and my instinct is that at least one of these claims is doing illicit work — but I cannot tell which, because I do not know the primary material or the secondary literature well enough to adjudicate. I would therefore like you to treat the passage the way a referee treats a suspicious claim in a submitted manuscript: go through it and separate what a specialist in Newton's theology would endorse, what is defensible but oversimplified, and what is straightforwardly mistaken, explaining in each case what the underlying issue is. I am especially interested in whether the concluding characterization of Newton as a deist follows from the premises the passage itself supplies. Please be explicit about your confidence in each part of your assessment, and single out the one point on which you are least sure.
