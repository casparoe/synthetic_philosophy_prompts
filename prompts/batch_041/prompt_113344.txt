I'm in a reading group working through Scanlon's contractualism, and we keep colliding with the aggregation problem before we've agreed on what a solution would even have to look like. Rather than read another round of proposals, I want the specification first. Write me the desiderata that any theory of interpersonal aggregation has to meet in order to be adequate for the purpose of justifying a principle to each person it burdens. I mean the full list: which cases it must get right, which distinctions it must respect rather than collapse, which intuitive verdicts it is not permitted to explain away, and what structural resources it needs to have. For each requirement, give me the case or consideration that motivates it, and say whether you think it is non-negotiable or merely the preference of one camp — in particular, whether the demand that justification be given to each person individually, with no appeal to the sum of benefits to everyone else as a reason for anyone to bear a burden, is a genuine constraint or an overreaction to utilitarianism. I don't need citations or a reading list; I want the criteria, not the literature.

The cases that keep coming up in our group, and that I want the list to be answerable to, are these. One person will lose a hand unless we act, and two people will each lose a hand unless we act differently, and it seems obvious that we save the two — so a theory has to explain how numbers can matter at all. A very large number of people would each get a minor pleasure from being allowed to do something, and one person would suffer a serious injury as a result, and it seems obvious that the many do not get their way — so the theory has to explain why the sum of small benefits cannot outweigh a serious harm to one. And here is a case I've been turning over that I haven't seen discussed: take that second scenario and redistribute it so that all the small pleasures and the one serious injury fall within a single life, at different times. If the prohibition was really about the separateness of persons, the intrapersonal version should come out differently, and I want the desiderata to say which way and why. Add to that what the theory must say when the numbers are enormous and the stakes are trivial — a loss so slight that no one would notice it, suffered by an astronomically large number of people, against a moderate loss to one — and whether it needs a cardinal measure of complaint, an ordinal ranking of claims, or nothing quantitative at all, and whether it must handle numbers mattering outside rescue cases, in the distribution of scarce resources and in ordinary political justification. I'd also like you to say what the theory must not do: whether it may help itself to a separate tie-breaking rule for numbers, and whether it is allowed to fall silent on some cases rather than deliver a verdict.

The part I most want is the friction within the list. Flag any pair or subset of the requirements that cannot be jointly satisfied — my suspicion is that explaining why numbers matter in the rescue case and explaining why they do not matter in the trivial-benefit case pull in opposite directions, and if so I want to know which of the two the list should surrender and what surrendering it costs. Tell me which single requirement does the most work in ruling out otherwise attractive positions, and describe those positions in plain terms rather than by name. Write it as continuous prose, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables — the way you would write it up in a letter to a colleague who asked for the spec before the seminar.
