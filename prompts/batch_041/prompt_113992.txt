I teach a second-year undergraduate seminar in philosophy of action and moral psychology — sixteen students, one ninety-minute session — and I have decided to build the session around an activity rather than a lecture. The topic is the relation between weakness of will and hyperbolic discounting, and the reason I want it taught through an activity is that students reliably leave the readings with the lazy identification: they treat akrasia and temporal preference reversal as the same phenomenon, or they treat akrasia as a vivid name for impatience. One student put it to me last term as though it were obvious — "isn't weakness of will just caring more about now?" — and I could not think of anything to say that would not sound like a distinction without a difference. I want them to run into the two phenomena in their own choices and then have to decide, on the evidence of what they have just done, whether they are one thing or two.

Take the role of a philosopher of action with a serious interest in intertemporal choice, and design the activity. It should have at least two modules that can come apart. In the first, participants should confront a genuine preference reversal — choosing the larger-later reward while both options lie in the future, then choosing the smaller reward once it becomes available now — and I want the reversal to fall out of the rules rather than out of my telling them it happens. Give them a way to spend some of their resources on a binding commitment device, so that the difference between the naively inconsistent, the sophisticated who anticipate their own reversal, and the resolute who simply stick shows up in what they choose to buy. In the second module I want the temporal structure removed altogether: a small aversive task that everyone has just sincerely endorsed as worth doing, with no discounting in play, so that when some of them fail to do it the room is forced to ask whether that failure is the same kind of thing as the reversal in the first module. Then a debrief that maps what happened onto the concepts — the difference between acting against a judgment one holds at the moment of action and merely having a different preference later; whether the later self who takes the smaller-sooner reward has done something wrong or has simply changed; whether the better judgment in the discounting module was genuinely the participant's or was assigned by the experimenter; and whether weakness of will even requires a temporal gap, given the old skeptical worry that no one can knowingly act against their own all-things-considered judgment.

Keep the materials cheap and self-contained — tokens, slips of paper, a timer, no real money — and assume nothing beyond a whiteboard. Tell me the rules, the payoff scheme, the running order with rough timings, and the debrief. Before you commit to a design, think carefully about the constraints and lay out the two or three possible rule-sets that could generate the reversal, saying which you chose and why; then set out what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to recover, and finish with one honest paragraph on the feature of the akrasia/discounting relation that this activity cannot make participants feel and that I will have to supply myself.
