Im putting together a session for a second-year seminar on the later Wittgenstein — meaning as use, language games, family resemblance, the whole business about not hunting for the essence of a game but just looking at what the various games have in common — and I want to pair the Investigations with Through the Looking-Glass, mainly because everyone in the room read it as a kid, so nobody gets to hide behind not having done the reading.

Read the book through that lens and give me exactly five numbered points, each a sentence or two, no intro and no wrap-up at the end. Here's what I want covered, roughly one point each, though use your own judgement about where the lines fall:

Whether Humpty Dumpty is the villain of the piece or the one who's actually right. He says a word means whatever he chooses it to mean and that the only question is who's to be master, which sounds like everything Wittgenstein is against; but he's also the character who unpacks the Jabberwocky words for Alice by spotting which two ordinary words each nonsense word was packed out of, and if he can do that reliably then he knows the conventions better than his own theory says he should. Is the book refuting him, or dramatising something Wittgenstein can't quite answer?

The wood where things have no names. Alice walks through it with the Fawn, neither of them knowing what it is, and they go along together quite happily until they come out the other side, at which point the Fawn remembers it's a fawn and that she's a human child, and bolts. Does that scene support meaning-as-use or undermine it? My instinct is that it's an argument for a name being inseparable from a practice — prey and predator — rather than a label stuck on a thing, but I'd like to know if that's too neat.

The looking-glass insects — the rocking-horse-fly, the snap-dragon-fly, the bread-and-butter-fly. These look like family resemblance made literal, since none of them has the defining feature of a fly and they're all flies anyway. But I think they're actually built by crossing two things, a horse and a fly, which is closer to a combinatorial definition than to a rope of overlapping similarities. Say which it is, and whether the joke depends on there being a real fly underneath.

The chess game, which is the bit I most want help with. The whole book is layed out as a chess problem, Alice is a pawn and doesn't know it, she gets the rules in a form she can't apply, and she only sees the board from outside at the very end. Say what that's doing for rule-following, and whether it's the same point Wittgenstein is making or a different one — and in particular whether being moved by a rule and following one are being distinguished here or just confused.

And last, where the reading breaks down and where the book gets something the philosophy doesn't. My suspicion is that Carroll is far more interested than Wittgenstein in who gets to enforce a meaning — Humpty and the Red Queen settle what words mean by rank and volume, and neither of them has anything you'd call a community of speakers behind them — so tell me if that's just me, and what the book adds that the argument alone doesn't.

Don't reduce the book to an illustration of the Investigations, and don't bend Wittgenstein to fit the book. If a scene resists the reading, say so rather than forcing it.
