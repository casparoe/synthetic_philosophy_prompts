I am writing up the commitments I mean to defend in a graduate seminar on language and default assumptions, and I would like them audited before I present them. Here is where I stand.

(1) The offense in so-called generic masculine usage lies neither in etymology nor in what speakers intend, but in the default assumption the expression installs in a hearer: to bring women within the term's scope, the hearer must do work the expression does not do for them, and the generic reading gives way to the male-specific one as soon as the sentence turns to marriage, careers, or violence.

(2) Hofstadter's Person Paper, the satire collected in Metamagical Themas, is the best text I know for making (1) visible, because it runs the entire debate through a single substitution — a race term standing where we say "man", with pronouns coined to match — and makes the reader's own default assumption do the work from the other direction, with nothing inside the piece announcing the substitution.

(3) A satire of that kind is legitimate only where its target cannot be mistaken for the author's own position; a piece a reader can take as sincere has failed, and it does real harm by handing a polished script to the people it was aimed at.

(4) What a text means is fixed by how a competent reader would take it, not by what the author intended.

(5) Since (1) is a claim about how actual audiences process language, the evidence for it is psycholinguistic rather than conceptual.

Which of these genuinely pull against one another, which are only apparently in tension, and what would have to give? Rank the tensions by severity rather than treating them as equal, and for each say what the cheapest repair is and what it costs. Tell me how confident you are in the verdict as a whole, and name the point you are least sure of.
