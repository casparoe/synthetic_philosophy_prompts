Write a philosophical essay on the following controversy in the interpretation of John Stuart Mill's political thought.

Mill spent most of his working life in the service of the East India Company, and he defended British rule in India as a form of trusteeship: government by a foreign power was, in his view, justified so long as it aimed at the moral and intellectual improvement of the governed and prepared them for eventual self-government. The liberal principles for which he is chiefly remembered — above all the harm principle, and the account of individual liberty as the precondition of individuality — were, by his own admission, framed for communities he called civilised; and he held that despotism was a legitimate mode of government in dealing with those he considered barbarous, provided the end in view was their improvement.

The controversy is whether these two commitments — the liberalism and the colonial position — can be held together.

Two broad readings are on offer. The first holds that there is no real inconsistency: Mill's liberalism was always conditional on a society's having reached a certain stage of development, and his colonial writings are simply the application of that condition, so the only live question is whether the condition itself is defensible. The second holds that the exception is an ad hoc carve-out, and that its improvement clause is not a genuine constraint, because Mill never allowed it to condemn any actual instrument of colonial rule; on this view the inconsistency is real and cannot be dissolved, only explained by Mill's biography and by the racialised notion of civilisation he inherited.

A more recent attempt at reconciliation reframes the justification in epistemic terms: the argument is not that subject peoples are inferior, but that they are, at that historical moment, unable to deliberate effectively about their collective good, so that the justification is time-indexed and self-liquidating — it expires as soon as the incapacity does.

Write an essay that adjudicates this controversy and reaches a defended verdict. In doing so:

- State as precisely as you can what the improvement or tutelage condition would have to require for the reconciliation to succeed, then test it against the actual practice of British rule in India — asking whether the condition could in principle have condemned any of the central instruments of that rule (for instance, the extraction of revenue, or the use of force to suppress rebellion), or whether it was framed so loosely that it could always be satisfied.
- Assess whether the epistemic reframing genuinely rescues the reconciliation or merely relocates the arbitrariness into the criterion of readiness; and say what independent evidence could, even in principle, settle a claim about a people's readiness for self-government.
- Address the objection that any verdict here is anachronistic — that we are judging Mill by standards he did not hold — and say whether and why that objection should move you.

Do not end on a non-committal note. Say whether Mill's colonial position is consistent with his liberalism, and defend that verdict. The essay should be written in American English.
