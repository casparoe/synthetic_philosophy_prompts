I'm in a phil of biology seminar this semester and we've spent like three weeks going in circles on "what is life," so I wrote a draft essay for my final paper and I want you to tear into it before I hand it in. Not looking for praise, I want to know where the argument actually breaks and whether it's even interesting or if I'm just restating stuff everyone already says.

Here's the gist. My definition: something is alive to the degree that it is a self-maintaining, heritable dissipative structure. Unpacking that into two conditions, which I treat as coming in degrees rather than being all-or-nothing:

(1) active self-maintenance — the system keeps its own low-entropy organization going by exporting entropy to its surroundings, i.e. it eats, burns, repairs itself;
(2) heritable variation under selection — the mechanism that builds and maintains it gets copied into descendants with occasional errors, so the copies can themselves be selected on.

Then I run the borderline cases through it:
- Bacterium: both conditions, clearly alive.
- Virus: satisfies (2) in a parasitic way — it replicates with variation and evolves — but has no (1) of its own, it borrows the host's. So I call it "half alive" or alive-only-in-context.
- Prion: propagates a conformation, so arguably heritable-ish, but there's no meaningful variation to select on and no metabolism. Not alive.
- Fire: does (1) beautifully, it's a textbook dissipative structure, but zero heritability. Not alive.
- Mule: does (1), and it's part of a lineage, but it can't pass its organization on. This one embarasses my view, because obviously a mule is alive. So I think (2) has to attach to the lineage rather than the individual.
- Computer virus / a self-replicating bit of code: (2) yes if you squint, (1) no since it just parasits the hardware.
- A frozen or dried-out tardigrade: (1) suspended, (2) intact — alive, dormant, or neither?
- Mitochondria: own lineage, own genome, exports entropy — and yet we file it under organelle, not organism. Which makes me think the boundary is partly about level and granularity, not just about which properties are present.

My punchline is that "life" might be a degree concept or a family-resemblance term, so "is a virus alive" is more like "is a tomato a fruit" than like "is this water boiling" — a partly verbal dispute that dissolves once you say what you're using the category for. But I half-suspect that's a cop-out and a way of dodging the hard cases.

So: which of my calls are wrong, and why? Does the degree view actually dissolve the borderline problem or does it just relabel it? And be honest — is the two-condition definition doing any real work, or is it just the standard thermodynamic account and the standard evolutionary account stapled together with nothing new in between? If it's the latter, tell me what a genuinely different cut at the problem would look like.
