im building a doc for a reading group on when some things add up to a further thing -- the when-do-parts-make-a-whole question -- and before we look at anyones answer i want the spec first. what does any theory of composition have to deliver to count as adequate? give me the list: the cases it has to get right, the distinctions it can't just collapse, the bits of real science it can't contradict, and the consideration behind each requirment, not just the name of it.

the cases that are bugging me: a pile of sand, a cloud, a flame, a whirlpool, a river, a forest, a coral reef, a slime mold, a beehive, a queue, a corporation, a statue and the lump of metal it's made of, and me -- i've swapped out most of my atoms since i was five. the theory needs to say something sensible about all of them, and it can't just answer yes to every gerrymandered sum anyone names, nor no to everything but the smallest bits. i want it spelt out as a requirement why each of those extremes is worse than some middle view, not just asserted.

here's a half-formed criterion i want the spec to test: things compose a new thing when they're caught in one self-maintaining loop that keeps them arranged that way against the drift toward disorder -- a flame, a whirlpool, a cell, maybe a species or a market if you squint. could that even be made precise? and what would a theory built on it have to get right that the boring answers don't?

i specifically want these in the list, each with its case:

- vagueness: a heap becoming a heap one grain at a time, a cloud shedding droplets, a lump melting. is composition itself vague, or is it just our words?
- counting: if every sum of things is a thing, how many things are in this room? does that break how we count and quantify?
- work: does the whole have to do any causal or explanatory work of its own, or do the parts do all of it? is that a genuine requirement on the theory, or a demand smuggled in from the philosophy of mind?
- the statue and the lump: one thing under two descriptions, two things sharing parts, or something else? which answer is the theory obliged to give?
- persistence: if composition is temporary, when a thing gains or loses a part does it become a different thing, and where is the theory allowed to stay quiet?
- the weird limits: does it have to leave room for a world where nothing is simple, with no atoms and just stuff dividing forever, and for a world where nothing is ever the biggest thing? i'm definately not sure these are real requirements, so say if they are.
- science: no natural joints in physics, entanglement, organisms that are colonies, species as populations, self-sustaining far-from-equilibrium systems. which of these are real constraints and which are just one camp's taste?

then mark which requirements are non-negotiable and which are preferences of a camp -- the people who think composition is a mind-independent fact of the matter versus the ones who think it's interest-relative or conventional. and if some pair or trio of the requirements can't all be had at once, say which; i suspect the list is overfull and that's the bit i most want. skip scoring the contenders, we'll do that in the group, and no reading list please.
