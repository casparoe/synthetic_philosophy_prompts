so im a junior and i have to give the closing speech on the last night of this two-week logic and philosophy camp i go to every summer (its at a college, forty of us in dorms, four grad student instructors, and on the last night the parents drive up for dinner so the room is half kids who spent two weeks doing this stuff and half parents who think the whole thing is adorable). i want to build the speech around the problem we spent the most time on, logical omniscience, because by the end of the two weeks it had gotten weirdly emotional for a bunch of us and i think i can make that land for the parents too.

heres the thing i want it built on, in case you dont know it: our instructor said that in the standard textbook way of modeling knowledge, an agent knows all the logical consequences of what they already know. if you know p, and you know that if p then q, you already know q, and you know everything that follows from q, forever, instantly. so an ideal knower can never learn anything, because anything new was already implied by what they knew before. they can never be surprised, because a surprise is just a conclusion you hadnt drawn yet, and theyve drawn all of them. they never stare at a proof for an hour and then go ohhh. she said this is obviously a bad picture of us and the field has spent decades patching it, like: give the agent an awareness operator so they only reason with the bits theyre actually aware of, or let them reason in little separate bundles of belief that dont talk to each other, or bring in impossible worlds so that not every logical truth comes out true in every world theyre considering. i dont need you to teach me any of that and i definitely shouldnt try to explain it on stage, i just want the speech to get the problem exactly right and then do something with it.

what i want to say is that the whole point of the two weeks is that we are the kind of thing that doesnt already know the answers, and that the ideal reasoner is a fiction that would be awful to actually be. a surprise party only works because somebody didnt put two and two together, not because the conclusion wasnt sitting right there in what they knew.

so write the speech. 700-900 words, like six minutes. it has to work for both halves of the room: the instructers should be able to tell i actually understand it (closure, why learning and surprise get impossible, why people bother inventing fixes) and the parents shouldnt feel lectured at, they should just hear something true about what their kids were doing all fortnight. make it a real goodbye speech, warm and a little funny, and land it on being able to get things wrong and be caught off guard as the point rather than the bug. please dont name any philosopher or paper or book, i will absolutely get it wrong, and dont quote anybody. and dont make me sound like a forty year old professor, im sixteen.
