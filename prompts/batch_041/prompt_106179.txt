I am a novelist, and for the better part of a year a historian friend and I have been having the same argument about a book I am writing, without either of us giving an inch, and I would like you to diagnose the argument rather than settle it.

The book concerns a town that proposes to demolish something old and load-bearing, and the idea at the centre of it is the one usually called Chesterton's fence: the injunction not to clear away an arrangement until you can say what it was for. My position is that the principle names something real — that an arrangement which has survived is a rough record of problems solved, including problems no living person can now state, and that a reformer who cannot say what the fence was for is not entitled to assume it was for nothing. My friend's position is that this is not a principle but a mood with a veto attached: taken literally it would have blocked the abolition of nearly everything that deserved abolishing, and its actual use is selective, invoked against changes one dislikes and quietly forgotten when one wants something gone. We know the same history, we cite the same cases, and we have not moved.

My own suspicion — offered only as a suspicion — is that we are not disagreeing about history or about consequences at all, but about who bears the burden of proof, and perhaps about what it could even mean to know why a fence is there. I notice that the phrase can pick out either the builder's original intention or the work the fence is doing now, and these can come apart: a fence raised for a reason that no longer holds may still be load-bearing, for a reason its builder never had.

Give me your diagnosis. Is this a substantive disagreement about how the world works, a disagreement about epistemic standards, a disagreement about which cases count as the data, or two people answering different questions under one name? If the last, say precisely which questions. And tell me what, if anything, would count as progress here — a distinction, a case neither of us has considered, a formal result about deference under uncertainty, or an argument that nothing will move it and why.

Please structure the response in four sections. First, "The two positions, stated fairly," roughly 200 words, setting out both views as their better advocates would put them, without caricature. Second, "What kind of disagreement this is," roughly 400 words, which is the diagnosis itself. Third, "Settled versus contested," roughly 200 words, separating what people who work on this actually agree about from what is genuinely in dispute among them, and not presenting a contested reading as the consensus. Fourth, "What would count as progress," roughly 200 words, giving your honest assessment of whether the impasse can be broken and by what.
