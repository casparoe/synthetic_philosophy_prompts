I am putting together an application for one of the modest internal seed grants our faculty offers — a semester of partial teaching relief plus a small sum for travel and a one-day workshop — and I would like you to draft the two-page proposal. The project is on the definition of life, but I want it to be clear from the first paragraph that this is not another attempt to add a fourth clause to the usual formula. My half-formed idea, which I have been turning over since a seminar discussion in which a biologist and a philosopher talked past each other for a solid hour, is that the debate is stuck because the participants run together three questions that only look like one: whether being alive is a matter of degree along a gradient of capacities, whether it is a threshold drawn by our word, and whether it is a matter of historical descent from the earliest replicating systems. Nearly every counterexample in the literature — the giant viruses whose genomes are larger than those of some bacteria and the smaller viruses that parasitize them, prions, plasmids, transmissible tumours, microbes revived after long dormancy, the sterile hybrid that is plainly alive but cannot evolve — is fatal only to a view that answers one of these questions while being defended as an answer to another. If that diagnosis is right, the interesting question is the third: that life is primarily a genealogical predicate rather than a capacity predicate, in which case the standard hunt for necessary and sufficient capacities is a category mistake and the boundary cases resolve by descent rather than by achievement. The consequence I am least comfortable with, and which the proposal has to confront rather than bury, is that a synthetic organism assembled from non-living parts and capable of robust open-ended evolution would not be alive on that account; I cannot yet tell whether that is a reductio of the view or a bullet worth biting, and I want the proposal to say how the project would decide.

The proposal should state the question so that it has a determinate answer, explain why the existing answers leave it open — both the capacity-based definitions and the sceptical line that the concept is a family-resemblance term with no essence — and say why the diagnostic approach I am proposing, separating the questions before answering any of them, is better than the obvious alternative of simply defending a new definition. For method I want something concrete and unglamorous: an inventory of the disputed cases and what each does to each of the three readings; a small study of how the term is actually applied where it has consequences, in biosafety rules about what counts as a biological agent, in planetary-protection protocols, and in patents on engineered organisms, since those are places where a definition does work rather than being admired; and then a positive account, with the synthetic-organism case as the test. Say what a result would look like — a paper of roughly eight thousand words, plus a short decision procedure a non-philosopher could apply to a new case — and be honest about what would count as failure: if, once the questions are separated, the residual disputes turn out to be verbal all the way down, or if the genealogical account turns out to be a redescription of the first-replicator lineage with no independent consequences, I would want to know that before spending a semester on it. Assume one semester, assume I am working alone and teaching two courses, and do not include any references or a bibliography; I will add those myself.
