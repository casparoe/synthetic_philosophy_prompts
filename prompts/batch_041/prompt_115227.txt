I'm a software engineer on a small platform team that maintains a widely used wire protocol and a compatibility registry: third parties submit their own clients and servers, and we decide whether to list each one as a conforming implementation. Today we decide case by case, mostly by running the submitter's code against our reference test suite, and we have been inconsistent enough that rejected submitters have started appealing in public. We get roughly a submission a month, and I want to replace our ad hoc judgment with a written procedure my teammates and I can actually run.

A colleague who studied philosophy says our problem is the same one philosophers of mind have about minds and brains, and that the position usually called functionalism, together with the observation that one and the same function can be realized in many different physical systems, gives the right criterion: what makes something an implementation of our protocol is the causal role it plays — the mapping from inputs to outputs and the way it interacts with the rest of the system — rather than the structure or the substrate of the thing playing that role. I don't know whether that is right, but I want the procedure built on it so we can see where it strains.

Please write the procedure: the questions to ask, in a fixed order; what counts as a sufficient answer to each; where the procedure terminates in accept or reject; and where it must stop and hand the decision to a person because the remaining question is a value judgment rather than a test result. It has to be something two engineers with no philosophy training can run in about an hour and defend in writing when a rejection is appealed.

It has to survive at least these cases: a submission that passes every test we have but does so by shipping a giant precomputed table of answers, or by quietly forwarding each request to our reference implementation over the network; a submission that matches on every defined input but diverges on inputs the spec leaves undefined; a submission that is correct but whose timing, memory, or error-handling behavior under load is visibly different from the reference; and two submissions that are indistinguishable on every test we can write, yet whose maintainers insist their internal states mean different things.

Then run the procedure on two or three concrete submissions, including one where it returns an answer I will not like, and tell me plainly what the procedure cannot settle — the cases where it will make us look decisive while in fact making a value judgment.
