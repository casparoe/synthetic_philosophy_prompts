Two friends of mine have been arguing for about a year and I want to force them to write it up properly, because I think they're mostly talking past each other. One of them thinks Condorcet's jury theorem is the deepest thing anyone has ever said about why majority rule is more than just a fair way to decide things. Her line: if each voter is even slightly better than a coin flip at getting the answer, and they form their opinions independently, then the majority converges on the truth as the group gets bigger -- so majority rule isn't just legitimate, it's truth-tracking. My other friend, the sceptic, thinks the theorem is a gorgeous machine made entirely out of assumptions that never hold: real people's errors are correlated, becuase they read the same papers and catch the same flu of the moment; real competence is all over the place; people vote strategically or stay home; and for a lot of political questions there's no "correct answer" to converge on in the first place.

They've been going around in circles, mostly becuase the optimist is defending a theorem and the sceptic is attacking its applications, and neither of them has noticed. So write the joint statement they could both sign after a proper good-faith adversarial collaboration. Numbered list of agreed claims first -- and yes, that should include the theorem itself as a piece of mathematics, stated precisely enough that the sceptic signs it too: what exactly has to hold, what exactly converges to what as n grows, and what the finite-n version of the claim looks like. Then a disagreements table, one row per surviving disagreement, with a column giving the exact wording of the optimist's position (phrased so she'd accept it as her own) and a column giving the sceptic's, plus a column for the crux -- the sub-question that, if we actually settled it, would move one of them.

For every crux, give the test or arguement both sides agree would bear on it, and each side's prediction, written down in advance. I especially want the following covered, since it's where I think the real action is: whether the theorem survives dependence between voters' errors, and whether there's some notion of "approximately independent" under which it does; whether it needs every voter above one half or only the average voter above one half; how large the group actually has to be before the majority is any good when competence is barely above chance; whether it survives people voting strategically instead of sincerely, or whether abstention by the uninformed ends up doing the work instead; whether the majority is genuinely better than the single best-informed voter or only better than the average one, and whether that distinction matters for the claim that democracy is epistemically good; and what happens once there are more than two options and majority preference starts going in circles -- whether that can be held together with the cheerful two-option result at all. If you decide some part of their fight is purely verbal -- say they mean different things by "competence" or "independence" -- tell me which part and what each of them actually means by it. No citations or reading list needed, and don't tell me who's right; the whole point is a document they can both put their names to.

Finish with a single last line in the form \agreed{...} containing the one sentence about the jury theorem that both of them would sign.
