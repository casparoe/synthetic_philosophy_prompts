I am a final-year secondary school student writing an extended essay for my school's philosophy prize, and I would be very grateful for thorough comments on the draft below. The finished essay has to be around 1,500 words, so what follows is a compressed version of the argument. It is meant to stand on its own, so please do not send me a reading list or citations; I am more interested in whether the reasoning works than in who has said what.

**Knowing How Without Knowing That You Know How**

Most discussions of the difference between knowing how to do something and knowing that something is the case present two options. On the first, knowing how to φ is a species of knowing that: to know how to swim is to know that a certain method — kick thus, breathe so — is the way to swim, together with the practical ability that this knowledge normally brings. This view is attractive because it explains how instruction works: a coach tells us truths, and our performance improves. On the second, knowing how to φ is simply being able to φ; the phrase reports an ability and nothing more. This view is attractive because it respects the regress argument usually traced to Ryle: if every intelligent action required a prior piece of knowledge about how to perform it, then acting on that knowledge would itself require a further piece, and so on without end.

I want to argue that both options are mistaken, and that knowing how is better understood as a tuned capacity — a reliable sensitivity to the demands of a task that is neither a stored proposition nor mere present ability. Two cases suggest the gap.

The first is the case of the injured coach. Suppose someone taught swimming for years, and can no longer swim at all because of a shoulder injury, yet still watches a pupil and says, correctly and usefully, that the pupil's left arm is entering the water too early and should reach further. If know-how were propositional, the coach plainly has it, for she knows the relevant truths. If know-how were ability, she plainly lacks it, for she cannot swim. Neither verdict matches how we would actually speak, which suggests the concept answers to something else.

The second is the case of the choking violinist. A violinist can play a difficult passage perfectly in performance, but when asked to explain how she does it she gives an account that is simply false, and when she tries to follow an account that is true she plays worse. Here the knowledge, whatever it is, seems to be unavailable to the agent as a description, and indeed damaged by the attempt to turn it into one. That is hard to square with the idea that know-how consists in knowing a true proposition about method, since possession of a proposition is supposed to be the sort of thing one can consult.

The natural objection is that a tuned capacity is just a disposition, and dispositions are cheap: a glass is fragile even if it never breaks, and by the same token anyone who would swim well if only their shoulder worked would count as knowing how to swim. I do not think this is fatal, but it forces a qualification. What distinguishes a tuned capacity from a bare disposition to succeed is that its manifestations are guided by the agent's own sensitivity to the task rather than merely caused by favourable circumstances; the lucky swimmer who succeeds by flailing is not thereby a knower. The difficulty, which I have not resolved, is whether this qualification can be stated without smuggling ability back in, and whether the injured coach's sensitivity is really hers in the relevant sense, or merely a description of what her pupils would do.

Please give me comments of the following kinds, in this order.

1. Accuracy. Have I mischaracterised either of the two standard positions in a way that a knowledgeable reader would wince at? In particular, is it fair to treat the ability view as committed to saying that the injured coach does not know how to swim?

2. Coherence. Is a tuned capacity a genuine third option, or a relabelling that collapses into one of the two views under pressure? If it collapses, tell me which way, and why.

3. Fairness of the cases. Is the coach case rigged — for instance, does it only seem to work because I have quietly assumed that teaching a skill requires having the skill? And is the violinist case really about know-how rather than about the limits of introspection?

4. Interest. Is the thesis doing any work, or is it the sort of thing that sounds deep and turns out to be a restatement of the obvious?

5. The strongest objection I have not considered.

6. Practical suggestions for tightening the prose and for the order in which the two cases should appear.

Where you think I have simply got something wrong, please say so plainly rather than softening it; I would rather find out now.
