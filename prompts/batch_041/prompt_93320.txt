A colleague of mine in the department — a good philosopher, but one who works in ethics and has never specialized in the philosophy of mind — has written the following précis of the Dennett–Searle dispute for a graduate reading group we run together. I am not enough of a specialist to trust my own reaction to it, and I would rather have a proper audit than a set of half-remembered objections. My own inclination is that the two positions are further apart than she makes them out to be, but I could not defend that claim under questioning. Here is what she wrote.

Dennett's leading example is the two-bitser, a coin detector of the kind found in vending machines. She describes one wired so that it accepts both genuine quarters and counterfeit slugs of a certain kind, and reports Dennett's observation that there is no fact of the matter about whether such a device is mistaking slugs for quarters or correctly responding to a disjunction of properties. The moral she draws is that the device does not represent anything at all: talk of what it takes its input to be is metaphorical, and its internal states have no content of their own. Generalizing, she has Dennett holding that intentionality is never intrinsic to a system — that a state is about something only relative to the interpretive stance we adopt toward the system, so that intentionality is derived from the interpreter rather than located in the system. Searle, she continues, accepts a version of this distinction but insists that it is not the whole story: some states have original intentionality, which they possess in their own right, whereas artifacts such as books, computers, and paintings have only derived intentionality, conferred on them by the minds that use them. The Chinese room is meant to establish that a computer's symbols are at best derived, and therefore cannot amount to understanding. It follows, on her reading, that Dennett and Searle agree on more than is usually supposed: both deny that a computer could possess original intentionality, and their real disagreement is only about whether a purely computational system might someday acquire intentionality of the kind that brains have. The two-bitser, on this account, far from undermining Searle, actually supports him — if a machine's representations are as-if at best, then the Chinese room's occupant is not understanding anything either.

I would like you to audit this précis claim by claim. For each substantive assertion, tell me whether a specialist working on intentionality would accept it as stated, would treat it as a permissible simplification, or would reject it as a misrepresentation; and where it is a misrepresentation, say precisely what has been conflated or overstated and what the correct formulation would be. I am particularly concerned with three things. First, whether the précis reports accurately what the two-bitser example is meant to show, and whether the moral she draws from it is one Dennett could own. Second, whether the two philosophers really share the premise she says they share, or whether attributing that premise to Dennett distorts his position. Third, whether the closing inference — that the example supports Searle's conclusion — survives once the preceding claims are corrected. Please also state, as compactly as you can, how each philosopher would restate the disputed points in his own voice, and flag any sentence that is not so much false as ambiguous between a true and a false reading. Finally, tell me whether her overall verdict — that the two positions are closer than they are commonly made out to be — can be sustained in any form, and if so, in what form.
