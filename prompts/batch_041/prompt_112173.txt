I have been following rationalist blogs and forums for years, and I keep ending up in the same argument about whether an ant colony can have a mind. The latest round started when a friend, defending the Aunt Hillary thought experiment—the imagined colony that talks, reasons, and seems to have a personality—kept saying that the colony is the mind and the ants are its neurons. He used that line to brush aside the worry that Aunt Hillary is just a useful fiction: if the colony is the mind, then asking which ant is conscious is like asking which neuron is conscious. I think the line is doing too much work, because I can hear at least four different claims in it, and they do not all support his conclusion. I want a precise disambiguation before I reply.

Please lay out, as a menu rather than a single interpretation, the distinct theses that the slogan could be expressing, from the trivial to the radical. For each reading, say what it commits you to about the relation between ant-level facts and colony-level facts—identity, supervenience, emergence, reduction, or mere summary—and about what kind of mind is supposedly present: consciousness, intentionality, cognition, agency, or only information processing. Distinguish readings on which the colony must be a unified subject of experience from readings on which it need only integrate information or support useful predictions, and readings on which the ants' states must represent something to the colony from readings on which they merely cause colony-level states. Mark each reading as a platitude, a substantive and defensible thesis, a contentious one, or false, and say what evidence or thought experiment would count for or against it—for example, what should we say if we replaced ants with silicon chips one at a time, or if the colony's behavior were indistinguishable from a person's but its internal economy were utterly unlike ours. Finally, tell me which reading my friend most likely needs in order to get from the slogan to the conclusion that Aunt Hillary really has a mind, and whether that reading actually supports the conclusion or whether it quietly changes the subject. A numbered list with short verdicts is fine; I do not need citations, reading lists, or a survey of who said what.
