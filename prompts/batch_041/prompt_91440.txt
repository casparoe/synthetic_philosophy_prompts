ok so I'm putting together a small internal grant application — six month, one paper plus a half-day workshop, no money for surveys or fieldwork — and I want the proposal to be about something I keep circling: the way peple lump the American Dream, Jantelagen and tall poppy syndrome together as if they were three positions on one axis, pro-success versus anti-success. I dont think that's right, and I want a proposal that shows why in a way that's actually arguable rather than just a taxonomy.

Here's the half-formed thing I'd like you to build on. Two distinctions seem to cut across each other.

First, who the norm is addressed to. The ten Jante rules in Sandemose's 1933 novel are, as I read them, mostly self-addressed — they're phrased as things you're not permitted to think about yourself, not as instructions to a crowd about how to treat someone who stands out. (I know the novel is satire and that Sandemose denied inventing the attitudes.) Tall poppy syndrome, as the term is actually used, is other-addressed: it names what a group does to the person who rises. And the American Dream is addressed to the striver, but I suspect most of its normative work happens at the point of failure — it's a doctrine about whose fault it is when you don't make it.

Second, what the norm is for. Cutting down the tall poppy has an ancestor that isn't about envy at all: in the Roman and Greek stories, the gesture is a tyrant silently telling his son or ally to kill the leading men of a city. So a top-down technique of rule seems to have become a bottom-up story about egalitarian resentment. Is that migration real, or is the modern usage just borrowing a vivid image?

So the question: are these three norms answers to one question — how should a community respond to unequal achievement? — or to three different questions that happen to share a vocabulary? And if they're different, what happens to the standard objection that the American Dream is cruel and the Scandinavian norm is merely envious?

Write me the proposal. Statement of the question so that it has a determinate answer, why the usual framings leave it open, what I'd actually do month by month (I'm a philosopher, not a sociologist, and there's no budget for surveys, so the method has to be conceptual without being hand-wavy), what a result would look like, what would count as the project failing, and an honest read on whether six months is enough or whether I'm fooling myself.
