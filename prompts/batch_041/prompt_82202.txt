You are a professional philosopher of mind with a research specialism in the metaphysics of the self, writing for an audience of advanced students who are comfortable with technical vocabulary and impatient with hand-waving. Take the task seriously and think the positions through before you begin: a superficial exchange in which each character merely restates a familiar slogan will not do.

Write a dialogue between two philosophers who agree on one large thing and disagree, sharply and irreconcilably, on a smaller but deeper one. What they agree on is that there is no substantial ego — no simple, persisting, immaterial thing that thinks. What they disagree about is what, if anything, is left once the ego has been removed, and whether the residual self is essentially a narrative construction or essentially a matter of experience.

The first character, A, defends the narrative-gravity view. On this view the self is not a thing in the head but an abstract point — a centre of narrative gravity — around which an organism's memories, dispositions, commitments and self-descriptions organise themselves, much as a centre of gravity organises the parts of a physical body. A insists that this is not a debunking move: centres of gravity are respectable, explanatory and perfectly real features of the world, even though they are not concrete objects. A accordingly treats selfhood as coming in degrees, and as being partly a matter of the interpreter's stance rather than a brute fact about the organism.

The second character, B, defends a rival view on which the self is first and foremost an experiential achievement rather than a narrative one: a pre-reflective self-awareness that is already in place before any story is told, is present in creatures with no language and no autobiography, and cannot be conjured out of the mere availability of a coherent narrative reading. On B's view, narrative is a cultural practice superimposed on a self that is not itself narrative; the narrativist mistakes the story for the thing.

The dialogue must engage at least three of the following four points of friction, and must treat them at length rather than name-checking them:

1. The abstraction objection. If the self is merely an abstract centre, in what sense can it be the subject of experiences, the bearer of interests, or the thing that is wronged by death? B should press this hard; A should answer without retreating to the claim that it is merely a way of talking.
2. The language problem. Consider a stipulated case: a person who suffers severe aphasia in mid-life and can no longer formulate or recount any life-story, yet who retains a robust sense of being a particular someone with a particular past. If the self is narrative, is such a person's selfhood diminished? The characters should disagree about what the case shows, and about whether narration must be linguistic, occurrent and told, or may instead be dispositional, silent and sub-personal.
3. The multiplicity problem. Suppose a person — or a divided brain, or a person whose decades of diaries record two mutually unreconcilable self-portraits — supports not one but two equally good narrative readings. Does the narrative-gravity view deliver a determinate answer to the question of how many selves there are, or does it show the question to be ill-formed? One character should treat the indeterminacy as a virtue; the other should treat it as a reductio.
4. The borrowed centre. Much of the material from which we build our self-narratives is inherited — from family, institutions, genres, and now from systems that predict and feed back our own descriptions of ourselves. If the centre of gravity is assembled largely out of other people's stories, whose centre is it, and has the view lost the individual it was meant to explain?

Observe the following constraints throughout. Both characters are first-rate: neither is a strawman, neither is stupid, and neither scores points by being rude. Each must state the other's position in terms the other would accept before criticising it, and each must concede at least one genuine point. They must respond to each other's actual words and arguments rather than delivering alternating monologues; where they do begin to talk past each other, one of them should notice and try to locate the source of the disagreement. Do not name or cite any philosophers, works or studies, and do not supply a reading list or bibliography: present every position in the character's own voice, and use no real quotations.

Begin with three or four sentences of your own setting out the two positions and identifying the single question you take to be the crux of the dispute. Then write the dialogue. Do not resolve it tidily: end with the crux exposed, and with one character stating precisely what evidence or argument would move them. Aim for roughly a thousand to fourteen hundred words of dialogue, in British English.
