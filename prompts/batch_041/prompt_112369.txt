I recently moved from a reinforcement-learning engineering role onto an alignment team whose agents are trained with an explicit impact penalty — a term meant to discourage an agent from changing the world in ways its task did not require — and I have been struck by how much my new colleagues disagree, in ways that surface only in design review, about what that penalty is actually measuring. One camp treats it as a cautious way of avoiding irreversible damage to things people value; another insists it is a value-free proxy and must stay value-free to be useful at all; a third thinks the whole enterprise is a dodge, since any impact measure already presupposes a ranking of states as better or worse. I want a classification instrument I can put in front of the team, not a survey of the literature.

Please write a decision tree or taxonomy of the positions on the question: can "avoiding side effects" be defined without a full specification of value? Build it so that every branch node is a question a colleague can answer without already knowing which position that answer leads to, and every leaf is a position named by its defining commitment — use a standard name only where one is genuinely widespread, and otherwise a descriptive name, or "unnamed" if nobody actually holds it.

For my purposes, the distinctions that must appear as branch points are: whether impact is measured against the agent's own inaction or against some default or "natural" continuation of the world; whether the measure ranges over raw world-states, over features someone selected in advance, or over agents' ability to achieve their goals; whether a value-free proxy is claimed to approximate true value or merely to be a conservative sufficient condition that errs only toward inaction; whether impact avoidance is a standalone objective or an auxiliary constraint alongside a task objective; and whether "keeping futures open" is treated as itself the good to be protected or as a stand-in for whatever people happen to want.

Aim for seven to nine leaves. Where a combination of answers is unoccupied, mark it as such and say in one clause whether it looks incoherent, merely unattractive, or simply unexplored. Flag any two leaves the tree cannot separate. End with a single line of the form \answer{...} naming the one question on the tree that, if a colleague answered it differently, would move the largest number of positions — the load-bearing node.
