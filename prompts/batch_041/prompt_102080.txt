I'm a teaching assistant on a second-year philosophy of biology module, and next week's seminar covers the units of selection debate. My students have already met the gene's-eye view and the classic group-selection controversy, so I don't want another rehearsal of the replicator/vehicle distinction; what I want is something that forces them to argue about which entities genuinely earn their keep as units of selection, rather than reciting the textbook positions. Please score each of the candidate units listed below from 0 to 10, where 10 means the entity does substantial, indispensable explanatory work in accounting for adaptation and clearly satisfies the standard criteria for being a unit of selection (heritable variation, differential reproduction, and a causal role in producing adapted form), and 0 means it is either not a coherent entity or adds nothing that a lower-level account cannot supply. Then group the candidates into tiers on the basis of those scores, and give each one two or three sentences of justification for where it lands.

The candidates are: the gene or selfish genetic element; the individual organism; the kin group or family; the cultural meme; the species or clade as a lineage; the holobiont, by which I mean a host together with its resident microbiome treated as a single reproducing and selectable whole; transgenerational epigenetic inheritance, meaning heritable chromatin or methylation states that are transmitted across generations without any change to the underlying DNA sequence; and non-human behavioural traditions, such as song dialects or tool-use customs that persist in a population by social learning. I should also flag a ninth candidate that is my own invention and that I would like you to treat seriously rather than dismiss: the replicating consortium, defined as any set of entities so tightly coupled in reproduction that selection cannot prise them apart, with the obligate symbiosis of a lichen, or a plasmid and its host, as the paradigm cases. Tell me whether that last one deserves a place of its own or simply collapses into the holobiont, and whether it is a genuine biological kind or merely a description of several unrelated arrangements.

Beyond the ranking itself, I would like three further things. First, name the candidate you regard as most overrated and the one you regard as most underrated, and explain the gap between reputation and merit in each case. Second, consider whether the whole exercise presupposes a replicator-centric framing of selection, and if a developmental-systems or process-oriented view would reshuffle your tiers; if it would, say which candidates move and why. Third, close with a short verdict on whether this debate is substantive or largely a dispute over how to apply a word, since my students will inevitably ask. Please write the whole response in British English.
