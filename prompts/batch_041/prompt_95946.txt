I'm a third-year graduate student in philosophy, and next month I'm leading the discussion section on Jain epistemology in an undergraduate survey. There's a short passage I keep assigning and keep failing to explain, and I'd like help working out what it actually commits its author to. It comes from the Internet Encyclopedia of Philosophy's entry on Jain philosophy, and it runs: "Since reality is multi-faceted, none of the pramanas gives absolute or perfect knowledge (except kevala, which is enjoyed only by the perfected soul, and cannot be expressed in language). As a result, any item of knowledge gained is only tentative and provisional. This is expressed in Jain philosophy in the doctrine of naya, or partial predication (sometimes called the doctrine of perspectives or viewpoints). According to this doctrine, any judgment is true only from the viewpoint or perspective of the judge, and ought to be so expressed." Earlier in the same entry the author says that reality is multifaceted "such that no one view can capture it in its entirety; that is, no single statement or set of statements captures the complete truth about the objects they describe." I've also seen the whole doctrine compressed into a line attributed to Bimal Matilal: "no philosophic or metaphysical proposition can be true if it is asserted without any condition or limitation."

What I can't get straight is how these sentences hang together, and the students smell it. My rough grasp of the accompanying apparatus is that the Jains distinguish partial standpoints from a sevenfold scheme of conditioned predication, in which a thing may be said, in some respect, to be; in some respect, not to be; in some respect, both to be and not to be; in some respect, to be indescribable; and then in three further modes that combine the indescribable with the first three. I know translations and orderings of these modes vary, so please don't lean on a particular rendering or tell me which text says it.

Here is what I need. First, the passage says a judgment is true only from the perspective of the judge. That sounds like it makes truth a function of who is speaking, which would be relativism, but the same passage insists reality is many-faceted, which sounds like a claim about the object rather than the speaker. Is the doctrine actually running these two ideas together, or does it have the resources to keep them apart, and if so, what does the separating work? Second, when the tradition prefixes a claim with the qualifier meaning "in some respect," what is the qualification ranging over: aspects of the object, standpoints of the knower, or the inadequacy of language? The answer changes what the view is, and I want to see the consequences spelled out rather than asserted. Third, the mode rendered as indescribable: if saying a thing is indescribable is itself a predication, how is that not incoherent, and is the point about a thing's simultaneously holding contradictory properties, or about language's limits, or both? Fourth, does the third mode mean that a thing both is and is not something at the same time, or only that it is at one time and is not at another? A lot seems to hang on which, and I'd like to know how much. Fifth, why seven modes rather than six or eight, and is the list meant to be exhaustive of what can be said about any subject, or merely illustrative? And sixth, does the doctrine survive being turned on itself: the claim that no unconditioned assertion is true is itself an unconditioned-sounding assertion, so is it conditioned too, and if so, is the position then trivial, or self-undermining, or genuinely stable?
