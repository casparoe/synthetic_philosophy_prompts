I have spent the better part of a year reporting on how historians, epidemiologists, and policy analysts argue about causes, and I keep running into a move that I can describe but cannot name. It goes roughly like this. An analyst wants to explain some large outcome — a war, a recession, a bridge failure — and offers a claim of the form: had the trigger not occurred, the outcome would not have followed. Pressed for support, the analyst points to the ordinary correlation in the record: the trigger is present in the cases where the outcome followed, absent in the cases where it did not. When a critic wants to rebut the claim, she points to a single case in which the trigger occurred and the outcome did not, and treats that as decisive. What strikes me is that both sides are borrowing the standards of a quite different sort of claim — the everyday predictive "if the trigger occurs, the outcome will follow" — and applying them without noticing that they have changed the subject. A single contrary case can indeed sink the predictive claim, and frequencies in the actual record are just the sort of thing that support it; but it is not at all clear that the same evidence bears in the same way on a claim about what would have happened had things gone otherwise, since that claim concerns a course of events that never took place. I have watched this happen in print, in seminars, and in my own drafts, and each time the participants seem to slide between the two kinds of "if"-statement without a flicker of discomfort.

Perhaps there is a standard diagnosis of this and I simply do not know it. I am not asking for a reading list or citations — I would not know what to do with them — but for the concept. Is there a recognized name for the confusion of these two kinds of "if"-claim, or for the asymmetry whereby the one can be refuted by a single actual case while the other seems answerable to a different sort of evidence altogether? If there is a name, I would like to know how narrowly it is drawn: whether it covers only the outright conflation, or also the looser habit of treating evidence for the one as evidence for the other. If there is no single name, I would be grateful if you would coin one and define it, marking the boundary. How does the thing I am describing differ from a merely careless equivocation on the word "if," from the general difficulty of saying what makes a claim about how things would have gone otherwise true or false, and from the historian's ordinary appeal to a decisive cause or a butterfly effect? What I want is not the label but the carving: where exactly the error lies, which of the two claims is being misused, and whether the mistake is best described as one of logic, of evidence, or of something else entirely.
