I'm TAing an intro causal inference course this term and I've gotten into a running argument with the professor about Simpson's paradox. She teaches it as "when the pooled table and the subgroups disagree, trust the subgroups," and I think that's basically a lie — or at least it's the kind of thing that leaves students with a rule instead of an understanding. The examples dont help either; the kidney-stone one and the batting-average one are so old that everyone in the room has already seen them, and nobody has to think about why one table is the honest one. So for next week's worksheet I want to build two fresh cases, and I want them to be cases where the pooled table and the stratified tables disagree about the *direction* of the effect, but where the two cases point in opposite directions about which table you should believe. Construct them, with actual numbers, not just a description of the shape.

Case 1: a realistic setting (medicine, admissions, hiring, sports, whatever — please not a made-up disease with a letter for a name) where group A beats group B in the pooled table but loses to B in every stratum of some third variable, and where it's the pooled comparison that is causally correct. It has to be fully specified: give me the whole data-generating story, including any lurking variable, so that someone who reads the story can see the right answer is forced — I don't want it resting on "well, there could always be confounding we don't know about." Give the observed data as integer counts in a small table I could check on a napkin, and describe the causal structure in words, a little arrow diagram is fine. Case 2: the mirror image, same kind of reversal in the numbers, but this time the stratified comparison is the right one and the pooled one is misleading, and I want the causal structure to be genuinely different from Case 1 — not just Case 1's diagram with an arrow reversed by fiat. I have a vague feeling the difference has something to do with whether the third variable is a common cause of treatment and outcome or a common effect of them, but I can never keep straight which is which or how to get the numbers to come out, so sort that out for me.

Then the part I actually care about: walk me through each case. State what the pooled table says and what the stratified tables say, say which one you're endorsing and why, in terms of the structure, and be explicit about whether A vs. B was assigned at random in each case and why that turns out to matter. Tell me which single feature of the setup is doing all the work, and then what happens if I remove that feature or swap that variable for the other kind — does the reversal collapse, flip, or stay but stop mattering? Finish each case with how a smart student who's defending the *other* verdict would push back: what they'd have to assume, or which of your assumptions they'd have to reject, to keep their reading. Keep it tight, this is going on one side of a worksheet, so no padding and no history-of-the-paradox section.
