I have been reading the rationalist blogosphere for the better part of two decades, and the habit it has left me with is a low tolerance for institutions that quietly import a metaphysics and never own up to it. Here is one that has been nagging at me, and I should like you to do the reconstruction.

The practice, as I understand it: since 2024 most development in England has had to demonstrate a measurable net gain in biodiversity. A developer who grubs out a hedgerow, drains a pond or builds over a meadow must produce, on the site or elsewhere, a quantity of biodiversity greater than the quantity destroyed — the statutory figure is ten per cent more. The quantity is not a matter of intuition. It is computed with an official metric that treats the landscape as a set of habitat parcels and scores each parcel by its area, its habitat type, its condition and its location, with further discounts for how difficult the replacement habitat is to create and how long it takes to reach maturity. The output is a number of biodiversity units. Units can be bought, sold and banked: a developer who cannot generate enough on site may purchase them from a landowner who has created habitat elsewhere, or, as a last resort, buy credits from the government. The scheme has trading rules — an offset should generally be of the same habitat type as the loss, or better — and it has one conspicuous exception. A short list of habitats is designated irreplaceable, ancient woodland being the paradigm case. These are not priced at a very high rate. They are removed from the market altogether: their loss cannot be made good by units of any kind, and planning permission is refused rather than conditioned.

Two features of this strike me as odd, and I suspect they are the same oddity wearing different clothes.

The first is that the scheme is habitat-based and almost entirely species-blind. Species enter only as a modifier here and there. The species problem — that there is a long list of defensible species concepts, that different ones draw different boundaries and yield different counts, that the same organism can belong to several kinds depending on which concept one picks — is exactly the sort of thing that would wreck an accounting system, if the accounting system had to count species. This one does not. It counts hectares of habitat type instead. I want to know whether that is a solution to the species problem, an evasion of it, or an implicit bet that it was never a real problem.

The second is the exception. A scheme that will trade a mature oak wood for a newly planted one, with a discount for the century the new one will take to mature, has committed itself to the view that the two are commensurable. It then turns round and declares that ancient woodland is not commensurable with anything. Either the scheme's real axiology is lexicographic and the scalar arithmetic is a convenient fiction for the easy cases, or the exception is a piece of political cowardice tacked onto an otherwise consistent consequentialism. I should like to know which.

So: what does the practice presuppose? Please give me the response in the following sections, in this order and at roughly these lengths.

1. **Restatement** — about 150 words. State the practice back to me as a single object of analysis, correcting me if I have misdescribed anything that matters, and name precisely the two features that generate the philosophical pressure.

2. **The commitments it presupposes** — about 500 words. This is the core. What view of value does a tradeable biodiversity unit require? What does it presuppose about the measurability of biodiversity, about the commensurability of losses and gains of different kinds, about substitutability across space and across time, and about the separability of the measurement from the valuation? And what, if anything, does it presuppose about species — realism, conventionalism, or a studied neutrality? Be explicit about which commitments the scheme cannot do without, as opposed to those it merely happens to have.

3. **What it rules out, and what it merely fits** — about 300 words. Which rival views about the value of nature are excluded by the practice, and which are compatible with it? I have in mind thoroughgoing incommensurability, views on which species rather than habitats are the units of value, animal-welfare views on which a species-poor pasture full of contented animals might score well, and views on which value is place-bound and non-transferable. Say where the practice is genuinely underdetermined, such that several incompatible theories would license exactly the same spreadsheet.

4. **The exception, examined** — about 300 words. Treat the irreplaceable-habitat carve-out as philosophical evidence rather than as a political concession. What does it reveal about the scheme's actual axiology? Can the scalar arithmetic and the carve-out be reconciled, or does the carve-out show that the arithmetic was always a proxy for something non-scalar? Is this a consistent consequentialism with a threshold, or two incompatible theories stapled together?

5. **What would have to change** — about 300 words. Suppose we took the presupposition seriously and replaced it: with a species-based or phylogenetic accounting, say, or with a regime permitting no cross-type trading at all, or with one that abandons the compensatory framing and simply forbids certain losses outright. For each, say what the scheme would gain philosophically and what it would lose practically, and whether the result would still be recognisable as a system for permitting development.

6. **Verdict** — about 100 words. Your own view of which philosophical commitments the practice really presupposes, and which of the two oddities is the deeper one.

I should say that I am not after a reading list. I want the reconstruction and the argument, not a survey of who has said what; where you need a distinction, introduce it and name it yourself rather than gesturing at a literature I may not have read.
