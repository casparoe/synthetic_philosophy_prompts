I am a novelist by trade, and I also sit on the management committee of a small housing co-operative — forty-odd households, a shared garden, and a boiler older than most of the children. Our constitution is unremarkable: monthly general meetings, an agenda set by the chair, decisions by simple majority. Six months ago a new member, a retired schoolteacher named Ruth, began arguing that our way of doing things is hollow. Counting heads, she says, is not democracy; it is merely the arithmetic of preference. Real democratic governance, on her account, is a continuous exercise in shared inquiry: a group identifies a problem together, frames it, tries a small-scale answer, watches what happens, and revises. She now proposes that we stop voting on most matters and instead run our affairs as a series of experiments — pilot schemes and temporary rules with built-in review dates, decisions taken by those affected together with whoever has relevant experience — and she wants the boiler replacement, the rent review and the garden redesign all handled this way. Half the committee is exhilarated. The other half says this is a recipe for rule by the loudest and the most confident, that it quietly discards the one protection we all share, the equal vote, and that we have real deadlines. I am drawn to Ruth but uneasy, and I am chairing the next meeting, at which her proposal is the first item.

I would like you to act as a political philosopher whose specialism is democratic theory, and in particular the pragmatist tradition that treats inquiry as central to democratic life, and to advise me both on the substance of the disagreement and on how to run the meeting.

Before you answer, please think on paper for a short while: set out what is really at stake here, disentangle the several questions that are bundled together in the dispute, and note where the word 'democracy' is doing different work for each side. Then give me your advice, addressing in turn: (a) whether Ruth's conception of democracy is defensible, and what its most serious internal difficulties are; (b) what a thoughtful defender of her view should say to the objection that it undermines the equal vote and hands power to the articulate; (c) how that view would have us handle the awkward practical constraints — deadlines, expertise, and decisions that cannot be trialled in advance; and (d) how I, as chair, might run the meeting so that the disagreement is genuinely inquired into rather than merely won. Please be concrete: tell me what I should actually do at the meeting, and say plainly what Ruth is right about and where she overreaches. Avoid merely rehearsing stock positions; I want the reasoning brought to bear on this particular committee and this particular boiler.

Write your answer in British English, in continuous prose rather than bullet points, and assume that I have no formal training in philosophy.
