I have ended up defending the procreation asymmetry — the one usually associated with Benatar — but in a form that I suspect is doing more work than its standard statement, and I want to find out how much of it is load-bearing. The form I hold is this: the asymmetry is not a claim about the comparative value of existing and never existing (I don't think never existing is better than existing happily; there is no one for whom it could be better), but a claim about the structure of reasons and complaints. Suffering is bad unconditionally — its presence is bad, its absence is good, and the latter holds even when no one exists to enjoy the absence. Pleasure is good only conditionally — its presence is good, but its absence is bad only when there is someone who is deprived by it. So there is a reason not to bring into existence a person whose life would be net negative, and no corresponding reason to bring into existence a person whose life would be net positive, even when the magnitudes are equal. One friend says this is just the familiar four-part statement in different clothes; another says it is worse than that, because it makes me impersonal about the bad and person-affecting about the good, and a hybrid like that is either unstable or ad hoc. I would like to know what would actually count against the view in this form, and which of the objections I should treat as fatal rather than merely awkward.

Rank the defeaters for me, and be explicit about which ones a committed defender of the asymmetry would concede as counting against the view rather than dismiss as a change of subject. Four things I have wondered about and cannot settle on my own: first, whether the asymmetry can be stated as a ranking of outcomes at all, or whether it is irreducibly a claim about reasons, and what follows either way — I have heard that the impossibility results in population ethics bear on this, but I don't know the details and don't want them asserted unless they genuinely apply; second, whether the asymmetry survives being restated about the continuation of an existing life rather than about coming into existence — if it does, the view is far stronger than most of its defenders seem to want, and if it does not, I want to know whether the line at birth or at the onset of sentience is principled or merely convenient; third, whether the intuition is debunkable, say as an artefact of our being able to imagine our own suffering but not our own nonexistence, or of omission bias and loss aversion; and fourth, where the asymmetry's predictions come apart from those of a plain total view and a plain person-affecting view, and whether it wins any of those cases on anything other than intuition. I would be most moved by a demonstration that I cannot state the asymmetry without either an incoherent comparison with nonexistence or a commitment to impersonal goods that then swamps the person-affecting clause; failing that, by a showing that the form I hold is risk-aversion in value-theoretic costume.

Give me exactly five numbered items and nothing else — no introduction and no closing summary — each a short paragraph of two or three sentences, ordered from the defeater I should take most seriously to the one I should take least seriously. Let the fifth item be the verdict on whether the view as I have qualified it has been insulated against criticism, and whether that insulation is or is not a problem for a view of this kind.
