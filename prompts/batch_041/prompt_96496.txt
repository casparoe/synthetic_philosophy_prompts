I'm a novelist, four books in, and for about two years now I've been circling a project I can't quite start: a novel that runs backwards. It's not only a gimmick, though its a little bit a gimmick. The reason I want it is that I've talked myself into a view about time and I want the book to be true to it. Here's the view, as plainly as I can put it. The direction of time isn't a feature of the world. The fundamental laws don't care which way you run them — film two billiard balls colliding, play it in reverse, nothing in the physics objects. What objects is entropy: the rack scatters, the coffee mixes, the egg breaks, and never the other way. And the story I've absorbed is that what people call the thermodynamic arrow isn't a law at all, it's a consequence of two things: the sheer statistics of large numbers, since there are vastly more ways for a system to be mixed than unmixed, plus one brute fact about the early universe, that it started in a staggeringly low-entropy state. Some people call that the past hypothesis and, as far as I can tell, nobody has the faintest idea why it holds. If that's right, then the arrow of time is downstream of a single unexplained initial condition, time doesn't flow, before and after are just directions on a manifold, and my narrator — who remembers backwards and not forwards, who has scars but no premonitions — is in that position not because memory is deep but because her brain is a system that relaxes and leaves records behind it. I find this wierdly consoling and I want to build a hundred thousand words on it, which is exactly why I need you to try to break it.

Not with the usual objection — I've made my peace with the feeling that time passes, that's not the kind of thing I mean. I want a map of what the view is exposed to. Which parts are load-bearing? If the past hypothesis is just a posit, is this an explanation of the arrow or a relabeling of it? I keep half-hearing about records, something to the effect that a view like mine owes an account of why there are traces of the past and not the future, and I honestly can't tell if that's a problem for me or a selling point. A man at a party told me that if I take the statistical story seriously I should expect to be a Boltzmann brain and that this is fatal, though he also told me the same thing about everything else, so I'm unclear. My friend who did a physics degree said something about an asymmetry in particle physics that would, in his words, ruin my whole thing, and then changed the subject, and I've been quietly uneasy about it for months. And there's one I can't get past on my own: if all the asymmetry lives in a boundary condition, why is it the past one? Why doesn't the same reasoning hand you a future hypothesis just as easily — and if it doesn't, what's doing the work? Rank these from fatal to merely awkward, tell me which of them the view's own defenders would themselves concede are real costs as opposed to critics' talking points, and add the ones I haven't thought of, especially anything that would count as a decisive test rather than a vague worry. Then the honest question underneath all of it: has a view like this been qualified into unfalsifiability? I have a horrible suspicion that anything I observe, including the fact that I observe it, can be absorbed into statistics-plus-a-low-entropy-start, and if that's true I'd rather you said so plainly, and then told me whether it's actually a problem for a view of this kind or just the price of admission. I don't want a reading list, I'll take your word for the literature — I want the shape of the thing.
