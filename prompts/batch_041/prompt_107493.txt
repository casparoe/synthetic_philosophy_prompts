I'm a retired field geologist—spent most of my life mapping sedimentary basins, now doing a part-time MA in philosophy because I finally have the time and the library. Last Tuesday in a seminar on pain we got onto pain asymbolia, which I'd never heard of. As I understand it, it's a rare neurological condition where people still feel pain in the sense that they can say where it is and roughly how intense it is, but it doesn't bother them—the awfulness or the suffering is gone or greatly reduced. Some descriptions say they don't even flinch or guard the injured part; other descriptions say the withdrawal reflexes can still be there. Our instructor let us run with it, and two people in the class, Marta and Dev, got into a proper argument. I was just taking notes, but I definately couldn't tell who had the better of it.

Marta said: if you can have the sensation of pain without the suffering, then the badness of pain can't be intrinsic to the sensation itself. What makes pain bad is the second thing—the unpleasantness, the ouch quality, the way it grips you and makes you want it to stop. So asymbolia is a natural experiment: it shows pain and the badness of pain can come apart. Dev said that's too quick. He said pain isn't bad just because it feels bad; it's bad because it's a signal of bodily damage, a perceptual representation of something wrong with you. A smoke alarm isn't bad because its noise is unpleasant; it's bad because it's telling you there's a fire. If asymbolia patients still have the signal, then their pain is still bad for them, even if they're not bothered—maybe they're just missing the alarm's volume, not its content. Marta shot back that if they're not bothered and don't suffer, it's hard to see what the remaining badness consists in. She said Dev was smuggling in the idea that the signal is bad for the person independently of how it feels, but a signal is only bad for you if it moves you or matters to you in some way; otherwise it's just information. Dev said that can't be right either, because we can be wrong about what matters to us—someone can be so depressed or so brain-damaged that they don't mind a broken leg, but the leg is still broken and still bad for them. Marta said that's exactly the point: if you say the pain is bad for them whether or not it ever enters their experience as something to avoid, you're no longer talking about the badness of pain; you're talking about the badness of tissue damage, which is a different thing. Dev said fine, but then Marta's view makes the badness of pain entirely a matter of felt unpleasantness, and that seems to make it arbitrary—why should a certain raw feel be bad in itself? Marta said because that's just what unpleasantness is: a state we're built to be averse to. Dev said that just pushes it back to biology, and biology can be wrong.

They went a few more rounds like that. I think there was also something about whether a creature could have pain that it didn't mind and whether that would still be a reason to give it an aspirin, but I lost the thread. Can you adjudicate this for me move by move? I'd like to know which of their claims are right, which are confused, and what both of them are missing—especially about the difference between pain as a sensation, pain as a signal, and pain as something that matters to the creature that has it. Please don't give me a reading list; I'm not looking for citations, I just want the argument sorted out. And if there's a way to make the issue sharper with a thought experiment—say, a drug that leaves the sensory intensity but removes the unpleasantness, versus a creature that has the aversive pull but no bodily-damage signal—feel free to use it.
