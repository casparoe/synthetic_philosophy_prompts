Every protocol that goes out has to pass my sign-off, usually in twenty minutes on a weeknight, and I'm the one who then has to look the researcher in the eye and either approve it or explain why not. The part I keep getting stuck on is consent. What actually makes a consent valid? The cases that keep biting us:

- cover stories and deception (participants think its a memory task, it's really about cheating)
- incentives and dependency (we recruit from our own Discord, we pay people, sometimes we run studies with people who want to be in the group)
- competence (someone shows up drunk, or mid-crisis, or is just really young)
- consent to X when you only know Y -- broad consent, open-ended follow-ups, "we might contact you later about related studies"

Please write me a decision procedure I can actually run on a protocol: the questions in order, what counts as a good enough answer to each, where it stops with a hard no, and where it stops with "this is a values call, a human has to own it." Keep whatever the philosophy of consent actually says that survives being turned into a checklist -- the difference between consent as an inner state and consent as an act that changes whats permitted, whether deception only wrecks consent when the hidden fact is one a reasonable person would care about, whether paying someone can ever be coercive when they were already badly off, competence as a cliff versus a slope. Don't hand me a reading list, I can't fetch it.

We have an internal word, "consent-shaped," for a yes that ticks every box but that everyone involved knows wasn't really consent. The procedure has to catch those, and it has to be honest about which of its steps are just judgment calls wearing a lab coat.
