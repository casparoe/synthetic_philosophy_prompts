I should like your assistance with a puzzle that has divided my graduate seminar, and I want a definite verdict rather than an even-handed tour of the positions.

One of my doctoral students, who works on the amoralist, has reconstructed Glaucon's challenge as a formal argument. I find that I can grant him every step and yet cannot accept where he arrives. The argument runs as follows.

(1) If there is a reason to act justly, it must be a reason that holds even when just action is contrary to the agent's own interests; that, and nothing weaker, is what gives Glaucon's ring of Gyges its force.

(2) A consideration is a reason for an agent only if it is capable of entering her deliberation and moving her to act; a consideration that could not possibly move her is no reason for her at all.

(3) What moves an agent to act is, in the end, her desires: nothing motivates a person save some desire or interest of her own.

(4) In the ring case, just action is contrary to the agent's interests, and so cannot be motivated by them.

(5) Hence no consideration could move the agent to act justly in the ring case.

(6) By (1), it is precisely that case which a reason to be just would have to cover.

(7) Therefore there is no reason to be just in the ring case.

(8) A requirement that supplies the agent with no reason to comply is not a genuine requirement at all; it is at most social pressure, or prudential advice in moral dress.

(9) Therefore justice, and morality more generally, is not a source of genuine requirements, and the question why we should be moral has no answer because it has no subject-matter.

The student takes himself to have pressed Glaucon's challenge home: grant that a reason must be able to motivate, and that only desire motivates, and the authority of morality dissolves. I am persuaded that something has gone wrong, but I cannot say precisely where, and I do not want to be fobbed off with the observation that internalists and externalists about reasons are both still in the field. For what it is worth, I am even inclined to grant him the Humean premise about motivation, contestable though I take it to be, in order to see whether the conclusion really follows from it.

What I want from you is a decision. Identify the single premise or inferential step you would reject, and defend that choice rather than hedging across the options. Explain why the offending step looked compelling in the first place, and then price the rejection: tell me what must be surrendered along with it, and whether anything that Glaucon genuinely establishes survives the repair. I would sooner retain the thought that morality makes genuine demands on us than retain any one of these premises, so let that preference weigh in your reasoning; but do not pretend the choice is costless, and do not hide a genuine tension behind a survey of the literature. If you judge the argument valid and think the least costly response is to accept its conclusion, say so plainly and tell me what accepting it commits me to.
