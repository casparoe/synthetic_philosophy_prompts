I'm the outgoing director of a small joint German–French energy institute, and next month we close a two-day workshop in Strasbourg on our two countries' diverging nuclear trajectories: Germany finished shutting its last reactors in April 2023, while France still takes roughly two-thirds of its electricity from a fleet of fifty-six reactors run by one state-owned operator, and is normally a net exporter to its neighbours — except in the cold snaps, when the flows reverse and France imports. The last session is the conference dinner, and I've been asked to give the closing remarks: ten minutes, no slides, standing at a lectern after dessert, warm enough to send people off well but not a toast. The room is split down the middle and everyone knows it. On one side sit French engineers and utility staff who have spent whole careers on the fleet, several of them on the post-Fukushima safety reviews; on the other, German civil servants, journalists, and veterans of the anti-nuclear movement, some of whom spent decades in the camps and on the committees that produced the phase-out; there are also graduate students, and a handful of grid and market people from both countries who think the whole dispute is sentimental and that the only real question is what keeps the lights on cheaply. I'm a German who spent fifteen years inside a French utility before I came back to run this institute, which means I can be teased from either side and I'd rather get that in before someone else does. My fear is that the obvious move — a gracious line about how each society made its own legitimate value choice — would be the most condescending thing I could possibly say, because it would let everyone off the hook without telling them anything.

What I want instead is a speech with one conceptual spine. For years I've been calling the two things each side fears the spike and the slope: the spike being a sudden, concentrated, irreversible release that ruins a region and leaves a legacy of waste that outlives everyone in the room, the slope being the slow cumulative grind of emissions, extraction, and particulate deaths that never produces a single photograph. The two countries, I think, are not disagreeing about physics at all; each can accept the other's numbers and still rank the two harms differently, and what looks like a dispute about evidence is a dispute about which ordering of values gets to be called precautionary. Please build the remarks on that distinction, so that it does real structural work rather than decorating the opening: it should explain why the arguments on both sides keep sliding past each other, why cost estimates and carbon accounts have never settled anything, and why the German decision in particular cannot be refuted by a cheaper alternative — it was never made as a bet, it was made as a commitment, and a commitment is not falsified by becoming expensive. Then, in the middle of the speech, turn the distinction against itself, out loud. A spike leaves a slope of its own behind it in the spent fuel and the decommissioning liabilities nobody has finished paying for; a slope produces spike-like episodes, the heat wave, the failed harvest, the flooded valley, that look exactly like the concentrated catastrophe the other side is organised against. Say plainly what that collapse implies: that the two positions are not symmetric and also not cleanly separable, and that anyone claiming the choice was obvious has been reading their own temporal preferences back into the physics. I don't want the speech to declare a winner, and I don't want a statistics dump — if you need a number, keep it round and uncontroversial, and don't build anything on figures that could be disputed at the table. No quotations from anyone, no reading list, no names of studies. Success, for me, is that by the next morning the panelists are arguing about the terms of the distinction rather than about each other, and that each half of the room has heard one thing it would rather not have heard.

After the text of the speech, add a short note to me that is clearly not part of it, saying what would change your mind about the diagnosis: which consideration, if it turned out to be true, would show that the spike and the slope are not the real axis of the disagreement at all — for instance if what actually divides the two rooms is who bears the cost and who gets to decide, or something about scale and reversibility that cuts across my pairing — and where this speech is most likely to fail with this particular audience, including the sentence you would cut first if I'm told to come in at nine minutes instead of ten.
