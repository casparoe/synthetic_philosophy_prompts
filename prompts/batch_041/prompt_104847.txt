I've got a seminar to give in a few weeks and I need to prep for the Q&A rather than the talk itself.

The crowd: it's a staff college thing, so the room is mostly serving officers who teach the law of armed conflict, plus two or three academics who work on IHL, plus a just war philosopher or two who'll be there for the free wine. The officers are legal positivists about the LOAC in practice — for them the symmetry of the rules is what keeps the whole edifice standing — and the academics are mostly of the orthodox "combatants are morally equal" persuasion.

What I'm defending: the moral equality of combatants is true as a claim about permissions and false as a claim about liabilities. Two soldiers firing at each other are equally permitted to fire — but only the one fighting for the unjust side incurs a liability afterwards, at the level of reparations and punishment. So I seperate the in bello permissions from the post bellum liabilities, and I ground the equal permission not in the soldiers' ignorance of their cause or in the equal value of their lives, but in the fact that both are stuck inside the same coercive structure — neither can walk away — and what they're permitted to do is resist the threat the other poses, not kill as such.

And tell me the one I'm hoping nobody asks.
