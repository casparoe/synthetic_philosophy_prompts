I'm teaching a grad seminar on explanation in the social sciences, we just finished the Protestant ethic, and I've been having the same arguement with a colleague in the econ department for two months now, so I want to assign a dialogue as a teaching text. Could you write one between two people who are both genuinely good at this. One is a Weberian: she thinks a value commitment can be an autonomous cause of economic change, not a decorative rationalization layered on top of interests, and that the materialist story is missing something real. The other is a materialist: he thinks "culture did it" is either economics in disguise or a reification of a pile of individual attitudes, and that values are usually downstream of material conditions. Neither of them is a dope, neither is a strawman, and they have to actually answer each other's points rather than deliver dueling monologues. I'd like the materialist to have some genuinely strong moves in him, and the Weberian to concede where the ground is soft without folding.

To keep this from turning into the usual Weber-101 back and forth, anchor it in a concrete case. Here's the one I use in lecture (it's made up, but the shape of it is realistic): in two adjacent river-valley districts of the same country, a pietist revival took hold in one and not the other around 1890; the movement preached thrift, sobriety and deferred gratification, and over the next thirty years the district with the revival pulled ahead on savings, small-business formation and school attendance, even though the two districts had the same soil, same crops, same distance to the railhead, and the same confessional mix before the revival. I want them to fight about what that case can and can't show. Is the causal work done by the beliefs themselves, or by the institutions the revival left behind (a mutual aid fund, a lending co-op, a network of congregational schools), or by the selection of who joined? Push them on the deeper question too: whether it even makes sense to call a value a cause when the value has to be stored in individual heads and transmitted somehow, and what a real test would look like. If one of them wants to float the idea that the values mattered mainly as a coordination device — a shared way of knowing what everyone else would do — let that come in and let the other one push back on it. End with them agreeing on one thing they'd need to find out and disagreeing about what it would mean if they found it out. Don't have them cite literature at each other, just argue.
