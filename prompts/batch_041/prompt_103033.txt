For a piece I'm writing on the science of the self, I've been trying to pin down my own view of when a single body houses one mind and when it houses two, and I keep tripping over my own verdicts. I'd like you to tell me what principle, if any, unites them, and where it forces me to give one up.

Here is what I find myself thinking, case by case. Take the classic surgical case: a patient whose cerebral hemispheres have been disconnected, shown two different images at once, so that one hand reaches for one object while the other hand reaches for another, and the speaking hemisphere denies any knowledge of what the other is doing. I want to say this is one person with two streams of experience, not two minds. Now imagine the same disconnection in someone whose right hemisphere also had language, so that each hemisphere could hold its own conversation, keep its own secrets, and make its own plans: here I want to say two minds sharing a body. A child who loses a hemisphere in infancy and grows up with only one seems to me to have one mind, and nothing about that is puzzling.

Then there is the case that unsettles me. Suppose two people were joined not at the skull but at the brain, by a wide bridge of tissue rich enough that each could feel the other's sensations, recall the other's memories, and finish the other's thoughts. Their two streams would be as tightly interwoven as the two hemispheres of an ordinary brain. I still want to say there are two minds there, and I cannot give a principled reason for it. I have also read that people born without a corpus callosum, the very bridge the surgeons cut, often seem to live single, unified mental lives, which is the opposite of what the surgical cases would lead me to expect. Meanwhile, a person whose hand acts on its own, reaching for things she does not want and experiences as alien, still seems to me to be one mind, however unruly one of its parts; and if a normal brain were briefly silenced on one side, with one hemisphere asleep while the other stayed awake, I would still say one mind, merely running on reduced hardware.

So I seem to hold both that two streams can belong to a single mind and that fully interwoven streams can belong to two. I suspect I cannot keep both. My own tentative principle is something like this: a mind is individuated by the number of distinct centers of first-person perspective, each capable of taking itself as a subject. But I do not know whether that saves the verdicts above or only sounds as though it does.

What I want from you is a principle, or a short list of candidate principles, that captures as many of these verdicts as possible. What is being counted, exactly, when we count minds? The number of streams of experience? The number of centers of agency? The number of subjects who can think "I"? Something about the capacity for unified deliberation, or about the boundary of a single autobiographical narrative? For each clause you propose, tell me which case forces it. Then tell me what the principle predicts about cases I have not listed, for instance a patient split only in a small commissure, a person whose two hemispheres can communicate only through a slow external channel such as typing, a creature with one brain and two bodies, or a system whose hemispheres are joined by an artificial bridge that carries only emotion and not information. Finally, be honest about which of my verdicts the principle cannot save, and whether the better response is to drop the verdict or to complicate the principle.
