As research for a novel whose protagonist is a constructivist mathematician, I need to render a philosophical dispute faithfully rather than atmospherically, and I would rather it not collapse into "one of them likes classical logic and the other does not." Please compare two ways of grounding the constructive refusal of the law of excluded middle. On the first, the refusal follows from a conception of mathematical truth itself: a statement counts as true only when a construction establishing it has been or can be carried out, so that the disjunction cannot be affirmed in general because for some statements we possess neither a construction of the statement nor one of its absurdity. On the second, the refusal follows instead from a theory of meaning for the logical constants, on which each connective is fixed by what counts as a proof of a sentence built with it, so that the law fails for reasons internal to the semantics of negation and disjunction rather than to any thesis about mathematical objects. Which of the two does the better job of explaining a mathematician's refusal to assert the disjunction in a case that is decidable in principle but has not in fact been decided, and at what points do the two readings license genuinely different mathematical or philosophical commitments? Close by saying whether a working mathematician could hold either of these positions without implicitly holding the other, and if not, which one is doing the real work.
