I recently moved from academic philosophy into a communications post at a national statistical agency, and the transition has made Arendt's writing on political lying feel less like a period piece than like a description of my working environment. The pressure is rarely to assert something flatly false; it is to keep the official story internally consistent, to sand down whatever complicates it, and to make the correction of a misleading figure feel like a breach of etiquette. I would like your honest credence in a single Arendtian claim — stated so that it could be true or false — together with the reasoning decomposed.

The claim is this: that the distinctive harm of sustained, organized political lying is done to the shared factual world as such — to the possibility of a common reality that people can jointly appeal to — rather than to the particular false beliefs the lies install. Put crudely, that the technique matters more than the content, and that the damage outlives the exposure of any individual lie. My own credence sits around 0.7, though I suspect it is inflated by the fact that I now watch the technique from the inside; a colleague who studies propaganda puts the same claim near 0.3 and thinks I have mistaken a vivid description for a mechanism. Give me your own number, and tell me where you think the disagreement between the two of us most plausibly lives.

Then decompose it. I would like at least the following sub-questions, each with its own rough probability and a line or two of justification, followed by how you combine them into the final figure:

1. Whether the harm is better located in the content of the lies (people end up believing false things) or in the technique (consistency, repetition, the construction of an alternative world), and how much the Arendtian thesis depends on the latter being primary.
2. Whether the harm is asymmetric — whether a degraded shared world can be restored by later fact-checking, or whether it is a ratchet.
3. Whether the operative mechanism is epistemic (the loss of particular beliefs), testimonial (the erosion of the standing practice of treating others' assertions as reasons), or phenomenological (the loss of a world in which appearances can be trusted). A colleague who works on the epistemology of testimony presses the second of these on me: his suggestion is that what sustained lying kills is not this or that belief but the very practice of relying on another's word, so that even accurate reporting afterward arrives at a public that no longer knows how to receive it. Treat that as one candidate mechanism, not as a foregone conclusion.
4. Whether Arendt's claim is a determinate thesis at all, or a piece of rhetoric whose apparent content dissolves under pressure — and, if it is determinate, what would count as confirming or refuting it.
5. Whether contemporary conditions (continuous news cycles, algorithmic amplification, the sheer volume of competing narratives) change the phenomenon in kind rather than degree.

After the decomposition, tell me which sub-question your final number is most sensitive to, which one you are least confident about, and what single piece of evidence or argument would move the number most. I am not looking for the standard exegesis of Arendt; I want the weakest joint in the reasoning marked explicitly.
