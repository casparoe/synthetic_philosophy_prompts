I am a fifth-year PhD student in philosophy, working on the foundations of expected utility, and I sit as one of the two graduate-student members of my university's research ethics board. The board votes on Thursday on a protocol from the behavioural economics group, and I have to circulate a short written opinion beforehand.

The study concerns how people price gambles whose expectation is undefined, and the researchers insist it needs real stakes to be credible. Participants make a series of choices; one choice is drawn at random and played for real money. Their preferred design uses what they call a Pasadena game: a fair coin is flipped until it first lands heads, and if that happens on flip n the participant wins 2^n/n dollars when n is odd and loses 2^n/n dollars when n is even. Our analyst's note observes that the probability-weighted payoffs are just the alternating harmonic terms, so the series summed in its natural order comes to roughly seventy cents, while any rearrangement of the same outcomes can be made to converge to any real number at all, or to diverge; the sum of the absolute values diverges, so there is no expected value in the ordinary sense. The researchers offer a fallback in which every payoff is positive, 2^n/n for a first heads on flip n, which they concede has infinite expected value.

Our standing policy is written entirely in expected-value terms: compensation must be proportionate to the time and burden of participation and must be prorated for participants who withdraw early; no payment may exceed a set hourly ceiling; and no participant may bear financial risk above a set cap. Applied to this protocol the first two rules cannot be applied at all, and the third is violated outright, since the loss on any even n is unbounded. Two colleagues have proposed the obvious repair, capping the loss at the ceiling, but the analyst's note shows that capping the downside makes the expected payment infinite in the participant's favour, while capping the upside makes it infinite against the participant, so the laboratory's exposure becomes either unlimited or indeterminate in either direction; the lab's compensation budget is fixed by its grant, so a game with unbounded expected payout could not be honoured in practice. Half the board wants to reject the protocol as unapprovable in principle; the other half wants to approve it on the ground that our mandate is the protection of participants, not the resolution of a puzzle about expectation, and that the study is valuable precisely because the phenomenon is unresolved. The researchers argue, not unreasonably, that switching to hypothetical payments would make their results uninterpretable.

Please draft the opinion I will circulate: a clear recommendation (approve, reject, or approve with conditions), a concrete timetable, the reasoning the board can actually act on, and — since I expect the vote to be close — a fair statement of the case on the other side. Say explicitly what should take the place of the expected-value test in cases like this, and if your recommendation requires amending the standing policy rather than only ruling on this protocol, give me the amendment language as well.
