John Stuart Mill spent his final years in an unusual posture toward socialism: without renouncing his commitment to individual liberty, he came to treat the existing wage-labor system as provisional, to champion worker cooperatives and profit-sharing, and to argue that the "unearned increment" in land values — the rise in land's worth attributable to social development rather than to any owner's effort — ought to be captured for public purposes. Imagine that this late Mill, at the close of his life, could somehow observe the political economy of the early twenty-first century.

Consider a society in which automation and machine learning have displaced a large share of human labor, so that an increasing portion of income flows to the owners of capital and of trained models while wages for many workers stagnate. Three reforms are on the table: (1) a universal basic income funded substantially by taxes on the unearned increment of land value and on the data and network effects from which large firms profit; (2) a legal mandate that firms above a certain size be jointly owned and governed by their workers; and (3) the creation of a "digital commons," in which knowledge, culture, and the parameters of publicly funded models are shared freely rather than enclosed as private property.

Write an essay defending a definite verdict on the following question: On which of these three reforms would the late Mill most likely have been an enthusiast, on which ambivalent, and on which opposed — and would he have judged the package as a whole a genuine step toward the cooperative order he anticipated, or as a novel form of the despotism he feared?

In arguing your case, reconstruct Mill's stance from his actual commitments rather than from a generic image of nineteenth-century liberalism. You should engage at least the following: his conception of the unearned increment and the grounds on which he thought land ownership differed from other property; his view of cooperation as the likely successor to the wage relation; his insistence on individuality and "experiments in living," and the related suspicion that any comprehensive system might impose a stifling uniformity; and the possibility that he regarded a general socialist reorganization as premature given the character and education of the working population of his day. Give weight to the strongest objection to your reading of Mill — including the objection that he would have balked at compulsory worker ownership or at a guaranteed income detached from labor — and answer it rather than merely noting it. Address, concretely, what he would predict about incentives and about the formation of character under each reform.

Do not end on a non-committal note; commit to a reading and defend it, indicating where your reconstruction rests on extrapolation rather than on anything Mill stated outright. Write in American English.
