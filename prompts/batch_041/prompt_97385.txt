I'm a TA for a philosophy of social science course, and the week on Popper's critique of historicism keeps landing flat -- the students nod along at the thesis that there can't be laws of historical development of the kind that would license large-scale prophecy, but they don't feel the stakes of it. I want to try pairing it with a novel instead of another paper, and I've been rereading Asimov's Foundation books (the first trilogy mainly, though I'll take anything from the whole series) and it seems almost designed for this: here's a whole civilization resting on "psychohistory," a science that claims to derive the long-run behavior of vast populations from statistical laws and to issue precise, dated predictions -- the Seldon Plan, those recorded messages that play at each crisis and tell the Foundation what is about to happen and what to do about it. It's the historicist dream turned into a plot device.

What I want from you is a reading of the Foundation stories through Popper's Poverty of Historicism. Where does psychohistory look like exactly the thing he's attacking -- the confusion of trends with genuine laws, the claim to prophecy, the treatment of individual agency as mere noise to be averaged out, the whole vision of a managed future? And where does Asimov seem to see the objection and actually dramatize it? The Mule is obviously the hinge: a single unpredictable individual who breaks the plan, which is almost definately Popper's point about the impossibility of predicting the growth of knowledge and the unpredictability of human creativity, dressed up as a mutant. But I don't want the lazy reading that just says "the Mule refutes historicism." The Second Foundation exists precisely to absorb and correct such deviations, so the series may be smuggling historicism back in at a deeper level -- a hidden elite steering the masses, which is a Popperian nightmare in its own right. And there's the Oedipus-effect angle: does the plan work because it's true, or because people who believe it act so as to fulfill it, or only because its details are kept from the people living inside it?

So take both the books and Popper seriously. Tell me which scenes I should put in front of the class and why, where the analogy is genuinely illuminating and where I'd be distorting either Asimov or Popper to force it, whether the series ends up vindicating historicism, refuting it, or quietly trading on both at once, and -- this is the part I care about -- whether the fiction dramatizes something about historical prediction that Popper's argument, taken as a bare argument, can't quite get across to a room of undergrads. Throw in the two or three objections a student will inevitably raise ("it's just a story with a mutant in it," "the Second Foundation is a deus ex machina") and how I should handle them. No need to quote anything or send me to secondary literature; I can't assign more reading anyway.
