I got into a long arguement on a Discord server (the one that spun off from a rationalist meetup, so you know the vibe) about whether we should do anything about wild animal suffering. Someone said we shouldn't intervene, because predation and disease and all that are 'for the good of the species' -- it keeps populations healthy, weeds out the sick and the slow, and anyway that's how things evolved to be. I said something about individual animals having bad lives and got told I was thinking at the wrong level, that nature optimizes for the species, not for the individual. Then in the same week, in a completely seperate thread about whether group selection is real in humans, someone else waved off an argument about cultural group selection with basically the same line: oh, that's just the good of the species talking, which we all know got debunked in the 70s. So I've got one phrase doing work in two very different fights and I want to know if it's even the same phrase.

I've read enough of the gene's-eye-view stuff to feel like it's doing about four jobs at once, and I can't tell which one is load-bearing. Maybe it means a trait exists *because* it was selected for at the level of the species -- groups with the trait outcompeted groups without it. Or maybe it means the trait happens to benefit the species even though it evolved for some other reason, which would be a coincidence rather than an explanation. Or maybe it means species are the kind of thing that can be a unit of selection at all, with their own fitness and their own adaptations, which is a much bigger claim. Or maybe it's a bookkeeping claim, that when you add up everyone's interests the species comes out ahead, which sounds like it's about welfare and not evolution at all. And I have a nagging suspicion there's a boring reading where it's true by definition and a reading where it's the old group-benefit teleology everyone says is dead, and the two get swapped mid-argument when it's convenient. What I want: lay out the distinct precise claims that 'for the good of the species' could be making, from the trivial to the radical, each with a one-line verdict -- platitude, substantive but defensible, or just false -- and then tell me which reading is the one people are actually leaning on when they use it to shut down a conversation about intervening in nature, and whether that reading can actually do the work they want it to do. Same for the group-selection thread, if it turns out to be a different reading. Numbered list, hard cap 800 words.
