Patent offices have this weird double practice around laws of nature. They won't grant a patent on a perpetual motion machine — an examiner can call the thing inoperative and even demand a working model before really examining it, on the grounds that it can't work because it would violate the laws of nature. But the same office also refuses patents on laws of nature themselves, on the grounds that a law is discovered rather than invented, so you can patent a new use of a law but never the law. What does that pair of practices presuppose about what a law of nature is?

Heres my puzzlement. The office grants patents on all sorts of gadgets that later turn out not to work, and basically every law we've got has been revised or replaced at some point. So when an examiner says the machine can't possibly work because a law forbids it, what is he actually comitted to? If laws are just compact summaries of what happens to have happened (I think this is the Humean picture?), the reasoning looks circular: if the machine ran, then the summary was wrong, not violated, so the office is just betting on the past rather than ruling something out. But if laws are the things that make events go the way they do, and constrain what's possible in the first place, then violating a law really does mean impossible, and the practice makes sense as stated.

So reconstruct the implicit theory of laws the practice needs. Say which parts of the practice strictly require that theory and which parts would survive fine on the summary view, and what the office would have to say instead about perpetual motion if we swapped the other view in. Also: does the discovery/invention line — the reason you can't patent a law itself — need the same metaphysics, or a different one?
