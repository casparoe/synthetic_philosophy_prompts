I'm the volunteer treasurer of a small nonprofit and I also sit on my town's capital projects committee, so a few times a year I'm in a room voting on whether to keep paying for something that's already half-built, over budget and unpopular. The words 'sunk cost' get thrown around at our meetings but they're useless in practice, because the people who want to keep going always have real arguments — we'd be throwing away what we've spent, we'd look incompetent, the contractor would walk, and half the money is committed for next year anyway. What set me off: at our fundraiser we auctioned a mystery box where the top two bidders both had to pay — winner gets the box, runner-up gets nothing — and two people bid each other up for ages until the winner paid about five times what the box was worth and the loser paid nearly as much for nothing. Watching them, it's definately the same shape as our pool fight, just faster.

Act as an expert on decision-making under commitment problems — someone who actually knows the theory of escalation of commitment and of auctions where the losers also pay — and help me build a procedure I can run live in a 45-minute meeting with no spreadsheet and no analyst. Think it through step by step, and start by brainstorming the different ways continuing can be genuinely rational versus just stubborn, because I suspect I've been swinging 'sunk cost' like a hammer and sometimes voting to kill things that were worth finishing.

What I want: a short ordered list of questions to ask about any 'should we keep funding this' proposal, what a good answer to each looks like, and where the list stops with a no; an entry test for before a project starts or before I raise my own paddle, since I suspect most of the damage is done at that moment; and the places where the procedure hands the decision to a human because it's politics or values rather than arithmetic. Then run it on three cases: the pool (70% done, 40% over budget, genuinely worth having when finished, but we've cut everything else to pay for it), a charity-auction bidding war over a weekend cabin stay where both top bidders pay, and our org's half-finished custom donor database where the vendor keeps asking for one more payment.

Two things it has to handle honestly. Sometimes continuing really is right and the mistake was upstream, so I don't want a rule that mechanically says quit. And sometimes being known as someone who never backs down is worth real money, so I don't want to be talked out of that either. Tell me what the procedure can't do, and where it'll give me an answer I won't like.
