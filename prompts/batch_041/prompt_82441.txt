Next spring I have ninety minutes with a group I have taught before — graduate students in ecology and evolution, most of whom use the word function a dozen times a day and none of whom believe there is anything in it to argue about. I want those ninety minutes spent on the split between the etiological, or selected-effects, account of biological function and the causal-role account, and I want a teaching plan rather than an exposition: what has to be in place before either account can be stated at all without sounding like theology on one side or a change of subject on the other, the order the pieces have to come in and what depends on what, and where the session has to stop. My main worry is that the students will hear a quarrel about words, so the plan has to make the two accounts come apart on a single case they can hold in their heads; I would rather not use the heart again, and I would rather you choose the case than choose it myself, but tell me what it has to have and what it must not have, and in particular whether it can be a case in which one account simply wins, since I want the students to feel what each account costs. Keep three questions visibly distinct the whole way through: what the trait is for, why it is there, and what it now does. The students collapse these into one, and I think that collapse is what makes the dispute look verbal; the sequence should be built so that the collapse becomes visible as a mistake rather than being announced as one. Tell me the misconceptions you expect in the order they will actually surface and the precise point in the sequence where each should be headed off — I am expecting them to say that function just means what a trait does, that selection is nature's intention, that vestigial means functionless, and that a trait with several functions shows the question was never well posed — but say which of those I am wrong about and what I have left out. End with one question I can put to the room that will show me whether they have the distinction rather than the vocabulary. I am tempted to close by asking whether medicine's talk of dysfunction quietly commits it to the etiological account; tell me whether that is the thing to cut, and what else to leave out at this level and to say so out loud. Whiteboard only, no slides.

Write the plan as continuous prose — no headings, no numbered or bulleted lists, no bold, no tables — the way you would write it to a colleague who has to run the same session next week.
