I'm a novelist — three books, working on a fourth — and I've got a craft problem that I'm starting to suspect is really a philosophy-of-language problem wearing a costume. I'm hoping you can tell me whether the thing I keep bumping into has a name.

Here's the pattern. When I write dialogue, some of what a character gets across rides on the content of what they say, and some of it rides on the fact that they said it that way instead of some other way. And those two behave very differently when the character tries to take them back, which mine do constantly, because I write people who are always half-apologizing.

The first kind comes off clean. A character says "some of the money is gone," and the reader hears: not all of it. If I have her add "not that I'm saying the rest is safe," the reader adjusts and the extra thing is gone. I can even build this kind out of nothing she says — someone asks "were you with him on Tuesday?" and the answer is "I was with him on Wednesday," and the reader hears a denial nobody made. Then "I'm not saying I wasn't with him Tuesday" and it evaporates.

The second kind won't come off with a denial, but it does come off if I change a single word. "She's broke but generous" versus "she's broke and generous" — the raised eyebrow is entirely in the "but." If she follows up with "I'm not implying there's anything strange about a broke person being generous," the eyebrow stays exactly where it was; the denial just makes it more visible. Swap "but" for "and," though, and it's gone. Which is definately not how I was taught to think about subtext — this thing seems to live in the word, not in her intent.

The third kind won't come off either way, and it's the one that costs me scenes. A character says "I've stopped taking them." The reader now knows she was taking them, and it's not a hint or a suggestion, it's just floor. "I'm not saying I ever took them" isn't a take-back, it's incoherent. And weirdly, "I haven't stopped taking them" keeps it too — the thing survives being denied outright. Unlike the "but" case, no word swap fixes it. The only way out is to rewrite the line so the thing becomes something she actually asserts: "I used to take them, and now I don't." And then it's out front where she can deny it. So the same content moves from un-deniable to deniable just by being promoted from background to main point, which I don't fully understand.

I have a vague sense Grice is somewhere in this neighborhood, and a linguist freind of mine kept using a word starting with p that I've since forgotten. But I don't want a label, I want the actual carving. Are these three genuinely different kinds, or have I chopped one thing into three? Where exactly does the first kind end and the second begin — is that a difference in kind or just in how gracefully the take-back lands? And what do I do with a line that reads like one kind in one scene and another kind in the next?

Give it to me as a numbered list of exactly five items, a sentence or two each, no intro and no wrap-up. If nothing established fits, coin the term yourself and define it.
