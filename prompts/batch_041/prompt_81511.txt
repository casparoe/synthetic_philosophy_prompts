Hey, I'm a second-year PhD student in philosophy (mostly phil of mind, but I'm TAing a huge intro course this term and prepping comps on top of it), and I've got a handout idea I can't get to work by myself. The topic is the cogito, but not the boring is-Descartes-right-about-his-existence thing. What I actually want is the diagnostic question underneath it: once you grant the one thing you basically can't doubt — that while you're thinking, thinking is going on — what does that hand you? I want to give students a decision tree that walks them from that single starting commitment to a named position, so they discover what they actually think rather than reciting that the meditator proves he exists. Root node is that commitment; every other node has to be phrased as a question a student can answer without already knowing which leaf it leads to, and the branches have to be labeled by the claim that does the separating, not by the names of the views. Leaves should carry the name of the position (or unnamed if nobody defends it) plus one sentence on its most awkward commitment. I want it built from the logical space first and the actual positions second, so work out the combinations of answers the questions allow and then tell me which ones nobody occupies and whether that's because the view is incoherent, unattractive, or just unexplored.

Here's the angle I care about, since otherwise this collapses into an encyclopedia entry. I want the tree to come apart over (a) whether thinking-occurs entails a thinker at all, or whether you can have thoughts with no owner, (b) if there is an owner, whether it's a persisting thing that's there during dreamless sleep or only a thin subject that exists just for the duration of the episode, (c) what kind of thing the owner is — a mental substance, the human animal, or neither, just a formal condition that experience has to meet, and (d) whether what you get is first-personal and indexical, holding only for whoever is currently doing the doubting, or a general claim about minds. Please make sure these leaves show up somewhere: the Cartesian mental-substance reading; the Kantian transcendental-condition reading; the Hume/Parfit bundle with psychological continuity but no owner; the no-ownership view where there are thoughts but no thinker; animalism (the thing that thinks is the human animal); a minimal or momentary thin-subject view; and the deflationary reading where the whole thing yields a tautology or a performative and nothing else. If you can fit a Wittgenstein-ish grammatical reading in too, great, but don't force it. Eight leaves max, and if any two of them can't actually be separated by your questions, say so out loud instead of papering over it.

A few cases I want the questions to run through, because they're what my students get stuck on: waking up after dreamless sleep and the thought resuming, split-brain patients, the possibility of two simultaneous thoughts, and the weird case of a thought that nobody thinks. Don't give me citations or a reading list — I'll do the history and the references myself, positions by name are plenty. And honestly if the honest answer is that two of my requested leaves are the same view wearing different hats, tell me that instead of inventing a distinction to seperate them.
