I'm in a third-year seminar that's half philosophy of mind and half animal ethics, and we've spent the last three weeks on insects. The discussion has definately stalled and I'm trying to work out what kind of disagreement we're actually having, because it feels like our prof (philosophy of mind) and the TA (ethics) are having two different conversations and neither notices.

The setup. Team A: there are something like 10^18 individual insects alive at any moment (I might be off by an order of magnitude, but it's absurdly many), and nobody knows whether any of them are conscious. Even if the chance for any one insect is minuscule -- one in a million, one in a billion -- the expected number of sentient beings is still huge, so the expected suffering is huge, and insect welfare should swamp almost everything else we care about. Team B fires back on several fronts: nobody has a calibrated credence for "this ant is conscious," so you can't just multiply a number you made up by another number you made up; the multiplication treats each insect as an independent coin flip when it obviously isn't (if sentience shows up in a lineage, it shows up in all of them, and if it's a threshold property then what matters is the probability that *at least one* is sentient, which saturates at 1 and makes the big number do no work at all); if sentience is a matter of degree rather than yes/no, the whole product is a category error; and anyway this is just Pascal's mugging with antennae. Neither side has budged an inch.

My own guess -- and I want you to tell me if it's wrong -- is that this isn't a disagreement about insect neuroscience at all, since both sides agree it's uncertain, and it isn't about whether suffering matters. I think it's hiding a formal question about which model of sentience each side is implicitly running (threshold vs. graded, independent vs. correlated across individuals) plus a decision-theoretic one about whether expected value is even the right thing to compute when the probabilities are this inscrutable.

So: diagnose it. Is this a verbal dispute about "probability," two groups answering different questions under one heading, a hidden empirical or formal question, a clash about standards of evidence, or bedrock intuitions about whether vast numbers can dominate tiny chances? And given your diagnosis, what would actually count as progress -- a distinction, a model, a formal result, a change of question -- or should we just stop expecting any?
