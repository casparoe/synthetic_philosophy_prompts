In March 1964, Kitty Genovese was attacked and killed outside her apartment building in Kew Gardens, Queens. What turned the killing into a byword was a newspaper report that dozens of her neighbors had seen or heard the attack and done nothing — a report that later scholarship has substantially qualified, though the case's hold on the public imagination, and the research program on bystander behavior it helped launch, have not faded. I want you to set the social psychology aside and treat the episode, as it is popularly remembered, as a test case for the ethics of supererogation.

The framework is this. Supererogation is the category of acts that are morally good but not required — the heroism or saintliness that goes beyond duty, where the rest of us are permitted to stop. The category is contested. Some philosophers treat it as indispensable to any morality that leaves room for ordinary life; others suspect it is unstable, on the grounds that if an act is better than every available alternative it is hard to see how it could be optional; and some consequentialists deny it outright, insisting that when the good at stake is large enough, doing it is simply required. The law mirrors the disagreement: Anglo-American common law has generally refused to impose a duty to rescue a stranger, while several continental systems impose one where the rescue is easy and the risk small.

I want a disciplined analysis that runs in both directions. First, the theory onto the case: on the assumption that the neighbors heard and could have intervened at little risk to themselves, does the supererogation framework say their inaction was wrong, or only that it fell short of the heroic? Be careful about what the framework actually licenses. A great deal turns on whether there is a duty of easy rescue, on how costly the rescue really would have been, and on whether the neighbors' failure was a failure of nerve rather than a considered judgment about what was required. Second, the case onto the theory: does the episode, as popularly remembered, tell for or against the view that supererogation is a genuine and stable moral category? I am especially interested in whether it exposes a problem with the way the line is usually drawn — the suspicion that once we grant that a rescue was possible at little cost, the supererogatory label collapses into an ordinary duty, so that the inaction was not merely unheroic but wrong; and, on the other side, whether a morality that made the rescue obligatory would be intolerably demanding, so that the category is doing real work after all.

Take a definite position and defend it. Say where the framework is genuinely silent rather than forcing it to speak, and do not let the answer drift into the causes of bystander behavior; the question is normative, about what the situation required, not explanatory. Do not hand me a survey of positions with the verdict left to me. Write in continuous prose, without headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, the way you would write a letter to a colleague, and argue from the ideas themselves rather than sending me to a reading list.
