I belong to a working group on animal and machine cognition, and we keep ending arguments with a slogan: ontogeny recapitulates phylogeny. It has become the group's all-purpose reason for thinking that nothing arrives late without having arrived early, and I want a careful inventory of what the sentence can actually mean before I let it keep doing that work. My suspicion is that the readings that are true are too weak to settle anything, and that the readings which would settle something are either false or are not really about recapitulation at all.

Some background, so that the inventory is anchored in something concrete. We have spent the term on the four grades of mind that Dennett popularized. Grade one: a creature whose behavioural policies are fixed by natural selection across generations, so that it arrives with its policies built in and cannot revise them. Grade two: a creature that can also revise its policies within its own lifetime, by reinforcement, keeping what works. Grade three: a creature that can run the trials off-line, generating candidate hypotheses internally and discarding the bad ones in imagination rather than in action, so that its mistakes cost it nothing. Grade four: a mind equipped with culturally transmitted tools -- words, numerals, notations, and the practices that go with them -- so that its off-line trials are structured by equipment it did not invent, cannot fully inspect, and can hand on.

Two episodes prompted this. First, someone in the group argued that a language model cannot be a grade-three reasoner at all, because it never passed through grade two. It never had to learn by consequence, in a body, with the costs falling on it; so whatever it does when it generates and rejects candidate answers is not the off-line version of a selection process it has actually run. Grade three, on this view, is grade two moved indoors, and one cannot have the indoor version of a process one has never run outdoors. The slogan was offered as the general law behind the inference: no skipping. Second, at dinner afterwards, a friend who teaches primary school made the same move about children -- you must drill them on getting answers right before you can teach them to reason hypothetically -- and cited the slogan again.

What I want is the following.

First, a numbered menu of the distinct, precise claims the slogan could be making, ordered from the near-vacuous to the strong, and stated so that each item is either clearly true or clearly false rather than blurry. I expect the useful distinctions to run along at least three axes -- whether the claim concerns an individual's development or a lineage's history; whether it asserts a temporal order of appearance or a relation of dependence; and whether it asserts a law or merely reports a contingent sequence -- and I would like the menu to make clear where each reading falls on all three. Somewhere in the list I want the reading under which the slogan is barely distinguishable from the platitude that later and more complex capacities are assembled out of earlier ones, and the reading under which it is the much stronger claim that the earlier stage is a necessary precondition of the later rather than merely its ancestor.

Second, for each reading: whether it is trivial, substantive and defensible, dubious, or false; what sort of evidence would bear on it; and whether it is a claim about minds at all or only about bodies. The slogan began as a summary of a nineteenth-century embryological law that was abandoned in its literal form, and I want to know what survives of it, and what is merely carried along by the metaphor, when it is transplanted from embryos to minds.

Third, the part I actually need: for each of the two episodes, which reading of the slogan would license the inference being drawn, and whether any reading does. If the grade-three argument survives in some form, say what its strongest version is once the slogan is replaced by the premise really doing the work -- I suspect that premise is a claim about scaffolding rather than about recapitulation, and that the word "recapitulates" is decoration. If the argument does not survive, say which of its premises fails and whether a weaker conclusion about language models, or about children, still goes through.

Finally, tell me which reading the people who repeat this slogan almost certainly mean, and whether they are entitled to it. Please keep the whole thing as a numbered list I can take to the group, with the verdicts attached to each item rather than gathered at the end.
