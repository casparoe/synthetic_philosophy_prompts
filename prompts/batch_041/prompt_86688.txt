I am a staff software engineer at a mid-sized technology company, and in two weeks I will deliver the opening remarks — roughly fifteen minutes, first item on the agenda — at our two-day internal engineering summit. This year the theme is the AI coding assistant we have been piloting since spring, and the organizers have asked me to frame the two days: to explain why the question of what this tool is doing to how we think deserves our attention, without turning it into another generic talk about AI changing everything.

I have no formal training in philosophy. Some years ago I read a popular book on the evolution of minds and I have been rereading it in the evenings; the part I keep returning to is its four-rung ladder. Darwinian creatures are wired by natural selection. Skinnerian creatures can learn from reinforcement, from what happens to them. Popperian creatures can try a hypothesis inside themselves and let the hypothesis, rather than the animal, be the thing that fails. Gregorian creatures have minds stocked with thinking tools they did not invent — words, numerals, diagrams, instruments, and, in our trade, compilers, type checkers, profilers, debuggers — tools that change what they are able to think. I want the talk built on that ladder, and I want the ladder to do the work rather than sit in the introduction as ornament.

What I need from you:

- Structure the remarks so the ladder is the spine: each turn of the talk should be a rung, and the close should be something the framework genuinely licenses — a live question the summit is supposed to work on — rather than a generic exhortation to build responsibly.
- The room is split. Perhaps half are senior engineers who have read the same material and will notice the moment I flatten it or use a term loosely. The rest are product managers, designers, and a few people from finance who have no patience for jargon. Every term of art has to be paid for in plain language as it is introduced, without the informed half hearing it watered down.
- I want two things stated in the weeds, not softened into platitudes. First, the difference between intelligence that lives in a head and intelligence that lives in the workshop: a jig, a notation, a type system are cognition someone built and then leans on, and I want the audience to feel that a compiler is a piece of somebody's mind. Second, the asymmetry nobody on my team likes to say out loud: when I accept or reject an autocomplete suggestion I am running a reinforcement schedule on a system I cannot inspect, and the same loop is running on me, shaping my taste at least as much as I am shaping it. Say this plainly, without the it-is-only-a-tool deflation and without the machines-are-coming inflation.
- There is one turn I have not solved, and I want it left unresolved. Does the assistant belong on the fourth rung with us, as a new appendage to a Gregorian mind, or is it itself a creature climbing the ladder — and what happens to the first three rungs if the things we build start to occupy them? Leave that as the question the next two days have to answer, and give the audience one concrete thing to watch for in the tool on Monday morning.
- No citations and no reading list. Nobody in that room will read a paper, and I would rather have the argument than the bibliography.
- Fifteen minutes is roughly 1,800 to 2,200 words. Write it to be spoken: short sentences, no bullet points in the body of the speech, no references to slides.
- Keep it in my voice — someone who writes software for a living and is careful not to pretend to be anything else.

After the speech, add a short separate note, not part of the speech, saying how confident you are in the claims the talk makes about the taxonomy, and singling out the one point you are least sure of — whether that is a place where I would be overclaiming what the framework says, or where someone who knows this material better than I do would push back. Also flag anywhere the speech takes a liberty with the ideas, or leans on a reading of them that is contested. I would rather cut a sentence than have the informed half of the room wince.
