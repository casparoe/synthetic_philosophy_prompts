I am a retired experimental physiologist — forty years at the bench on cardiovascular control, a good deal of that time spent building and debugging feedback models of the baroreflex — who has only recently begun reading philosophy of biology in earnest. I have run into the debate over what it is for a trait to have a function, and I want to see it in the vocabulary I actually think in: control engineering, feedback loops, set points, transfer functions, design specifications, tolerances, redundancy, failure modes, reverse engineering, and the ordinary physiology of how a system holds a variable in range. Treat that as my native language. I am fluent in it and a novice in the philosophers'.

Two accounts are at issue. The first says that a trait's function is what it was selected for — the effect that, over the evolutionary history of the lineage, explains why the trait is present at all. The second says that a trait's function is the contribution it makes to some larger capacity of the system it belongs to, identified by analyzing how that system works here and now, with no essential reference to history. I am deliberately not naming anyone; I want the ideas, not the citations.

Restate each account, one at a time, as faithfully as you can in my vocabulary. Then give me the residue report: what carries over cleanly, what carries over only with distortion, and what cannot be said in my vocabulary at all. Do not quietly help yourself to engineering notions — design intent, a specification, a designer's purpose, a nominal operating point — and present them as though they were translations of what the philosophers mean; where you need such a notion, say so and flag it as an addition. I care far more about the failures of translation than the successes. If something in the debate has no counterpart in control engineering or physiology, tell me whether that is a gap in my vocabulary or a sign that the philosophers' claim was less substantial than it sounded. And say which account the translation ends up favoring, and whether that is a real result or an artifact of translating into an engineer's tongue.

Please write in American English, and organize the reply under exactly these headings, in this order:

1. The two accounts in their own terms — a short paragraph each, no more than about 150 words apiece, so I can check that you have them right before you start translating.
2. Translation of the selected-effects account — roughly 300–400 words.
3. Translation of the systemic, causal-role account — roughly 300–400 words.
4. What survives the translation — bullet points, the clean cases, each with a one-line justification.
5. What survives only with distortion — bullet points, naming the distortion in each case.
6. What does not survive at all — the residue, roughly 300–400 words, with your verdict on each item: gap in my vocabulary, or weakness in the original?
7. Which account the translation favors, and whether that is a result or an artifact — a short paragraph, leading with the bottom line.
