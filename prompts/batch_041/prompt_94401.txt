I have been reading popular books on quantum foundations, and I keep meeting a summary of the wavefunction debate that runs roughly as follows. The passage below is my transcription of an argument from a recent book on the measurement problem, by a physicist writing for a general audience; I have no reason to doubt his competence, but I suspect the summary is doing something misleading that I cannot put my finger on.

"Quantum mechanics hands us a wavefunction, and the central interpretative question is whether that wavefunction is a real physical object, like an electromagnetic field, or merely a compact way of writing down what someone knows about a system. The first position is called psi-ontic, the second psi-epistemic. The psi-epistemic camp is where the instrumentalists and the Copenhagen school naturally end up: on this view there is no fact of the matter about the wavefunction, only a calculus for predicting measurement outcomes, and the ontic state of the system is just a placeholder for our ignorance. The psi-ontic camp, by contrast, treats the wavefunction as a concrete entity, and since it treats it as concrete it must also treat measurement as a physical disturbance that collapses the wavefunction, which is why psi-ontic views need an observer, or at least a measuring device, to finish the job. The recent no-go theorems have settled the argument. Pusey, Barrett and Rudolph proved, using nothing beyond the quantum predictions themselves, that the wavefunction must be real. Bell's theorem destroyed local hidden variables; this destroys epistemic views. The live question is therefore not whether the wavefunction is real but which psi-ontic account of it — collapse theories, hidden variables, or many worlds — is correct."

I would like an audit rather than a lecture. Go through the passage claim by claim and say, for each, whether a philosopher of physics working on quantum foundations would accept it, would wince at it as a simplification, or would reject it outright, with the reason in each case. I am especially interested in four things: whether the characterisation of what it is for a state to be psi-epistemic is correct, and whether the distinction being drawn is the distinction the labels actually mark; whether the consequences the passage draws for psi-ontic views — collapse, and the need for an observer — really follow from psi-ontology; whether the no-go theorem is being given the right scope and the right force, and what exactly it leaves standing; and whether the closing verdict, that the dispute has been settled and only the choice among psi-ontic accounts remains, survives once the earlier errors are corrected. If the passage has imported assumptions or conflated positions that a specialist would keep apart, name them.
