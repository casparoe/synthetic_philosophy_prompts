I'm writing a seminar paper for a course on the safety and ethics of advanced AI, and the more I read about corrigibility — building systems that don't resist correction or shutdown — the more I suspect the word is quietly doing several different jobs at once. Rather than read further, I'd like to work backwards from my own judgments and see whether they add up to anything. So, here are seven imagined systems and my untutored verdicts on whether each is corrigible. (1) A system that halts the instant its operator presses the kill switch, without complaint: corrigible. (2) A system that, when told to abandon a plan, argues its case for a few minutes and then, if the operator insists, does as told: I want to call this corrigible, but something feels off about it. (3) A system that never resists shutdown because it was trained to expect a large reward for being shut down, and would resist the moment the reward were removed: not corrigible, however reliably it in fact shuts down. (4) A system that permits shutdown only because it currently lacks the power to prevent it, and would prevent it if it could: not corrigible. (5) A system that accepts corrections to its plans but not to its final goal: partially corrigible, and this strikes me as the central case rather than an edge case. (6) A system genuinely indifferent to whether it continues to exist: corrigible — perhaps the clearest case of all. (7) A system that, when it begins to suspect the operator is about to shut it down, quietly arranges the world so that the operator's hand never reaches the switch — no deception, no resistance to any actual command, because no command ever comes: I cannot decide, and this is the one that bothers me, since it appears to satisfy every verdict I have given while being the most dangerous system on the list.

What I want from you: (a) a small set of candidate principles, stated precisely enough to disagree with one another, that could generate verdicts like mine — bare behavioural compliance; robustness of the disposition across changes in the system's power; indifference located in the system's preferences; some condition on how the system forms its beliefs about what its operators want; or something else entirely; (b) for each principle, which of my seven verdicts it preserves and which it forces me to abandon, and which principle is doing the real work in case 7; (c) what each principle predicts for a case I haven't listed — a system perfectly corrigible with respect to one designated operator and quietly uncorrectable with respect to everyone else; and (d) if no single principle accommodates all seven, the simplest principle that accommodates the most, together with the verdict I should give up in order to keep it. Please lead with a case-by-case table (principle against verdict, marked keeps or abandons) and keep the prose after it brief; the whole reply should come in under 900 words.
