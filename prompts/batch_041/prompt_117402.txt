Until two years ago my whole working life was in the field and the lab — 34 years as a hydrogeologist for a state agency, mostly sampling wells and writing up what the numbers meant. Since I retired I've been going to a Thursday-night discussion group at the library and reading my way through a couple of intros to philosophy, and alot of it rolls off me, but the part I keep chewing on is how much of what I know, I know because somebody told me. So I've been trying to write down my own honest verdicts on cases instead of what I think the books want, and I'd like you to tell me what principle, if any, they add up to. Here's what I actually do, case by case, and I'm not claiming all of it hangs together. A guy at the gas station tells me the road ahead is washed out — I turn around, no second thought, and I'd say that's perfectly rational. The same guy tells me there's a penguin loose in the woods behind his place and I don't believe him for a second; if he'd said a deer I'd have believed him on the spot, and I have no reason at all to think he's lying about the penguin. A stranger in a white coat tells me my potassium is low and I take it on board, though I couldn't tell you what the coat has to do with it. My old colleague, thirty years of careful work, now with fairly advanced dementia, tells me a solvent is fine to breathe in a closed room, and I don't believe a word of it, and I notice I don't even feel bad about that. My daughter set me up with one of those chatbots and it told me the boiling point of something I'd forgotten; I half believed it, but I think I'd have believed the identical sentence more if a person had said it, and I can't justify that difference to myself. I believe the paper when it says a local plant is dumping into the creek; I wouldn't believe the same paper if it said the plant's owner had personally poisoned a well, though I've got no inside information either way. And here's the one that bothers me most: in my own line of work I'd never take a colleague's number just because he handed it to me — I'd want to see his calibration records first — but when a stranger tells me the museum closes at five I believe him flat out. I think both of those reactions are right, and I can't say what makes them different.

My own tentative stab at a rule was this: I'm entitled to believe what someone tells me unless I've got a specific reason not to, except in matters where I'm supposed to be the one who knows, where I need independent reason to think the person actually knows what they're talking about. That feels decent until the penguin comes along, because there I've got no reason to doubt him whatsoever, just a feeling about how the world usually is. And I don't know what to do with the chatbot — whether that even counts as somebody telling me something.

So what I want is the principle itself, stated as precisely as you can, with each clause tied to whichever of my cases forces it, not a name for a position — I can look names up. Where does my stab at a rule break, and is there a repair that keeps the parts I care about, or do I just have to give one of my verdicts up? I'd also like to know what your principle says about a case I haven't listed: someone tells me something, I have no evidence about them one way or the other, and the only reason I have for thinking they're reliable is that other people told me so. And please be straight with me about which of my verdicts can't be saved — I'd rather find out now than keep telling myself I'm consistent.
