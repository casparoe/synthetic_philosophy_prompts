I'm in a slow reading group that's been grinding through Machiavelli since January and I've got stuck on virtù and fortuna in a way I suspect is my own fault but I can't see where. The bit in The Prince where he says fortune rules about half of what we do seems to leave the other half for virtù, so in principle you could take any prince's career and split it up the way you'd split a variance — this much fortune, this much virtù. My friend Duncan takes that seriously and says it means we can audit Cesare Borgia: Machiavelli reckons the man did everything a prudent, capable man could have done, so his collapse was pure fortune, and by the same token anyone who fails simply didn't have enough virtù. That strikes me as making the whole thing unfalsifiable — virtù ends up being the name we give to whatever worked, and fortuna the name for whatever didn't — but Duncan says no, it's a genuine empirical claim about how much of politics is skill. Then there's the later passage where Machiavelli says the man who suits his methods to the times will prosper, which sounds sensible until he adds that a man can't easily change his nature to suit the times, so whether your natural mode happens to fit your century looks like luck after all, and virtù starts dissolving into fortuna. And in the Discourses he'll use virtù of an entire city and its institutions rather than of one man, which I can't sqaure with the Prince at all.

So: roughly what is Borgia's split, on Machiavelli's own view — how much fortuna, how much virtù? And if Duncan's reading is right, is there any version of virtù left that isn't just survivorship bias with extra steps? Give me exactly five numbered points, each a sentence or two, no introduction or conclusion, and please write it in British English.
