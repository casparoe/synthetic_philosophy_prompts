I'm a science-and-ideas journalist by day, and for the past few years I've also taught an upper-level philosophy of mind seminar as an adjunct at a state school near me — the pay is a joke, the arguments are great. The final is due and I'm stuck on one essay question.

Topic is the causal exclusion argument. Every year I get the same paper: they tell me it runs on supervenience plus causal closure of the physical plus a no-overdetermination premise, they say you can reject closure, or accept overdetermination, or go reductionist/eliminativist, they name Kim, they stop. I want a question that makes them actually walk through the machinery, so the ones who only know the shape of the argument can't fake it.

What I want the question to force them to do:

- Say what the causal relata are supposed to be. Events or properties? They can recite the argument fine and then fall apart when I ask them at which level the exclusion is happening, and whether token identity of the mental event with the physical event buys you anything if the mental property is still distinct.
- Deal with the multiple-realizability wrinkle: if the same mental property is realized by different physical properties in different cases, is there even a single physical property for it to be excluded by? Is the mental property then just a disjunction of realizers, and can a disjunction have causal powers? I want them to notice that this cuts both ways.
- Take seriously the inheritance move — that a realized property inherits whatever causal powers it has from its realizer — and say why it looks like a rescue and then why it seems to collapse back into the problem.
- Take a stand on keeping the mental as causally relevant while giving up on its being efficacious: real position or verbal dodge, and what it would then have to say about explanation and prediction.
- Say whether it's a reductio that the argument appears to drain causal powers out of chemistry and biology too, or just an honest consequence of the picture. I dont want a shrug here, I want them to pick a side and defend it.

And I want the question to hang on at least one concrete case, not just abstractions — a pain causing a wince, a belief causing you to grab an umbrella, something with actual properties in it — so they can't answer purely at the level of schema.

Mechanics: Its a closed-book, in-class final, 90 minutes for this one question, worth 20 marks, upper-level undergrads (a few are sharp, most read the night before). So the question has to be answerable in 90 minutes by hand. No citations, no reading list, no "discuss the literature" — they can't look anything up and I'm not grading name-drops. If you need a term like supervenience or overdetermination, define it inline in the question itself so nobody loses marks over vocabulary.

Give me: the exam question as it would appear on the paper; a model answer of around 700 words in plain prose with no citations; and a marking scheme of five must-hit points worth 20 marks total, where for each point you tell me what a B answer typically does versus what an A answer has to do to earn the mark. Add a one-liner per point on the most common way students get it wrong.

One more thing — write the model answer the way a good student would write it under time pressure, not like a journal article. If it starts sounding like an abstract, rewrite it.
