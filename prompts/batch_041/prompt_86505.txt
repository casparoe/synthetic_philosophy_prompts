So I'm a novelist, about 250 pages into a book about a guy who builds a recommendation algorithm in his dorm room and twenty years later is worth more than most countries, and I keep circling the question of whether any of that money is actually his. I was at dinner with a philosopher friend last week and we got into it over Nozick's Wilt Chamberlain thing — you know, the argument that if you start from a just distribution and people voluntarily hand over money to watch someone play, the resulting inequality has to be just too, because it came out of free choices, and so no patterned principle of distribution can survive liberty for very long. My friend says the whole argument is a trick, basically a magic trick where the rabbit was already in the hat. I don't think its a trick, but I also don't know how much I actually believe it, and I'd like a number instead of a vibe.

Here's what I want: your credence, as a number between 0 and 1, that the following is true — that if we grant that people are fully entitled to whatever holdings they start with, and that the transfers in question are genuinely voluntary (no fraud, no coercion, everyone competent and informed), then no patterned principle of distributive justice can be maintained over time without continuously violating someone's entitlements. Not "is that the correct theory of justice," I know thats a different question — just: does the argument, as an argument, go through. And I want the decomposition, not just the number. How much rides on whether liberty really does "upset" patterns or whether that's just a stipulation baked into the setup? How much on whether the toy example scales to a real economy, where the starting holdings are never actually just and the supposedly free choices are made under massive background inequality? How much on whether a patterned theorist can just say "fine, then I forbid the later transactions," and whether that's a real reply or a concession that the argument worked? And how much on whether entitlement even makes sense as a purely historical notion with no reference to the shape of the whole? Number and one line for each, then how they combine, then which term you'd bet against first and what would actually move it.

Then the thing I care about for the book, and maybe this is where the argument has a seam: does any of it survive when the resource being transferred isn't money but attention? In my version the Chamberlain isn't paid in cash, he's paid in fifteen seconds of your attention, or your data, which is worth nothing to you one unit at a time and a fortune in aggregate, and alot of the people paying him were born into a world where he was already famous and they weren't — they never chose the starting distribution of attention-worthiness. Does that change your number, and if so which term moves? I'm less interested in whether the platform guy is a good person than in whether the argument has a weak joint there and where exactly it is. Also, for calibration: my credence that the argument goes through in the clean toy case is like 0.75, but it drops to maybe 0.35 once the initial holdings get realistic — I'd like to know if that's roughly where you land, and if not, which of us is confused about what the argument is even claiming.
