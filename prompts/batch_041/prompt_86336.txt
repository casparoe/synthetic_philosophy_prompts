here's the question:

A tutoring company tested two ways of running its after-school algebra program. Students got either the new StudySprint method or the standard method. The company also recorded whether each student had already taken the prerequisite pre-algebra course. Pass rates on the final exam:

Took pre-algebra already (120 students):
- StudySprint: 19 of 20 passed (95%)
- Standard: 90 of 100 passed (90%)

Had not taken pre-algebra (110 students):
- StudySprint: 60 of 100 passed (60%)
- Standard: 5 of 10 passed (50%)

(a) Compute the overall pass rate for each method. Is there anything strange about how the overall comparison relates to the comparisons inside each group? If so, name the phenomenon and explain what's going on.
(b) The company announces: "overall the standard method has the higher pass rate, so we should stick with the standard method." Is that a good argument? What should the company actually conclude, and why?
(c) Could this same kind of flip happen if, instead of splitting students by pre-algebra, we split them by something the program itself affects, like how many practice problems they finished during the program? Explain.

and here's what my partner wrote:

(a) StudySprint: 60 + 19 = 79 passed out of 100 + 20 = 120 students, so 79/120 = 65.8%. Standard: 5 + 90 = 95 out of 10 + 100 = 110, so 95/110 = 86.4%. So overall the standard method is way ahead, 86.4% vs 65.8%. But inside each group StudySprint is ahead (95% vs 90% for the pre-algebra students, 60% vs 50% for the others). That's Simpson's paradox. What's going on is that the two methods got handed out to different kinds of students: almost all the StudySprint kids (100 of 120) were in the no-pre-algebra group, which has low pass rates for everybody, and almost all the standard-method kids (100 of 110) were in the pre-algebra group, which has high pass rates for everybody. So the overall number is mixing together two different kinds of students. Pre-algebra is a confounder here because it's related to both which method you got and whether you passed.

(b) No, it's a bad argument. The overall comparison is confounded, so it isn't measuring the method, it's mostly measuring which students ended up where. If you compare students to students who are alike on the pre-algebra thing, StudySprint does better in both groups, so the company should conclude StudySprint is the better method. The pooled number is misleading because it's really comparing different students rather than different methods. So the rule i'd take away is that whenever the overall numbers and the within-group numbers disagree, you should always go with the within-group numbers, since the overall number is just an artifact of how the groups got mixed.

(c) No. The flip only happens because pre-algebra is a confounder. If you split by something the program itself causes, like practice problems completed, then there's no confounding, so the overall result and the group results would have to agree. Splitting on a variable that comes after the treatment is like conditioning on a collider or adjusting for a mediator, which isn't a valid thing to do, so the overall number would be the one to trust.
