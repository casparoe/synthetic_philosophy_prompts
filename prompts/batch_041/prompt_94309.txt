I've spent the last several years as a pure mathematician and have recently moved into a group that does theoretical physics, and the question of why mathematics applies to the world — what's sometimes called its unreasonable effectiveness — has stopped being an after-dinner puzzle and become something I actually need to get straight before I put anything in writing. I'd like you to audit my position rather than argue me out of it.

Please act as a philosopher of mathematics and physics: someone at home both in the technical literature on structural realism and in the day-to-day practice of mathematical physics, who is rigorous but is trying to help me see my own commitments clearly rather than score points. Before you judge anything, take a moment to work out, for each item below, what it actually entails and what it rules out; I want the reasoning on the page, not just a verdict.

Here is roughly where I've landed:

1. Mathematical realism. Mathematical structures are discovered, not invented. They exist independently of any human practice, and mathematical truth is not up to us.

2. The fit is a genuine explanandum. That the mathematics we develop should describe physical reality — and, far more strikingly, predict phenomena nobody has yet observed — is a deep and contingent fact about the world. It is not to be dissolved as trivial, definitional, or inevitable. I care especially about the predictive side: fitting a formalism to data already in hand is cheap, whereas a structure derived on purely mathematical grounds anticipating an unseen phenomenon is not.

3. Structuralism as the explanation. The best account of the fit is that what physics describes just is mathematical structure — the world is, at bottom, mathematical — so the "fit" is not a coincidence between two independent realms but a matter of identity.

4. Our mathematics is a contingent human product. The mathematics we actually have is shaped by history, by psychology, and above all by what has proved useful. Most mathematics is never applied at all, and the parts that stay in the toolkit are, unsurprisingly, the parts that worked.

5. Methodological naturalism. I want no pre-established harmony and no theological guarantee; whatever explains the fit has to be continuous with ordinary scientific explanation.

Now go through these and tell me which pull against each other, which only appear to, and how badly. Rank the genuine tensions by severity. For each one, say what would have to give — which commitment would need to be weakened or dropped, and what the cheapest repair costs me. Where a tension dissolves once you spell out what the commitments actually mean, say so explicitly instead of counting it as a conflict; I don't want everything flagged as equally problematic, and I don't want a survey of the literature. Close by telling me which single revision would clean up the most trouble at the least cost.
