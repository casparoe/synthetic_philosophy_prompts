I'm a high school student, and for my school's philosophy-of-science club I have to write a short dialogue in which two statisticians try to talk each other out of their preferred response to the replication crisis. The first works in a lab that publishes pre-registered replications and defends the classical frequentist machinery of error control; the second is a Bayesian who thinks the crisis is largely a consequence of reading p-values as if they were posterior probabilities. Please make both of them competent and well-informed -- no strawmen, and real back-and-forth rather than slogans -- and center their argument on one concrete case: a small first study reporting p = .048, and a much larger pre-registered replication reporting p = .06, which the lab's press release announces as a failure to replicate.

I'd like the Bayesian to explain why he thinks that announcement is a misreading of the evidence, the frequentist to explain why a p-value of .048 was weak evidence to begin with, and then for each to press the other where it hurts. The Bayesian should have to answer whether his rescue of the finding depends on a prior that was chosen precisely so that it would rescue it, and what he says when a large enough sample makes a conventional default analysis favor the null hypothesis despite a small p-value. The frequentist should have to answer why long-run error rates should matter to someone who will only ever run one study, and whether his own machinery can stop a researcher who analyzes the same data in many defensible ways and reports the one that happened to work.

Let each of them concede one point to the other, and end the dialogue with a disagreement they cannot resolve. No citations are needed.
