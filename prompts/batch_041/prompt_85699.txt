Not lying exactly, more like: what has to be true of you before the sentence is one you get to say. I've been collecting my gut reactions to little cases and I can't get them to line up under one rule, so I want you to tell me what principle (or small set of principles) actually captures them, stated precisely, and which of my reactions I should give up. And please don't just say "it's the knowledge norm" and stop — that's the one I keep reaching for, it's exactly the one some of my verdicts seem to fight, and I want to see the fighting done. Also is my flatmate right that half of these are just etiquette/pragmatics and have nothing to do with a norm of assertion at all?

Here's where my gut lands. (1) Stopped clock: I glance at the station clock, it says 3:00, I tell my friend "your train's at 3." The clock actually died two days ago and just happens to still read 3:00, and the train really is at 3. I believed it, it was true, I had no reason to doubt the clock — and I still feel I had no business asserting it. (2) Sincere mistake: I tell my sister the party's Saturday, I'm certain, I read the invite; I misread it, it's Sunday. Not fine to have said, though I don't think I'm a bad person for it. (3) The plane: my friend is terrified of flying and I say "this plane is not going to crash." I don't know that, nobody can, it's just overwhelmingly probable — and this one feels completely fine, refusing to say it would be the weird thing. I know it's true, and I don't think I should say it — though I suspect that's about the file and not about assertion, and I can't tell if that distinction saves anything. (5) The lottery: friend asks if my ticket won, I say "no it didn't." I'm basically sure but I don't know. Feels wrong to assert. (6) Retraction: I told my flatmate the corner shop closes at 9; later I realize I was going off a hazy memory rather than anything solid, but it turns out the shop does close at 9. I still want to take it back or apologize for saying it so flatly, even though I was right — that one confuses me. (7) The wedding: I announce "my brother is definitely coming," I believe it completely, it's pure hope with no evidence behind it; he does come. I still think I was wrong to say "definitely," it was wishful, not something I knew. (8) The spokesperson: I read out a company statement I privately think is spin. Reading it seems fine — but I'd say I'm not asserting it, or I'm asserting something else (that this is the company's line), so maybe belief is required for asserting proper but not for some neighbouring act. Not sure if that's a real distinction or me cheating.

What I want: the principle, stated so I could apply it, with the specific case that forces each clause of it; what it predicts for a couple of cases I haven't listed — say, asserting a moral claim you can't argue for, and asserting something you know but can't for the life of you explain how you know; and an honest list of which of my eight verdicts it can't accommodate, so I know what I'd be buying.
