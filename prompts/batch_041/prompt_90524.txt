I run a small philosophy discussion group and I need a dialogue for next month's session. Heres the setup. Two founders of a tiny company -- six people, they hand-build bicycle frames -- are sitting at a kitchen table with the accounts open. Four years ago they both quit good jobs and sank basically all their savings plus a small inheritance into tooling and a workshop, and the frames are genuinely lovely, but they can't sell them for what they cost to make, and there's maybe eight months of runway left. One founder trained as an economist before this; the other did a PhD in philosophy and then spent a decade in manufacturing. The economist says: close it, sell the tools, go back to consulting. The four years and the money are gone, and the only thing that counts now is what happens next -- anything else is the sunk cost fallacy, and she means the textbook version, the one that says honoring a sunk cost is always irrational. The philosopher says that's too quick, and not because he's sentimental about the money: he thinks the fallacy is being applied to a decision that hasn't been properly described, and he thinks some of their reasons to keep going do come from the past without being costs.

Write the dialogue, somewhere around 800 words. The important thing: neither of them is a strawman. The philosopher needs real arguements, not vibes, and the economist has to answer them instead of repeating herself. Have them actually go back and forth -- let the economist push on whether his view makes the fallacy unfalsifiable, and whether a reason from the past is ever anything but a prediction about the future, and let the philosopher push on whether her description of the choice is as neutral as she thinks, and on what "the project" even is here. At some point let the philosopher try the line that visibly having sunk all this into it is a commitment device that changes how customers and suppliers treat them, and let the economist ask what's left of the sunk cost itself in that story. No citations, no name-dropping, no lit review -- I want them arguing, not citing. And please don't let them collapse into a polite "well, it depends" at the end.
