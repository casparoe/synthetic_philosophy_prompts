I teach a graduate seminar on decision theory, and last week a student pressed me on the one-shot prisoner's dilemma and on what is usually called superrationality — the thought that two agents who know themselves to be each other's duplicates should both cooperate, because the only outcomes worth comparing are the ones in which they act alike. I want to press the idea harder than the textbook examples allow, so I built a small game for the seminar and now find I cannot settle my own view of it. The game is this: two players, one shot, no communication, each privately choosing a real number x in the interval from 0 to 4, and a player who chooses x while her counterpart chooses y receives 4x − y². On the diagonal, where both choose the same number, the payoff is 4x − x², maximized at x = 2 for a payoff of 4 to each; but x = 4 is strictly dominant, and the unique Nash equilibrium is (4, 4), yielding 0 to each. The game therefore poses the superrational question in an unusually stark form: the recommended action is not merely outside the equilibrium set, it is strictly dominated.

What I would like from you is a credence, decomposed. Take the claim to be: in this game, played once by two agents each of whom knows the other to be an equally rational reasoner who has confronted the same problem, the uniquely rational choice for each is x = 2. Give me your probability that the claim is true, and then the sub-probabilities it rests on, each with a line of justification — the probability that reasoners in identical situations must reach identical conclusions at all (the symmetry-of-reasoning premise); the probability that, granting that premise, rationality requires maximizing along the diagonal rather than assessing each action against a counterpart who would respond to it as an independent agent would; the probability that the presence of a strictly dominant action defeats the superrational prescription rather than being simply irrelevant to it; and whatever further terms you think the decomposition needs, including your confidence that 'rational' has a determinate extension in this case at all. I would also like to know how the number moves under two variations. First, suppose each player is handed an independent fair coin and may let her choice depend on it, though both are still running the same algorithm: does the superrational recommendation change, and if it does not, is that because the diagonal argument is sound or because a different argument happens to deliver the same answer? Second, suppose each player knows only that her counterpart is a duplicate with probability p and otherwise plays the dominant action: what is the recommended choice as a function of p, and does your credence in the original claim survive that generalization?

For what it is worth, my own credence in the claim is about 0.55; a colleague in our department who works on causal decision theory puts it near 0.1, and the doctoral student who raised the topic puts it near 0.9. Beyond the number itself, tell me which single sub-probability my number and my colleague's most likely come apart on, and what argument or evidence would move that term most for you. Please reason from the game as stated rather than directing me to literature; I am after your derivation, not a reading list.
