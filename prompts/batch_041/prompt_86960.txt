I sit on the board of a human rights documentation center, and after an argument with an old friend — an international lawyer — that ran well past midnight, I've been drafted to write the board's decision memo on our draft naming protocol, which the curators drew up after our last exhibition drew complaints from both directions. The protocol would govern when the center may apply the word "genocide" in catalogue text, exhibitions, and public statements. It sets out three tiers: "mass atrocity" for any documented pattern above a factual threshold; "crimes against humanity" where the acts are widespread or systematic against a civilian population; and "genocide," reserved either for a determination by a competent court or UN body or for our own researchers concluding that intent to destroy the group, in whole or in part, is the only reasonable inference from the evidence. It also carries a cultural-destruction annex that would let us use the word where a group's language, religion, or heritage is systematically destroyed even without mass killing — the curators say this recovers Raphael Lemkin's original, broader idea of genocide from the 1940s, before the 1948 Convention narrowed it into a specific-intent crime against a protected group.

The case before us: an upcoming exhibition on a campaign of mass killing, village burning, and displacement against a minority, where the perpetrators deny intent and call it counterinsurgency, and where the monitors are split. My friend insists that "genocide" is a legal term of art a documentation center has no standing to apply, and that using it loosely both cheapens it and smuggles in a verdict; I think waiting for a court is how the word dies, and that the Convention's definition is itself a political artifact narrower than what Lemkin meant.

Please write the memo I'll circulate to the board: a clear recommendation — adopt the protocol as drafted, adopt it with specific amendments, or reject it and say what should replace it — with the reasoning, a workable trigger and review process, and a fair statement of the strongest case against my position. Engage the hardest objections rather than reassuring me, and address directly whether naming an atrocity is a descriptive act or an act of accusation, and what follows for us either way.
