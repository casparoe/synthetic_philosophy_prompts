So I got into it again last night with my friend Dan, who does econ history, and we've now been having basically the same fight about Malthus for two years and I'm starting to think we're not even disagreeing about the same thing. His line is that Malthus got falsified, full stop: the industrial revolution happened, agricultural productivity ran ahead of population, the demographic transition brought fertility down without famine or plague doing the work, and a prediction that fails is a prediction that fails. My line is that this feels like cheating, because the whole point of the Malthusian model was a conditional or equilibrium claim about what happens when you hold technology roughly fixed and let population grow, and the fact that the world changed the antecedent out from under him doesn't obviously count as the model being wrong so much as it not being the right model for what came next. Then he says "so it's unfalsifiable," and I say "no, it's just a model with a scope," and we go around again. I keep feeling like "right but early" ought to be a coherent thing to say about a long-run prediction, but I can't tell whether I'm just doing the ad hoc rescue work that Popper warned us about — saving the theory by tacking on excuses every time it misses.

What I actually want from you isn't a verdict on Malthus, it's a diagnosis of the fight. Is this a substantive disagreement about the facts of the last two centuries, or a verbal one about what "falsified" is even allowed to mean for a theory that makes conditional or ceteris paribus predictions?
