My friend and I spent most of last night arguing about "collapse," and we went to bed no closer to agreement than when we began. She takes the transformationist line: that "collapse" names no event in the world, only a failure of archaeological imagination; that when the palatial centres of the Late Bronze Age, the Classic Maya cities, or Rapa Nui's statue-raising society stop doing the things by which we identify them, the people do not vanish or fail — they reorganize — and that the word smuggles in an outsider's judgment that one particular kind of complexity was the kind worth having. I take the collapse-realist line: that something really was lost in these cases, and that a vocabulary of failure and loss is not mere projection. To break the deadlock she proposed an operational test I think is too crude — that a collapse has occurred only if the defining institution fails to reappear within five generations — and I could not say precisely why I thought it was wrong, which is where we gave up.

We agreed on far more of the evidence than either of us expected and disagreed mainly about what to call it; but each time we tried to localize the real disagreement we slid back into talking past each other. Write the document we should have produced instead.

Draft the joint statement of an adversarial collaboration between the collapse-realist and the transformationist camps. Use three shared test cases — the Late Bronze Age eastern Mediterranean, the Classic Maya lowlands, and Rapa Nui — and include: a numbered section of claims both sides can sign, concerning the archaeological and demographic record and what any theory of "collapse" would have to explain; the precise points of remaining disagreement, each phrased in wording its own side would accept; the cruxes, including whether the disagreement is one of degree or of kind, whether population decline is essential or merely incidental, and whether a collapse can be defined by the loss of a single integrating network even where local welfare improves; and, for each crux, the case or evidence both sides agree would bear on it, with each side's prediction recorded in advance. Treat her five-generation test as one candidate criterion to be assessed rather than assumed. If any part of our dispute is merely verbal, say which part and why. Format the agreed claims as a numbered list and the disagreements as a table with columns for the question, each side's formulation, whether it is a genuine crux, and the evidence and predictions.
