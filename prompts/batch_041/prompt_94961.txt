I've been arguing about this for weeks with a friend who's a psychiatrist — it started at her sister's wedding, of all places, and now its a running text thread — and I want to see it actually hashed out. Her line is that self-deception obviously happens, she sees it in her office constantly, and the fact that I can't draw a clean diagram of it doesn't make it stop. My line is that the literal version is incoherent: to deceive someone you have to know the truth and intend for them to end up with a false belief, and if the deceiver and the deceived are the same person you'd need to know and not know the same thing at once; you also can't just decide to believe something (offer me a hundred bucks to believe my name is Kevin and watch me fail), and engineering a belief in yourself by accident doesn't sound like deceiving anybody either. So I think "self-deception" is a metaphor that got out of hand, and the real phenomena are motivated attention, selective evidence-gathering, pretense, wishful thinking, or plain avoidance. Write me a dialogue between two people who hold these two views — one a philosopher convinced literal self-deception is impossible and who has already thought through why the obvious replies fail, the other a clinician who insists it's a real and common phenomenon and who has thought through why the philosophical argument is too tidy. Both should be sharp and neither should be a strawman, and they should be replying to the specific thing the other just said, conceding a point when it lands rather than sailing past it. The philosopher should be forced to say what he thinks is really going on in someone's head, not just that the thing is impossible; the clinician should be forced to say what distinguishes her cases from the ones that are clearly something else.

Make them keep circling one case I can't get past. A man with a heavy family history of a fatal genetic illness decides not to get tested. He tells people, sincerely and with numbers, that he's certain he doesn't have it, and he lives like it — plans for retirement, buys a bike, gets prickly at anyone who hints otherwise. But when his adult son mentions he's thinking about getting tested, the man immediately pushes him to go, rattles off the inheritance statistics accurately, and says you can't just bury your head in the sand about these things. He also keeps a folder of research papers on the disease in his study that he reads, and he can recite the recurrence risks from memory. I want the dialogue to keep coming back to him: is he believing something he knows isn't so, or hoping, or pretending, or "accepting," or acting as if — and does it matter which word we use? Have them fight about whether that case is even the same phenomenon as the guy who hides his own cigarettes from himself, or the person who "forgets" to open the bank statement, and about whether the whole disagreement is really about the mind (is it one room or several? can a person hold two things that can't both be true?) or just about words. Something like 900 to 1200 words, and have them end by each naming the one thing that would move them, or the strongest point of the other's they dont have an answer for yet. Please don't lean on famous names or "the literature says" — I want them arguing it out, not citing.
