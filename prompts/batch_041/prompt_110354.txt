For a philosophy elective I am writing about Escher's Drawing Hands, the print in which two hands rise out of a sheet of paper and each draws the other into existence, so that neither can have come first. The resolution I keep meeting is that a tangle of this kind is only possible because something sits outside it — the paper, the artist, the level at which it is settled what counts as a line — a level the picture itself cannot show, and which I have seen called the inviolate level. I have spent three seasons on stage crew for my school's theatre department, so I know that world's vocabulary well: the house and the wings, the fourth wall, calling cues, the difference between the fiction the audience is watching and the physical stage and crew that make it possible, actors playing actors, plays with plays inside them. Please translate the Escher idea into that vocabulary as faithfully as you can, first the tangled hierarchy and then the inviolate level, and then give me the residue report: what goes across cleanly, what goes across only if theatre's own assumptions are allowed to do work they should not, and what has no theatrical counterpart at all. I am especially interested in a suspicion of mine — that a stage always marks its levels, with a curtain, a costume, a change of lighting, whereas the print deliberately marks nothing, so that what looks like a paradox may be only a stage with its frame removed. If that is right, say so and tell me what is left of the original claim; if it is not, tell me where the two cases come apart, and whether the remainder is a limit of theatre or a sign that the Escher idea was vaguer than its admirers make out.
