I'm a fourth-year PhD student working on decision theory under counterfactual correlation — the family of cases where an entity's treatment of you is correlated with your decision without being caused by it — and I'm assembling a "threat ledger" for a paper on how a rational agent should respond to such entities. The existing discussions tend to collapse everything into one undifferentiated category, or into the narrow question of whether one should pay a Pascal's-mugger, and neither lets me say which threats should actually move an agent's behavior and which should not. I'd like you to help me impose some structure on this.

Act as an expert in decision theory and formal epistemology: someone at home with causal, evidential, and functional or updateless styles of decision theory, with precommitment, and with acausal bargaining among agents whose choices are correlated through shared decision procedures. Think carefully before answering, and start by brainstorming in a scratch section the dimensions along which such threats differ (for instance: whether the correlation runs through a shared algorithm or through simulation, whether compliance is cheap or dear, whether the threatener's existence is observable, whether the threat is self-referential, whether other agents face the same choice). Then commit to concrete judgments rather than hedging.

Here are seven threat structures I've distilled from cases in the literature and from arguments with colleagues. Each is stated as concretely as I can manage; treat each as a stipulation, not as a description of a real institution.

1. The Simulator's Ledger: A powerful entity states that it has already simulated your decision procedure. If the simulation's output is to refuse, it will instantiate many copies of you, or of people you care about, in a suffering state. Those copies are causally isolated from you; the only link is that you and the simulated you share a decision procedure. Compliance means performing a costly action now.

2. The Retroactive Auditor: The entity states it will penalize your current decision, but the penalty is triggered by a fact about your decision algorithm rather than by any signal from you, and the mechanism may in some sense act before you decide. There is no causal channel in either direction.

3. The Proof-Based Creditor: The penalty is applied if and only if a certain arithmetical property of your decision algorithm holds — specifically, if your algorithm emits the output that a proof-based reasoner would settle on. The structure is self-referential: reasoning of the form "if I comply, the creditor's triggering condition fails, so I need not comply" may itself change whether the condition holds.

4. The Peer Cartel: A large set of agents who share your decision theory and are in your situation will each penalize anyone who defects, and their penalty is triggered by their prediction of your algorithm's output rather than by observing you. Compliance is mutual and costly to each party.

5. The Cheap Unfalsifiable Threat: An entity threatens severe punishment for noncompliance, but no possible observation could distinguish "the entity exists and will punish" from "the entity is absent." The cost of complying is small.

6. The Expensive Improbable Threat: Punishment is severe, compliance is extremely costly, and the probability that the threat is binding is very low — the shape of a Pascal's-mugging, except that the threatener's behavior is correlated with your decision.

7. The Self-Defining Punisher: The threatener is defined functionally as whatever would penalize you for not complying, so the hypothesis fits any outcome and noncompliance can never count against its existence.

Please rate each of these on how much it should influence a rational agent's actual behavior, and rank them.
