I'm a final-year undergraduate in a seminar on free will and the cognitive sciences, and I'm trying to write a paper on what the Libet-style experiments do and don't show. The trouble is that every version of the story I meet is essentially the same, and I suspect it has been smoothed in transit. The version I know best comes from a lecture I attended and from a popular science book I have since lent out, and it runs roughly like this: Libet hooked subjects up to an EEG and had them flick their wrist whenever they pleased, timing the movement and asking them to report the position of a fast-moving dot on a screen at the moment they felt the urge to move. What he found, on this telling, was that a readiness potential in the motor cortex began building about half a second before the muscle actually moved, whereas subjects dated their conscious urge to move to only about a fifth of a second before the movement — so for something like a third of a second the brain had already committed to the action while the person had no idea a decision was under way. The moral drawn is that the conscious decision is not what causes the movement; it is a report, generated after the fact, of a decision the brain has already taken; the same goes for every deliberate choice a person ever makes; and free will in any sense worth wanting is therefore an illusion, which is why the experiment is routinely described as the empirical refutation of free will. I have also been told that later neuroimaging work stretched the gap to several seconds, which is supposed to make the picture worse still. My roommate, who is a compatibilist, says this whole line of argument only threatens a picture of the will as a punctate inner act of deciding, which nobody serious ever needed — but my professor calls that a dodge, so I do not know what to think.

Please take on the role of a philosopher of mind who knows the empirical literature well and give me an accuracy audit of that summary, sentence by sentence. For each claim, I want to know what a careful researcher would accept as stated, what they would want rephrased, and what is flatly false or unsupported — and please mark, for each error, whether it is a mistake about what the experiments measured or a mistake about what any such experiment could show. Then tell me whether the conclusion, that free will in any sense worth wanting is an illusion, follows from whatever survives the audit, and what the strongest version of the skeptical argument from this literature would look like as opposed to the one I have been given. Finally, say whether my roommate's compatibilist point is a real reply or a dodge, and what would settle it. Begin by laying out in your own words what the experiments actually measured and what they left untouched, then work through the summary; be explicit about which parts of your answer rest on contested or uncertain empirical ground rather than on things you are confident of, since I would rather have honest hedges than false precision.
