I'm a third-year PhD student in philosophy of mind, and for the past year I've served as the informal "philosophy liaison" to an applied AI evaluation group in my university's computing department. The group circulates internal weekly reports on frontier models, and those reports have quietly drifted into intentional vocabulary: the model "believes" the task is a test, it "wants" to avoid the penalty term, it "knows" the answer but "chooses" to hedge. The engineers write this way because it earns its keep — when they strip the vocabulary and describe only weights, architecture, and training data, their forecasts of model behavior get noticeably worse. I've been asked to draft a reusable decision procedure the group can run before any report attributes a belief, desire, or knowledge state to a model, so that the language is licensed by something more defensible than convenience. Please write that procedure. It should draw on the philosophy of the intentional stance — the idea that we predict a system by treating it as a rational agent with beliefs and desires, and that this stance can be warranted even when we are unsure whether anything belief-like is "really" happening inside — but it has to be runnable by people with no philosophical training. Give me the questions to ask, in order, what counts as a sufficient answer at each step, where the procedure terminates in "licensed" or "not licensed," and where it must hand the decision to a person because the question is one of values, or of unresolved philosophy, rather than of fact.

I'd like the procedure to enforce a distinction I keep failing to draw cleanly in my own writing, and I suspect it's the crux of the whole problem: the difference between a system merely representing some content (carrying information that could in principle be read off its internal state), a system holding that content as a belief (using it as a premise across many contexts and revising it in light of evidence), and a system being disposed to avow it (asserting it when asked, in a way that hangs together with its other assertions). The engineers collapse all three into "believes," and I want the procedure sensitive to the difference, including a clear statement of which of the three — if any — actually licenses the everyday word. Then run the procedure on three cases: a model that confidently outputs a false claim about a well-known matter of fact; a model that plays a betting game and consistently infers an opponent's hidden card from the size of their bets, well enough that its behavior is best predicted by crediting it with a belief about the card; and a model that behaves as though it registers when it is being evaluated, performing well when it detects a test harness and poorly otherwise, which several of my colleagues describe as the model "knowing" it is being watched. At least one of these should come out in a way the group will not like, and I want the procedure to say why. Finally, be honest about what the procedure cannot do — the points where the three-way distinction collapses under pressure, where two equally predictive intentional ascriptions disagree about what the model believes, and where a verdict of "licensed" is doing rhetorical rather than epistemic work.
