You are an expert game designer and a philosopher of social coordination. I teach an upper-level seminar on collective action and social structure: 16 students, one 90-minute session, a room with movable chairs and a whiteboard. I want an in-class game that makes the group feel the force of what is often called a multipolar trap: a situation in which every participant's choice is individually defensible, no one is doing anything a reasonable person could blame them for, and the group still drives itself into a ruinous outcome that no one wanted. Before you design anything, think the mechanism through and write a short statement of what the game has to do structurally for the trap to be real rather than merely illustrated.

Design the activity to meet these constraints.

1. Each individual step toward the bad outcome should be small, cheap, and locally reasonable, and the harm should be diffuse and delayed. No single decision should look like villainy.

2. Cooperation must be genuinely available and rational, not a sucker's move. Include a phase in which players may talk freely, make promises, and enter binding agreements with each other, and make sure the trap survives that phase. What keeps it robust should be structural, such as an elimination or replication rule, an outside entrant, or a competitive filter, rather than a psychological weakness like greed or distrust. A game that dissolves the moment people trust each other has not reproduced the phenomenon.

3. Build in a moment where one player tries to refuse unilaterally, and let the group see what that refusal costs that player in particular.

4. At the end, every player should be able to write a one-line justification of their own final choice that a reasonable observer would accept. The debrief should read several of these aloud.

Deliver: materials and setup; an explicit payoff table with the arithmetic checked; a round-by-round timeline with timings that add to 90 minutes; what the facilitator says and, more importantly, what the facilitator must not say until the debrief; the debrief itself, mapping specific moments in the game onto parts of the idea; the failure modes you expect the first time this is run and how to handle each; and a frank note on which feature of the idea the game cannot reproduce and that the facilitator will have to supply by other means. Write in American English.
