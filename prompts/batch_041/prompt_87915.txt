Hey, I'm a software engineer and never studied philosophy formally, so bear with me. I keep running into two Marx-ish ideas and I can't tell if they're actually two theories or one theory wearing two hats. First one: exploitation, the claim that when you sell your labor for a wage, the value you produce is more than what you get back, and that gap is the point of the arrangement, not an accident or a bargaining failure. Second one: alientation, the claim that capitalist work splits you off from what you make, how you make it, other people, and eventually yourself. A friend says these are just the objective and subjective sides of the same coin: exploitation is the structural fact, alienation is how it feels. But that seems too neat. Compare the two as ideas. Are they independent? Could you have alienation without exploitation, say a hobby project you pour yourself into and feel weirdly disconnected from even though no one's extracting surplus? Could you have exploitation without alienation, a job that pays you a lot and that you find meaningful but where the surplus-value story still holds? And what happens to the distinction in a worker-owned co-op or an open-source project with a foundation and paid maintainers? If the co-op doesn't have a capitalist extracting surplus, is it still exploitative in Marx's sense, and if not, does alienation just disappear too? Also, is exploitation supposed to be a moral accusation or a technical description of a flow of value? Because if it's technical, then saying a co-op exploits itself sounds like a category error, but if it's moral, then the co-op case is about whether anyone is actually wronged. I'm mostly trying to figure out which concept is doing the foundational work: is alienation the deeper diagnosis and exploitation a downstream symptom, or is exploitation the hard economic fact and alienation just its cultural aftertaste? Don't send me a reading list, just compare them and tell me where the analogy breaks.
