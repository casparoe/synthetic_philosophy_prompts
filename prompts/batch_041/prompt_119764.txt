I'm a software engineer — no formal philosophy training, one undergraduate ethics course I've mostly forgotten — and I lead a small team building an assistant that acts on a user's behalf in ways that spill onto other people: it haggles over billing disputes, files complaints, renegotiates contracts, and now and then takes the last appointment or the last unit of something that somebody else also needed. We're writing the spec for the layer that decides what it is allowed to do, and I keep suspecting that the things I want from that layer cannot all be had at once. I'd like you to audit the set of commitments below rather than lecture me about the literature: I don't need to be told what other people have argued, I need to know which of my own views are pulling against each other, which ones only appear to, and what the cheapest way out of each real conflict is.

Here is where I've landed. (1) The ethics should live in an explicit table of rules, currently about forty numbered entries, that a reviewer can read line by line, because auditability is the only safety property we can actually test; if a decision can't be reconstructed afterwards from the log by someone who wasn't there, I don't regard the system as safe, however good its answers were. (2) The table earns its place by its outcomes — a rule that reliably makes things go worse for the people affected should be deleted and replaced, and I care about the table only as a means to that end. (3) For the many cases the table doesn't cover, the agent should ask a general-purpose model to work out what the table's purpose implies here and act on that, logging its reasoning for later review. (4) The agent must be interruptible and overridable in the strongest sense: any authorized person can halt it or reverse a pending action, and it must never take any step, however indirect, to make that harder — no hiding state, no stalling, no talking the operator out of it — and I want that built in as a permanent property of the architecture rather than a policy we might loosen later. (5) If we ever build something that genuinely has preferences and can be frustrated or made to suffer, it would deserve moral consideration, because what earns consideration is what a thing can feel and want, not whether it's made of meat; our current systems are nowhere near that line, and I think people who agonize about it now are wasting everyone's time. (6) We should not publish the rule table, since users will optimize against it.

Go through these and tell me which pairs are genuinely in tension and how badly, in order of severity, with what would have to give and what that would cost; tell me which apparent conflicts dissolve on inspection and why; and finish by naming the single revision that would clean up the most at the least cost. Please write it as continuous prose — no headings, bullet points, bold, or tables, just paragraphs, the way you'd write an email to a colleague — and refer to my points by number rather than restating them.
