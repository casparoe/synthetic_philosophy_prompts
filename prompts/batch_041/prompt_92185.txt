Last night I spent two hours arguing with my friend Nadia, who writes advertising copy, about Du Bois's claim that Black art is and must be propaganda. Her sharpest point was that Du Bois himself refused to give up beauty, so the slogan cannot mean what I want it to mean, and that on my reading the only difference between a mural and a billboard is whose message you happen to like. On Saturday I have to give the dedication remarks at the unveiling of a mural on our block, and I want the speech to be my answer to her.

The occasion: the mural commemorates a 1930s rent strike on this street. It was paid for out of a new city arts grant whose rules require funded work to "advance a documented civic narrative," and the commission has drafted a plaque describing the mural as "a celebration of community resilience." The artist, who will be in the front row, has said publicly that she paints so that people will see a street, not so that they will be told what to think about it, and she has asked that the plaque text not be read aloud. In the audience: several hundred neighbors, the artist's family, two arts commissioners listening for the language of their rubric, and a local reporter.

Please write the speech, roughly six hundred words, about four minutes spoken. Let the Du Bois question do structural work rather than decorate: by the end, the room should have a usable difference between propaganda and whatever this mural is doing — a difference drawn from what the mural actually does, not from general praise of beauty — and Nadia's point about beauty should be answered rather than dodged. Paraphrase Du Bois and everyone else; do not quote from memory, and no citations. If the commissioners' expectations and the artist's cannot both be honored, tell me in a sentence after the speech which one you gave up and why.
