My friend and I spent most of last night arguing about laws of nature, and I've come away unable to tell how much of his case is a genuine objection to the Humean picture and how much is a caricature that happens to be stated with confidence. He put his position roughly like this:

'Humeans think laws of nature are just patterns in what happens. The world is a vast mosaic of local facts, and a law is a true generalization that gives the most economical summary of the whole mosaic. So on this view the generalization that every fork in my kitchen drawer is stainless steel counts as a law, since it's a true universal generalization; the only extra ingredient is that some summaries are simpler and stronger than others, and we should prefer those. Nothing in the world makes anything happen — laws don't push or constrain, they are descriptions we give, and whatever explanatory work they do is just the economy of the description. Anti-Humeans think a law is a relation of real necessity between universals, a connection in nature that makes the regularity hold, so it explains the regularity instead of merely recording it. The fatal objection to Humeanism is that it can't distinguish laws from coincidences, and since science discovers laws that do explanatory work, and genuine explanation requires more than a summary, Humeanism is refuted. In the end it's just Hume's point about induction: the Humean is someone who says we can't know the future will resemble the past, wearing metaphysical costume.'

I'd like an audit rather than a rebuttal. Go through this sentence by sentence and tell me which parts a careful Humean would sign, which are simplifications a defender would call unfair, and which are straightforwardly wrong. Is the anti-Humean half a fair picture of that side, or does it flatten a family of positions into one? And does his closing sentence — that the dispute reduces to the problem of induction — follow from anything true in the rest, or is it a separate and much more dubious claim?
