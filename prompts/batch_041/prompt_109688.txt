I chair the acquisitions committee at a small contemporary art museum — five of us, maybe $200k a year to spend, eight or nine acquisitions, minutes go to the city because half our funding is public, so every purchase has to be defensible in one sentence. We've drifted into time-based and performance work and thats broken our process, because for a lot of what we now buy there's nothing to stand in front of: you buy a score and the right to stage it, or a nine hour video nobody on the committee has sat through, or a piece that only exists as documentation of a night that already happened.

Our charter says the collection should be built on works of the highest quality, and I've realized that's a criterion none of us can honestly apply to anything we haven't experienced. There's an old idea in aesthetics — the acquaintance principle — that you can't genuinely judge a work's aesthetic value without experiencing it for yourself, and that someone else's verdict that a thing is beautiful or moving doesn't transfer to you as a reason to believe it. If that's right, then a good chunk of our acquisitions are being decided on grounds we can't state out loud.

Please write me a decision procedure the committee can actually run at the table, in order, on any proposal: what to ask first, what counts as a sufficient answer to each question, where it stops with a no, and where it hands the decision to a person because the question is values rather than facts. It has to keep the distinctions that matter — between testimony about facts (provenance, condition, the artist's standing, whether a piece fits our holdings) and testimony that something is good, and between a work we've all seen and one we can't see at all — without turning into a lecture. We meet for 90 minutes, one of us is the only person who knows anything about video, and the treasurer has to sign off, so it has to be usable by people with no philosophy.

Then run it on three proposals: a nine hour video that only the curator who proposed it has watched, and she says it's the best thing the artist has ever done; a performance work we'd own as a score plus rights, where two members saw different versions and came away loving and hating it; and a work nobody can see before we buy, because it's locked up in a private collection, cheap and historically significant, and everyone wants it. One of the three should come out against what I want. Also tell me where the procedure gets gamed — I'm worried someone will launder a taste verdict into a fact about art history to sneak it past step one — and what it flatly can't do.
