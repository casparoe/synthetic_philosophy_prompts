I want a considered third-party verdict rather than another round of an argument I had last night with a close friend, and I'd rather you reason in the open than hand me a verdict. We were at dinner — he ordered the steak, I ordered the risotto, which he found funny — and his case, stated as well as he could state it, was this: a single individual's decision to eat one more meat-containing meal, take one more transatlantic flight, or cast one vote in a national election makes no difference to any outcome anyone could care about; no additional animal is bred, no additional ton of carbon enters the atmosphere, no election turns on it; and since individual obligations attach to what individuals can affect, no obligation attaches to these acts, however bad the aggregate. My reply was that this mistakes the moral structure of the case, but I could not locate the mistake precisely, and I'd like you to act as a philosopher who works on individual responsibility for collective harms — careful, unhurried, willing to distinguish claims that are usually run together — and give me your credence in one proposition, decomposed. The proposition is a conjunction: a typical individual act of eating one meat-containing meal is pro tanto morally wrong, and its wrongness does not depend on its making a marginal difference to the number of animals raised or killed. Report your credence in the conjunction, but arrive at it as P(wrong) decomposed into P(the act makes some marginal causal difference) times P(wrong | it does) plus the complement times P(wrong | it does not) — and feel free to tell me that decomposition is the wrong frame if you think it is, provided you then say what frame you'd use and why.

Spend a few sentences first brainstorming the space of sub-questions, then commit to a table of at most eight rows, one per sub-question, each with a rough probability and one line of justification. Candidate sub-questions, which you may keep, drop, split, or supplement: the supply-chain granularity question, i.e. whether production actually adjusts at the margin to one meal or instead moves in batch sizes and contract cycles that make a single purchase causally inert; whether causal contribution is necessary for wrongdoing at all, or whether complicity, authorization, or the sustaining of an institution can be wrong in its own right; whether there is a threshold or trigger structure such that each act is a ticket in a lottery over which act crosses the line, so that tiny individual contributions inherit the significance of the crossing; whether the relevant unit is the act, the pattern of consumption over a life, or the person's character; whether an act can be wrong as the expression of a policy one could not will universally even when it changes nothing; and whether the difference between being part of a cause and being part of an occasion on which a harm occurs is doing real work or only rhetorical work. Then give the combined number, name the single sub-question your number is most sensitive to, and say what argument or piece of evidence would move it most. Keep the whole response under 500 words, lead with your final credence as a number before the table, and do not hedge the number itself — hedge the terms.
