I am a software engineer with no formal training in philosophy, and I would like your help diagnosing a dispute that has stalled in the reading group I attend. Please answer in British English, and please treat the request as one for a diagnosis rather than a verdict: I want to understand what kind of disagreement this is, not which side is right.

The dispute concerns a view that says we should give priority to the worse off, in the sense that a benefit to someone badly off matters more, morally, than the very same benefit to someone already doing well. I believe the view is called prioritarianism, and that it is usually contrasted with egalitarianism, the view that equality is good in itself. The objection meant to separate the two is the levelling-down objection. If equality is good in itself, then a world in which the better off are dragged down to the level of the worse off, with nobody made better off, is an improvement; and that is supposed to be absurd. The prioritarian replies that this is precisely why they do not value equality as such: levelling down makes things worse, because it lowers somebody's well-being and nobody gains. So the prioritarian claims to escape the objection, leaving the egalitarian holding it.

What I cannot understand is why that does not settle the matter. In the group, the egalitarian restates the objection and the prioritarian restates the escape, and nobody moves. Here is my hypothesis, offered as a hypothesis for you to test rather than assume. The two sides do not actually disagree about whether levelling down is a good thing; they both agree that it is not. They disagree about what the objection is a test of. The prioritarian hears it as asking whether a view treats equality as valuable in itself, and answers no, on purpose. The critic hears it as asking whether a view can register the complaint of the worse off, which is that they are worse off than others, a complaint that is essentially comparative, and answers that a view which makes the value of a benefit depend only on the recipient's own level, never on how it compares with anyone else's, cannot register that complaint at all. If that is right, the impasse is two debates under one heading.

I would like you to tell me what kind of disagreement this actually is. Is it verbal, turning on what the objection is taken to mean? Is it substantive, turning on the grounds of value? Is there a hidden question about whether well-being can even be measured on a scale comparable between people? I notice that the prioritarian weighting looks to me like nothing more than a concave function applied to each person's well-being before summing, and if that is all it is, I cannot see how it differs from a certain kind of utilitarianism, nor what fixes how much priority the view grants. Or is it a clash of starting intuitions that neither side can argue for?

Two cases sharpen it for me. In the first, the better off are lowered to the level of the worse off and nobody gains; both sides agree this is worse, so I take it the case proves nothing. In the second, the worse off gain a little while the better off lose a great deal, so that the total falls; I understand the prioritarian to say that the second arrangement can still be better, and a colleague calls that levelling down in all but name, while the prioritarian insists it is not levelling down at all, since somebody gained. I would like to know whether the second case is really the first objection wearing a disguise.

Given your diagnosis, tell me what would count as progress, whether a distinction, a formal result, or a change of question, or, if you think none should be expected, say so and explain why. I am not asking for a survey of the literature or for reading recommendations; I am asking you to diagnose the structure of the disagreement and to justify the diagnosis by what each side actually says.
