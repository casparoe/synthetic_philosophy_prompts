Ok so I've been going down a bit of a rabit hole on the philosophy of time and I want to try a counterfactual. The modern fight is roughly this: presentists say only the present moment exists, and the past and future are literally nothing rather than just dark or empty. Eternalists (block-universe people) say past, present and future are all equally real and just laid out there, with the "flow" of time being either an illusion or a fact about our perspective on it, sort of like how "here" is just wherever the speaker happens to be. Then there are the middle positions: the growing block, where the past and present are real but the future hasn't come into being yet, and the moving spotlight, where all of it is real but a spotlight of nowness slides along. A lot of the modern pressure on presentism comes from physics, since once you accept relativity, simultaneity is frame-relative and there's no single cosmic "now" left to be the privileged moment.

Now drop William James into that. He died in 1910, before any of this was a standard debate, but he had a bunch of views that seem relevant: the present we actually experience isn't a point but a stretch of duration (he said the practically cognized present is "no knife-edge, but a saddle-back"), he was a pluralist who thought chance and genuine novelty were real features of the world rather than illusions, and he was a pragmatist who always wanted to know what practical difference a metaphysical dispute makes.

So where would James land? Presentist, eternalist, growing block, or would he say the question is confused and badly posed? Which of his commitments are doing the work here, and does the specious present actually cut against the whole framing, since presentism wants a durationless now and nobody has ever experienced one?
