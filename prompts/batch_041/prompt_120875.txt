**Question 3.**

Call a world *large* if it contains very many agents in causally disconnected regions, such that no agent can affect or observe more than a tiny fraction of the others, and a substantial fraction of those agents are running decision procedures that are identical to one another, or close variants of one another, having arisen from similar selection or training processes.

Consider two agents, A and B, in such a world, in regions that cannot interact. Each must adopt one of two policies. If both adopt COOPERATE, each does well. If both adopt DEFECT, each does badly. If exactly one adopts COOPERATE, the defector does best and the cooperator worst. A cannot affect B's payoffs and will never observe B's choice; A knows only that B is running a decision procedure of the same type as A's own.

It has been argued that A may nevertheless have reason to adopt COOPERATE, on the ground that A's choice is evidence about B's. Compare this evidential cooperation with the cooperation that sustains a repeated interaction between two neighbours who can observe each other. Is the large-world case merely the repeated-interaction case with an unusual mechanism of information transmission, or is the difference deeper? State the assumptions each argument requires, and identify the most serious objection to the large-world argument.

---

**Script (candidate 4).**

"The two cases are different in kind, and the difference matters for what the argument can establish.

In a repeated interaction, cooperation is sustained because my present choice affects my neighbour's future choice: he observes what I do and responds to it. My cooperating today is a cause of his cooperating tomorrow. The argument therefore needs two things that have nothing to do with decision procedures: that defection be detectable, and that the interaction continue long enough, and be valued enough, that the future gains from mutual cooperation outweigh the one-off gain from defecting.

In the large world nothing I do can reach the other agent, so no such conditions can be met, and yet the case for cooperating is not obviously weaker. The argument rests instead on the fact that A and B are running the same decision procedure. Since B's choice is an output of the same function that produces A's, A's choice is evidence about what that function yields when it is run on B's situation. What is doing the work is not P(B cooperates | A cooperates), understood as a causal effect of the one on the other, but the fact that the policy itself is a shared object. For this to be sound, two conditions must hold. First, the correlation must run through the shared procedure and not through a shared environment: if A and B merely happen to have observed the same signal, then conditioning on A's choice tells A nothing about B's choice beyond what the signal already told it. Second, A and B must have the same preferences over the outcomes. If their interests diverge, the policy that is best from A's point of view is not the policy that is best from B's, and there is nothing to be gained by cooperating with an agent who wants a different world.

Granting these conditions, the conclusion follows: defecting is irrational in the large world even though the interaction is one-shot and unobservable. The most serious objection is that the argument proves too much. If correlation of decision procedures is all that is required, then any agent whose policy is correlated with mine is a partner, including agents whose aims I deplore and whose success I would regard as a disaster; the argument seems to license sacrifices on behalf of strangers whose projects are irrelevant to my values or opposed to them. And nothing in the argument bounds the size of the sacrifice, so it is unclear why the reasoning stops at small costs rather than recommending that I hand over everything I have to any correlated party.

The right thing to say is that the large-world argument is a limiting case of the repeated-interaction argument. The mechanism by which the other party comes to act as I would like has changed, from causal influence to evidential correlation, but the structure of the reasoning — cooperate because your choice stands in the right relation to his — is the same."
