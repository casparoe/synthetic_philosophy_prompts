Analyze Bem's "Feeling the Future" paper (JPSP, 2011) as a case study in the frequentist/Bayesian split -- not as a debate about whether psi is real. Here's the shape of it, since you can't look any of this up: Bem ran nine experiments, most of them landing at p < .05 under ordinary NHST; Wagenmakers and co. published a comment in the same issue saying the whole result was an artifact of one-sided p-values, and that default Bayes factors computed on the same data actually favored the null; Rouder and Morey then did a Bayes factor meta-analysis over all nine and reported that the data moved the odds by something like 40:1, which they called negligible given how implausible psi is a priori; Bem's side shot back that the default Bayes factors were the real artifact. I want the mapping done carefully: for each framework, what exactly did those data license Bem to claim, and where does the disagreement actually live -- in the likelihood, in the prior, in the stopping rule, or in what counts as a single experiment? Then run it in both directions. Does the episode show frequentism is broken, given that the most damaging critique was itself frequentist (the p-values should have been two-sided) and peer review waved the paper through anyway? Or does it show the Bayseian critique was doing disguised prior work and would have rejected psi no matter what the numbers looked like -- and if so, is the "40:1 is negligible" move honest calibration or unfalsifiability with math? And does the repliction wave that followed settle which side was right, or does it just mean the original studies were noisy? End with a verdict: was this a statistics failure, a peer review failure, or two frameworks answering different questions while the real fight was over the prior?
