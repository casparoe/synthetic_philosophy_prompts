I have been chewing over the dogmatism paradox for a while, and I would like to see it run against a real episode rather than the usual invented case. The paradox, as I reconstruct it, has three plausible-looking steps. First, if I know that P, then P is true. Second, if P is true, then any evidence I later encounter that counts against P must be misleading evidence — it points away from the truth. Third, if I can know that a piece of evidence is misleading, then I am entitled to disregard it, or at least to give it no evidential weight. Close the argument up — you will need something like the closure of knowledge, or the thought that what is known cannot be rationally undermined — and it seems that knowing P licenses me in dismissing, unexamined, every consideration that turns up against P. That conclusion is the counsel of a crank, yet none of the steps is easy to surrender. I do not want a solution to the paradox. I want to watch it do work.

The episode I have in mind is the reception of the bacterial theory of peptic ulcers. The facts, as I understand them, are these. In the early 1980s the Australian pathologist Robin Warren and the physician Barry Marshall, working in Perth, found spiral bacteria in stomach biopsies and proposed that a bacterium — later named Helicobacter pylori — was a major cause of gastritis and peptic ulcers. The settled view held that ulcers came from stress, diet and excess acid, and that no bacterium could survive the stomach's acidity; the stomach was assumed to be more or less sterile. Warren and Marshall's early paper was rejected by their national gastroenterological society, and ranked by its reviewers in the bottom tenth of that year's submissions. Marshall went on to drink a broth of cultured bacteria himself in 1984 and promptly developed gastritis; the pair were eventually awarded the Nobel Prize. It is worth adding that some historians of the episode insist the medical establishment showed not dogmatism but a defensible scientific scepticism, holding out for more evidence before abandoning a well-supported theory.

Treat both sides as agents inside the paradox, and answer the following. Who, on the establishment's side, is the knower, and what would the proposition known have to be for the first two steps to apply? If the received view really was known, does the paradox then license the reviewers in disregarding Marshall's evidence without examination — and if it does, is that a reductio of the paradox or an honest description of good scientific practice? Now run it the other way. Marshall was confident his hypothesis was correct and pressed on against near-universal rejection; by the paradox's own lights, if he knew, he was entitled to dismiss his critics. But from the inside he could not distinguish knowing from merely believing, and neither could they. Say what that symmetry does to the paradox.

I would like you to be strict about the mapping and honest about its limits. Which features of the episode does the paradox capture — the closure step, the retrospective identification of misleading evidence, the asymmetry between the winning and the losing side — and which does it misdescribe? Does it conflate dogmatism with ordinary evidential conservatism, or is that distinction simply unavailable to it? The paradox treats knowledge as static and evidence as arriving in clean units to a single mind; the case involves institutions, reputations, technique-dependent evidence, and a verdict that can only be reached after the fact. Where the paradox is silent, say so rather than forcing it to speak. Finally, tell me what the episode shows about the paradox: a confirmation, an anomaly, or a case it never anticipated.
