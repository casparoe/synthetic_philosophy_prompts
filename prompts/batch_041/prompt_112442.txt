For the past few months I have been sitting in on a reading group that is working through the abolitionist project associated with David Pearce: the proposal that biotechnology, rather than politics or moral persuasion, should be used to abolish suffering throughout the living world — wild animals included — by rewriting genomes and eventually redesigning whole ecosystems, so that pain is replaced by some engineered signal that carries information without the unpleasantness. I admire the ambition, but what really holds me is the underlying ethics: that suffering is the thing to be minimised, that its badness does not depend on whose suffering it is or on whether its cause is natural or human, and that we therefore stand under a standing obligation to reduce it wherever it occurs. A friend in the group, however, has produced a short argument which he says shows that anyone who takes that ethics seriously must end up somewhere none of us is willing to go, and although I am confident he has gone wrong somewhere, I cannot for the life of me see where. It runs as follows.

(1) Suffering is bad, and it is the only thing that is intrinsically bad; pleasure and every other apparent good are valuable only insofar as they bear on suffering. (2) If something is intrinsically bad, there is a reason to prevent it, and the weight of that reason depends solely on how much of it there would otherwise be. (3) That reason is impartial in three respects: it does not privilege my suffering over a stranger's, a human's over a non-human animal's, or the present over the future. (4) Sentient life left to itself generates suffering on an enormous scale — predation, starvation, disease, the slow attrition of age — because natural selection optimises for reproduction and not for contentment, and this will continue for as long as there are creatures capable of suffering. (5) Hence whatever most reduces the total quantity of suffering in the world is what we are required to pursue. (6) The most reliable way to reduce that total to zero is to bring the number of future sufferers to zero — that is, to allow no new sentient beings ever to come into existence. (7) Therefore the consistent abolitionist is committed to extinctionism, a humane winding-down of all sentient life, and not to the engineered paradise that Pearce actually wants. (8) Since that conclusion is unacceptable, one of (1) to (3) must be false.

He insists that the culprit is (5), the slide from reducing suffering to reducing the number of sufferers, and he takes this to show that the whole framework is confused. I would much rather keep (1) and (3) if I possibly can, since negative utilitarianism of some kind is the only thing that makes the abolitionist project more than a hobby. So: which step should I actually give up? If it is one of the premises, tell me precisely what it costs me — in particular, whether I can abandon it and still hold that we are obliged to intervene against suffering in the wild. And if it is (5) or (6), explain exactly where the mistake lies, and why it is a mistake rather than an uncomfortable truth.
