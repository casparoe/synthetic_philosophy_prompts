I'm a philosophy prof (mostly phil of science, though I keep drifting into social ontology) and a colleague in the history dept has talked me into running one 90-minute session of her grad seminar on "contingency and necessity in history." My angle is path dependence -- the whole cluster of ideas around history getting locked in, and the question of whether the same outcome would have arrived anyway. The students are smart but mixed: some historians, a couple of economists, nobody with much formal apparatus, and they've all read a bit of popular stuff on the topic, so they'll arrive with the QWERTY story half-remembered and the word "inevitable" doing a lot of silent work.

Don't explain path dependence to me -- plan the session. I want the order the pieces have to go in, i.e. what has to be in place before the question "would the same outcome have arrived anyway" is even well-posed. My suspicion is that you can't ask it until they've genuinely got the multiple-equilibria picture, and they can't have that until they've felt the increasing-returns / positive-feedback mechanism as a mechanism and not just as a slogan -- but tell me if I've got the dependency backwards, or if there's something that has to come before both. Then the one example to carry the whole 90 minutes. I'd rather not do QWERTY, partly because it's overexposed and partly because the claim that QWERTY was actually inferior is exactly the sort of thing I don't want them swallowing unexamined, so propose something else with the right shape (a small early accident, positive feedback, two or more stable configurations, and a surviving rival that lost) and tell me what the example must have and must not have -- or argue me back into QWERTY if the alternatives are worse. Also: the misconceptions they'll bring or form, and exactly where in the sequence each one gets headed off. Off the top of my head I'd guess (i) path dependence = "history matters", (ii) lock-in = literally irreversible, (iii) path dependence = the outcome is inefficient, (iv) contingency = randomness, no causes -- plus whatever I'm missing. And give me one question to put to them at the end that would actually seperate a student who has the idea from one who has only learned the vocabulary, plus what I should explicitly refuse to settle in the room at this level.

One more thing, and I care about this alot: flag separately which parts of the plan rest on claims that are genuinely contested among people who work on this -- how common lock-in really is, whether the classic inefficiency story survives its critics, whether the counterfactual is empirically answerable at all -- as opposed to things that are pretty settled. I don't want to stand up there and present a live controversy as the textbook line, and I'd like to know which bits to mark as "we're taking a position here."
