I recently spent an afternoon at a departmental open day with a friend who is finishing a PhD in philosophy, and over lunch I asked her what she actually works on. She said her thesis is about material constitution, and she walked me through the statue-and-clay problem roughly as follows. A sculptor makes a statue out of a lump of clay; a week later someone squashes it back into a shapeless heap. The statue, she said, has ceased to exist; the clay has not. The temptation is to conclude that there were two objects on the pedestal all along, a statue and a lump, distinct things occupying the same place at the same time. That, she said, is a mistake, and Leibniz's law shows why: the statue weighs exactly what the clay weighs, is composed of exactly the same molecules, sits in exactly the same place, and has exactly the same shape and colour — there is no property the one has that the other lacks, so there is one thing there, not two. The only genuine puzzle, she continued, is that identity in these cases is relative: the same object counts as a statue under one sortal and as a lump of clay under another, so a question like "does it survive being squashed?" has no answer until you specify what sort of thing you are asking about. On her telling, the whole literature on constitution is largely an artefact of philosophers failing to notice that they are asking a question about words rather than about the world; and she added, almost as an afterthought, that once you see the statue is nothing over and above the molecules, the idea that some one privileged collection of molecules is "the statue" falls away too, which shows how much of the debate is an illusion generated by our concepts.

I have no philosophical training — I write backend software for a living — and my instinct is to agree with her, partly because the closest thing I have to a worked example is the way a file on disk and the particular sequence of bytes it is stored as seem to be one thing under two descriptions. But I have learned to be suspicious of confident one-liners about philosophy, and something about her account feels like it is doing several different things at once, so I would like an audit. Please go through it sentence by sentence and tell me which parts a specialist in this area would accept as accurate, which are defensible but simplified in ways that matter, and which are straightforwardly wrong or question-begging. In particular: does the appeal to Leibniz's law really settle the matter, or does it assume something that is itself in dispute? Is the point about relative identity the same point as the point about constitution, or a different and stronger one — and does it sit comfortably alongside the Leibniz's law argument she gave just before it? Does the conclusion that the dispute is merely verbal follow from either of those points, or is it an independent thesis that could be denied by someone who accepted everything else she said? And does her closing remark about the molecules support her conclusion, or does it cut against it?

Please also say what the philosophers who defend the two-object view would say in reply — I want to know whether their position is a live option or a confusion, and if you think my friend is wrong, what the most defensible version of a "constitution without identity" view actually comes to. Finally, give me your own verdict on the squashed-statue case: was there one thing on the pedestal, two, or is the question itself somehow defective? And tell me whether my file-and-bytes analogy is any help here or whether it quietly imports assumptions that the statue case does not license.
