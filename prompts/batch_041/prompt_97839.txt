I'm in a graduate seminar on the philosophy of the social sciences, and one of my classmates keeps invoking Rorty with a line I can only half-reproduce from memory — something to the effect that truth is whatever our peers will let us get away with saying. She has now used it twice, and I can't tell whether the two uses even rest on the same claim. The first time, she deployed it to declare that an argument two of us were having — over whether a model in our econometrics reading actually represents the labour market or merely forecasts it well — was a pseudo-dispute: the model either earns its keep or it doesn't, and asking whether it mirrors anything is a leftover from what she calls the mirror-of-nature picture of knowledge. The second time, in a session on objectivity in journalism, she used it to reassure someone that we needn't lose sleep over objectivity at all, since justification is always relative to an audience and solidarity is enough. I'm fairly confident the first use and the second use are not the same thesis, and that the second is far stronger and far less defensible than the first, but I can't lay out the space of positions clearly enough to say why.

What I'd like is a menu of the precise theses that this slogan could be standing in for, ordered from readings so weak that nobody could object to them, through the recognisably Rortyan antirepresentationalist ones, to readings that amount to a flat denial that our sentences answer to anything at all — and for each reading, an assessment of whether it is plausible and whether it would actually license the moves my classmate made on each occasion. I keep conflating three different kinds of claim and would like them kept apart: claims about what the word truth means or does, claims about the nature of justification and who gets to grant it, and claims about what there is in the world. Please answer as a numbered list of exactly five items, each one to two sentences, each item naming one reading and giving your verdict both on the reading itself and on which of my classmate's two uses, if either, it would support, with no introduction or conclusion.
