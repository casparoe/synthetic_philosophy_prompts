Ok so I've fallen down a rabbit hole about these lawsuits I never knew existed, and I can't let it go. There's two kinds and I keep mixing them up so bare with me. In one, the parents sue a doctor because he didn't warn them or didn't run some test, and they say if we'd known we would of ended the pregnancy. Courts in a lot of places actually let the parents win that one. In the other kind, the kid themself — grown up by then, or through a guardian — sues and says I shouldn't of been born, my whole life is the injury. And courts almost always throw that one out. The reasoning is the part that's got me stuck. They say you can't treat being alive as a harm, that whatever the kid's suffering, existing is better than the alternative, or that there's nothing to compare it against because non-existence isn't a state you can be worse off in. So: being born is never something that happened to you in a way that hurt you.

But the same legal system, same courthouse, will turn right around and say death is the biggest harm there is. They'll let a family sue for wrongful death, they call it a loss, they punish people hard for causing it, there's even damages for loss of enjoyment of life when somebody gets injured. So one minute non-existence is not a harm and can't be compared to life, and the next minute life is so valuable that ending it is the worst thing you can do to someone.

Somewhere in there I read about a philosopher, Benatar I think, who says there's this asymetry: it's good to spare someone pain even if there's nobody around to enjoy the not-pain, but it's not bad to miss out on pleasure unless there's actually somebody there to be deprived of it. And he says that adds up to coming into existence is never a benefit but can still be a harm. Which seems like the opposite of what the courts assume? Or maybe it's the same idea pointed the other way? Honestly I can't tell.

What I want to know is this. What view about the value of existence are these rulings actually commited to — is there a real theory in there or is it just hand-waving to dodge the hard cases? Does the practice really need that you can't compare existence with non-existence move, or is there some other theory of what can count as a harm to a person at all that would give the same answers without it? And does the whole thing hang together, because it really looks like the courts are running two different theories of harm, one for the kid and one for the corpse. If you took them at their word and swapped the underlying view for Benatar's, what would actually have to change — would anybody owe anybody anything, and would the wrongful death side have to shrink? Last thing: tell me which parts are genuinely forced on them by the practice and which parts they could swap out without anything changing, and where the practice is just underdetermined and a few different theories would all fit.
