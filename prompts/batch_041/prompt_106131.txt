Last Friday my girlfriend dragged me to her alumni fundraiser, and between the speeches they ran one of those auctions where they sell a twenty dollar bill — highest bidder pays what they bid and gets the twenty, second-highest bidder also pays what they bid and gets nothing. I'd heard of these but never actually watched one, and it was definately the strangest hour of my year: it ended with a guy paying forty-one dollars for the twenty and a woman in the front row paying forty for nothing, and both of them looked like they'd won a war. In the car home my friend Beth and my friend Ravi went at it the entire drive and neither would give an inch, and because I'd been the one narrating the whole thing to them all night they decided I should be the referee, which I can't be. Beth's line was that it was the sunk cost fallacy with an audience — everyone should have stopped the second the bidding passed twenty, and the two at the end should be embarrassed rather than smug, the guy especially, he paid forty-one for a twenty and he's strutting around like he pulled something off. Ravi said that's not the sunk cost fallacy at all. Put yourself in the runner-up's chair at forty, deciding whether to go to forty-one: if she bids and wins she's out twenty-one, if she bids and loses she's out forty-one, if she doesn't bid she's out forty. The forty is identical in every branch, which is exactly what sunk means, so it isn't doing any of the work — what's doing the work is that bidding forty-one is a genuine bet, risking one more dollar for a real shot at getting nineteen back. That's a wager, not a fallacy, and you can't tell someone to ignore the past and then complain when she looks at the future, where quitting is the single most expensive option on the table.

Beth came back with: the other bidder is running the identical calculation, and he knows she's running it, and she knows he knows, so the chance he folds isn't something she gets to bank on — and anyway the argument proves far too much, since the same reasoning was sitting there at twenty-five and thirty-five and would still be sitting there at four hundred, and a reason to keep bidding that never once tells you to stop isn't a reason, it's a story you tell yourself while you lose. Her one concession was that if you're going to keep going, a big jump bid — straight to sixty or a hundred, so the other person's next bid really hurts — is the only version of continuing with any strategy in it, and neither of them thought of that. Ravi said a step being defensible at every point while the whole thing is a catastrophe is just what a trap is — nobody calls a prisoner's dilemma a fallacy, we call it a bad game — and that blaming these two for not finding a magic exit is like blaming someone for losing at a rigged carnival game; the real error, if there is one, is sitting down, and even that isn't obvious, because a person who sits down with a hard rule can walk off with a twenty for five bucks. Beth said nobody in that room had a rule, and what makes it a personal mistake and not just a sad structure is that you know perfectly well what you'd tell a friend beforehand — never go past twenty, whatever happens, get up and walk — so a person who keeps bidding is doing something they'd never have endorsed in advance and wouldn't recommend to anybody else. Ravi said a rule is only worth anything if it's credible, and credibility isn't free: it means being the sort of person who genuinely prefers eating the forty to bidding the forty-one, which costs you every single time it gets tested, so the fix Beth wants might not even exist at the level of one person. That's when I made the mistake of mentioning that my club was thinking of running one of these every month with the same crowd, and apparently I'm supposed to settle that too.

So can you take their exchange apart move by move and say who's right about which specific step — not just who wins overall — and what each of them is missing? And then tell me whether repeating it monthly with the same people makes the escalation better or worse, because they never got to that and I need an answer before Thursday.
