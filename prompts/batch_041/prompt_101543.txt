Municipalities that adopt ranked-choice voting publish voter guides instructing electors to rank the candidates in the order they sincerely prefer, and the reform is sold as the end of the wasted vote: put your true favorite first, because if that candidate is eliminated your ballot will still count for your next choice. The same jurisdictions discard ballots that remain exhausted before the final round; the strategic advice circulating around these elections still turns on whether a supporter should leave a compromise off the ranking; and the counting rule is known to reward a candidate less, in some configurations, when more voters rank that candidate higher. What does the practice presuppose about what a ballot is for and about the kind of security a voter is being promised? Does the sincere-ranking advice require the counting rule to be immune to strategic ranking for any number of candidates, or only to satisfy the weaker promise that adding a lower preference never damages a higher one -- and can the institution be read as presupposing the weaker property while advertising the stronger? Identify which commitments the practice strictly needs, which it merely fits well with, and what would have to change in the voter guides if the weaker reading were made explicit.
