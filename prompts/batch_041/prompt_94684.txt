I've been going back and forth with a couple of friends about quantum suicide and it's starting to drive me a little nuts. One of them has basically convinced himself that under the many-worlds / Everett picture he should be willing to take any bet at all, no matter how bad the odds, as long as some branch somewhere pays out big — his line is that from the first-person perspective death just isn't an outcome you ever actually reach, so there's no real downside to gambling with your life. He's built this into a whole decision rule he calls "branch-maximizing rationality": when you face a choice, look at all the branches your choice splits into and pick the option whose single best branch is best for you, ignoring the weights entirely, on the grounds that weighting is what smuggles in the assumption that you could end up dead. I don't think he's right but I keep getting tangled trying to say why without just repeating that the Born rule makes your death near-certain, which he waves away as question-begging.

So what I want from you is an actual argument — not a survey of what other people have said, I've read enough of that — for the position that even if the Everett picture is correct, and even if some sense of "you" survives down every branch, this gives you no prudential reason to take lethal bets. Heres the distinction I want you to work with, because I think it's the crux: call immortality thin if it only means that there's always at least one successor branch containing a version of you, and thick if it means the surviving measure is substantial — that you should actually expect to keep existing, rather than that a copy somewhere does. My hunch is that thin immortality is true, thick is false, and that everything my friend wants follows only from thick; but I haven't nailed the step from the measure over branches to what I should expect for myself, and I'm not fully sure thin immortality is as cheap as I'm making it sound. So argue for that position if you can — and if you actually think my friend wins, don't fake it, just say so and argue his side instead. You can assume whatever you like about personal identity as long as you flag it, but I don't want the whole thing to turn on settling a theory of identity first; I want it to turn on the measure. Either way, finish by telling me which piece of your own reasoning you'd bet against, and what argument or consideration would genuinely move you to the other verdict — I'd much rather have an honest weak spot than a confident story.
