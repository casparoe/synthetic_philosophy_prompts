ok so I've been going down a rabbit hole on the old Gongsun Long "white horse" dialogue, the one where a guy argues that a white horse is not a horse — one argument being that horse is the word for the shape and white is the word for the color and the word for the color isn't the word for the shape, and another being that if you ask for a horse somebody can bring you a black one, but if you ask for a white horse they can't, so a white horse isn't a horse. (I gather the whole thing is really about what "is not" is up to there — not-the-same-as vs. not-a-member-of — and the two speakers may be using different senses. Also apparently "School of Names" is a later filing decision by Han librarians and the guys themselves were just called disputers, which is a fun detail.) Everyone files this under clever-but-pointless and I used to, but I started writing down my gut verdicts on the same sentence-form in English and now I can't tell which ones I'm supposed to give up. Roughly in order of confidence:

- a white horse is a horse (reading it as a claim about animals; if you read it as a claim about the two phrases, then sure, "white horse" isn't "horse", but that seems like a freebie that makes every case trivially true)
- a black duck is a duck
- a bad dancer is a dancer
- a dead parrot is a parrot (a dead one, but still a parrot)
- a stone lion is not a lion
- a toy duck is not a duck, and a rubber duck is not a duck
- a fake diamond is not a diamond
- a former senator is not a senator
- a lab-grown diamond IS a diamond — this one and the fake diamond one feel like they should be the same case and they definately aren't, and the only difference I can put into words is that one of them is really a diamond and the other is just pretending to be one, which I don't think counts as an explanation
- and two counting ones that seem to cut against everything above: "the zoo has three lions" is fine to say even when two of them are stone, and "there are four fake guns in the evidence bag and zero guns in the evidence bag" is also fine to say, which I take it shouldn't be if "is not a gun" means what it says

Candidate principles I keep cycling through: (a) some modifiers cancel the noun (fake, toy, stone, former) and some just narrow it (white, black, bad, dead) — but stone and white are both just properties the thing has, so I don't know what the difference is meant to be; (b) the modifier has to name a way of resembling, or of having been, or of being for show, rather than a way of being — that gets fake/toy/former/stone and leaves white/black/dead alone, but I worry that "resembling a diamond" vs "being a diamond" is just me asserting the answer; (c) it's all the "is not" ambiguity and my verdicts are quietly sliding between senses of "is". I'd rather not end up with a list of adjectives, so if it takes more than one clause tell me why.

What I want: the principle, or the smallest set of them, that gets the most of the pile, precise enough that I can apply it myself, with the case in the pile that's doing the motivating for each clause; what it predicts for cases I haven't listed — a wooden horse, a decoy duck, a forged passport, an ex-husband, a painting of a horse, a synthetic ruby, someone who bought his medical diploma; which of my verdicts it forces me to drop; and whether the old argument is the same phenomenon as the fake/toy cases or a different one. My suspicion is that the ask-for-a-horse bit is about what answers a request and not about kinds at all — run the same trick on "tall basketball player" and you'd prove a tall basketball player isn't a basketball player — but that the shape/color bit might be a real point about compound names that I'm not getting. The same crowd apparently also did "Today go to Yue but arrive yesterday", which feels like an entirely different genre of puzzle to me; if you can say what makes it different, I'd like that too.

Please rank the candidates by how much of my pile they actually save rather than how elegant they sound, and be straight with me about which verdict is the one that has to go. Last line: one sentence, starting "Principle:".
