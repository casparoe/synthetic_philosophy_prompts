Our reading group has spent most of a year on a question that looks trivial and has proved not to be: when we say of a large language model that it believes Paris is the capital of France, or that it suspects a character in a story is lying, what have we committed ourselves to? The exchange runs the same way every time. One side says the attribution is licensed by the intentional stance as Dennett describes it — if the stance yields predictions you cannot recover from a lower-level description, the belief is as real as anything else in science, and asking for a further fact about inner representation is precisely the confusion the stance was built to expose. The other side replies that this is fine as a heuristic and beside the point as an answer: the question was whether anything in the machine plays the role the belief plays in us, and predictive success is perfectly compatible with nothing there playing that role. Each side's rebuttal is the other's premise, and we can no longer tell whether we are disagreeing or talking past each other.

Two features of the case seem to make it worse than the ordinary version of this dispute. First, the usual route to belief attribution — asking the subject — is either unavailable or worthless, since the model's self-report is generated by the same process as the behaviour it would be evidence for. (It will also cheerfully deny having any beliefs at all, and neither side knows what to do with that: one calls it another utterance, the other calls it a datum.) Second, the dispute has a history that predates the machines: it resembles the old quarrel about whether posits in a special science are real or merely useful, which is not obviously something any fact about language models could settle.

Here is a piece of apparatus I want you to take seriously rather than treat as decoration. A state earns the name 'belief' only if it is separately intervenable — roughly, if you can change it while holding fixed both the stimulus that produced it and the response it produces, and observe downstream effects that are not just that response. The interventionist picture of positing representational states gives us a test with teeth: for a real believer there is a double dissociation between what the system takes the world to be and what it does, and the state occupying the middle of that dissociation is the belief. Applied to a language model, the question becomes whether any internal state is like that, or whether everything we can intervene on sits either on the input side (prompt tokens) or the output side (sampled text), with nothing mediating.

My hypothesis — offered as a hypothesis — is that we have two questions under one word. One is predictive: is there a stable, projectible pattern here that only belief-talk captures? The other is constitutive: is there a state with the right causal profile? The first is nearly settled in the affirmative and the second is nearly settled in the negative, and the reason we never move is that neither side will say which one it is answering. If that diagnosis is right, the debate is not exactly verbal — both questions are real and both answers are interesting — but the apparent conflict is an artefact of a single noun doing two jobs.

I want a diagnosis, not a survey, and I want it to engage the actual positions rather than a tidy reconstruction of them. Is my hypothesis right, or is there a genuinely substantive disagreement underneath that survives once the questions are separated — something about what counts as an explanation, or about which counterfactuals an attribution has to be sensitive to? Does the interventionist criterion help, or does it smuggle in a mechanistic assumption that the intentional stance is entitled to reject? Is a hidden empirical question doing the work — say, whether a model's behaviour is sensitive to semantically irrelevant features of the prompt, which would tell us whether it tracks the world or the distribution — and if so, what experiment would settle it? And then the part I actually want: given your diagnosis, what would count as progress here? A distinction, a formal result about when behaviourally equivalent systems must share states, an experiment, or an honest argument that this is one of the disputes where progress should not be expected and the right response is to stop asking?
