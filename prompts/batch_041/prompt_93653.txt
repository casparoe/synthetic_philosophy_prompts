For a seminar on democratic theory I have been working through the so-called boundary problem — the suspicion that a democracy cannot legitimately settle who belongs to it, since any such settlement seems to presuppose a demos that already contains the right people. A friend in the seminar proposed a homely illustration: a city council votes on whether to annex an adjacent unincorporated area, so that the residents there will henceforth be bound by the city's ordinances, taxed by it, and liable to its courts, yet they cast no ballot in the annexation vote because they are not yet citizens of the city. She says the example generalizes to every act of founding, secession, partition, and naturalization. I tried to write the worry out as a clean argument, and now I cannot find a step I am willing to give up, because the conclusion is one I refuse to accept. I would like you to tell me which step to reject, why it looked compelling in the first place, and what rejecting it commits me to.

(1) A rule has legitimate authority over a person only if it is authorized by a collective that includes her, or at least by a collective she has reason to count as her own.
(2) Any collective that authorizes rules is itself defined by a boundary rule that fixes who is inside it.
(3) A boundary rule is itself a rule: it is enacted and enforced, and it imposes real burdens — it can disenfranchise someone, subject her to deportation, conscript her, or tax her.
(4) So by (1), a boundary rule has legitimate authority over a person only if it is authorized by a collective that includes her.
(5) But the collective that would authorize a given boundary rule is precisely the collective that rule defines; its members must already be members in order to authorize the rule that makes them members.
(6) Hence no boundary rule can be authorized by the collective it constitutes, and by (1) no boundary rule has legitimate authority.
(7) But a state's ordinary laws are legitimate only if they are made by a legitimate demos; so no ordinary law is legitimate either, and there has never been a legitimate state.

I am not willing to accept (7). If forced to choose, I would rather keep (1), which seems to be the whole point of democratic legitimacy, than concede that legitimacy is a fiction. But I cannot see which of the other steps to drop without buying something that strikes me as worse. Where does the argument go wrong — is one of the steps doing work it is not entitled to, or is the argument valid and I simply have to pay up somewhere?
