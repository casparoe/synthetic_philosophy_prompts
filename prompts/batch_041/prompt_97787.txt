So my reading group has been stuck on 'what is life?' for months now and its driving me a bit nuts. The same moves keep getting made: someone says a virus isn't alive because it can't reproduce on its own, someone else says that's just parasitic dependency and plenty of undisputed organisms depend on their hosts, someone brings up mules or mitochondria, someone brings up fire or crystals or computer viruses, and then we all agree the cases are hard and go home. Nobody moves and I dont think it's because anyone in the room is being dumb.

My hunch — tell me if it's wrong — is that the fight isn't really about the facts of the borderline cases at all. I think there are at least four different things people are actually doing while all using the word 'life': (a) hunting for the one real essence, the thing that would make something alive whether or not anyone's around to say so; (b) treating 'alive' as a cluster or family-resemblance term, so the only question is whether the cluster is dense enough; (c) treating it as a practical or institutional category — a line we draw because it's useful for some purpose (biosecurity, ethics, funding, what goes in the curriculum) rather than because nature has a joint there; and (d) smuggling in a different question entirely, like whether the thing is an independent agent, or whether it's the kind of thing natural selection can act on, or whether it's a self-maintaining far-from-equilibrium system.

So which is it: a verbal dispute, several distinct questions wearing one word, or a genuinely substantive disagreement about the natural world that we just don't yet have the science to settle? And whichever it is, is there anything that would actually move it — a discovery (a second independent origin, say, or a real continuous gradient of almost-life), a formal result, or just a collective decision to stop asking the question in that form? One of us floated replacing the predicate 'alive' with a scalar 'degree of aliveness' — does that dissolve the problem or just relabel it? Give me a diagnosis with reasons, grounded in what each side actually says about the cases, not a survey of proposed definitions.
