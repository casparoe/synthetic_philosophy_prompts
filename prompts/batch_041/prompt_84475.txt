I have been trying to work out what I actually believe about Kant's universalizability test by going case by case rather than by adopting any formula off the shelf, and I would like to know what principle, if any, my verdicts add up to. Here they are.

1. Borrowing money on a promise to repay, when I know I cannot repay, because I need the money: impermissible. Universal adoption would make the promise useless for the very purpose for which I make it.
2. Declining every request to lend money, though I could easily afford to lend: permissible. A world in which nobody lends is bleak, but lending has not been made impossible in it, and I do not think I owe anyone a loan.
3. Accepting charity when I am in need while, as a settled policy, giving none: impermissible. This differs from (2), on my view, because it depends on a practice it does nothing to sustain.
4. Lying to a would-be murderer about where my friend is: permissible, and not merely excusable. I take this to rule out any reading that condemns every false statement.
5. Jumping the queue whenever the wait inconveniences me: impermissible, even though a world in which everyone did it contains queues — bad ones, not impossible ones.
6. Taking one extra flight for a holiday, knowing that my own seat's emissions are negligible and the aggregate effect serious: impermissible. Universal adoption is perfectly coherent, and that is precisely why the test as usually stated seems to me to let the wrong thing through.
7. Letting a talent of mine go undeveloped: permissible. I know this contradicts a famous case, and I am not persuaded by it.
8. Always being the last to speak in any conversation — or any similar maxim whose universal adoption is impossible in the way that everyone being the tallest is impossible: not wrong at all, merely impossible. So the impossibility of universalizing a maxim cannot by itself be what makes an act wrong.

The pattern I think I see is this: in every case I condemn, acting on the maxim would have me leaning on a practice, an expectation, or a forbearance that the maxim's universal adoption would undercut — and this holds even where the universalized world remains perfectly possible, as in (5) and (6). In the cases I permit, either nothing of the sort happens or the practice survives. So my provisional principle is: a maxim is impermissible just in case acting on it leaves me relying on something that its universal adoption would destroy. I offer this as a first attempt, not a settled view.

Test it against the eight verdicts and tell me what has to give. I am especially interested in three things.

First, does the principle actually deliver (6)? On the face of it no practice is undercut by one more passenger, so either the principle fails there or it needs a clause I have not thought of; if so, say what the clause is and what it costs.

Second, the maxim-description problem. Almost anything is universalizable if described thinly enough, and almost nothing is if described thickly. Described thinly, (5) is just acting so as to arrive sooner, and (6) is just travelling. Described thickly, (2) becomes a maxim of never helping anyone. What fixes the description of the maxim, and can that constraint be stated without appealing to the very verdicts it is meant to explain?

Third, what does the principle say about cases I have not ruled on: a maxim of never voting, on the ground that one vote is negligible; a maxim of eating meat whenever I like; a maxim of paying taxes only when evasion would be detected; and a maxim of breaking my own resolutions whenever keeping them becomes inconvenient, which is at least a test of whether the principle reaches maxims addressed only to myself.

If no single principle accommodates the verdicts, give me the smallest set that comes closest, state each precisely, and say honestly which of my verdicts none of them can accommodate. Finally, say what would change your mind: which single consideration, if it came out one way rather than the other, would move you from the principle you settle on to a different one, and where in your reasoning you think you are most likely to be wrong.
