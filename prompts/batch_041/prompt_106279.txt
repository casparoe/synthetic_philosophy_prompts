I'm a PhD student in philosophy of economics, finishing a chapter on intergenerational discounting, and I'd like an audit of whether my axiological commitments can be held together — not a literature survey, and not a defence of any one of them. Here is where I've landed:

(1) Pure time preference is ethically indefensible: the bare fact that a benefit or burden falls later is not itself a reason to weight it less, so I set the pure rate of time preference to zero.
(2) Stationarity, weak Pareto, and continuity: the ranking of two infinite consumption paths should be unchanged when every generation's consumption in both paths is shifted forward by one period; if a path is at least as good for every generation and better for some, it ranks higher; and the ranking should be continuous in the usual sense.
(3) Anonymity: no generation is intrinsically privileged by its position in time, so swapping the wellbeing of any two generations leaves the ranking of streams unchanged.
(4) The consumption discount rate used in cost–benefit analysis should be derived from the Ramsey rule, so with a zero pure rate every observed discounting is generated by growth in per-capita consumption and by uncertainty about the future.
(5) Prioritarianism: I give extra weight to the worse off and reject aggregative totalism, so adding many barely-worth-living people need not improve a state of affairs.
(6) A feasibility constraint: a principle of intergenerational justice must not demand unbounded sacrifice from the present generation.

My tentative reconciliation is to stop discounting by date and discount by rank instead — weight a generation according to how far down the wellbeing ordering it falls rather than according to when it lives — so that the weighting stays anonymous yet the sums remain finite. I want to know whether that genuinely reconciles (2) and (3) or merely relocates the difficulty.
