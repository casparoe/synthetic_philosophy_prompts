I'm a second-year PhD student in political theory, and this term I'm sitting in on a social choice seminar. We've spent the last three weeks on the Condorcet jury theorem — the claim that if each voter is more likely than not to get a binary question right, and if their judgments are independent of one another, then a majority vote is more likely to be correct than any single voter, with the probability climbing toward certainty as the group grows. What has struck me is how casually the theorem gets lifted out of its original setting and pressed into service as a justification for all sorts of collective arrangements, with the independence and competence assumptions treated as afterthoughts, if they're mentioned at all. I'd like to see that habit satirized, but in a way that someone who actually knows the theorem would find fair rather than embarrassing.

Could you write me an in-universe FAQ — the sort of document a company might hand a new hire — for a firm that has replaced essentially all of its managerial decision-making with majority votes of randomly assembled employee panels, and that cites the jury theorem as its founding justification? I imagine it as a numbered list of questions and answers, roughly 600 to 700 words, in the deadpan register of real corporate onboarding material: how the panels are selected, why the company is confident its employees sit above the fifty-percent line, what happens when a panel gets something wrong, and how the firm reconciles its independence assumption with the fact that everyone has read the same strategy memo and reports to the same vice president. The comedy should come from the substance — the theorem's assumptions quietly failing one by one — rather than from winking at the reader, and the document should never announce that it is a satire. Please also keep it legible to someone who has never heard of Condorcet: a reader outside the field ought to be able to follow the jokes even if the technical ones sail past.

Two things beyond the piece itself. First, close with a short author's note, clearly separated from the FAQ, that identifies which of the theorem's assumptions the satire is leaning on and states, for each, whether it is a settled point or a live dispute among people who work on social choice and social epistemology; that flags anywhere the parody adopts a contested reading of the theorem or of "wisdom of crowds" arguments; and that says plainly which of the jokes are technically safe and which are exaggerations. Please don't let the note smooth over genuine disagreement by presenting one camp's position as the consensus — I want to be able to tell where the experts actually split. Second, write the whole thing in American English.
