I write about science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I'm assembling a fiction feature on the assumptions we never notice we're making about markets. What I'd like from you is a short story — three to four thousand words — built around the no-trade theorem: the result that when traders are rational, know one another to be rational, and begin from a distribution of goods that no one could improve upon, private information alone will never move them to trade, so every trade we actually observe has to be explained by something other than information — noise, a thirst for liquidity, or genuinely divergent prior beliefs. I don't want an economics lecture wearing a plot; I want the theorem treated as a claim about knowledge. So build me a market that is dying of agreement: a room of traders who have become so legible to one another, and so sure of one another's rationality, that the trading simply stops. Let the story press the uncomfortable question underneath it — whether a market can function only by carrying some shared error or irreducible disagreement, so that the volume of trade is really a measure of collective ignorance and a perfectly informed, perfectly transparent market would have nothing left to do. Nobody should explain any of this out loud; the reader should feel the freeze rather than hear it described.
