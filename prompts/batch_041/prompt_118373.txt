Got into a four hour fight with my friend Devorah last night about Sidgwick — she does Victorian science and thinks the psychical research is an embarrassment his admirers quietly explain away, and I ended up defending a reading of it that I now actually have to present, so partly this is her fault.

Here's the deal. Next week I'm giving a work-in-progress paper to our history-of-philosophy reading group. The room: mostly people who know the Methods of Ethics cold and are allergic to reading the psychical stuff back into the ethics; two analytic epistemologists, one who works on testimony and disagreement and one Bayesian type who thinks in terms of probative weight; and one emeritus guy who sat on the council of the Society for Psychical Research for years (Sidgwick co-founded it in 1882 and was its first president) and will absolutely go to bat for the medium investigations.

My line: the psychical research isn't a sideline. Go to the end of the Methods, the dualism of practical reason — reason alone can't get you to prefer the general good to your own, and he leaves open that the conflict only dissolves if the universe turns out to be morally ordered. Psychical research is the empirical arm of that: he needed a fact about the world that no argument could hand him, so he went hunting for evidence of it. And the epistemology is the interesting part — his model of evidence was actuarial, not forensic. You don't need one decisive case, you pile up a mass of weak testimony and look for a signal in the aggregate. Which is why the census-of-hallucinations kind of work, thousands of respondents, a weak statistical effect, is the authentically Sidgwickian project, while the séance work, where the method is really cross-examining a single witness, is the strand he was bad at — and, I want to say, why catching out a medium never shook him the way it shook everyone else: for him individual fraud was a datum to absorb, not a refutation. He'd already argued, in the address he gave the Society about canons of evidence, that the whole question is how to weigh testimony in bulk.

What I want: the objections I'm actually going to get, in the order they'll come, in each person's own vocabulary, with a two minute reply each and a flag on where the reply is thin, plus what to concede in the first five minutes so I don't burn the whole Q&A on it. No reading lists, I know the texts. My soft joint is the move from "he hoped for a moral governor" to "so the psychical research is part of the ethics" — hope isn't an arguement — so if that's where I'm going to get gutted, say so. And tell me the question I'm dreading, the one I'm hoping nobody asks.
