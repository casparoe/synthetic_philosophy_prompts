Thirty-five years in an experimental laboratory left me with a comfortably naive picture of technology — the instrument is a tool, the politics reside in who pays for it and who is permitted to use it — and I have spent the two years since my retirement reading philosophy in an attempt to complicate that picture. I have largely succeeded, and I now hold, or am at least strongly tempted by, a much more interesting thesis: that a technical artifact can itself be political, not merely in the weak sense that its adoption has political consequences or that its designers harboured political aims, but in the strong sense that a given design or arrangement admits of only one workable distribution of authority, so that choosing the artifact is choosing a political settlement — a constitution, as it were, that arrives without ever having been voted on. I have in mind the position that circulates under the question "Do Artifacts Have Politics?" I am unsure whether to state the thesis as *requires* or merely as *is strongly compatible with*, and I suspect that difference matters more than is usually acknowledged.

What I want from you is a map of this position's exposure. Supposing it to be false or badly overstated, what would the world have to look like, and what would we have to observe, for that to be shown? I am after the defeaters ranked from fatal to merely awkward, with your reasons for the ranking, drawing on observations, historical cases, conceptual objections, and results from neighbouring disciplines (history of technology, sociology of science, the economics of standards, engineering practice itself) as each deserves. Three things in particular. First, what does the strong thesis predict that its rivals do not — the rival holding that artifacts are politically neutral and that politics lives entirely in use and intention, and the rival holding that everything about a technology is socially constructed, so that the "requirements" it seems to impose are only sedimented social choices? Second, the famous parkway-bridge example is the one I know, and I gather its historical accuracy has been challenged; tell me whether the thesis survives the loss of that example, and whether the accuracy of the example is even relevant to the philosophy. Third, what would a committed defender of the strong thesis concede as counting against it — not merely what critics allege, but the concessions that come from inside?

Then the question I care about most, which is whether the view is insulated. If "workable" is defined by the social conditions that happen to obtain, does the claim not become circular — and if it does, is that fatal or simply the price of a conceptual claim? How much weight should I give to cases in which the same artifact, transplanted unchanged, is made to serve opposite political orders? Does the demonstrated availability of a viable alternative design count as a defeater, and if so, how are we to establish viability without the benefit of hindsight? And is there a real risk that the thesis collapses at both ends — into physics and engineering constraint, where the politics has evaporated, or into social choice, where the artifact has dropped out and we are left with a familiar social determinism wearing a new coat? For what it is worth, my own working life supplies the examples I find hardest to classify: a national facility where access was allocated by peer-reviewed proposal, where a single calibration standard dictated the shape of every experiment performed on three continents, and where the division of labour around the machine was fixed long before anyone arrived to use it. Tell me whether such cases support the strong thesis or only a weak cousin of it. I would be most moved by a case in which an artifact, unchanged, is turned to opposite political purposes; I would be least moved by evidence that a designer intended a political effect. Finally, say plainly whether this position is well insulated from evidence, and if it is, whether that is a defect for a thesis of this kind or merely what we should expect of a conceptual claim. Argue it out rather than directing me to further reading; I have the time but not the library.
