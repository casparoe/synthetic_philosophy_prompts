I cover social science and philosophy for a magazine, and I am building an interactive feature around the oldest puzzle in the study of voting: in any large election, the chance that my single ballot decides the outcome is small enough that it seems not to justify the trouble of casting it, and yet hundreds of millions of people vote, including many who can recite that argument from memory. What has surprised me while reporting is that nearly everyone who writes about this converges on the same phrase — the paradox of voting — while holding positions that have almost nothing to do with one another. Some think the voter is simply mistaken about how causation works; some think the arithmetic is right but that the voter has a non-instrumental reason to go anyway; some think the question is malformed. In a fifteen-minute interview they all sound identical. I want a decision tree that sorts them, built from the logical space of the disagreement rather than from a roster of famous names, so that a reader arrives at a position because of what she says about concrete cases and not because of a label she already likes.

Start the tree from the shared commitment that a single vote in a large election is very unlikely to decide the outcome and that voting costs something. The first branch should be the one I keep finding people on opposite sides of without noticing: which do you trust more, the calculation that says voting is a bad bet, or the conviction that voting is an entirely reasonable thing to do — bearing in mind that a third answer, that the two do not conflict because the question is confused, is a real position and not a dodge. From there the tree must pass through the following distinctions, each as its own branch node, each phrased as a question a reader can answer without knowing where it leads. First, whether the account is debunking or vindicating: does it treat a voter's own explanation (that she votes because it might matter) as accurate, as systematically false, or as beside the point? Second, what the unit of assessment is — the individual ballot, the practice of voting, or the collective outcome — which is where the if-everyone-did-that arguments and the thought that my vote is part of the cause that produces the result both live. Third, whether the good attributed to voting is instrumental, expressive, or constitutive of membership; and for the expressive branch, whether the ballot box is a specially good vehicle for the expression or whether the same value could be had by wearing a button, which is the objection I have never seen answered to my satisfaction. Fourth, what happens under a case I have been putting to everyone, which I call the oracle: a perfectly reliable oracle tells you that your vote will not be pivotal and will not affect anyone else's decision whether or how to vote — does your reason to vote survive, and if so, what is it? Fifth, whether pivotality is even the right measure of a vote's causal contribution, or whether the position substitutes something else, such as being part of the set of votes that produces the margin, or the effect of a lopsided result on what the winner can claim to have a mandate for. Sixth, whether the position needs the voter to be deceived for the practice to hold up, which I suspect is the real dividing line between the cheerful and the gloomy readings of mass democracy. One thing to get right: this is not a puzzle about selfishness, and the tree should not treat it as one — the problem survives the voter who cares only about the public good.

The positions I need at the leaves, at minimum, are these: the corrected instrumental voter, for whom pivotality is the wrong measure of causal contribution; the debunked instrumental voter, for whom turnout rests on a false folk theory of causation and democracy runs on error; the expressive or consumption voter; the duty-bound or rule-consequentialist voter, for whom act-level accounting is the wrong level; the team-reasoning or collective-agency voter, for whom the individual calculus is a category error; the costly-signaling or solidarity voter; the minimax-regret voter, who says the size of the regret swamps the small cost; and the deflationist, who says the paradox comes from a bad theory of practical reason or of causation and is not a genuine problem at all. Add anything else the logical space leaves standing. Where the tree cannot separate two of these, say so rather than papering over it, and tell me what further question would separate them. Where a path leads nowhere — a combination of answers that nobody seems to hold — mark it as unoccupied and say whether that is because the view is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. Give me the whole thing as a single JSON object, in American English, with these fields and nothing outside the object: nodes, an array of branch nodes, each with id (a string such as n1), question (the question as a reader would read it, in plain language rather than terms of art), and options (an array, each entry having option_id, text, and leads_to, which is either another node id or a leaf id); leaves, an array of positions, each with id, position_name, one_sentence_commitment, most_awkward_commitment, unit_of_assessment (one of individual ballot, the practice of voting, or the collective outcome), verdict_on_the_calculation (one of the calculation is right, the calculation is wrong, or the calculation answers the wrong question), and generalizes_to_other_inefficacy_cases (one sentence on whether the same verdict applies to cases like one person's emissions or one person's diet, and whether the position treats that parallel as a feature or an embarrassment); unoccupied_paths, an array whose entries have path (the sequence of option_ids) and diagnosis (incoherent, unattractive, or unexplored, plus a clause of explanation); inseparable_pairs, an array whose entries have positions, why_the_tree_cannot_separate_them, and what_would_separate_them; and a final note field for anything that does not fit the schema. Names for the positions are welcome, since I want the taxonomy to be usable, but I do not need citations, dates, or reading lists, and I would rather have an unattributed position than a guessed-at attribution.
