You are an expert in early modern philosophy and the history of economic thought, with a deep knowledge of George Berkeley's philosophical system and his lesser-studied writings on economics. I am a philosophy professor preparing the final examination for an upper-level undergraduate seminar on Berkeley's philosophy, and I need your help drafting one essay question, a model answer, and a marking scheme.

The seminar has covered Berkeley's idealism, his theory of vision, and his philosophy of language, and we have spent the last three weeks on The Querist and his proposal for an Irish national bank. The students are bright but tend to default to reciting the standard line that Berkeley is an idealist who thinks everything is a mind or an idea. I want an essay question that forces them to engage with the specifics of his monetary thought and to think carefully about whether his views on money are a natural extension of his broader philosophy or a separate, practical intervention.

The seminar has focused on the following passages from The Querist:

- Query 5: "Whether money be not only so far useful, as it stirreth up industry, enabling men mutually to participate the fruits of each other's labour?"
- Query 23: "Whether money is to be considered as having an intrinsic value, or as being a commodity, a standard, a measure, or a pledge, as is variously suggested by writers? And whether the true idea of money, as such, be not altogether that of a ticket or counter?"
- Query 25: "Whether the terms crown, livre, pound sterling, etc., are not to be considered as exponents or denominations of such proportion? And whether gold, silver, and paper are not tickets or counters for reckoning, recording, and transferring thereof?"
- Query 32: "Whether there be any vertue in gold or silver, other than as they set people at work, or create industry?"
- Query 33: "Whether it be not the opinion or will of the people, exciting them to industry, that truly enricheth a nation?"

These passages are the focus of the exam question.

Please draft one essay question suitable for a 90-minute in-class exam. The question should be unanswerable by a student who has merely memorized the summary of Berkeley's idealism. It should require students to grapple with the apparent tension between Berkeley's claim that money is "altogether that of a ticket or counter" and his claim that wealth depends on the "opinion or will of the people," and to assess whether his account of money can ground the value of money without appealing to the intrinsic value of gold or silver. The question should also invite students to consider how Berkeley's idealism bears on his monetary nominalism — for instance, whether his view that material objects are collections of ideas has any implications for what money is, or whether his monetary views are independent of his metaphysics. The crux I want students to confront is whether Berkeley's conventionalism about money is self-undermining: if money is valuable only because people believe it is valuable, what prevents the belief from collapsing, and does Berkeley have the resources to answer this question?

After the question, please provide:

1. A model answer of approximately 700-900 words that a strong student could produce in 90 minutes. The model answer should not merely summarize The Querist but should take a clear position on the central interpretive question and defend it with specific reference to Berkeley's text. You may refer to specific queries by number, but rely only on the passages provided above and do not invent quotations from Berkeley or from any other source. The model answer should also acknowledge and respond to at least one serious objection to its position.

2. A marking scheme worth 20 points total, broken into five or six must-hit points. For each point, specify the marks available, what a top answer must do to earn full marks, and the most common mistake that should cost marks. The marking scheme should distinguish an A answer from a B answer by naming the specific moves each must contain — for example, whether the student recognizes that Berkeley's conventionalism about money is not the same as the claim that money is arbitrary, or whether the student can explain how the "opinion or will of the people" is supposed to do any grounding work.

Before you write, please think carefully about the structure of the question and the crux of the philosophical issue. Begin by brainstorming the key interpretive options a student might take, then choose the one that makes for the best exam question, and only then write the question, model answer, and marking scheme. Write in American English. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations to secondary literature — students will not have access to them during the exam, and I want the question to stand on its own.
