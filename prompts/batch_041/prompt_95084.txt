I'm doing a film-and-philosophy night on WarGames and want to use it to teach superrationality — Hofstadter's idea that two identical reasoners in a prisoner's dilemma should both cooperate, because each one can predict the other will reach the same conclusion. The obvious hook is the ending, where the WOPR plays itself at tic-tac-toe, finds nobody can win, and decides the only winning move is not to play. But I suspect that's a different argument from superrationality, and I want you to untangle which is which. So: read the film through superrationality. Does the machine's tic-tac-toe reasoning — two identical players, guaranteed draw — actually illustrate superrational cooperation, or is it just an ordinary no-advantage point that would go through even if the opponent weren't a copy of you? Same question for the US/USSR standoff: is mutual non-launch superrational cooperation, or deterrence, or something else wearing its clothes? And does the logic actually need the two sides to be identical, or does the film quietly get by on a weaker assumption? I'd also like the sharper modern version of the idea, where cooperation comes from each agent knowing the other is running the same decision algorithm rather than from any spooky symmetry, and a verdict on whether WarGames dramatizes that version or the spooky one. Where the film resists the reading, please say so — I don't want it flattened into an illustration, and I'd like to know if it adds anything the decision theory doesn't.
