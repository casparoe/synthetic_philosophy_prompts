I have been arguing with a friend about counterfactual mugging, and I want the argument audited rather than restated. The case as we both accept it: Omega, whose predictions are reliable, flips a fair coin. If it lands tails, Omega asks you for $100. Had it landed heads, Omega would instead have paid you $10,000, but only if it had predicted before flipping that you would pay in the tails case. Omega is honest, and the prediction has already been made. The coin has landed tails, you know this, and Omega asks you for the $100. Nothing you do now alters anything else in the actual world.

My friend, who favours decision theories that evaluate whole policies rather than individual acts, says that paying is simply the purchase of insurance. The $100 is the premium; what is insured against is your not being in the heads branch; the $10,000 is the payout; Omega is the underwriter; and the coin's having come up tails no more shows the premium to have been wasted than a year without a fire shows a fire premium to have been wasted. Her conclusion is that refusing to pay rests on the same error as regretting an insurance premium in hindsight, and that the unease people feel about the mugging is merely that error in dramatic costume.

Please audit the analogy.

1. Give the correspondence table: what plays the role of the premium, the payout, the insured event, the loss, the risk pool, the underwriter, the actuarial probability, and the information available to the payer at the moment of payment.

2. Say where the mapping breaks, and separate the breaks that matter for her conclusion from those that are decorative.

3. Press two points in particular. First, in ordinary insurance is there ever a case in which the premium is paid in one state of the world and the payout collected in another, by someone who did not pay? If not, say precisely what the insurance picture assumes about the standpoint from which the two branches are being weighed, and whether that assumption is the very thing at issue between causal and evidential (or policy-evaluating) accounts of rational choice. Second, does the insurance picture require the premium decision to be taken before the outcome is known, and does the shift from acts to policies restore whatever that timing removes?

4. It has occurred to me that what she describes is less like insurance than like a wager on heads whose stake is collected only when the bet loses. Tell me whether that diagnosis is right, and if it is, whether it helps or damages her case.

5. Then the verdict: does the analogy support her conclusion, or does it presuppose it? If it fails, propose a better analogy — one that keeps whatever is legitimate in the thought that it can be rational to pay for a state of affairs one knows does not obtain, without assuming that branches one knows are not actual can be treated as risks one bears. Finally, say briefly whether the verdict changes for the blackmail-shaped cases that circulate alongside the mugging, where payment is demanded to avert a threat rather than offered to secure a reward.
