I'm teaching a session on counterfactual reasoning in a philosophy of history seminar next month -- the room is mostly historians plus two philosophers, and they've all got a low tolerance for the what-if-Hitler-had-been-shot genre. I want to build the session around The Man in the High Castle, because I think Dick does something to the counterfactual imagination that no historian's essay manages, and I need a reading that won't fall apart in front of that crowd.

So: read the novel through the lens of counterfactual history and the problem of historical inevitability, and tell me what it actually shows. Its point of divergence is about as minimal as they come -- Zangara's shot in Miami in 1933 lands, where in our world it hit Mayor Cermak instead -- and everything downstream is the Axis victory: the partitioned States, Hitler decaying somewhere while Bormann runs the Reich and then dies, the drained Mediterranean, the rockets and the colonies. The historians I know who do counterfactuals for a living care about which divergences are minimal and which consequences were overdetermined; whether American intervention was the sort of thing that was going to happen anyway once the economic facts were in place. Dick does none of that reasoning, but he dramatizes people living inside something that feels to them like an inevitability, with a past they can't check and a future that might not arrive (the German strike plan, Dandelion I think it's called, that one character is carrying word of). Is the novel arguing that inevitability is just a story told afterward, or is it committed to a stronger claim about which outcomes were locked in?

Second thing, and this is what I care about most. Wyndam-Matson's two lighters -- one supposedly in FDR's pocket, one not, indistinguishable -- is the obvious image for telling the real past from the retrofitted one. But the more interesting move is the nested counterfactual: Abendsen's book has the Allies winning, and if I remember it right, that world isnt ours either; it diverges again into a British-dominated order and a later Anglo-American standoff. Three worlds, and none of them converges on the others. That seems to cut against how counterfactual history normally works, where our actual history is the fixed benchmark you measure the deviation from. What does the refusal to converge buy him, and does it undercut the historians practice or just make it more honest?

Third: the novel was written with the I Ching -- Dick cast for plot turns -- and inside it Abendsen wrote his book the same way, and at the end the oracle gets the last word on which version of history is the true one. I keep going back and forth on whether that's deep or a dodge. If the narrative is generated by divination, its events are neither contingent nor causally inevitable; they're settled from outside the causal order. Is that a genuine third option between it-had-to-happen and it-could-have-gone-otherwise, or is Dick cheating? And does it matter that the characters treat the oracle's verdict as settling the metaphysics, which is arguably what historians do when they read the outcome back as the reason it couldn't have gone otherwise?

There's also the counterfeit/authenticity thread -- Childan's fake Americana sold to Japanese collectors hungry for a past their own side helped destroy, and Frink's handmade pieces that are the real thing and get read as carrying some kind of charge. I think that's doing work for the epistemology of the counterfactual: how would you know which past is the authentic one if the evidence looks the same either way? Tagomi's vision, sitting with the handmade piece and slipping into a world where the war went the other way, is where I think the novel either answers that or refuses to, and I want you to say which.

What I don't want is a survey of the counterfactual-history literature or a reading list. I want the reading: where the lens fits, where the novel resists it, what Dick sees about inevitability that the arguments don't, or what he gets wrong that they get right. My own hunch is that the book is committed to contingency but can't admit it, because then the oracle would just be arbitrary -- push back on that if it's wrong. And concretely: which scenes do I put in front of the students, and what do I warn them not to conclude from the ending? Don't quote the novel; they'll have it open.
