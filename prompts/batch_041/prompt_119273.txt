ok so I'm putting together a session for our eval team — we mostly do ML benchmarks but also some org-level KPIs — on Goodhart's law and specifcation gaming, and I want to hand out a decision tree instead of yet another slide of platitudes about measures becoming targets. Can you write it?

Here's what I want it to do. Start from the symptom "we optimized a metric and the thing we actually cared about didn't improve, or got worse" and end at a named failure mechanism. Every branch node should be a question someone can answer by looking at their own case, in plain language, phrased so you can't tell from the question which mechanism it leads to. So not "is this causal Goodhart?" but something like "if we'd forcibly moved the metric by 10% without touching anything else, would the thing we care about have moved too?" — that's an example of the flavour, don't just reuse it.

Leaves I want in there, at minimum:

- the proxy is a noisy readout of the target, so selecting on extreme proxy values is mostly selecting for luck
- the proxy is a downstream effect of the target, so pushing on the proxy doesn't push on the target
- somebody has an interest in the proxy diverging from the target and is actively steering it (fraud, teaching to the test, reward hacking)
- the proxy was only ever valid in a narrow range or near the current operating point, and we drove way outside it
- the metric itself quietly got redefined mid-flight so the time series is a fiction — I care about this one a lot, it's the boring one everyone forgets, like when a government changes how it counts something
- nobody ever agreed what the target was, and the proxy was doing construct-work all along
- plus one honest "no failure here, the number went up and the world actually got better" leaf, and a "can't tell yet, the feedback loop is slower than the reporting cycle" leaf

Then, for each leaf, one sentence on what it presupposes about whether Goodhart's law is basically a tautology (a proxy isn't the target, so optimizing it obviously optimizes something else) or a substantive empirical claim about selection pressure. Half my team is in each camp and I want the tree to expose which leaf commits you to which.

Also tell me which combinations of answers are unoccupied and why — incoherent, just unattractive, or nobody's bothered to look — and flag any two leaves your tree can't actually seperate. I'd rather know than have you paper over it.

Test the tree on two or three famous cases you're confident about (nail factories, cobra bounties, whatever) — flag anything you think is apocryphal rather than guessing at numbers.

Keep it to something I can print on two sides of A4, casual tone since I'm gonna be reading this out loud. And at the end, say which branch you think is carrying most of the weight, and what kind of case would make you redraw the tree — something that lands in two leaves at once, or none.
