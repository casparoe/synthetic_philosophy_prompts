My friend and I spent most of Saturday evening arguing about the bank case — the one where you will happily say you know the bank is open on Saturday morning when nothing much rides on it, and where you say you don't know and had better go and check when a mortgage payment depends on catching it before noon — and about whether the difference between those two versions is a difference in what knowledge is or merely in what we are willing to say. His position is deflationary, and he rested it on an analogy he is clearly fond of: he says that 'knows' is a gradable, context-sensitive term in exactly the way 'flat' is. Nothing is flat, strictly speaking; a surface is flat enough to park on and not flat enough to see your face in, and nobody supposes that the parking-lot surface's flatness is a different property from the mirror surface's flatness, or that the two of us are contradicting each other when I call it flat and he, measuring it against the mirror, calls it not flat. The threshold is fixed by what we are using the term for, and that is the whole story. His conclusion is that the bank case is the same trick — the two parties simply set different thresholds for the purposes at hand — so the entire literature on pragmatic encroachment, with its talk of knowledge itself being sensitive to what is at stake for the subject, is chasing a shadow; and his proposed reform is that we should stop asking whether someone really knows and instead state the threshold explicitly, saying that someone knows to standard T the way we say a surface is flat to tolerance T.

I think he is not simply wrong, but I do not think the analogy can carry the weight he has put on it, and I want a careful audit rather than a verdict. Begin with the correspondence: say what in the flatness case is supposed to map onto the conversational context, onto the subject's practical situation, onto the strength of the subject's epistemic position, and onto the truth of the knowledge attribution. Then say where the mapping breaks. I am particularly interested in three things: whether knowledge is gradable in anything like the way flatness is, given that knowledge is usually taken to be absolute rather than a matter of degree; whether the salient-possibility-of-error structure that does the real work in the bank case has any counterpart at all in the flatness case, where the threshold seems to be set by a purpose rather than by a possibility that has been raised; and whether the analogy, if it holds, does not in fact support contextualism about knowledge rather than undermine the encroachment view, since the flatness model locates the variability in the ascriber's purposes rather than in the subject's stakes — in which case he has not dissolved the dispute but quietly taken a side in it. Separate the disanalogies that matter for his deflationary conclusion from the ones that do not, say whether that conclusion survives once they are on the table, and tell me whether there is a better comparison for the bank case, or whether the honest thing to say is that no analogy in this vicinity could settle whether the disagreement is substantive or verbal. Finally, given your diagnosis, tell me what would actually count as progress here — a distinction, a case, a demonstration that the two sides are answering different questions under one heading, or a reason to think the question is simply ill-posed.
