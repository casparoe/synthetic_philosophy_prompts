At our department's weekly reading group last term, two colleagues — one a philosopher of mind, the other a cognitive neuroscientist — fell into an extended argument about Libet-type experiments and what they do or do not show about free will. I was moderating and kept my own view to myself, but I could not referee the substance of the exchange, and I would now like your help in adjudicating it. What follows is my reconstruction of the argument as faithfully as I can manage.

The neuroscientist, Marek, opened as follows. In the classic paradigm, subjects make a spontaneous wrist movement and report when they felt the urge to move by noting the position of a fast-moving clock hand. The readiness potential — a slow negative shift in the EEG over the scalp — begins roughly 550 milliseconds before the muscle contracts, while subjects place their reported urge only about 200 milliseconds before the movement. That leaves something like a third of a second of neural preparation preceding any awareness of having decided. And later work pushed the gap much further: pattern classifiers reading brain activity can predict which hand a subject will use several seconds before the subject reports having chosen. Whatever we want to call the conscious decision, it is preceded by the neural event that settles what will be done. The ordinary picture, in which conscious deliberation issues in a choice that then causes the act, has the order backwards.

The philosopher, Iris, replied on two fronts. First, she said, the timing of the reported urge rests entirely on subjects introspecting and reporting when an urge occurred while simultaneously watching a rotating clock. That is a divided-attention task directed at a vague phenomenal event, and the reported time is exactly the sort of judgment that can be shifted by where attention is allocated and by what the subject takes the question to be asking. The 350-millisecond gap may therefore be partly an artifact of the measurement rather than a window into the mind. Second, and more important, the readiness potential is not a decision. It is a slow, broadly distributed shift that appears before many kinds of movement, that is not yet specific to which movement will be made, and that shows up even on trials where the subject ends up not moving at all. A signal that precedes both the action and its cancellation, and that does not encode which action it is, looks like general preparation or a readiness to act rather than a commitment to act.

Marek answered with two points. The veto, he said, is a concession rather than a rescue. If the only free element in the sequence is the capacity to call the act off, then the veto is itself an intention, and it will have its own antecedent neural story; the subject cannot exempt the canceling from the very argument that was applied to the acting. And second, the later studies did not merely time a generic readiness. They decoded the content of the choice — left hand versus right — well before the report. So the pre-conscious signal is not just arousal or undifferentiated preparation; it carries information about what will be done.

Iris responded that predicting above chance is not determining. A classifier that is right sixty percent of the time is wrong forty percent of the time, and even a perfectly accurate predictor would not by itself show that the outcome was fixed in advance rather than merely correlated with a slowly drifting bias. She then offered a reading of the readiness potential that she said undercuts its interpretation as a decision signal: the potential may be nothing more than a slow accumulation of ongoing background noise that eventually crosses a threshold, with the crossing time set by the fluctuations themselves. On that reading, the onset of the potential does not mark a moment of commitment at all; it marks when the noise happened to arrive.

Marek said this did not help. If the timing of the act is set by random fluctuations in the brain rather than by an intention, the act is not thereby less determined — it is determined by something even less like the agent. Randomness is not agency. Either way, what precedes the conscious report is not the agent's decision.

Iris closed by saying that randomness is not the relevant contrast, and that this is the deep error in his framing. What matters for agency, she argued, is not whether some earlier event is random or lawful but whether the process producing the act is the kind of process that can be sensitive to the agent's reasons — whether it would have gone differently had the agent had different reasons. The Libet paradigm was designed to exclude precisely that, since subjects were instructed to let the movement arise on its own rather than to decide on any basis, and the question of when to flick a wrist is not ordinarily the sort of thing that is responsive to deliberation. The experiment, she concluded, may be well designed to study arbitrary movements and simply silent about the decisions anyone cares about.

Neither of them would budge, and I genuinely could not tell who was winning. Please adjudicate the exchange move by move, in the order given. For each move, tell me whether it is clearly mistaken, defensible, or clearly right, and give a brief justification for the rating — I would rather have definite ratings than an answer that finds everyone partly correct. After the move-by-move treatment, add a short overall assessment: who has the better of the argument as it actually stands, and what, if anything, both of them are missing. I am reporting this as a bystander and have deliberately not indicated which side I favor, so please do not assume I am looking for a verdict for either one.

Please write your answer in American English.
