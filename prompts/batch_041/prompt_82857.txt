I've got a 40-min slot at our department's epistemology workshop in three weeks (20 min talk, 20 min Q&A) and what I actually need from you is the Q&A prep sheet, not the talk. So: tell me the objections I'm going to get, who's going to make them, and what to say back.

The thesis, roughly: the regress problem is an artifact of the axiom of foundation. Support is a relation on beliefs; foundationalism says every chain of support terminates, infinitism says it doesn't have to, coherentism says chains can loop. Now, the well-founded/non-well-founded distinction was drawn by Mirimanoff back around 1917 and he didn't think well-foundedness was an axiom at all — it became one later, and it's optional: you can drop Foundation from ZF, add an anti-foundation axiom instead, and the result is consistent if ZF is. In Aczel's version, AFA, every accessible pointed graph has exactly one "decoration", i.e. exactly one set whose membership structure that graph pictures; x = {x} has exactly one solution, the Quine atom; and the well-founded sets sitting inside that universe still behave perfectly classically. My claim is that the classic objection to coherentism — you can't justify a belief with a circle — is structurally the same move as "no set can be a member of itself", and it's a choice, not a theorem. Second claim: the interesting part of AFA isn't that it lets loops in, it's that it disciplines them — uniqueness of decoration means a circle isn't automatically legit, it has to be picturable by a well-defined system of equations. That's my answer to the "any old circle will do" worry, and honestly it's the part I'm least confident about. Third, smaller claim: the loop beats the infinite chain because a finite agent can be in a looping justificatory state without having completed anything, whereas the infinitist has to say something uncomfortable about finite minds.

Who's in the room: three foundationalists, two of them of the "seemings/perceptual experience stops the regress" reliabilist flavor, one who thinks it stops at self-evident necessary truths. Two formal epistemologists who do probabilistic coherence and accuracy-first stuff, one of whom lives in Bayesian networks and will want a model with numbers in it. One person who works on infinitism and will not be pleased with my third claim. One logician who actually knows non-well-founded set theory and will check my math line by line. One historian of ancient and early modern philosophy who thinks this whole debate is a redescription of the Pyrrhonists plus Descartes. And a few grad students who will ask the naive question that turns out to be lethal.
