I run a second-year graduate reading group in epistemic logic, and we stall at the same place every year. The standard treatment of knowledge and belief as sets of possible worlds implies that whatever is entailed by what an agent knows, she also knows; no actual reasoner satisfies this; and the students can state the problem and list the responses they have read about without being able to say which response they are committed to, or why the responses do not simply collapse into one another. I want a diagnostic handout that settles this by making them take stands on cases rather than choose labels.

Please write a decision tree, followed by a taxonomy of the positions its leaves correspond to.

Build the tree out of the logical space, not out of a list of approaches. Begin by identifying the questions that genuinely divide the responses — where the failure is located, what has to be given up, whether the closure principle was ever intended as a claim about anyone — and let combinations of answers generate the leaves. Locate positions at the leaves afterwards. A student should be able to walk the tree by answering questions about cases, without knowing in advance what a given answer commits her to. The space is not exhausted by the following, but it should at least be reachable from here: responses that keep the semantics and add something to the agent's state; responses that change the semantics of content; responses that deny the closure principle was ever a descriptive claim; and responses that deny that propositions, rather than representations, are the objects of knowledge.

Requirements for the nodes.

- Each node is a question with two or three possible answers, phrased so that a student answers it by taking a stand on a case or on a commitment, not by naming a school. No node may take the form of asking whether she accepts some named approach, and no node may presuppose a position that only appears further down.
- At each node, mark which of the eight cases below does the separating work there.
- If one of the eight cases turns out to do no work anywhere in the tree, say so and say why.
- Every path must terminate in a leaf. If some combination of answers is unoccupied, do not invent a leaf to fill it; record the combination for the closing section instead.

Cases that must appear as node content, since this is where the responses come apart.

1. The unconsidered trivial consequence: an agent has been reliably told that the box holds exactly seventeen marbles, and has no opinion about whether it holds exactly sixteen. Does she fail to know something she should know, and if so, is the failure hers or the theory's?
2. The uncombined pair: she has good evidence for one claim and good evidence for a second, acquired separately, and has never considered the two together. Does she know their conjunction? What does the answer cost you if she also, separately and without ever combining them, believes that the two are not both true?
3. The long derivation: she accepts the axioms and the inference rule, but the shortest derivation of a target sentence is a thousand steps she has not taken. Does she know the target? If the answer is that she knows only what she can derive within some bound, say what becomes of the closure principle at that bound: is she now closed under derivability-within-the-bound, and has the problem been solved or moved one level up?
4. The two formats: the same result is presented to her twice, once as a symbolic derivation and once as a diagram; she can follow the second and not the first. Does she know the result? Which part of your theory has to be sensitive to the format in which a content is stored?
5. The awareness regress: if knowing requires being aware of the content known, and awareness is itself a content one can be aware of, does knowing that she knows require awareness of that awareness, and does the requirement stop anywhere?
6. The unsurprisable ideal: an agent with unlimited computational power and perfect classical logic, for whom every consequence of what she knows is already known. Is this an ideal of rationality, a degenerate limit that no epistemic norm should aspire to, or not an agent at all? If nothing valid can surprise her, does she have any use for inference?
7. The silent logician: an agent who never in fact draws a conclusion entailed by what she believes, though nothing prevents her from doing so and she has simply never been prompted. Did she know it all along?
8. Contradiction without explosion: an agent who has arrived at a contradiction, and who does not proceed to believe everything. Does your account have to restrict the logic, restrict the closure rule, or deny that she is in a single belief state?

Requirements for the leaves.

- Give each leaf a descriptive label in ordinary words, of the form of a short claim about the case rather than the name of a school. Do not attribute any position to a named philosopher, and do not supply titles, dates, or citations; I will add the attributions myself.
- For each leaf, one sentence naming its most awkward commitment — the bullet it bites, or the case it handles worst.
- Where a leaf is really a family of positions, say what further question would split it and note that we currently have no case to settle that question.

Two closing sections.

- Unoccupied combinations of answers, each diagnosed as incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored.
- Pairs or clusters of leaves that the tree cannot separate from one another, each with a sentence on why: they agree on all eight cases, or they differ only in formulation.

Format and length: a two-page handout. Six to eight branch nodes, nine to twelve leaves, with at least one path four nodes deep. Define each technical term in a clause the first time it appears; the audience has had one course in modal logic and no more. No names of philosophers, no titles, no dates, no citations, no reading list.
