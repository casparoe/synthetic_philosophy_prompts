At dinner, two friends argued about a text our friend Sam sent to a coworker: "I'll cover your shift Saturday." The coworker never replied, Sam didn't show, and Sam now insists he never promised—he only said what he would do. Nadia says that in the context of shift-swapping those words were a promise, not a prediction, and that the coworker's silence is irrelevant because promises can be unilateral. Omar says a promise requires uptake: without acceptance it was at most an offer. Nadia replies that uptake need not be verbal—the coworker's showing up expecting Sam would suffice—and that "I'll" here makes the world fit the words rather than the words fit the world, which is what marks it as a commitment. Omar says that is a philosopher's label, not a rule; reliance is a consequence, not acceptance; and if insincere promises are still promises, then sincerity and intention cannot be what make them promises, yet Nadia keeps appealing to what Sam really meant. Nadia says she is appealing to the conventional practice, not Sam's intention. Omar adds that the "hereby" test settles it: one can say "I hereby promise" but not "I hereby cover your shift," so Sam's words were not performative. Nadia answers that the test only identifies explicit performatives and that most promises are implicit. Please adjudicate their exchange move by move—rating each move from clearly mistaken to clearly right—and say what both parties are missing. Do not simply declare a winner.
