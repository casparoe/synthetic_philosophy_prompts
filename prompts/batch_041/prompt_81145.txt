I staff a commission that our city council appointed to consider structural reforms to how the city makes contested budget decisions, and one of the ideas on our agenda is a limited pilot of futarchy: for a handful of line items, two competing proposals would be priced by a conditional prediction market, and the branch the market favors would be adopted, with its outcome measured afterward. To prepare, I read a long explainer in a policy magazine. The author is a journalist rather than a specialist, and a colleague on the commission warned me not to trust the piece but declined to say why. I would like an audit I can rely on at our next hearing.

Here is the article's account, compressed. Futarchy is the form of government proposed by the economist Robin Hanson. Elected representatives define and manage an after-the-fact measurement of national welfare, whether a version of gross domestic product or some broader index, and market speculators then bet on which policies will raise that measurement. Proposals are priced through conditional bets, which are called off if the policy in question is not adopted, so that the market's estimate of welfare under a proposal can be compared with its estimate of welfare without it. The basic rule is that when a betting market clearly estimates that a proposed policy would raise expected welfare, the proposal becomes law. The article reports that the motto is that we vote on our values and bet on our beliefs, which it glosses as meaning that citizens put their money behind the outcomes they personally value, so that the policies with the most money behind them are the ones that get implemented. It adds that because speculative markets are efficient aggregators of information, the policy the market selects is the correct one, and that Hanson has in effect shown that markets get such questions right. It closes with two objections. First, whoever is willing to spend the most can move a price, so futarchy would hand policy to the wealthy and is therefore not a serious proposal. Second, because only the winning proposal is ever implemented, the markets for the losing proposals are never resolved, so no one can ever check whether the market was right about them, which makes the scheme unfalsifiable.

How much of this should I trust? Please go claim by claim and separate what a careful defender of futarchy would accept as accurate, what is a defensible simplification that a specialist would still flag as loose, and what is wrong or would be rejected outright. I am especially unsure about the division of labor between what gets voted on and what gets bet on, and about whether having the same body define the welfare metric and then measure it later creates an opening the article never mentions. On the closing objections, I am inclined to think the first is decisive and the second is a technicality; tell me if that is backwards, and where a defender would say the objection identifies a design problem rather than a refutation. Finally, say whether the article's verdict, that futarchy is not worth piloting, follows from whatever survives correction.

Please write in American English.
