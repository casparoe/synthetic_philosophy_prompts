For six years I covered the trade beat — tariff schedules, safeguard actions, countertrade deals settled in goods because neither side would hold the other's currency — before I moved to the ideas desk, and I still reach for that vocabulary whenever I have to explain anything unfamiliar. There is a proposal in moral philosophy that I cannot stop reading through a trade lens, and I would like you to do the translation properly and then tell me what the lens destroys.

The proposal, as I understand it: two people who disagree about what matters most can often each get more of what they care about by trading. Suppose you believe the great neglected problem is animal suffering and I believe it is some distant risk to future generations. I am entitled to spend my money as I like and to spend my working life as I like; so are you. Left alone, I give my money to my cause and you give your career to yours, and each of us regards half of the other's life as close to wasted. If we swap — I take the career you would have had, you direct your money where I would have directed mine — we may each end up with more of what we value than either of us could have managed alone. The awkward cases are the interesting ones. A trade may require one party to do something she thinks is mildly wrong, or to support a cause she finds distasteful; and an offer can be framed as something close to a threat — "give me this, or I shall do the thing I am perfectly entitled to do and which you detest".

Restate this proposal as faithfully as you can in the vocabulary I actually use: comparative advantage and the gains from trade, terms of trade, market access, tariffs and non-tariff barriers, most-favoured-nation treatment, safeguards, barter and countertrade, non-trade concerns, dispute settlement, inconvertible currency. Then give me the residue report. Which parts carry over cleanly? Which survive only in a distorted form — and say plainly where the distortion lies? Which parts have no counterpart in my vocabulary at all? I am especially interested in whether moral value behaves like a tradeable good or like a currency nobody will accept, whether a threat dressed as an offer is a trade or an extraction, whether dealing with someone whose values you find repellent is like dealing with a pariah state, and whether commitments that feel untradeable are best modelled as non-trade concerns or as something the framework simply cannot represent. Close by telling me whether the residue is a defect of my vocabulary or a sign that the original idea was less clear than it sounded. I want the argument, not the literature — no citations, no reading list.

End with a single line in exactly this form: Residue: <the one element of the proposal that trade vocabulary cannot carry, in no more than fifteen words>.
