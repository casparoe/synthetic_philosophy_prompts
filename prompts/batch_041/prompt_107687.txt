Right, so I run the philosophy club at my school — sixth form, about a dozen of us, we get one hour on a Tuesday after lessons. I want to do a session on this idea I read about, evidential cooperation in large worlds, and I'm worried it'll just sound like nonsense if I stand up and explain it at them.

The gist is this: if the world is big enough there are probably loads of other agents out there whose decisions are correlated with yours — not because you can affect them or talk to them, you'll never meet them, never message them, no causal link whatsoever — but because you share the same decision procedure, so the very fact that you're about to pick one option is evidence about what they're going to pick. And that can apparently make it rational to "cooperate" with them even though you'll never know whether they exist.

So design me the session. I want an activity where the rules themselves make them feel the pull of it — they should end up in a spot where cooperating with someone they can't contact, meet or verify actually pays off, and only twig afterwards what they were really doing. I need the rules, the materials (no real money, we're definately skint), a payoff scheme, roles if it needs them, and a proper debrief that maps what just happened back onto the idea. Also tell me what'll predictably go wrong the first time I run it, and wich part of the idea the game can't actually capture, so I know what I'll have to say myself.

Keep it under 700 words, and lead with the activity itself, not the theory.
