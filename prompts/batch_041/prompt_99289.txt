I am the instructor of a graduate seminar in epistemology and am now marking the final examination. One of the questions, reproduced below, asks students to work through a case involving higher-order evidence. The answer that follows it is a student's. Please grade it.

Examination question.

Consider the following case.

Nadia sits on a hiring committee. She has read one candidate's file carefully and judges, with credence 0.8, that the candidate meets the bar. She then reads a memo from the committee's methodologist, a person whose judgment she has every reason to trust. The memo reports that reviewers at Nadia's level of experience who form a judgment that the candidate meets the bar on a single pass are mistaken about 25 per cent of the time on files of this kind, and that a single reviewer's impression is not on its own a reliable basis for a decision. The memo says nothing about this candidate in particular. Nadia has no time to read the file a second time.

(a) Is the memo first-order evidence, higher-order evidence, or both? Say what it is evidence about, and distinguish higher-order from first-order evidence in a way that accounts for this case.

(b) Nadia keeps believing that the candidate meets the bar, and she also believes that this belief of hers is not supported by her total evidence, so that holding it is, evidentially speaking, a mistake. Is she thereby irrational? In answering, say what epistemic akrasia is and whether the case as described is an instance of it.

(c) Suppose epistemic akrasia of the kind described in (b) is sometimes rationally permissible. What follows for the enkratic principle, the principle that one cannot rationally believe p while believing that believing p is irrational? Does the case for or against that principle turn on whether higher-order evidence is a distinct kind of evidence, or only on how it weighs against the first-order evidence?

Student's answer.

(a) The memo is higher-order evidence. First-order evidence bears directly on the question at issue: here, the candidate's record, the publications, the letters, the quality of the work. Higher-order evidence bears on the epistemic position of the person doing the believing, and specifically on whether the first-order evidence actually supports the conclusion she drew from it. The memo is of the second kind: it tells Nadia how reliable reviewers like her are when they judge a file on one pass. It is not the opinion of another reviewer about this candidate; it is information about a class of judgments, and that is why it is higher-order rather than merely somebody else's first-order verdict.

It matters that the memo is about reliability rather than about the candidate, because that is what generates the puzzle. If the memo were simply one more item of first-order evidence it would enter the balance alongside the file and Nadia would weigh it up like anything else. Because it is about the process that produced her belief, it threatens to undercut the belief without adding anything to the case for or against the candidate.

(b) Epistemic akrasia is believing something that you have good reason to think is false. It is the epistemic analogue of weakness of will: just as the akratic agent acts against her own better judgment, the epistemically akratic agent believes against her own better judgment. As the case is described, Nadia believes that the candidate meets the bar while believing that this belief is not supported by her evidence, which is the akratic structure, so the case is an instance of it.

Whether Nadia is irrational is a further question. On a strict evidentialist view, what she may believe is fixed by her total evidence, and if the memo is genuine evidence then her total evidence no longer supports 0.8, so she is irrational to keep the belief and the akrasia is not permissible. On a coherence view, the norms governing what she believes and the norms governing what she believes about her beliefs can come apart, and she may keep the belief while acknowledging that the higher-order evidence tells against it. I find the second view more plausible, because the memo cannot make the candidate's file any worse than it was.

(c) If akrasia can be rationally permissible in some cases, the enkratic principle is not a genuine requirement of rationality at all. It is at most a norm of self-consistency, the sort of thing one may be criticised for flouting but which does not fix what one may believe. The whole dispute, it seems to me, reduces to whether higher-order evidence is a distinct kind of evidence. If it is distinct, then evidentialism will say that a person's total evidence can point one way at the first order and another way at the higher order, and akrasia is a real possibility; if it is not distinct, then the higher-order material is just more first-order evidence and there is no gap for akrasia to occupy.

Please return (i) a grade out of 10, and (ii) the comments that justify it, organised point by point against parts (a) through (c). For each part, state what the answer gets right, what it gets wrong or leaves unsupported, and how serious the defect is; where a move in the answer is arguable rather than simply wrong, say what would have to be true for it to succeed. I want the comments a careful grader would write rather than a general impression, so if a defect is of the kind a fast reader would pass over, say so explicitly and say what it would cost the student. The seminar is run without a fixed reading list, so do not require citations and do not supply any.
