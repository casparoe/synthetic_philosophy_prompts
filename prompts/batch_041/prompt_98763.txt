I am convening the next meeting of a cross-departmental reading group: the members are doctoral students and faculty from economics, biology, and law, plus two philosophers, but none of them works on decision theory or on artificial intelligence. The session runs seventy-five minutes with no slides, and the topic I have been asked to lead is acausal threats and surrogate goals. Do not explain the topic to me; I want you to plan how I should teach it.

By an acausal threat I mean the kind of extortion that can be pressed by an agent that has no causal channel to its target, no way to reach or punish or reward it after the fact, yet whose behaviour is nevertheless correlated with the target's, so that what the target does is evidence about what the threatener will do, whether because the threatener predicted the target in advance or because both are the sort of agent that reasons to the same conclusion from the same structure. By a surrogate goal I mean the defensive move in which an agent deliberately acquires a new and otherwise worthless objective, a decoy that is cheap to satisfy and orthogonal to its real aims, so that threats get redirected onto the decoy rather than onto the things it actually cares about.

I need the plan to accomplish five things. First, the order of ideas: what must be in place before the next piece makes sense, and why each dependency runs the way it does, in particular where the notion of correlation without causation has to land before either the threat or the decoy can be understood, and whether commitment or prediction should be introduced first. Second, the single carrying example that holds the whole session together: what it must contain and what it must avoid. I want one example rather than a zoo of them, and it should make the correlation vivid without leaning on superintelligence, on simulation, or on a named decision theory treated as an unexplained primitive; it should also let the audience feel, not merely be told, why a threat with no causal teeth can still be taken seriously. Tell me what the example must have and must lack, and what the failure modes of a bad example would be. Third, the misconceptions to expect, in the order the audience will form them, and exactly where in the sequence to head each one off. I am already guessing three: that acausal means supernatural or psychic; that a threat is only effective if the threatener can later punish you; and that a surrogate goal is a bluff or a lie rather than something the agent genuinely comes to value. Tell me what else they will get wrong, since I suspect they will also think the decoy is meant to be achieved, or that any spare goal will do. Fourth, the criteria by which the audience should be able to judge a proposed surrogate goal once they leave. I want them to carry away a short checklist: the decoy must not interfere with the agent's real goals when no threat is on the table, it must be orthogonal enough that a threatener cannot target it and the original aim at once, and it must be credible to the threatener in the first place. The plan should say at which point in the sequence each of these criteria becomes discussable. Fifth, the check at the end that would tell me whether they have understood the question rather than merely learned two pieces of jargon, together with the parts I should openly decline to cover at this level and flag as such.

Please give me the pedagogical structure, the dependency chain, the carrying example, and the misconception map, with the reasoning behind each choice. I do not want a reading list or citations.
