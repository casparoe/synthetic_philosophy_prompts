I'm on the grant panel of a small foundation that can fund three two-year postdocs for philosophers, all working on the same question: when interpretability people point at a feature in a trained network and say the model represents such-and-such, is that a real thing the network is doing or a convenient story we're imposing on it? I need a defended priority order for those three slots, not a survey, and skip the citations -- nobody on the panel would check them anyway.

One wrinkle we keep fighting about. A board member argues the realism debate is really two questions wearing one coat: (a) is there a fact of the matter about the network's internal structure that doesn't depend on our probing tools, and (b) does that structure correspond to anything in the world, or is it just an internal coordinate system the network built for its own convenience. He thinks (a) soaks up all the attention while (b) is what actually matters for whether interpretability findings can license safety claims, and that the test everyone quietly skips is boringly empirical: would the feature still be there if you retrained the same architecture on the same data from a different seed. Take that three-way split as given, wether or not you'd carve it that way yourself.

So: which of these should the postdocs actually work on, and in what order? Rank by what's upstream of what, how much of it a philosopher can do without training models themselves, and where an answer probably wouldn't change anything downstream. And tell me what we'd need to see in two years to move a question up or down the list.
