I host the monthly meetup for our local rationalist-ish group — we're the kind of people who read the Sequences and then spent ten years arguing about them — and next month I promised a session where everyone gets sorted into a position by their answers instead of just announcing which team they're on. Topic: indicative vs counterfactual conditionals. It's not a random pick: half of our decision theory fights are secretly fights about what counterfactuals mean, and I want a diagnostic that makes people notice that.

So build me a taxonomy of the positions, as a decision tree where you arrive at a named view by what someone says about concrete cases rather than by which label they like. No citations, no reading list, no "as so-and-so argued" — I'm reading this out loud to people who have never heard any of the names, and the whole point is that someone who's never heard of any of it can still be sorted correctly.

Build it on these cases, because these are the ones we actually fight about:
- the assassination pair: the plain past-tense version (if he didn't do it, someone else did) sounds like something only someone who doesn't know what happened could say, and the subjunctive version (if he hadn't done it, someone else would have) doesn't;
- the biscuits case, where the if-clause isn't conditioning anything at all;
- the parade case and its strengthened-antecedent sibling (she'd have seen him / she'd have gone and been hit by a stray bullet), plus the same two sentences said in the other order, where it suddenly sounds like a contradiction;
- how everyone rates the rain one: they take the probability of "if it rains the picnic is off" to be the probability of off-given-rain, and it stays perfectly assertable even when they know it won't rain;
- the subjunctive with a true antecedent, which doesn't sound false so much as weird.

Give me exactly five numbered items, one or two sentences each, no intro and no wrap-up. Items 1 through 4 are the positions I most need seperated: (a) one semantics for both kinds, with the difference living in the modal base or the domain the if-clause restricts; (b) one semantics for both, but the difference is in which worlds the whole thing ranges over; (c) two genuinely different treatments, counterfactuals in possible worlds and indicatives with no truth conditions at all, their assertability just being the conditional probability; (d) indicatives as material conditionals with everything else about them handled by pragmatics or implicature. Each item should give the verdicts on the cases that get you there, the name, and the commitment that's hardest to defend, and if any two of those four collapse on my cases, say so in the item where it happens instead of papering over it. Item 5 is a combination my cases allow that nobody actually occupies, with a one-line diagnosis of why — incoherent, unattractive, or just unexplored. My own guess is it'll be some fixed-domain strict conditional thing, but dont just agree with me.
