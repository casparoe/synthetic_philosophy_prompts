I have no training in philosophy. I work in a care home and have spent years around people in pain, and I have arrived at a view I would like tested properly — I would rather hear what counts against it than what counts for it.

The view is this. Pain is bad for the person who has it because of how it feels, and that is the whole story. It is not bad because of what it tells you about your body, not because of what it stops you doing, not because you resent it or want it gone. Take away the damage, the disability, the fear and the resentment, and the hurting that is left is still the harm. The badness just is the sensation.

What makes me unsure is this. I have read about people who, after a stroke or some other injury to the brain, say that a stimulus hurts and yet show no distress at all — they do not pull away, they sometimes laugh — and about badly wounded soldiers who report feeling little or nothing at the time, and about hypnosis that seems to leave the sensation in place while taking the minding away. If those reports are honest, do they show that the badness comes apart from the feeling, or only that my word for the feeling has been covering two things all along? I have also been told that pain is less a report on the body than a demand, a shout rather than a description, and I cannot tell whether that rescues my view or ruins it.

So: lay out the case against my view and rank it, from what should genuinely move me to what is merely awkward, including anything I have not thought of, and say what someone who holds my view should concede rather than explain away. I am especially unsure of one thing. Can a person be in pain that is as bad as ever and simply not mind it, or does not minding it just mean the badness has gone? And if every case of hurting-without-misery is waved off as not really pain, what could ever count against me?
