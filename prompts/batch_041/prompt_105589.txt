I'm making a handout for a first-year grad seminar on Buridan's ass -- the case where you've got two options you take to be exactly equal and no reason to prefer either. I want a decision tree that walks a student from their gut reaction to a labeled position, built from the logical space first rather than from a list of names: so give me the branch questions first, each one a concrete case or a yes/no a student could actually answer without already knowing where it leads, and then the leaves with the positions sitting in that space.

The distinctions I care about (add whatever else you think earns its place): whether the "no preference" is a fact about the options or a state of the agent; whether the agent has to maximize or may satisfice; whether the agent is allowed to manufacture its own tiebreaker -- flip a coin, take the left one, adopt a standing rule like "when indifferent, pick the cheaper" -- and whether that's rational, merely permissible, or a cop-out; and whether the act of committing is itself supposed to generate a reason that wasn't there before. Also throw in two cases that I suspect split students' intuitions: one where waiting is fatal (the ass starves) and one where the choice is reversible. And one where the two options are identical in every respect you care about but differ in some respect you can't bring yourself to care about -- I want to see whether that lands as genuine indifference or as a hidden reason.

Each leaf should carry the position, one sentence on its most awkward commitment, and "unnamed" if nobody actually holds it. Flag any two leaves the tree can't separate, and tell me which answer-combinations come out empty and whether that's because the view is incoherent or just unappealing. Roughly eight leaves. Don't just hand me the stock labels -- I'd rather have descriptive ones, with a name attached only where your sure of it.
