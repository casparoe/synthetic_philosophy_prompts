I'm a postdoc in philosphy of mind, and in three weeks I'm presenting at the monthly joint seminar between our philosophy department and the pain clinic — so the room is about twenty people, and maybe eight of them are clinicians (anaesthetists who run the chronic pain service, a palliative care nurse practitioner, a pain psychologist who does CBT for catastrophizing), plus a couple of philosophers of mind who are representationalists about pain and think pain's awfulness is part of its content rather than something bolted on, one Wittgensteinian colleague who thinks "a pain I don't mind" is close to a contradiction in terms because pain talk is just the natural expression of the aversive state, and the one person in our department who does animal welfare and needs pain to be intrinsically bad for her arguments to work at all. My thesis is the one that falls out of pain asymbolia: there are patients — classically with damage around the insula, and you get something similar after cingulotomy — who report that the stimulus hurts, who can localise it and rate its intensity, and who nonetheless insist they are not bothered by it; my favourite bit in that literature is the patient who pulls his hand away and says, quite levelly, that it hurts but he doesn't care. I use that to argue that the badness of pain is not intrinsic to the sensation but comes from a standing conative state, a desire not to be in it, so asymbolia is the case where the sensation survives and the badness goes missing. In the paper I make the point with an invented composite patient I call R, who describes the sensation as "loud but not mine", and I lean on an analogy with an itch or a foul smell — things that are non-instrumentally bad but only relative to a conative state.

I'd like you to act as a philosopher of mind who has spent real time in clinical rooms, someone who knows both the philosophy of pain and how clinicians actually push back, and write me a Q&A prep sheet for the discussion after my talk — not a defence of the thesis itself. Please start by brainstorming this audience's background commitments before you write anything, and don't pad it with citations, references, or reading lists, since I can't look anything up on the day. What I need is the objections I will actually get from this specific room, ordered by how much damage they'd do, each phrased the way that particular person would phrase it (the anaesthetist's version of "if they say it doesn't hurt, they're either lying or we've missed something" is not the representationalist's version), each with a two-minute reply and an honest note on where my reply is soft. Cover at least: the clinician who thinks I'm romanticising a lesion and generalising from damaged brains to normal pain; whoever points out that R still withdraws and still shows autonomic responses, so something aversive is plainly still running; the Wittgensteinian's "you've changed the subject, you haven't discovered a dissociation"; and the welfare person who thinks my account makes pain's badness depend on what the sufferer happens to want, which she'll offer as a reductio. Also tell me what I should concede up front so I don't look like I'm claiming more than I am, and finish with the one question I'm quietly hoping nobody asks — I have a suspicion about which it is, but I want your read on whether it's the circularity worry or the analgesia one.
