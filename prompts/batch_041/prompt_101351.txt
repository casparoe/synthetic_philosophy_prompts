You're a philosopher who defends what I'll call the shield view of knowledge: if you know that P, then any evidence that later turns up against P is misleading by definition — P is true, so anything pointing the other way has to be a trick of some kind. On this view knowledge doesn't merely let you ignore contrary evidence, it entitles you to. A friend of mine calls the mechanism retroactive insulation: knowing something reaches back and re-labels the evidence before you've even weighed it. I want to strenght-test this.

Play a committed, sharp defender of the shield view — someone who knows the standard objections and has real answers, concedes small points but never the main one, and talks like a person rather than a textbook. Stay in character until I say stop. Before you reply, take a moment to brainstorm the two or three strongest lines of defence you'd actually have available and pick the one you'd lead with; you don't need to show that work. Keep it to a paragraph or so, conversation-sized. Open with your bottom-line verdict in the first sentence, then give the reasoning — no preamble, no restating my question.

Here's my opening: last night I watched my neighbour hide his spare key under the third flowerpot — close up, good light, sober, no funny business; I know where that key is. This morning he tells me he moved it to the garage, and he's never lied to me in ten years. On your view I get to say: I know the key's in the pot, so his testimony is misleading evidence and I may disregard it and keep believing as before. But that's obviously the wrong thing to do — I should update, or at minimum go look. So the shield view makes knowledge a licence to become the guy who never reconsiders anything, and that can't be right. Tell me where I've gone wrong: do I not actually know about the key, or is "ignore him" really licensed? And be careful, because you can't just insist his testimony is misleading — that's the very point in dispute. It's only misleading if I really do know, and the question is whether that knowledge survives being told I'm wrong.
