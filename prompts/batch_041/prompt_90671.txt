Last week a friend and I spent the better part of an evening arguing about Berkeley's views on money, and the argument has been nagging at me ever since. He had just read the queries Berkeley published in Dublin in the 1730s, and he came away convinced that the whole position is either trivial or incoherent. His complaint was this: Berkeley says money is not wealth but a conventional sign, a token whose value is conferred by public credit and whose only office is to facilitate exchange; but a token is only worth something if there is a real thing it is a token to, and the moment you try to say what that real thing is, you either point to gold and silver, which Berkeley is unwilling to treat as the essence of wealth, or you point to the industry and labor of the people, which, on Berkeley's own immaterialism, are no less mind-dependent than the money itself. So either money is backed by something material, which Berkeley cannot allow, or it is backed by nothing but other ideas, in which case the distinction between money and real wealth collapses. I tried to defend Berkeley by saying that his point is precisely that money's value is conferred by public credit, and that this is not a defect but the ordinary condition of value as such; but I could not make the reply stick, and we ended the evening with him accusing Berkeley of mercantilism with the metaphysics left out, and with me unable to explain why the immaterialism should make any difference to the monetary theory at all.

I want a short story that stages this problem rather than resolving it in exposition. Set it in Dublin in the 1730s, or in a present-day situation where the same question arises in a different idiom — a run on a small bank, a town issuing its own scrip during a liquidity crisis, a currency board trying to defend a peg, a community that has agreed to treat something worthless as a medium of exchange. The story should turn on the difference between money as a claim on labor and money as a store of value, and the characters should have to act on that difference without being able to settle it in theory. Please let the concept do structural work: the plot should be constrained by what the characters can and cannot bring themselves to accept as payment, and by what happens when the token and the thing it is a token to come apart. Do not put a lecture in anyone's mouth, and do not quote Berkeley directly — paraphrase his views in your own words if they come up. Aim for something between 1,500 and 2,500 words.
