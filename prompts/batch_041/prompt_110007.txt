ok so my reading group just wrapped a term on Hume and the last session was the suicide essay, the one that only saw print after he was dead. We did the historical side fine — the three-way framing he takes apart (crime against god, against your neighbours, against yourself), the point that someone leaving a life isn't deserting a post they never agreed to take up, the way he keeps insisting the whole thing is a matter of natural facts rather than of some authority being offended. But everyone in the room basically agreed that the interesting version of the question is the one we actually live inside now, and I'd rather have that worked out properly than as a dinner-table take. So: be Hume. Give me his positions, with the reasons he'd actually give, on the machinery of legislated assisted dying as it exists in practice — assessment by two clinicians, a waiting period, a legal category you have to fit yourself into, a form signed while you're still competent, and a profession whose job it is to say yes or no.

Then the specific knots I care about. First, the transfer problem: does his argument survive losing the theological premise, or was the god-shaped bit doing more work than he lets on — is there a buried "nature does nothing in vain / the whole is one system" premise that a secular Hume would have to replace or drop, and does dropping it change the conclusion? Second, would he want the thing decriminalized but unprovisioned, a right nobody is employed to administer, or does he sign off on clinical gatekeeping, and on what grounds? Third, advance directives, and here I want you to use his own theory of personal identity against him: no persisting simple self, identity as something we attribute for practical purposes. If the earlier competent person signed and the later one with dementia seems cheerful, whose preference is it? Does his account of the self commit him to an answer, or dissolve the question — and if it dissolves it, is that a dodge given that somebody has to decide? Fourth, the completed-life case, a healthy 72-year-old with nothing wrong who's just finished, alongside the disability-rights worry that offering it makes it expected and that when home care is underfunded death becomes the cheaper option. Would he count that pressure as a genuine harm or as sentiment? His machinery of artificial virtues and the utility of conventions seems to bear on it and I want to know which way it cuts. Fifth, the case Canada keeps kicking down the road, where the only condition is a mental illness: given his picture of reason as the slave of the passions, does "this preference is irrational" even function as a disqualifier for him, and does that make him more permissive or less? Sixth, the slippery slope, and I want the formal version separated cleanly from the empirical one, plus a Humean verdict on which claims there need evidence and what evidence would actually settle it.

Also tell me where he'd be out of his depth, and where the 18th-century assumptions are doing hidden work — property in one's own life, who counts as a person, the idea that suffering is legible to the people watching it. A few paragraphs, dont hedge everything into mush, I'd rather have a definite reading with the weak joints marked.
