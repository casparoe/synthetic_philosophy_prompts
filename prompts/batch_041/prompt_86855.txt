I'm presenting next month at our philosophy of mind reading group, and I want to be ready for the question period. My line is this: dreams are experiences, but a dream report is mostly a reconstruction. The dreaming brain has only a thin, fragmentary phenomenal field — a few vivid fragments and a lot of noise — and the coherent narrative we give on waking is inferred from that degraded trace rather than replayed from an experience that unfolded in real time during sleep. So Malcolm is wrong to deny that anything is experienced, and Dennett is right that the narrative is confabulated, but Dennett understates the real-time signal: the reconstruction is anchored to a sparse experience that genuinely occurred. The room will contain two Wittgensteinians who take the Malcolmian line that "I dreamt that…" is not a report of anything that happened during sleep, two people who work on predictive processing and active inference, one sleep researcher who studies dream recall and lucid dreaming, and a few agnostics.

Build me a Q&A prep sheet: the objections I will actually get from these people, not the ones a generic skeptic would raise. Order them by how likely and how damaging they are, and phrase each one the way that particular audience member would put it. For each, tell me what to concede up front, a reply that fits in two minutes, and where that reply is weakest. Include the question I'm hoping nobody asks.

Give it as a table with these columns: rank | objector | objection (in their voice) | severity (1–5) | what to concede | two-minute reply | where the reply is soft. Then give the hoped-nobody-asks objection on its own as a JSON object with the fields objection, why_it_worries_me, and best_response.

If the view as I have stated it is confused — for instance, if a "fragmentary phenomenal field" that can only be reached through the very report that is supposed to reconstruct it is not a coherent posit — say so plainly instead of assembling a prep sheet around it. I would rather hear that now.
