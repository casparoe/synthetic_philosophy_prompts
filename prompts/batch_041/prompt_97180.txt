A hospital administrator once told me, without embarrassment, that she couldn't defend the current nurse-to-patient ratio on the merits — she just knew that if they changed it, nobody would ever learn whether the change had been an improvement, because you can't run a hospital twice. So they kept it. I'm a journalist, I cover science and policy, and I've now collected versions of this argument about a paper's print edition, about a state agency's rulebook, and about a friend's decision to stay in a job he hates. In each one the arrangement is never defended as good — sometimes it's conceded to be bad — only as the thing whose alternatives are unknowable, and the unknowability does all the work, because it means whoever proposes the change can never discharge the burden of proof the defender has just assigned them.

I know the Leibniz move — perfect God, so this must be the best arrangement there could be — but that's not it. These people aren't optimists; some of them are openly gloomy about the status quo. What I'm after is the structure underneath, the one that doesn't need any optimism at all.

Is there a name for it? And I don't just want the label. Is it a fallacy, or is it honest deference to the fact that we never get to see the version of events that didn't happen? How does it differ from status quo bias, from Chesterton's fence, from whatever economists mean by second-best, and from conservatism as a temperament rather than an argument? Does it become something else when a reformer runs the same move in reverse — you can't prove things were better before? If nothing established fits, coin a term and tell me what it rules out.
