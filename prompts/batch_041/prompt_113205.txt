I'm in a second-year seminar on free will and I think I've built an argument that proves way too much, and I can't tell if it's a real argument or a trick. Here it is, cleaned up:

(1) You're morally responsible for an action only if you could have done otherwise.
(2) "Could have done otherwise" means there's some possible world with the same past and the same laws of nature in which you act differently.
(3) If determinism is true, then any world with the same past and laws is identical to the actual world, so no such world exists.
(4) So if determinism is true, nobody could ever have done otherwise.
(5) So if determinism is true, nobody is ever morally responsible for anything.
(6) Fine so far, but watch: exactly the same reasoning applies to believing. You believe what you believe because of your past and the laws; given that past and those laws you couldn't have believed otherwise.
(7) So by the same standard, if determinism is true, nobody is ever *responsible for* or *creditable for* their beliefs either.
(8) But then the determinist's own belief in determinism isn't something they can be credited with holding on the basis of reasons -- they just ended up with it.
(9) And if you can't be credited with holding a belief on the basis of reasons, then the belief isn't justified by reasons, it's just caused.
(10) So if determinism is true, no belief is ever justified, including the belief that determinism is true.
(11) So determinism, taken the way I've taken it, refutes itself -- which means something in (1)-(5) must be wrong.

I'd really rather keep (1) and (3), they feel rock solid to me. So which step do I give up, and what does it actually cost me? Also: is the move from (7) to (9) legit, or is that where it breaks?
