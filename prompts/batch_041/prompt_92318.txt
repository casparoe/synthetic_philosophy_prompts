I am a second-year PhD student in economics, working at the intersection of cooperative game theory and normative economics — fair division, distributive justice, the formal side of who should get what. I have roughly two and a half years before I need a dissertation, and room in that time for one substantial project plus one smaller one, possibly with a coauthor. I am comfortable with axiomatic methods and with the standard noncooperative toolbox (demand games, alternating offers, evolutionary stability); I am not an experimentalist and do not want to run human-subjects work. My department rewards work that other people build on, so upstreamness matters to me more than fashion.

The area I keep circling is the Kalai–Smorodinsky (KS) bargaining solution: the point on the Pareto frontier of a two-person bargaining problem at which each player's gain over the disagreement point is the same fraction of the most that player could get, given the feasible set — geometrically, the intersection of the frontier with the line from the disagreement point to the ideal point. It keeps Pareto optimality, symmetry, and invariance to positive affine transformations of the utilities, and in place of Nash's independence of irrelevant alternatives it uses a monotonicity condition to the effect that a player whose feasible possibilities improve at every payoff level of the other should not end up worse off.

What I want from you is an agenda, not a survey and not a reading list. Here are the lines of work I have been considering; rank them, tell me which two or three I should actually commit to, and defend the ordering. Feel free to add a line I have not named, or to tell me one of mine is a dead end and why.

1. The many-player problem. In the two-person case a compact axiom set pins KS down. For three or more players the natural extensions either fail to exist or force a choice among the axioms, and I am unsure whether that is a deep impossibility or an artifact of insisting on a particular list. Which relaxation is least costly, and can a defensible many-player analogue be built that is not simply one of the standard proportional or equal-loss rules wearing a new name?

2. A noncooperative foundation. Nash's solution has a canonical noncooperative story; I am not aware of an equally canonical one for KS. Is there a bargaining procedure whose equilibrium, or limit outcome, is the KS point — and if there is not, does that indicate that KS is normatively attractive but behaviorally unmotivated, or merely that nobody has looked hard enough?

3. Endogenous or adaptive reference points. KS is defined against a fixed ideal point. In real bargaining, what counts as the most one could get moves as offers are exchanged. Is there a coherent version with an endogenous reference point that updates, and does any axiomatic characterization survive the move?

4. Non-convex feasible sets. The line-to-the-ideal-point construction and the equal-fractions property both lean on convexity. What is the right analogue when the feasible set is not convex, and does the answer track the way the Nash solution is extended there?

5. Package deals and endogenous frontiers. When players bargain over several issues at once, trading concessions across issues expands the Pareto frontier. Does the KS proportionality idea say anything about which packages form — a formal counterpart to logrolling — or does the framework simply take the frontier as given and therefore miss the interesting part?

6. The informational basis. KS is cardinal yet scale-invariant: it uses more than ordinal preference information and less than full interpersonal comparability. Is there an ordinal characterization, and how does the equal-fractions criterion relate to the older equal-sacrifice traditions in public finance?

7. The ethical content. Is equalizing fractions of possible gain a defensible ideal or an artifact of the axioms? Why fractions rather than differences, and how does KS sit next to maximin, Rawlsian, and egalitarian criteria — does it capture a distinct and attractive conception of fairness, or is it a technical convenience?

8. Behavioral and computational uptake. Do human bargainers approximate KS rather than Nash? And more recently, when language-model or reinforcement-learning agents negotiate with one another, what do they converge to, and should that change what we think the right solution is?

For the ranking I want the criteria made explicit and weighed against each other: how much other work depends on the answer (upstreamness), tractability for someone with my tools and my timeline, how neglected the line is, and whether settling it would actually change anything downstream — in theory, in applied bargaining, or in how we evaluate machine negotiators.
