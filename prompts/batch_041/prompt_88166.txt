I am preparing a seminar on the Buddhist doctrine of anattā, and a colleague has handed me an argument that she believes shows the doctrine cannot be held consistently alongside the soteriology it is meant to serve. I have been through it several times and cannot locate the misstep; if there is none, I need to know which premise I should surrender and what surrendering it would cost. Please diagnose it.

The argument:

(1) To be a person is, at minimum, to be the kind of thing that can be the subject of experience and the bearer of responsibility.
(2) For there to be a subject of experience, there must be some single thing that persists through the experiences had; otherwise a claim such as "I am now undergoing the fruit of what was done" would not relate the one who undergoes to the one who acted, and memory, blame, and desert would each attach to different things.
(3) The Abhidharma analysis resolves what we call a person into momentary aggregates — form, feeling, perception, formations, and consciousness — each of which arises and ceases, none of which endures from one moment to the next.
(4) Nothing that persists is to be found among the aggregates, behind them, or beside them.
(5) Hence there is no persisting subject; by (2) there is no subject of experience; by (1) there is no person; and so no one is the bearer of responsibility for anything.
(6) Yet the whole practical teaching — the cultivation of merit, the avoidance of harmful action, the aspiration to liberation — presupposes that what one does now has consequences for the one who will undergo them.
(7) Therefore either (2) is true, in which case anattā undermines the very practice it is meant to serve, or (2) is false, in which case anattā has removed only the cruder, unchanging self while leaving the person intact.

I would rather keep (6) and the coherence of practice than keep (2), but I want to understand the price. Tell me which step to reject, why it looked compelling, and what rejecting it commits me to. In particular, address the following.

First, if the response is that the subject need not be a persisting thing but only a causally continuous stream, say what work "same stream" is doing that "same thing" was supposed to do, and whether it can bear the weight of desert, or whether responsibility then becomes a matter of degree proportional to the strength of the causal linkage.

Second, test the response against cases where continuity is interrupted or duplicated — deep sleep and coma, radical amnesia, and the fission of one stream into two — and say whether the stream view gives defensible verdicts there or merely redescription.

Third, assess the moves that have historically been made to escape this bind: the position that the person is neither identical with nor distinct from the aggregates, and the postulation of a subliminal consciousness that carries the traces of action forward. For each, say whether it is a genuine resolution or a covert reintroduction of the self under another name, and what it costs to adopt.

Finally, say what the doctrine loses if (2) is kept and practice is reconceived rather than rescued, and what it loses if (2) is dropped — in either case naming the claim that must be abandoned and the downstream consequences for how karma, memory, and liberation are to be understood.
