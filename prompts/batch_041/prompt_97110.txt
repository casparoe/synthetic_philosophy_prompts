I'm a science journalist at work on a feature about the second law of thermodynamics, and Maxwell's demon keeps surfacing in my interviews. I've assembled a picture of the debate, and before I commit it to print I'd like you to pressure-test it, because I suspect I've absorbed a tidy version that the specialists would not endorse.

As I understand it, the demon was a genuine paradox for roughly a century until Landauer's principle closed the case: because erasing a single bit of information must dissipate at least kT ln 2 of energy, the demon's eventual memory reset costs precisely the work it managed to extract, and the second law is thereby rescued. On this reading, the entire burden of saving thermodynamics rests on the erasure step — it is the one thing standing between us and a perpetual motion machine.

What has unsettled me is that I keep encountering two further claims that seem to sit awkwardly with that story. First, there are reports of so-called information engines — small devices that use a measurement to extract work from a single heat bath — which I take to be exactly what the demon was supposed to do. Second, I've read proposals suggesting a demon could in principle measure and reset its memory reversibly, with no erasure cost whatsoever. If erasure is the sole guardian of the second law, then a reversible, non-erasing demon ought to be a working perpetual motion machine, and these single-bath engines ought to be fraudulent. Since the researchers involved insist they are neither, I'm left with what looks like a forced choice: either the standard Landauer account is simply mistaken, or the demon is a real counterexample that physics has been quietly papering over for decades. Which is it?

I'd like you to tell me plainly where my framing goes wrong, if it does. Please separate what is genuinely settled among physicists from what remains contested — in particular, do not present any one reconstruction of the demon (Landauer's, or any other) as though it were the unanimous consensus if specialists still disagree about the details. And please end with a final line beginning "Verdict:" followed by a short phrase of no more than about fifteen words capturing whether my framing holds up.
