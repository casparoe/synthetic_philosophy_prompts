I write about science and ideas for a monthly, and I'm working on a piece about a particular flavor of collective failure that people have started calling Moloch, or a multipolar trap. What makes it intresting to me is that it isn't just a prisoner's dilemna: in the pure version nobody is cheating, nobody has secret information, and every player would genuinely prefer that the whole thing stop — and yet each keeps going, because whoever unilaterally backs off first gets eaten alive by the ones who don't. Arms races are the stock example, but so is a fishing fleet that can't stop expanding until the cod are gone, or a neighborhood where every family has to spend more and more on tutoring because every other family does. I rewatched Jaws last weekend (my brother in law insists it's the greatest American movie, I'm not so sure) and it hit me that the first half plays almost like a textbook case and the second half almost like a refutation of the whole idea.

So I'd like you to read the film through this lens for me. Where does the trap reading actually hold — the mayor and the merchants who won't close the beaches, the coroner who gets leaned on to sign off on a boating accident, the bounty hunters and amateurs who pile onto the water and make things worse, the way nobody can afford to be the one who says the word "shark" out loud during the Fourth of July weekend? And where does it fall apart, since the shark is a genuine external threat rather than a competitor, and the town isn't really a field of symmetric rivals racing each other downward — the mayor's interests and the swimmers' interests actually diverge, which is a different structure than Moloch. I'm interested in the honest version of this reading, not a cute one: what does the movie see about self-defeating collective behavior that the arms-race and tragedy-of-the-commons examples in the literature tend to miss, what does it get wrong or simplify, and is Brody even the right figure for the person who tries to step out of the trap? Which two or three scenes would you send readers to, and does the ending — where the trap gets resolved by blowing up the shark — undermine or quietly comment on the whole idea? I don't need a plot summary, and please don't quote any dialogue, just describe it in your own words.

Also, write it as continuous prose, the way you'd explain it to me over email — no headings, no bullet points, no bold, no tables, and lead with whatever your bottom line is before you get into the details.
