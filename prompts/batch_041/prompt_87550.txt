I am putting together a graduate seminar on comparative cognition, and before we examine any particular account I want the evaluative framework on the table. The object of assessment is a theory of cognitive kinds: a theory that sorts minds into grades or types rather than treating cognition as one undifferentiated capacity. The fourfold scheme we will use as our stalking horse distinguishes Darwinian creatures, whose behavioral repertoire is fixed by natural selection and expressed without individual learning; Skinnerian creatures, which acquire new behavior by trial and error and retain what pays off; Popperian creatures, which can run a candidate action or hypothesis through an internal model and discard it before committing to it in the world; and Gregorian creatures, which extend their competence by incorporating external devices, language and culturally transmitted instruments foremost among them. Treat that scheme as the target, not the answer. What I want from you is the specification any theory of this kind must satisfy.

Lay out the desiderata, ordered by how much work each does. For each, give the consideration that motivates it, and then classify it as non-negotiable or as a preference of one camp within the field.

I expect the list to cover at least the following, though you should add whatever else the area demands and drop anything you judge to be mere prejudice:

- The cases the theory must get right, stated concretely rather than by label. Include at minimum: an organism whose competence is realized only in a creature-plus-artifact loop, so that no intrinsic property of the creature alone accounts for the performance; a creature that behaves as a Popperian simulator but is plausibly a Skinnerian learner running against an unusually fast and forgiving feedback channel; a capacity that migrates from the Gregorian grade to the Darwinian one as it becomes canalized or genetically assimilated over evolutionary time; and a competence that is realized only across a social group, with no single member holding it.
- The distinctions the theory must not collapse: between a capacity being possessed and its being exercised; between the creature's intrinsic organization and the resources its niche supplies; between learning and simulating; between a genuinely new grade and a difference of degree along an existing one; and between what a creature does and what an interpreter can predict of it.
- The findings from ethology, comparative psychology, and evolutionary biology the theory cannot contradict, with the finding behind each constraint.

Then do the part I care about most. Identify any pair or subset of desiderata you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied, and say precisely why the tension is unavoidable rather than an artifact of a clumsy theory -- for instance, whether a requirement of discrete, mutually exclusive grades can be held together with a requirement that the theory respect continuous variation in the underlying capacities, or whether individuating a kind by intrinsic mechanism can survive the demand that it also track ecological function. Finally, name the single requirement that excludes the most otherwise-attractive candidate theories, and explain what it rules out and why the exclusion is a cost rather than a virtue.
