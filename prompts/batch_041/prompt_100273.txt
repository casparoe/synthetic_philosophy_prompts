I sit on the board of the Willow Flats Irrigation District, a cooperative of 214 member households that draws from a single shallow aquifer. Our annual meeting is in three weeks, and I have been asked to circulate a written opinion setting out what the board should adopt.

Some background you will need. The aquifer recharges from winter snowpack and is currently drawn down about four feet a year. The district's only affordable monitoring is one flow gauge at the head of the main canal, which records aggregate withdrawal; individual offtakes are unmetered and will stay that way under every option before us, since fitting 214 meters would cost more than the district's entire annual budget. So the board can see the total taken but never who took it. Extraction is seasonal, and demand and recharge are driven by the same snowpack, so a wet year is also a low-demand year and a dry year is also a high-demand year; a fixed cap calibrated to an average year is therefore either toothless or ruinous depending on the year. Canal maintenance is a second, separate burden: the ditch must be cleared each spring by volunteer labor, and no household can be excluded from the water that labor delivers. The western third of the district has pumped since the 1950s and holds senior claims; the eastern third was annexed in 1998 and holds junior claims, and the two ends of the district have opposed each other on every question for twenty years.

Three proposals are on the table. The first is the status quo: voluntary targets, an honor system, and a part-time ditch rider who reports only the aggregate. The second would cap total annual withdrawal at a figure tied to measured snowpack and auction the resulting permits each spring to whoever bids highest. The third, drafted by a member with training in game theory, would partition the district into cells of roughly six households; each cell would receive an aggregate allocation, would be free to divide it internally however it likes, would be penalized as a whole if its members' combined use exceeded the cell's allocation, and would earn a larger next-year allocation if it also logged canal-maintenance hours. The penalty falls on the cell regardless of which member over-pumped.

Draft the opinion I will circulate. It should recommend one of the three options — or a specific modification of one — state plainly what the board should do and by when, and give the reasoning in terms the members could actually argue with. It must engage the strongest objection to your recommendation honestly rather than dismissing it; in this case I expect the hardest objection to be that whole-cell liability punishes the careful for a neighbor's conduct, and that cells will sort themselves by wealth, seniority, and geography in ways that harden exactly the divisions that have paralyzed us. Roughly 1,000 words, addressed to the board, no citations.
