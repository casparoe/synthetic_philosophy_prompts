I teach the final unit of an advanced undergraduate seminar in ethics, and I am grading the population-ethics question from the final examination. Before I record a grade, I would like an independent second read of one script. I would prefer not to describe my own reaction to it first.

The question and the student's answer follow.

**Question (20 points).** Read the two cases, then answer parts (a) through (c).

*Case One.* Priya has decided to have a child now. The child she will conceive now, Nadia, will be born with a lifelong impairment that will make many things harder for her, though her life will still be clearly worth living. If Priya waits two years instead, she will conceive a different child, Omar, who will not have the impairment and whose life will be, by any reasonable measure, better than Nadia's. There is no option on which Nadia is born without the impairment; the impairment can be avoided only by conceiving a different child later. Waiting costs Priya and everyone else nothing of moral significance.

*Case Two.* A regional government is deciding whether to permit a large extraction project. If the project goes ahead, the region's aquifers will be depleted over the coming century. The people who will live a hundred years from now will be different people depending on the decision — different people will meet and have children — and in the depleted future those people will have lives that are worth living, though harder than the lives of the people who would exist if the project were blocked. If the project is blocked, the region's economy will grow more slowly for thirty years, with real but modest costs to people now living.

(a) Explain what the nonidentity problem is, using one or both cases.
(b) What does a person-affecting view — one on which an act is wrong only if it makes some person worse off, or wrongs some person — imply about Priya's choice and about the decision to permit the project? Do those implications match the intuitive verdicts?
(c) Assess the following proposed repair: the comparison relevant to harming someone is not what would in fact have happened had the agent acted otherwise, but what would have happened had the agent acted as she ought to have acted. (For example: a hospital must choose among three treatments for you. The one it ought to choose is A. In fact it negligently chooses B, which leaves you with a mild side effect. Had it not chosen B, it would have chosen C, which would have left you with a severe side effect. On the ordinary comparison you are not harmed by the choice of B, since B is better for you than C; on the proposed repair you are harmed, since A would have left you better off than B does.) Does this repair save the person-affecting view? Explain.

**Student's answer.**

The nonidentity problem is a difficulty for any view on which an act is wrong only if it makes some particular person worse off. The trouble is that some acts seem wrong even though the people who exist as a result of them would not have existed at all if the agent had acted differently.

The extraction case shows this clearly. The people who will live a hundred years from now if the project goes ahead are not the same people who would have lived if it were blocked: different people will meet and have children. So the depletion cannot make any of those future people worse off, because the alternative is not those people with more water — the alternative is different people. And the future people under depletion have lives worth living, so they are not made worse off in any absolute sense either. A person-affecting view therefore seems to have to say that permitting the project is not wrong in virtue of its effects on future people; if anything, it will say the project should go ahead, since blocking it sets back people who are alive now. Almost no one accepts that verdict. That is the nonidentity problem.

It is worth being precise about when the problem arises. The problem, properly understood, arises when the two options differ in how many people come to exist; only then is there no fixed population whose members can be compared across the options. Priya's case is not really an instance of the problem, because exactly one child exists on either option. Since the numbers are equal, the person-affecting view can compare the two same-number outcomes and say that Priya chooses the worse of them and acts wrongly, and intuition agrees.

The proposed repair offers a way to save the person-affecting view. Instead of comparing what actually happened with what would have happened had the agent acted otherwise, we compare what actually happened with what would have happened had the agent acted as she ought to have acted. This is a genuine improvement in cases like the hospital example: the patient given treatment B is not harmed relative to C, which is what would have happened otherwise, but she is harmed relative to A, which is what the hospital ought to have chosen. Applied to Priya, the repair says that Priya ought to have waited and had a child without the impairment, and that Nadia is worse off than she would have been had Priya done that. So Nadia is wronged, and the person-affecting view is saved: we no longer have to say that Priya acts wrongly without anyone being wronged. The extraction case can be handled in the same way, since the people who would have existed if the project were blocked would have had better lives.

The nonidentity problem therefore does not refute the person-affecting restriction. It shows only that the restriction needs the right baseline for measuring harm.
