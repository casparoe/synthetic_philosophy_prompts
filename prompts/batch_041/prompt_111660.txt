I'm a software engineer with no formal training in philosophy, and I've been trying to work out what I actually believe about museum restitution — the claims made for objects taken during the colonial period, and for objects whose path into European and North American collections runs through it. My opinions turn out not to come with rationales attached, so I've written them out the way I'd write out a set of test cases, and I want to know what rule, if any, they are instances of. Verdicts first.

1. The Parthenon sculptures in the British Museum. Return to Athens. Elgin's agents removed them in the first decade of the 1800s under a permit from the Ottoman authorities, whose scope is itself disputed, and I don't think a permit settles anything: I can't see how an authority that was an occupying power could alienate the fabric of a building that was the emblem of another people's history.

2. The bronzes taken from Benin City when British forces sacked it in 1897, now dispersed across European and North American museums. Return to Nigeria. This is my most confident verdict of the set — but see 6.

3. A painting sold in 1939 by a Jewish family under the threat of what was coming, with paperwork that is formally in order, bought in good faith by a museum that has held it since. Return to the heirs, or compensate them at full value if the object has to stay. The museum's good faith and the eighty intervening years don't seem to me to do any work at all.

4. Human remains collected for anatomical study in the nineteenth century, from a community that is still there and wants them for burial. Return, and more obviously than anything above. But my reason isn't the one I'd have expected: the remains were never property, so provenance isn't the issue; the claim is about a relationship to the dead that the collecting institution never had standing to interrupt.

5. An object given as a gift in the 1920s by a local ruler to a visiting European ethnographer, documented in writing, no force used. I lean toward keeping it. And I can't defend the difference from 1: in both cases the transfer was authorised by whoever held local power, and in both the transaction happened inside an imperial relationship that made the European party the one with options.

6. An object that the national government of the country of origin claims, where the community it actually came from says the national museum in the capital is a second extraction and wants it in the town. I think the community beats the state. Which makes me realise that in 1 and 2 I was quietly treating Greece and Nigeria as the owners.

7. Digital repatriation: the museum keeps the object and supplies high-resolution scans and a licence to use them. Not restitution — a consolation prize. But I can only justify that by claiming that the physical presence of the thing matters, and that's the kind of claim I'd want argued for rather than assumed.

8. An object that the source community probably cannot preserve, or that the government receiving it will likely sell. Return anyway. I don't accept that the present holder gets to keep something because it believes it will look after it better. This sits badly next to 7 in a way I can't articulate: there I'm saying location matters, here I'm saying custodial competence doesn't.

9. An object that the source kingdom had itself taken as war booty from a neighbouring people a couple of centuries before the Europeans took it, with the neighbouring people now claiming it. I have no verdict, and my instinct in 2 doesn't tell me which way to go.

The rule I'd write if I had to: return an object when the chain of title runs through a transfer that wasn't a real transfer, where a transfer is unreal if it was made under force or threat, or under an asymmetry of power severe enough that consent wasn't available. Return it also when the object is of a kind that a group can hand over physically without its ever ceasing to belong to that group — I've met this idea somewhere, probably in anthropology, and I'd want it stated properly, since it seems to be doing the work in 1 that the permit argument can't. Don't return objects that were transferred with real consent and are not of that kind, which is what keeps 5.

What I want: whether that two-clause rule actually generates the nine verdicts, and which of them it fails on; whether there is a one-clause rule that does better, and if so which verdict I'd have to give up to get it; and what either rule says about cases I haven't listed — an object with no provenance records at all, where the source community's claim is plausible but unprovable; an object the source community wants back in order to put it in a museum of its own rather than in ritual use; and an object held by a private collector rather than an institution.

Two things to be honest about. First, I suspect I'm running two different questions together — undoing a wrong, and putting things back where they belong — and that my list is really two lists. If that's right, say so and separate them rather than manufacturing one rule that spans both. Second, if the framing is confused — if nine verdicts from one person is too little to reverse-engineer a principle from, or if the honest answer is that these are a coalition of concerns with no unifying rule — tell me plainly instead of fitting a tidy rule to my list. I'd rather have the disagreement than the tidiness.
