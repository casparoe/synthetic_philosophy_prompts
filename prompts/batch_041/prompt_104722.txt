I'm running a 75-minute session for a mixed group next month — mostly cognitive science grad students, three or four philosophers, and one stray neuroscientist — and the topic is Hofstadter's careenium: the frictionless pool table full of tiny magnetic marbles that stick together into big slow clusters, simms and simmballs. About half of them have read some Hofstadter and half haven't, and all of them are primed to hear anything about marbles and minds as a consciousness argument, which is exactly what I don't want. Don't explain the thought experiment to me. Plan how I should teach the who-shoves-whom problem it's built around — do the simmballs push the simms, or the simms push the simmballs, or is that a bad question.

What I need from you:

First, the order the pieces have to go in. I want the who-shoves-whom question to land as a real puzzle with a non-trivial answer, not as a riddle where the answer is obviously both, and not as a trick where I've already decided the higher level is the real one. So tell me what has to be in place before the question can even be asked properly — what does the audience need to have accepted about levels, about explanation, about causation, before the question stops sounding like a Zen koan — and why each piece has to come before the next. If some piece turns out to be load-bearing only because of a misconception I'm about to create, say so.

Second, the carrying example. One example should do the work for the whole session, and I want to know which one and why that one and not the obvious runners-up. The ant colony is the one everyone reaches for; tell me whether it should carry this or whether it's already too theory-laden, and what a replacement would need to have. The example has to make the audience feel the pull of the simm-level story (it's complete, it's causally sufficient, nothing is left over) at the same time as the pull of the simmball-level story (the simmball pattern is what explains why the simms are where they are). If the example can't do both pulls at once, say so and tell me what to do instead. Also tell me what the example must not have — no consciousness, no agents, no goals, nothing that smuggles in a homunculus at the top level.

Third, the misconceptions, in the order they'll actually arrive, and where in my sequence to head each one off. I'm expecting at least these: that the simmball level is just a convenient fiction and the simms are the real causes; that the simmball level is a genuinely new force, downward causation, spooky emergence, take your pick; that both is a cop-out answer rather than the whole point; that causal sufficiency and explanatory sufficiency are the same thing; that this is secretly a position on free will or consciousness; and that the analogy is an argument for panpsychism or for strong emergence because someone heard the word levels once. I'd also like the ones I haven't thought of. For each, tell me the exact moment in the sequence where I should defuse it — before it forms, or after they've felt it — and what defusing it actually looks like. Some of these I probably want them to feel first, and I need you to say which ones.

Fourth, the check for understanding. One question I can put to the room at the end that will tell me whether they've got the level distinction or are just nodding along, and what a bad answer to it would look like versus a good one. I don't want a question they can answer by reciting the analogy back.

Fifth, what to leave out at this level and say out loud that I'm leaving out. I have 75 minutes and I'd rather cut cleanly than gesture at everything.

Don't give me readings or citations, I'll paraphrase the thought experiment in my own words and I don't need to lean on Hofstadter's exact phrasing. Just the plan.
