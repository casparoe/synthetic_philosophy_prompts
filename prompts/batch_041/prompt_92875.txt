Last week my reading group finished that late Nietzsche book where he gives himself all those grandiose chapter titles, and the chapter where he suddenly starts going on about food, where you live, the weather, and how he spends his free time turned into a two-hour fight that spilled out onto the sidewalk after everyone else had gone home. It kicked off because someone opened a bottle of wine, Marcus waved it off and said he was following the old man's advice, and Dana laughed and said the old man's advice on that front was a mess. Here's roughly how it went. (1) Dana: the whole diet-and-health business is either empty or contradictory. On one page health is individual — what's poison for one person is medicine for another — and on the next he's handing down blanket bans and rules: no alcohol, small light meals, nothing heavy, get the place and the climate and your recreations right, walk constantly, never sit still. You can't be a relativist about the body and also issue commandments. (2) Marcus: he isn't issuing commandments. He keeps flagging that he's only reporting on himself, on the one case he's watched longest, and that the report is supposed to make you run your own experiment rather than be a law for everybody. And the real thesis underneath isn't 'eat this way' — it's that the things moralists treat as questions of will, virtue and sin are mostly questions of regimen, physiology, climate, company. That's a genuine claim about where our 'spiritual' states actually come from, and it's the kind of claim that could turn out false. (3) Dana: that makes it unfalsifiable, which is worse than contradictory — every time a rule starts looking universal you just relabel it as personal. And he's not even consistent about the individualism, because half the time he talks about these things as fate, as inherited, as settled in your ancestors before you got any say. You can't be a radical individualist about diet and a determinist about diet in the same breath. (4) Marcus: those fit together once the unit isn't 'you, unique snowflake' but a type — a constitution with a history. The fate talk is about how much of our thinking runs downstream of the body; it isn't a claim that you can't tinker. And the reason the experiment has to be on yourself is precisely that the interesting variable is your particular constitution — a general dietetics for everyone would be exactly the thing he's denying exists. (5) Dana: then he owes us a method and he doesnt give one. 'Notice what suits you' is advice you could give without writing the book, and his own case is the weakest evidence imaginable — one subject, no controls, and the man was euphoric and weeks away from a total collapse while he was writing it. If regimen really shapes thought that much, his breakdown is a counterexample, not a proof of concept. Marcus said the collapse came after the most productive stretch of his life and that he never once blamed his habits, and that a report from the one life you've observed from the inside isn't nothing; Dana said a report from a man who's about to go mad is exactly nothing.

Can you go through this the way it was actually argued — rate each of the five moves from clearly mistaken to clearly right, say who's right about what, and then tell me who's closer to right overall? I'm especially curious whether the 'he's only talking about himself' move really saves him or just makes his claims unfalsifiable, and whether there's a coherent middle position where some regimens are individual while others (the booze, say) are bad for everyone. I also want to know if Dana's right that you can't hold the individualist line and the fate line at once, or if Marcus has a real way of reconciling them. Don't just split the difference — if one of them is making a bad argument I'd like to know which one and why.
