Background, because two of the steps depend on ideas from class and I don't want them read as throwaway lines:

- A compromise between two value systems is justified, when it is, by a gains-from-trade structure: each side gives up something it cares about and ends up with more of what it cares about overall than under continued conflict. Both parties prefer the deal to the standoff, by their own lights.
- Part of what makes moral compromise work — and I think this is doing all the work — is that a compromise can change what you value. Concede on X for long enough and X stops being the thing you'd have gone to the wall for. You don't just act differently, the ranking changes. Someone in class called this the ratchet, no idea if that's a term of art.

Now the argument.

(1) If an arrangement is a Pareto improvement over the status quo, there's a pro tanto reason to move to it.
(2) In most real value conflicts there exist mutual concessions that both parties prefer, by their own lights, to the standoff.
(3) So in most real value conflicts there's a pro tanto reason to compromise.
(4) Compromise can change the parties' rankings (the ratchet point).
(5) Because rankings can change, a sequence of pairwise deals is possible in which each step is preferred by both parties to the step before, and which loops back to an arrangement that is worse for both parties than where they started — worse by their own lights at the end, and far worse by their own lights at the beginning.
(6) At every step of that sequence there was a pro tanto reason to take the next step, and nothing that outweighed it.
(7) So there's a pro tanto reason to arrive at an arrangement that is worse for everyone than the status quo, and such chains can be walked from anywhere to anywhere.
(8) A principle that generates a reason to move to a state worse for everyone, iterably, justifies everything and so justifies nothing. Therefore (1) is false, and with it the whole gains-from-trade account of why compromise is ever good.
