I'm a philosopher of biology and next month I've got the outside-speaker slot at a molecular biology department's weekly seminar -- about 30 people, mostly bench folks working on bacterial genetics and protein engineering, a couple of computational people who do codon optimization for recombinant expression, one emeritus who spent his whole career on the evolution of the code, and one philosophy of science grad student who'll be quietly on my side. 45 minute talk, questions usually run long, and there are two labs there doing genome recoding / genetic-firewall work, which is honestly the only reason I think they invited me. My thesis, roughly: the slogan that a gene codes for a protein, or that DNA carries information for the organism, isn't just a loose metaphor everyone understands is loose -- its doing real work in how people reason about genomes, and the strong reading of it is false. Three moves. First, coding-for is a relation that only holds between a sequence and a product relative to a translation apparatus, so what a stretch of DNA means depends on machinery that isn't in the DNA; I lean on a swap test I made up, where you hold the sequence fixed and change the reader, and the meaning changes -- and the reader does change in nature, mitochondria and some protists read certain codons differently, so even the universality of the code is a local fact. Second, on the strong reading, information has to be the kind of thing that can be right or wrong, and I want to argue DNA can't misrepresent anything: a mutation isn't an error about the organism, it just makes a different organism, and the error only appears once you've already decided what the organism was supposed to be. Third, the difference-making sense of information (this nucleotide makes a difference to this trait) is fine and I'll grant it, but it doesn't support any of the blueprint or program rhetoric, and people slide from one sense to the other without noticing.

So give me the Q&A prep sheet for this room specifically. The objections I'll actually get from bench molecular biologists, not the ones I'd get from a room of philosophers of biology -- in their vocabulary, in roughly the order they'll come, ordered by how likely each one is and how much damage it does to the talk. For each: what I should concede up front, the two-minute reply, and a note on where the reply is soft. I already know the "it's just semantics, nobody thinks DNA is literally a blueprint" one is coming and I have a rough answer, so don't spend the most space on that one. Tell me which single objection I'm most likely to lose the room on, and the one question I'm hoping nobody asks -- and then actually make me answer it. Don't hand me a reading list, I need the replies, not papers.

At the end: tell me which consideration, if it turned out to be true, would make me drop the thesis, and which step of my own argument you think is most likely to be wrong. Be blunt about the second one -- I'd rather find out now than from the emeritus.
