I'm not a philosopher; I've just been reading William James on my own, and there's a tension in his view I keep circling. He took seriously the thought that experience might go all the way down, yet he also insisted that elementary experiences cannot simply add up into a composite one. That leaves a question that couldn't have been put to him: if experience does go all the way down, does anything follow for value — is there suffering, or anything that could be good or bad for anyone, at the level of fundamental physics? I've been dividing the answers into two. On the grain view, moral concern tracks the number of elementary subjects, so that merely rearranging matter is morally loaded even where no integrated subject exists. On the whole view, elementary experiences carry no valence and only integrated subjects like animals matter, so panpsychism has no ethical upshot at all.

What I'd like you to do is predict what James would have said about this, and in particular about three things that only became live questions after his death: whether anything can be bad for an entity with no needs or interests; whether a digital system computing in the right way would count as a candidate integrated subject under either view, and whether the grain view would absurdly make any computation a site of moral concern; and whether his pragmatism would have licensed him to dismiss the grain view as a distinction without a practical difference, or whether that dismissal costs him something he needs elsewhere. Anchor the prediction in commitments he actually held, say where he would have had to revise his view or concede that the question is genuinely new, and tell me where your prediction is least secure.
