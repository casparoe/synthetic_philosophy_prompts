I write about science and ideas for a living, so I'm constantly absorbing philosophy secondhand, and I've learned to be suspicous of my own summaries. Right now I'm working on a column about "expected value fanaticism" and I keep going back to a piece by a blogger I mostly trust. Before I repeat any of it in print I'd like to know how much of it survives scrutiny.

His argument, as I can reconstruct it:

"Expected value maximization says you should always pick the option with the highest expected value — the payoff of each outcome times its probability, added up. Take that seriously and you're led straight to Pascal's mugging: a stranger offers you a reward of a googolplex units of value if you hand over your wallet, on a probability he freely admits is one in a googol. Multiply it out and the expected value is still astronomically large, so the good expected-value maximizer hands over the wallet. This shows expected value maximization is self-undermining, because the same math tells you to spend your whole life chasing whichever tiny-probability jackpot has the biggest product, and you could never justify doing anything else. The only way out is to give up expected value maximization and adopt something like maximin, or just a blunt heuristic. In the end, though, the whole problem is really about infinities — it only bites if you let infinite utilities into the picture, so the fix most decision theorists now accept is simply to insist that every value is finite. And honestly, the fact that no one actually hands the mugger their wallet is by itself enough to refute the theory."

I'll admit I kind of buy the part about infinities — that sounds right to me — but the last line struck me as a cheap shot even before I knew anything about the topic. Could you go through it claim by claim and tell me what a decision theorist would nod along to, what's a real position being oversimplified, and what's flatly wrong? I especially want to know whether the "fanaticism" he's describing is actually the same thing as expected value maximization, and whether his proposed fix actually fixes the problem he set up. If some of his punchier conclusions only land because of a mistake earlier in the chain, point that out.
