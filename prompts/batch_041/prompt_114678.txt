I'm a phil prof and I've got a grad student who wants to spend a semester writing on the Pasadena paradox -- you know, the coin-flip game where the payoff alternates sign and grows fast enough that the positive and negative parts both have infinite expectation, so the expected value comes out undefined rather than just infinite. I want to steer them toward the open questions that actually matter most, not just the ones that are fun to state. So: if you had to hand a smart philosopher with one semester and no empirical resources an agenda in this area, what should they prioritize and why? I'm thinking of questions like: Is the right lesson that expected utility theory is incomplete, or that the game is somehow ill-posed? Does undefined expectation mean there's no fact of the matter about what's rational, or just that our standard representation theorems don't cover it? How much does the Pasadena case really add beyond St. Petersburg and its variants? What's the significance of the fact that every finite truncation has a well-defined expectation that can converge, while the full game's expectation is undefined? And does any of this connect to infinite ethics, Pascal's wager, or decision-making under deep uncertainty in a way that makes it more than a curiosity? Rank the questions. I want the dependency structure: which answers would make other questions easier, which are upstream of which, which are tractable for a philosopher rather than a mathematician, and what would move a question up or down the list. Also, I've been playing with a variant I don't have a name for: you're offered a Pasadena-style game, but you can also pay a small fee to have the payoff schedule reordered -- same outcomes, same probabilities, but the positive and negative terms get summed in a different order. Intuitively that shouldn't matter, but in the Pasadena case it seems to. Does that variant raise a genuinely new open question about the relationship between expectation, ordering, and rationality, or is it just a restatement of the original problem? If it's new, where does it fit in the ranking? I'm definately not looking for a survey or a reading list -- I want your own defended ordering and the reasoning behind it.
