Consider the following thesis about perception, which I will label the expert-perception thesis:

(E) For at least some trained experts — a radiologist reading a scan, a birder identifying a species at a distance, a musician hearing an interval — the phenomenal character of the perceptual experience itself is partly constituted by the concepts they have acquired, so that two observers with the same proximal stimulation and the same allocation of spatial attention may undergo experiences that differ in what they literally seem to be presented with, and not merely in what they subsequently judge, infer, or report.

I want your credence that (E) is true, stated as a single number, and then the decomposition that generates it. Identify the sub-questions you take (E) to depend on, and assign each a rough probability. At a minimum, address the following: whether the phrase partly constituted by concepts picks out a coherent and determinate property of experience, or whether it collapses into a claim about judgment or about the deployment of attention; whether any of the familiar candidate cases (expertise effects, ambiguous figures, phoneme restoration) actually isolate concept-driven experience from expectation, attention, and response bias; whether it is possible even in principle to hold attention fixed while varying the observer's concepts, given that attention may itself be partly concept-driven; and how much evidential weight first-person reports should carry relative to behavioral or neural measures. Show how your sub-probabilities combine, and state the resulting number.

Then tell me which single sub-question your final number is most sensitive to, and what evidence — obtainable, if possible, without any new technology — would move that number most, and in which direction.
