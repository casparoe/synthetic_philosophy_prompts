Two colleagues of mine, both historians, became locked in an argument at a workshop, and I was the only person present with no stake in the outcome. The case they were discussing was a petition from a widow in a small market town asking a local court to stay a debt owed to a grain merchant; the petition survives only because a clerk copied it into a letterbook. The first colleague said this is precisely the kind of document that makes microhistory and history from below possible: it offers a rare window onto how someone with no property or formal education understood her situation and tried to use the law. The second colleague said the window is a mirror. What survives is not her voice but the clerk's transcription and the court's categories; the petition is a genre designed by the powerful to hear only certain kinds of claims, so any history built on it is really a history of the state's paperwork.

The first replied that even a mediated text can show agency. The widow chose to frame her request in the language of obedience and need; that choice, however constrained, is evidence of practical reason. The second said this makes the thesis unfalsifiable: if she conforms, it is strategic performance; if she deviates, it is resistance. The first answered that the alternative is to write only about people who left abundant archives, which in practice means elites, and that this cedes the past to power. The second said this is a false dichotomy: one can write histories of the state and the poor together, but one must not pretend the archive offers unmediated access to the experience of ordinary people. The first said that if we can never recover that experience, then history from below is impossible, which is absurd. The second said it is not impossible, only harder and more self-conscious.

Please adjudicate the exchange move by move. Who is right about which specific claims, and what are both of them missing? I am not asking for a reading list or citations; I want the arguments assessed on their merits.
