I am a novelist, and next month I have been asked to run a two-hour session at a small writers’ residency—fourteen participants, most of them working on historical fiction or memoir—on the theme of collective memory and the historical record. I want to do something more than a panel discussion. Design a runnable simulation in which the participants, divided into a civic memory commission, must decide what a town’s new museum will say about a founding event whose archival record and living memory have come apart. The record, in the dossier you provide, shows that the town’s celebrated founders were complicit in a dispossession; the community’s memory, equally vivid, holds them as refugees who themselves suffered and who made something from nothing. The commission has three concrete resources to allocate—the central wall of the exhibit, a mandatory unit in the school curriculum, and a public acknowledgment or reparation—and each role (descendant of the founders, descendant of the dispossessed, town archivist, local journalist, school board member, and a historian brought in as a consultant) has different standing and different things to lose. I want the rules and payoffs to make the participants feel the force of both claims before they are asked to adjudicate: the historian’s insistence that the record, however partial, is the only check on self-serving memory, and the community’s insistence that its memory is not a defective copy of the record but what a friend of mine, a legal historian, calls a mnemonic constitution—a charter that distributes belonging, debt, and obligation, so that correcting it is not merely an epistemic act but a constitutional one. I also want them to discover, without my saying it first, that the record is not a neutral thing: it was compiled by the winning side, its silences are as consequential as its claims, and a counter-archive of oral testimonies in the dossier preserves what the official file omits.

Give me the full activity: roles, materials (including the specific contents of the dossier and the wording of the commission’s mandate), a timeline for the two hours, the payoff structure that makes compromise costly, and the moments where I should intervene and where I should stay out. Then a debrief that maps what happened onto the philosophical positions at stake—history as critical inquiry, collective memory as identity-constituting, the epistemic injustice of treating a community’s memory as mere error, and the hermeneutic worry that the historian’s own narrative is a construction—and that names the question the activity cannot settle: when the record and the memory conflict, which should give way? Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it (I expect the participants will split into a truth camp and a feelings camp and miss the constitutional point; I also expect someone to propose simply telling both stories, which lets everyone off the hook) and how to handle those failures without lecturing. Finally, be honest about what the simulation cannot reproduce—the slow, intergenerational work of memory, the way a community’s sense of itself is not decided in a single meeting—and tell me what I must supply in the debrief to make up for that.
