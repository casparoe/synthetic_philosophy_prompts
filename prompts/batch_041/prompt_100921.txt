Write a short story set in a near-future regulatory office where every automated decision — loan denials, parole recommendations, hospital triage rankings — must be filed with a "reason ledger": a legally required statement of the grounds for the decision, produced by a separate module that inspects the decision only after it has been made and was never permitted to influence it, and that is conspicuously good at producing plausible grounds. Follow the civil servant charged with drafting the audit standard for these ledgers, and build the story around the inversion that Darwin and Turing each perform in their different ways — Darwin explaining the design of an eye without a designer, Turing trading the question whether a machine thinks for the question whether it can hold up its end of a conversation. The story's pressure point should be whether competence at producing the outward marks of reasoning — an organ that sees, a machine that answers, a ledger that explains — licenses any inference at all about the process that produced it. Keep the human characters' own post-hoc self-justifications in view throughout, so that the reader cannot comfortably quarantine the problem inside the machines, and let the ending be a decision rather than a thesis.
