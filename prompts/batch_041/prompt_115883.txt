I am a doctoral candidate in philosophy working on animal welfare, and I want to use a written analysis to force myself into precision about a question I keep leaving vague.

The practice I have in mind is the ordinary content of animal-welfare regulation, which is strikingly uniform across jurisdictions that disagree about almost everything else. What the law regulates is experience rather than life. Animals must be rendered insensible before slaughter, so that what is prohibited is dying in pain rather than being killed; transport, housing and handling rules are written in the vocabulary of pain, fear and distress; and when a practice is defended or attacked, the argument is almost always about how it feels to the animal. Killing as such is not the wrong the law names. Yet the same regimes are incomplete in ways that look principled rather than accidental. Many of them carve out religious slaughter from the stunning requirement. They draw the boundary of protection at a sentience threshold rather than a gradient — vertebrates, and in recent years certain cephalopods and decapods, with everything below the line left outside. And they are silent about wild animals, whose suffering is vast, continuous and nobody's legal business: the law governs animals we use, not animals that suffer. A smaller case in the same family: the egg industry is replacing the culling of day-old male chicks with in-ovo sexing, and the change is defended as an improvement precisely because the destruction is moved to an earlier stage, before the animal can suffer.

Now the twist. Suppose one takes seriously the position — I have seen it called the hedonistic imperative — that suffering is the fundamental disvalue and that biotechnology has turned its abolition from a utopia into an engineering problem: livestock edited or dosed into insensibility, predators redesigned or their prey rendered incapable of pain, the total quantity of sentient misery deliberately driven down. I want to know what the regulatory practice I have described presupposes about value, and whether it leaves room for that programme or quietly forecloses it.

So: reconstruct the implicit axiology. Is it hedonism about welfare, on which pain is the only intrinsic disvalue and a painless death is no harm at all? A preference or desire view, on which killing and confinement harm by foreclosing futures? A rights view that the stunning rules merely implement? Or something thinner and relational — an ethic of use and husbandry, on which what is owed to an animal depends on our relationship to it, which would explain at once the exemptions, the threshold and the silence about the wild? Separate what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits, say which rivals it rules out, and identify where it is genuinely underdetermined, so that several theories would license the same rules.

Then press on the tension that made me start. If pain is the only thing that matters intrinsically, a permanently insensible animal should be the ideal product of welfare regulation. But veterinary opinion tends to treat the inability to feel pain not as a boon but as a defect — an animal that cannot feel pain will injure itself — and welfare codes that require animals to be able to behave as their kind behaves appear to be protecting something other than the absence of suffering. Tell me whether that is a genuine inconsistency in the practice, an artifact of the fact that insensitivity happens to be bad for the animal in other ways, or evidence that the practice presupposes a thicker conception of welfare than hedonism can supply. Finally, say what would have to change in the regulation — which rules would have to be added, dropped or rewritten — if it were rebuilt on the hedonistic imperative, and whether the resulting regime would still be recognisable as animal-welfare law or would have become something else.

End with a final line of the form \answer{...}, where the braces contain exactly one of: hedonism entailed; hedonism permitted but not entailed; hedonism ruled out; the practice's commitments are internally inconsistent. Choose the label your reconstruction best supports, and make the sentence immediately preceding that line a one-sentence summary of your verdict.
