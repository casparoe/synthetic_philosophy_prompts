I teach a graduate seminar on ethics and international development and I'm designing a single 90-minute session for about eighteen students, a mix of philosophers, public-policy students, and people who have actually worked for NGOs or donor agencies. What I want them to feel rather than be told is that corruption is not a concept they can apply case by case with a clean definition already in hand: that the formula one always sees on the first slide, about the misuse of entrusted power for private gain, is not a neutral test but smuggles in a substantive theory of what an office is for and who entrusted it to whom; that their judgments will in fact quietly track the size of the sum, the intimacy of the relationship, whether the transaction can be re-described in the idiom of the market, and whether the parties are rich or poor; and that the two easy escape routes, there is one universal standard, and it is all just local custom, both come apart once the cases get hard. I also want them to run into the more uncomfortable fact that corruption talk is itself a move in a political game, since naming a rival's patronage corrupt while one's own is called networking is one of the oldest tricks there is. The activity should put them on the hook for a decision before any of this is named to them.

Here is what I have in mind, and I'd like you to make it runnable. The centrepiece is a standards committee that has to write a code. Each participant gets a slip of paper with a verdict scale, unproblematic, questionable but permissible, or corrupt, and must fill one in for each of eight cases, together with a mandatory one-sentence answer to the question in virtue of what, and a separate field asking who, if anyone, is wronged. The cases I want seeded, though you should feel free to sharpen them: a procurement officer who receives a cash envelope at his wedding from a supplier, in a setting where wedding guests give cash and the amount equals a month's salary; a civil servant who hires her qualified niece into a post that was never advertised; a firm that pays a few hundred dollars to a customs officer to clear perishable goods before they spoil, where the alternative is a three-week delay and the loss of a season's income for a cooperative; a minister's brother-in-law appointed to a regulatory board who then recuses himself from the relevant votes; an NGO country director whose spouse's translation firm gets the contract at market rate without a tender; a legislator who votes for a subsidy that benefits both his constituents and, incidentally, his cousin; a public hospital doctor who accepts a customary thank-you gift after an operation; and a donor agency that makes its grantees sign a zero-tolerance declaration while its own staff routinely accept flights, hotels, and dinners from implementing partners. After the individual verdicts, the committee has twenty minutes to produce a written code of no more than five rules that classifies all eight cases without exception, and any rule that names a specific case is void, which will force them either into vacuity or into a substantive account of what offices are for. Then a mirror round: hand them four cases drawn from institutions like their own, a cash retainer to a local fixer, an unposted hire of a friend, an all-expenses trip from a vendor, a professor assigning their own royalty-earning textbook, and have the funder's compliance officer apply the code they just wrote. Throughout, each participant holds a hidden stake card, a cousin's firm, a spotless compliance record, a job that depends on the payments continuing, which they reveal only at the end; the point is to let the weaponisation dynamic arise on its own rather than in a lecture. You should give me roles (including a rotating local officer whose job is at stake and an external funder's compliance officer, with everyone playing at least one of them), a materials list, a minute-by-minute timeline, and instructions for the two or three distinctions I want them to have in hand but not necessarily to apply consciously, since part of the lesson is discovering which ones their verdicts actually tracked: the difference between a gift that is inalienable, carrying the giver and creating an obligation no cash can discharge, and a bribe that is a disguised price; the difference between what is wrong, what is legally proscribed, and what is actually enforced; the difference between corruption in the grand sense and petty speed money, including the argument that the latter can be efficient and the counter that it corrodes the very idea of an office; and the thought that the concept may be parasitic on a theory of the state, so that calling a payment corrupt is already to have taken a stand on what the institution is for.

Finally, give me a debrief that maps specific moments in the session onto these ideas, asks the group which features their verdicts actually tracked versus which they believed they were tracking, and makes them test their own five rules against their own institution. I need a candid account of what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle each failure: the group converging quickly on the easy cases and then treating everything else as merely grey and stopping there, participants performing the cultural relativist and the anti-relativist as caricatures, the hidden stake cards being read as a gotcha and producing defensiveness rather than insight, someone wanting to litigate real named politicians, and the code-writing round producing five variations on act with integrity. And be honest about what the activity cannot reproduce, so that I know what I have to supply myself: the coercion and kin obligation that make refusal costly in the cases as they occur in life, the insider's tacit sense of when a gift is a gift, and the fact that the local standard is never a monolith, since the culture defence is typically voiced loudest by those who benefit from it.
