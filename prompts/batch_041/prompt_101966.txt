The question was: "A chord of a circle of radius R is said to be chosen 'at random.' Give at least three different procedures for choosing such a chord that a reasonable person might accept, compute for each the probability that the chord is longer than a side of the inscribed equilateral triangle, and explain what the resulting disagreement shows about the principle of indifference. For each procedure, state the measure it induces on the space of chords. The student wrote:

"Bertrand's paradox arises when we ask for the probability that a randomly drawn chord of a circle is longer than the side of the inscribed equilateral triangle. Since there is no single way to 'draw a chord at random,' we get different answers depending on the procedure. (1) Random endpoints: pick two points independently and uniformly on the circumference and join them. By symmetry, fix one endpoint; the chord is longer than the triangle's side exactly when the other endpoint falls in the opposite 120-degree arc, so P = 1/3. (2) Random radial point: pick a direction uniformly, then a point uniformly along that radius, and draw the chord perpendicular to the radius through that point. The chord exceeds the side length iff the point lies in the inner half of the radius, so P = 1/2. (3) Random midpoint: pick a point uniformly in the disk and take it as the midpoint of the chord. The chord is longer than the side iff the midpoint falls inside the concentric circle of radius R/2, whose area is a quarter of the disk's, so P = 1/4. So three procedures that all look like fair ways of choosing a random chord give 1/3, 1/2 and 1/4. Each corresponds to a different measure on the space of chords, which is the root of the problem.

What this shows is that the principle of indifference is simply inconsistent. If we are equally ignorant about which chord will be drawn, the principle tells us to assign equal probability to each possibility; but since the same ignorance can be partitioned in different ways, it delivers contradictory verdicts. This is a genuine contradiction in the probability calculus, and it means any probability assigned on the basis of indifference is meaningless. It follows that the principle of indifference should be abandoned across the board. Even in the simple case of a fair die, saying we are equally ignorant about the six outcomes and so assign each probability 1/6 is not really justified, since we could equally well partition the outcome space some other way.

The paradox is sometimes said to be resolved by symmetry considerations, but this does not work. The only one of the three procedures that respects the symmetry of the circle is the random-midpoint procedure: the endpoint and radial procedures each secretly privilege a direction, since they single out particular points or directions on the circle. Once we require the procedure to be rotationally invariant, the midpoint procedure is the only survivor, and the correct answer is therefore 1/4."
