I am assembling a handout for the opening meeting of a graduate seminar on personal identity and prudential concern, and I have decided the handout should be a specification rather than a survey: a list of the desiderata that any theory of what matters in survival must satisfy. By that phrase I mean whatever it is about the relation between me now and some future being that justifies the distinctive anticipation and self-interested concern I take toward that being — the thing that would still be doing the work even if identity turned out not to be it. Write me that list. For each requirement, supply the case or consideration that motivates it, mark it as either non-negotiable or the preference of a particular camp, and flag any pair or subset of requirements you suspect cannot be satisfied together; the incompatibilities are the part I most want, since a demonstration that the list is inconsistent would be a result in its own right. I do not need references or a reading list — I want the argument behind each entry.

The list should at minimum confront the usual pressure points: the teletransporter, fission in which both halves survive with equal claims, the gradual replacement of the brain's parts by functionally identical substitutes, a brain transplanted into a different body, radical amnesia, and the case of a future being who is psychologically continuous with me but who will be wretched. I want two further things forced. First, I have invented a case and would like it treated as a genuine test rather than a curiosity: a transmitter that reconstructs the traveller at the destination only after a delay of several years, during which the original remains alive, awake, and in regular contact with the destination, together with a variant in which reconstruction proceeds only if the original, at the moment of transmission, consents to being destroyed upon arrival. Second, I want the list to settle what the theory must say about degree and structure — whether what matters admits of degrees, whether the relation is transitive, and whether two contemporaries can both stand in the relevant relation to a third.

Finally, include the constraints on the theory that come from outside metaphysics: that it not make what matters turn on facts the agent cannot in principle come to know, that it not require the agent to accept conclusions she could not be brought to accept while remaining coherent, and that it be able to answer the question why any of this should matter to the agent without simply presupposing that it does. Close with a single sentence naming which requirement does the most to exclude the theories currently on offer, and why that one rather than any of the others.
