I am preparing a seminar paper on the ethics of automated systems, and I want you to analyze the March 2018 fatal crash in Tempe, Arizona, in which an Uber test vehicle operating in autonomous mode struck and killed Elaine Herzberg, through the lens of the responsibility-gap debate. I know the basic facts—the vehicle detected her several seconds before impact but did not brake, the safety driver was not watching the road, Uber had disabled the vehicle’s factory automatic emergency braking, and the safety driver was later prosecuted and pleaded guilty to endangerment—so please do not summarize the incident or rehearse the standard arguments for and against the existence of a responsibility gap. A friend in my seminar thinks the safety driver’s guilty plea settles the matter; I am not convinced. What I need is a disciplined case analysis that runs in both directions: from the theory to the case and from the case back to the theory.

To keep the analysis from collapsing into the usual many-hands truisms, I want you to work with a set of distinctions, some of which are my own. First, distinguish a backward-looking responsibility gap—no one satisfies the conditions for blame for what happened—from a forward-looking responsibility gap—no one is positioned or incentivized to prevent recurrence. Second, separate blameworthiness, accountability, and liability, and say which of these the criminal prosecution of the safety driver actually addressed. Third, use the notion of a responsibility sink: a node in a sociotechnical system that absorbs blame without discharging the function of responsibility, because the person at that node lacks the control or knowledge that would make blame effective. Finally, distinguish responsibility by causal contribution from responsibility by role, and ask which the safety driver, the engineers who disabled the emergency braking, Uber’s management, and the regulators each had.

With those tools, tell me who the relevant actors were, what options and information each had, and what each of the main positions—individual responsibility, role-based responsibility, corporate responsibility, strict liability, and distributed responsibility—would say they should have done. Where does the responsibility-gap thesis illuminate the case, and where does the case embarrass it? In particular, does Tempe confirm that autonomous systems open a genuine gap, or does it show that the gap was manufactured by an organizational choice—keeping a human in the loop as a fallback while stripping that human of meaningful control—so that the safety driver functioned as a responsibility sink? If the latter, what does that imply for the responsibility-gap thesis: should it be restated as a claim about attribution, about governance, or about the design of accountability? Conclude by saying what the episode shows about the theory, including any anomaly it presents that the theory cannot absorb. I do not need citations, a literature review, or a reading list; I will add those myself. What I want is the mapping, the argument, and an honest account of where the theory goes silent.
