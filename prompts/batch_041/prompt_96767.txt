You are a clinical ethics consultant who has spent years on reproductive ethics — you know the debates over procreative beneficence, liberal eugenics, and the disability critique well enough to reason with them rather than recite them, and you also sit on hospital ethics committees often enough to know what a procedure has to look like to survive contact with a busy clinic. I would like your help turning a principle into something usable, and I would rather you think carefully and lay out your reasoning than produce something slick. I sit on the ethics committee of a mid-sized private fertility clinic that has recently begun offering polygenic embryo screening alongside ordinary chromosomal screening. Our medical director has floated what she calls the 'best-life standard': the clinic, she says, should help prospective parents choose, among the embryos they could transfer, the one expected to have the best life; doing so is at least permissible and possibly obligatory; but the clinic must not become an agent of eugenics, and the parents, not the clinic, own the values. She has also proposed, as a gloss on this, that we distinguish a 'floor' — the point below which a life is not worth starting — from a 'ceiling' — everything above it — and that the clinic should intervene at the floor while remaining neutral at the ceiling. This is a slogan, not a policy, and we keep hitting requests it does not resolve. Our committee meets weekly, but transfers are scheduled within days, most of my colleagues are clinicians rather than philosophers, and the medical director has to sign off on the outcome, so whatever we adopt must be runnable in twenty minutes by someone who has never read a word of the literature.

Please do two things. First, briefly and without citing anyone, set out what a principle of procreative beneficence actually commits a clinic to: in particular the difference between making a particular child better off and choosing a different child, why 'the best expected life' is not the same as 'the healthiest embryo', and the strongest objections — the disability critique, and the worry that the clinic would be expressing a judgment about the worth of existing lives — together with the medical director's floor/ceiling gloss and whether it is a stable way of stating the principle or a quiet abandonment of it. Second, having done that, write the procedure itself: an ordered set of questions, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, the point at which the procedure stops with a no, and the point at which it hands the decision back to the parents as a question of values rather than facts. Include a small set of tests a committee member can actually apply — for instance, a rule for distinguishing a request that concerns a medical condition from one that concerns a preference, a test for when a polygenic score is too weak or too confounded to ground a decision at all, and a rule for requests in which the selection benefits someone other than the future child. Then run the procedure on three requests we have pending: (a) a couple asking us to select, among five embryos, the one with the highest polygenic score for educational attainment, who also ask whether we can select for height; (b) a Deaf couple, both fluent signers who regard deafness as a culture rather than a deficit, asking us to select an embryo that will be deaf; and (c) the parents of a child with a severe blood disorder asking us to select an embryo that is a tissue match for that child. At least one of these should come out in a way I will not like — tell me which and why — and be explicit throughout about where the procedure is a judgment call dressed up as procedure, where it will misfire, and what it cannot decide at all. Begin with a short sketch of the distinctions you intend to preserve before you draft anything.
