I'd like you to answer this in the role of a philosopher who works on meaning, normativity, and the way rules constrain their own application — someone who knows the relevant literature deeply, but who is willing to say plainly when a question has no established answer rather than reaching for the nearest label. Please think the problem through before you answer, and begin by laying out the candidate concepts you are weighing, with a sentence on what each is usually taken to be, before you adjudicate between them. The label is the least of what I want; I would rather have an honest "there is no established term for this" than a name that doesn't quite fit.

Last night I argued for four hours with a friend — a very clever friend, and a persistent one — about whether a rule can tell you how to apply itself, and I have come out of it holding a pattern I can see perfectly and cannot name. Here is the pattern. Whatever rule I offered — a recipe, the rule for moving a knight, a line in a grading rubric requiring that a proof "show the key step" — he would construct a case that the rule as I had stated it did not cover, or covered only if you already understood how it was meant to be taken. When I patched the rule to cover that case, he produced a further case the patch did not cover. Every patch was individually reasonable, and every patch made the rule longer and less decisive, and after five or six rounds I could see that no amount of patching would close the gap. Up to that point I thought I was on familiar ground: this is roughly what people mean when they say that a rule can never contain its own application. But here is the part I actually want named. When I said, "fine — so no rule determines its application," he refused the conclusion. He said I had been looking for the wrong thing all along: a rule does not have to determine its application, because what counts as applying it correctly is not fixed by anything written down or thought through; it is fixed by what we do, by our going on in the same way. When I asked what makes our going on in the same way the right way rather than merely the customary one, he said my question presupposed the picture he was rejecting. When I asked whether that meant a community could never be wrong about how to apply its own rules, he said something I couldn't follow about the difference between a mistake and a different practice, and we went around again.

That last exchange is the one that has stayed with me, because it is not hypothetical. At the chess club I go to, half the members hold that a certain move is legal and half hold that it isn't, and both sides appeal to the same written rules and to "what chess is" — and the split has been stable for years, so it isn't as though the practice is quietly settling the matter. I want to know what to make of that, and I want the surrounding distinctions, not just a title. First, does the pattern itself — the patch-and-regress, where every repair to a rule presupposes the very understanding it was meant to secure — have an established name, and what exactly does that concept say that my description leaves out? Second, what is the name of the position my friend ends up in, the one that trades the authority of the rule for the authority of shared practice, and what are the names of the standard objections to it — in particular the one I kept groping for, that if correctness is simply what we do, then whatever we do is correct, so that the position cannot distinguish following a rule from being trained into a habit, and cannot allow that a whole community might be systematically mistaken? Third, and this is what I most want your judgement on, how does the pattern differ from the things I have been told it "really" is? One friend says it is just vagueness; a friend who practises law says it is the familiar fact that statutes cannot anticipate every case and that this has a name in legal theory — but her version seems to be about a lawmaker's failure of foresight, whereas what troubles me is that no amount of foresight would help. Someone else mentioned a puzzle about heaps, and someone else a problem about how one justifies a standard of knowledge. I suspect at least two of these are different things wearing the same clothes, and I want to know which, and on what ground. Fourth, does my friend's position survive the objection I keep trying to make, or does it have a standard repair I should know about? And fifth, if you conclude that the pattern has no name, coin one, define it precisely, and tell me what falls inside and outside it — including what a case would have to look like in which a rule genuinely did determine its own application, or, if you think there is no such case, what follows from that. Please keep the answer conceptual rather than bibliographic: I have no way to check citations, so don't send me to a reading list, and where you are unsure how a view is usually attributed, say so rather than smoothing it over.
