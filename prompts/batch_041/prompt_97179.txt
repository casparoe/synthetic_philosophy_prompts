I am a philosopher of mathematics presenting a paper at a departmental colloquium next month, and I would like a Q&A prep sheet rather than a summary of the paper. The audience will be almost entirely mathematical logicians and set theorists, including two model theorists who work on absoluteness and elementary submodels and a single philosopher of mathematics; there will be no historians of logic present and nobody from outside the department. Their default commitments are that the Löwenheim–Skolem theorem is a theorem and not a paradox, that the apparent contradiction dissolves the moment one distinguishes a model's membership relation from the genuine one, and that talk of what 'uncountable' really means is at best a confusion and at worst a refusal to do the maths. They care about absoluteness, Skolem functions, and the difference between a set and its trace in a model, and they have little patience for questions they take to be already answered.

My thesis is that Skolem's paradox is not dissolved by the standard move of separating the model's membership relation from the real one, and that the residual worry it leaves is genuinely epistemological rather than merely terminological. In outline I argue three things. First, the standard dissolution establishes only that 'uncountable' is model-relative, which nobody disputes, while leaving untouched the interesting question of whether our own 'uncountable' is absolute. Second, appealing to an intended or standard model in order to secure absoluteness begs the question, since any device for pinning down the intended model is itself a piece of set-theoretic vocabulary whose interpretation is precisely what is at issue. Third, this generalises beyond set theory to any theory whose quantifiers range over a domain that we can specify only from within, so that the paradox is the sharpest instance of a broader indeterminacy. I take this to be a form of Skolemite scepticism, though I want to stop short of the strong reading on which all mathematical reference collapses.

For the prep sheet, give me the eight or so objections I will actually get from this audience, not the ones a room of philosophers of language or of religion would raise. Phrase each one the way a model theorist or set theorist would put it, in their technical vocabulary, and order them by how much damage they would do to my position. For each, give me a reply I can deliver in roughly two minutes and a note on where that reply is soft — the point an opponent will press next. Tell me what I should concede up front in order to keep the rest of the defence alive, and flag the step in my argument that is most likely to be treated as a howler. Finish with the question I am hoping nobody asks, and say why it is the one I cannot answer cleanly.
