I am assembling a requirements document for a theory of cross-temporal moral assessment — an account of when, and on what grounds, it is correct to praise or condemn people who acted under moral and epistemic conditions unlike our own — and I want the specification before I look at any candidate theory. Treat the following as a design brief rather than an essay prompt.

Give me the desiderata: what any adequate theory in this area must deliver, each paired with the case or consideration that motivates it, and mark each as non-negotiable or as a preference of one camp. The deliverable is the list and the reasoning behind it, not a survey of who has said what, and not a reading list.

The theory must get the standard cases right, at minimum: a statesman honoured for one achievement while bearing contested responsibility for a catastrophe in his own tenure; a slaveholder who drafted founding documents proclaiming universal rights; an explorer whose conduct drew objection from at least some of his own contemporaries; and a figure whose worst conduct was unremarkable by the standards of his time and place, so that almost no one around him objected. Add whatever constructed cases you need in order to force distinctions the standard ones leave ambiguous — for instance a figure whose moral failure lay in an omission rather than an act, and a figure whose reputation changed only because new evidence about consequences came to light.

I expect the requirements to include, among others, and argued rather than merely listed:

- what the target of assessment is — the act, the agent, the agent's character, or the agent's public legacy — and why different targets yield different verdicts about the same person;
- the separation of the moral status of an act from the epistemic standing of the agent who performed it, and the fact that these can diverge;
- the several distinct readings of the man-of-his-time defence (excuse from non-culpable ignorance, denial that there was any moral truth to violate, a claim about what it was reasonable to expect of anyone then) and which of them survive scrutiny;
- a temporal-neutrality constraint: whatever rule is applied to them must be applied to us;
- the problem of partial compliance — that nearly everyone then fell short, so that a verdict on one figure implicitly indicts a whole society;
- structural versus individual causation, and whether a verdict on an individual is even warranted when the harm was produced by many hands;
- foreseeability, outcome luck, and the treatment of consequences no one could have predicted;
- respect-specific verdicts versus an overall verdict, and whether the theory is committed to a single bottom line about a person;
- the practical function of the verdict — belief, honour, commemoration, blame — and whether one verdict can serve all of these;
- revisability in light of new evidence, held together with the stability that any practically usable standard requires;
- the selective application of the defence, and whether the theory can explain or must merely tolerate its asymmetry.

Then tell me which single requirement does the most to exclude the most popular theories, and identify any pair of requirements you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied — that part I care about most. A table is acceptable for the requirement list, but the motivating case for each requirement should be stated in prose.
