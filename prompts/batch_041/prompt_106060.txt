For a small six-month research grant I'm applying to, I need a proposal of about two pages, and I have a half-formed idea I'd like you to turn into one. The subject is the old quarrel over whether the great evils of history can be ranked — whether saying that one was worse than another is a legitimate act of moral thought or something closer to a desecration. I've spent enough time in the blogs to distrust any argument that survives only because a single word is doing two jobs, and my suspicion here is that both sides have been treating comparison as one act when the thing we call comparison is at least four, and that once they're separated the obscenity charge lands on only two of them — leaving the two that carry the real stakes standing.

My rough cut of the four is this. Comparing two evils can mean (a) ordering them — is A worse than B; (b) scaling them — by how much, and against what common unit; (c) adjudicating between them — letting the order settle what we do, where money, blame, or commemoration should go; and (d) the expressive or self-implicating act of ranking — treating the ranking as something a person does, and asking what it betrays about the person. My hypothesis is that (a) is very hard to deny, that the obscenity charge is almost always aimed at (b) and (d), and that the interesting question is therefore not whether we can rank but what follows from an order once we have one, and whether the refusals to scale or to be seen ranking can be honored without giving up ordering.

State the question so that it has a determinate answer, and explain why the existing positions don't settle it. As I read them, the commensurabilists defend ranking wholesale and the incommensurabilists reject it wholesale, so the debate oscillates because each side's arguments are aimed at a different one of the four operations — which is why neither side ever seems able to land a blow.

Then give me the method, month by month, and be honest about what I can actually finish in six months part-time. I'd expect to spend most of the middle of the project on the load-bearing step: whether ordering can be had without the scaling that the incommensurabilist denies, or whether (as I fear) the separation I'm resting on is idle because (a) secretly requires (b). Say what a result looks like — at minimum a defensible assignment of each operation to possible or obscene, with at least one case I supply where the separation changes the verdict — and what would count as the project failing, including the possibility that the four operations turn out to be four descriptions of one act. Steelman the view I'm least sympathetic to, that the obscenity charge is itself a substantive moral claim about the act of ranking rather than a gesture of refusal, and say what it would take to defeat it.

I want a conceptual-taxonomy approach rather than a formal model or a close reading of particular atrocities, because the claim at issue is about the shape of the concept, not about magnitudes; say a word about why that is the right instrument and not, say, a decision-theoretic formalization. On why a philosophical rather than empirical project makes progress: the dispute is not about what happened but about what kind of thing a comparison is, so no further evidence about the past can settle it. And I don't want a bibliography or a reading list — I'll add the citations myself, and a model guessing at them is worse than useless to me. Scope it as a paper, not a book: a taxonomy plus one argument, not a theory of value.
