I have come to accept Strawson's account of moral responsibility — the one on which holding someone responsible is not a metaphysical verdict about whether their will was free, but simply the practice of having and expressing the reactive attitudes: resentment and indignation toward others, guilt toward oneself, and gratitude and forgiveness where those are called for. The account has an obvious attraction: it stops demanding something we never had, and describes what we actually do. But a friend has drawn a consequence from it that I cannot accept, and I cannot locate the misstep.

(1) On this account, to hold an agent responsible is nothing over and above participating in the practice of reactive attitudes toward them.
(2) There is no vantage point outside that practice from which to ask whether the practice, taken as a whole, is justified, since any consideration one could adduce for or against it would itself already be an exercise of, or a withdrawal from, the attitudes.
(3) So the traditional question whether we are ever really justified in holding one another responsible is not answered by the theory; it is dissolved.
(4) The practice is not monolithic, however: within it, particular attitudes are withheld or modified. We do not resent the agent who acted under coercion, or in unavoidable ignorance, or who is a young child.
(5) But these modifications are themselves just further reactions — we do not resent the coerced agent because the resentment is simply not there to be had, not because we have discovered a fact that makes resentment incorrect.
(6) Hence there is no sense in which an attitude can be unfitting beyond the fact that we do not, as it happens, have it.
(7) Therefore a community that resents people for a feature we should regard as morally irrelevant — their hair colour, say — has a responsibility practice exactly as legitimate as ours: equally self-sustaining, equally immune to external criticism.
(8) That is false. We do think some responsibility practices are defective, and not merely different from ours.

I would most like to keep (1) and (8); they are what drew me to the account and what I am unwilling to give up. Tell me which step to reject. I do not want a survey of positions or a menu of possible replies — I want your verdict on the single premise or transition that fails, your explanation of why it looked compelling in the first place, and an honest accounting of what I surrender by rejecting it, including any objection the repair invites.
