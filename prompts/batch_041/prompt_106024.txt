I sit on the Standing Committee on Language and Usage at my university, which maintains the institution's official style guide and rules on contested cases arising under it. We meet on Thursday and I am expected to circulate the committee's written decision in advance. Please draft that document for me.

**The revision before us.** The guide is up for its five-year revision, and the draft has two contested parts.

*Part One* is a house rule on what the drafting subcommittee calls false generics: forms whose inclusive reading requires the reader to treat one group as the default. The easy cases are *man*, *mankind*, *chairman*, and the generic *he*. Three additions this cycle are contested: *guys* used to address a mixed group; *freshman*; and the singular *they*, which the subcommittee proposes to make the default third-person singular for unspecified persons in all university documents. (Note that the subcommittee is not reopening whether singular *they* is grammatical — that was settled years ago — but whether the university should mandate it. Two members object that this simply installs a different default rather than removing one.)

*Part Two* is a proposed prohibition on re-substitution exercises in courses that satisfy the writing requirement. The exercise has run for over a decade in the writing program and has two stages. Students first read the satire Hofstadter collected in *Metamagical Themas* — the piece commonly called the "Person Paper" — a mock polemic in the voice of a peeved defender of good English that rewrites ordinary usage by swapping race terms for sex terms throughout: *chairwhite*, *mailwhite*, "a giant step for whitekind," and the pronouns *whe* and *ble* in place of *he* and *she*, so that the reader is made to feel the masculine default from the other side. Students then write a short flip of their own, substituting some other category for a category that their chosen source text treats as the default.

**What happened.** Last term a student's flip substituted religion. A screenshot of the student's piece, stripped of the assignment frame and the accompanying commentary, circulated off campus; some readers took it straight as an endorsement, others as an attack, and the resulting complaint reached the provost. The instructor has since added a prefatory frame to the assignment. Two students have petitioned to have the exercise dropped as a condition of remaining enrolled; three departmental colleagues have written to us arguing that a writing course should not require students to produce group-directed invective in order to be graded, however critical the frame.

**The classification the subcommittee proposes.** Slurs and group-generic terms are to be sorted into three tiers: (i) used in the author's own voice; (ii) quoted or discussed as an object of analysis; (iii) voiced by someone whose position the author does not hold — parody, satire, historical ventriloquism, a fictional speaker. The draft forbids (i), permits (ii), and is silent on (iii). The whole dispute is about (iii). Two criteria have been proposed for admitting a tier-(iii) text: a *legibility* test (a reader who does not know the author's stance should be able to identify the frame without external help) and a *non-appropriation* test (the exercise should not require a student to write in the voice of a category the student does not belong to, nor place a student's text where a hostile reader can use it as evidence for the very view the exercise means to expose).

**What I need.** Draft the decision memo the committee will circulate. It must recommend a concrete course of action with dates, rule on whether tier (iii) is admissible and on which of the two proposed criteria (if either) should govern, and say what becomes of the writing-program exercise in particular. My colleagues on the losing side will read this document closely, so the strongest consideration against the recommendation has to be stated fairly and answered rather than waved away — and the answer cannot be that the satire's intent settles the matter, because that is precisely what is in dispute. The memo should also make clear what a department would have to do, in practice, to satisfy whatever standard we adopt.

Write it in these sections and order, at roughly these lengths:

- **Recommendation** — under 150 words, the bottom line first, with the effective dates.
- **The question before us** — about 150 words.
- **What the exercise does** — about 300 words on the mechanism of the satire and the flip: what a student is supposed to learn that a style rule alone cannot teach, and what has to be true of a reader for the trick to work.
- **The case for prohibition** — about 300 words, stated at its strongest.
- **The case for keeping it, with conditions** — about 300 words, equally at its strongest.
- **Decision and operative criteria** — about 350 words: the ruling on tier (iii), the fate of the legibility and non-appropriation tests, the rule a department must apply, and any amendment to Part One that follows from it.
- **Implementation** — about 100 words: who does what, by when, and what we will review at the end of the year.

Do not include citations, references, footnotes, or suggestions for further reading; everything you need is above, and I would rather the memo not rest on anything you would have to guess at. Write as the committee, not as an outside commentator.
