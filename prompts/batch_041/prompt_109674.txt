I've just been made chair of our town's new citizens' panel — 24 of us picked by lot off the electoral roll, four Saturdays in a row, we make recommendations on a couple of contested budget decisions and the council either accepts them or has to override them by a two-thirds vote and publish its reasons. First meeting is in a fortnight and I'm in the chair. I can't vote unless its tied, I don't get to say anything about the substance, and the clerk takes the minutes (she's great but she is not a lawyer). Everyone's watching, because one of the items is whether we keep funding a community organisation that got itself into a mess last month — some genuinely ugly stuff was said by someone there, half the panel is furious about it, the other half thinks the fury is now the real problem, and I have to keep that conversation from turning into a brawl.

The reason I'm asking you and not just winging it: the three famous cautionary tales about Athenian democracy are all, when you actually look at them, procedural. I know them roughly. The assembly voted to kill every adult man in Mytilene, woke up the next day regretting it, held a second assembly and un-did the whole thing (that's the Thucydides bit, book three). The Sicilian expedition got voted through largely on what a delegation from a small allied city said they could pay for, and when Nicias tried to sink the plan by demanding an absurdly large force, the assembly just... gave him the force. And the Athenians killed Socrates for being annoying — a few hundred men who mostly wanted him to stop. So: rage, credulity, and intolerance of the one person who won't shut up. There's also something I half-remember about them having a rule where you could be hauled into court for proposing a decree that conflicted with an existing law, with the decree frozen while the case ran and the proposer personally copping the penalty — a weird and interesting bit of design, and I keep turning it over.

Here's what I want. Take the role of a political theorist who knows this material cold. Think it through carefully before you write, and start with a short brainstorm of the failure modes these episodes are actually evidence for — the specific mechanism in each, not just rage/hubris/vibes — then turn them into something I can run.

Give me a decision procedure for me, the chair, to run on every substantive item, in order: the questions I ask, in what order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops with a clear no, and where it hands the decision back to the panel (or the clerk, or the council) because the question is one of values and not something a procedure can settle. It has to fit a chair with about ten minutes per item, no legal training, no staff, and a room that will get bored and resentful if I start philosophising at them. If part of it can be a one-page card I keep in front of me, even better.

Two things I'd specifically like the procedure to do, because I think they're the hard bits:

- Separate the Mytilene situation from the Sicily situation. In one, the people who'd pay the price weren't in the room and weren't asked; in the other, the interested party was in the room, spoke first, and its claims were never checked. A step that just says consult the affected doesn't cover both, and I want to know what does.
- Do something with that frozen-decree, personally-liable-proposer idea. I want a step where, before we vote, I check whether the proposal contradicts something the panel or the council has already decided, and whether the person pushing it has something riding on it personally. But I don't want it turning into a weapon the majority uses to freeze anything it dislikes — so if you build it in, tell me how to keep it from being abused, or tell me it can't be done and why.

Then run the procedure on three items, and don't skip the parts where it gives an answer I won't like: (1) the defunding item, where I'll admit I think the furious half is right; (2) a proposal to commit most of our remaining budget to a big regional project, on the strength of a presentation by a delegation from the partner body who say the matching money is definitely coming; (3) a motion to formally censure the panel member who raises the same grievance at every session and won't let it go, after which everyone assumes he'll resign.

Finally, tell me what the procedure can't do, and be straight about how confident you are in the whole thing — and single out the one point you're least sure of, because I'd rather know where the soft spot is now than find out mid-meeting.
