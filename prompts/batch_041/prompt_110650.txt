I'm a novelist — four books, none of them about maths — and I run a monthly thing at a bookshop near me, "The Uncertainty Club": about 16 to 18 adults, mixed backgrounds, a couple of retired engineers, quite a few who announce they were hopeless at maths at school and then turn out to be very good at it. Next month I want to do Bertrand's paradox and the principle of indifference, and I want the group to walk into it rather than be told about it. So here's the ask: you're an expert in probability education and philosophy of science who designs in-person activities, and I'd like you to design me a 90-minute session. Please begin by brainstorming two or three different ways the thing could be staged, and think carefully about which will actually land with a non-mathematical crowd, before you settle on one and give me the full design — I'd rather see the reasoning than just the finished product.

The shape I'm imagining (tell me if it's wrong): everyone gets a paper disc with a circle and an inscribed equilateral triangle printed on it, and they're asked, individually and without conferring, to draw a chord "chosen at random" — then we pool the discs and count what fraction of the chords came out longer than the side of the triangle. The trick being that there are three perfectly reasonable ways to be random (two random points on the rim; a random direction plus a random distance out along the radius; a random point in the disc taken as the midpoint) and they give different answers — a third, a half, a quarter — with no way of saying which one is the mistake. Then I'd like a second phase with some sting in it: a small bet, real but trivial stakes, sweets or a pound, on whether a chord produced by some mechanical or arbitrary procedure is longer than the triangle's side, where two people who've internalised different methods quote different odds and one of them has to lose even though neither of them did anything wrong. What I'm after is that feeling that the unfairness isn't a trick and can't be dissolved by being cleverer.

Give me the rules in enough detail that a non-mathematician could run the thing from a sheet of paper; the materials (I have a printer, a pile of 10cm card discs and a laminator); a timeline for the 90 minutes; and a debrief that maps what happened onto the actual ideas — indifference, the competing parameterisations, invariance under a symmetry group, and why this isn't just a curiosity but bites whenever anyone says "assume a random X". Be honest about two things. First, what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it — I suspect at least one person will insist there must be a right answer and get frustrated, and I don't know how to handle that without deflating the room. Second, which part of the philosophical point the activity simply cannot reproduce and that I'll have to supply by talking at the end. Please write it in British English, and keep the notation light enough that the self-declared maths-phobes don't check out in the first five minutes.
