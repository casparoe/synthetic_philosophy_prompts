My reading group has spent two sessions on the principle that if you know something, you can know that you know it. A friend has produced the following argument, which he offers as a reductio of the requirement that knowledge leave a margin for error. I would rather keep the margin requirement, but I cannot see where his argument fails.

(1) If you know something, you could not easily have been wrong about it; knowledge requires a margin for error.
(2) Consider a series of experiences running from agony at one end to the complete absence of pain at the other, each step differing from its neighbours by an amount too small to notice, so that neighbouring members of the series cannot be told apart by the person undergoing them.
(3) Somewhere in the series two neighbours are such that one is a case of being in pain and the other is not; that is just what it is for the series to run from pain to no pain.
(4) So there are cases in which a person is in pain and yet could easily have been wrong about it, since the neighbouring case is one of no pain and the two cannot be told apart.
(5) By (1), in those cases the person does not know that they are in pain.
(6) But every case of being in pain is joined to the threshold by a chain of steps, no one of which can be told from the next, and at the threshold one does not know. Since nothing in one's experience marks where in the series one stands, one cannot know that one is on the painful side of it.
(7) So no one ever knows that they are in pain.
(8) Yet one does know that one is in pain when the pain is agonizing.
(9) So (1) is false.

I want to keep (1) and (8) and give up (7). Which step should I reject, why did it look compelling, and what does each available repair cost me? And does the argument have any force of its own against the principle that knowing entails knowing that one knows, or does rejecting the faulty step leave that principle untouched?
