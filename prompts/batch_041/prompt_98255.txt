Bit of an odd request, so bear with me. I spent 35 years as a chemist — statistical mechanics, thermodynamics, that sort of thing — and since I retired Ive been reading philosophy, mostly decision theory and the philosophy of probability, and I've gotten a bee in my bonnet about the St. Petersburg paradox. I want to apply to a small foundation that funds independent scholars for a one-paper project over about six months, and I need a two-page proposal out of you.

Here's my half-baked idea, turn it into something real. Everyone treats the paradox as a problem about the utility curve — the log-utility / diminishing-returns fix, or bounded utility, or risk aversion, or the observation that no casino has infinite reserves. But the more I sit with it, the more I think the real confusion is upstream of all of that: the question "what is this game worth?" is actually two different questions wearing one coat. One is the ensemble question — average over infinitely many hypothetical copies of the game. The other is the question a single person with a finite bankroll actually faces: they play once, or a handful of times, and their wealth compounds or gets eaten along the way. In my old line of work those two things come apart constantly — the ensemble average is not the time average, and it's the time average that governs the trajectory of the one system you actually have. My suspicion is that the standard resolutions quietly answer one of those questions and then claim to have disposed of the other, and that a good chunk of the "paradox" is that sleight of hand.

So the proposal should do the following. State the question precisely enough that it has a determinate answer — I'm worried "what is the game worth" is too mushy as it stands, so help me sharpen it into something that could actually be settled one way or the other. Explain why the existing positions leave it open rather than closing it. Lay out the method: I think this is mostly conceptual — define the right quantity for a single compounding agent, compare it against the ensemble expectation, and then see which of the classical moves (log utility, boundedness, and so on) fall out as special cases of one reading versus which are quietly doing separate work. Say why this angle rather than just adding yet another utility function to the pile. And be honest about what would count as the project failing — for instance, if it turns out the two readings really do coincide once you're careful, that's still a result, just a negative one.

Two practical things. No citations or reading list please — I'll fill those in myself, I just need the argument architecture. And tell me straight whether this is six months of work or whether I'm fooling myself about the scope; if it's too big, say what to cut.

One nagging worry I should flag, in case it changes the framing: is there a version of this where the paradox just dissolves — where once you separate the two questions neither of them is actually paradoxical anymore? If that's a real risk, I'd rather the proposal be honest about it than oversell the project.
