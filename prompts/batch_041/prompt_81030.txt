My former type-design teacher has been judging letterforms for decades, and her verdicts so far run like this: the two-storey 'a' of a text serif and the single-storey 'ɑ' of a geometric sans are both 'a's, neither more genuinely an 'a' than the other; a stencil 'a' whose counter is broken by a gap is still an 'a'; an ornate blackletter 'a' that untrained readers stumble over is still an 'a', since for her legibility is not what makes a letter; a glyph sitting exactly halfway between 'a' and 'e' is neither letter, and not merely a badly drawn 'a'; and the same glyph that is a well-formed 'a' in a cramped, spiky face becomes a mistake when dropped into a calm one. She now has to judge a student's typeface in which one and the same glyph serves as the Latin 'a' and the Cyrillic 'а', the two being indistinguishable, and a novelty font in which every glyph is systematically rotated 180°, so that the 'a' appears inverted. What will she say about each, and on what grounds? I want the rule she seems to be running, the rival rules that fit her earlier verdicts just as well, what each rival predicts for the two new cases, and the one further case that would best tell them apart.
