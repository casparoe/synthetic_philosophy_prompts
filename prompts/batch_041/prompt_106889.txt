In May 2010 the U.S. equity market briefly came apart: over roughly five minutes the Dow fell about 600 points on top of an already weak day (close to 1,000 points, near nine percent, at the trough), a few shares printed at absurd prices, and within about half an hour most of the loss was recovered. Analyze that episode through the idea of a free-floating rationale — the notion, which Daniel Dennett develops out of his design stance, that a bit of behavior can have a reason for it, a rationale, that no one need entertain or hold, in the way we can speak of the point of a reflex or of an evolved trait without positing a reasoner who grasps it.

Work the mapping in both directions. First, say what the free-floating rationale of the episode would be: what problem the prices and the algorithms were, in effect, solving or failing to solve, and how that rationale can be read off the aggregate behavior even though no participant held it. Then say what this reading explains that a story about panicking human traders, or about a single spoofing manipulator, does not. Finally, say where the notion strains or breaks: it was built for the products of natural selection and for animal behavior, and it is a further step to apply it to a designed, engineered, and legally regulated market whose participants do represent reasons to themselves. Judge whether that step is legitimate, mere metaphor, or something in between, and whether the same move licenses talk of the market having reasons even when the outcome — a crash — is one no one designed or wanted.
