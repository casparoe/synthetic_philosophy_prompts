I'm writing something for a seminar on AI governance and I keep getting stuck on the competitiveness constraint. Here's how it gets deployed: any proposal to spend time or compute on alignment gets shot down because someone less careful will reach the frontier first and set the terms. So the "alignment tax" is really a competitiveness tax, and no lab can unilaterally pay it. Fine, I get the shape of it. What I want from you is an argument that this move is much weaker than people treat it as — that the constraint, as usually stated, gives you almost no guidance and certainly doesn't license the corner-cutting it gets used to license.

A few things I want you to actually grapple with, not just wave at:

The constraint is a claim about *relative* position, but the thing anyone cares about is *absolute* risk. If every lab trims its safety spending by the same notch, nobody's relative position moves and everyone is worse off. So is the constraint doing any work here, or is it a collective action problem wearing a permission slip?

There's an asymmetry people lean on and I don't think it holds up. Being *ahead* is supposed to be the safe place to sit, because you get to set norms. Being *behind* is also supposed to justify corner-cutting, because you have to catch up. Try to say what's really going on when the leader and the laggard invoke the same constraint to justify the same behavior. And take a concrete case: lab A announces it's skipping evals to ship sooner, and lab B's board asks whether B should match them to "keep pace" — when B's safety spending isn't even the bottleneck for B's timeline, B is just slower for boring engineering reasons. Does the constraint apply to a race that isn't happening?

Last one, and I think it's the interesting case: suppose some safety work is capability-relevant — interpretability that actually tells you what your model can do, evals that tell you where to put your compute. Then the tax isn't a tax. Does that dissolve the competitiveness constraint, or just relocate it somewhere less convenient?

Don't give me a literature review, I don't need names and papers, I want the argument. Land on a clear position: when a lab believes its competitor is definately cutting corners, what should it do, and why doesn't that answer just collapse back into "match them"?
