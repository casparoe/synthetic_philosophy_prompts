Bit of an odd request, but I write about science and ideas for a living and I keep bumping into a genre of piece — the 'what history teaches us' column — and I want to get my own head straight about it before I write one myself.

A colleague of mine, a columnist who's smarter than this makes him sound, published a piece last month arguing that we should stop drawing lessons from history, and the core of it ran roughly like this. Historians and statesmen have always reached for the past to justify present action — Munich for appeasement, Vietnam for quagmires, the so-called Thucydides trap for great-power rivalry. Thucydides, he says, showed that war between Athens and Sparta was inevitable, because of the growth of Athenian power and the fear that growth produced in Sparta; the modern 'trap' named after him is simply the same law in a new setting — when a rising power threatens to displace a ruling one, war follows. The deeper reason these lessons fail, he argues, is that historical analogy is a uniquely unreliable form of reasoning: unlike the controlled comparisons you get in a laboratory or a clinical trial, history hands you a single run of the experiment, no control group, and the person doing the comparing is also the person who picked the two cases to compare in the first place. Munich is his showcase. The lesson everyone drew from it — stand up to dictators early — was itself a product of which parts of the 1930s people remembered and which they conveniently forgot, and that lesson licensed a great deal of damage in the decades that followed. His conclusion is that we should give up on the lessons of history altogether and decide each case on its own merits.

Something about this nags at me. It's persuasive, and I don't want to write a piece attacking a friend's arguement just because it's tidy. But I've read enough of the historians who actualy work on this to suspect a couple of the joints have been sanded smooth, and I can't put my finger on which ones.

So: take the role of a professional historian of the discipline — someone who works on how political actors use the past, and on the philosophy of historical explanation — and audit the summary sentence by sentence. Brainstorm a bit first if that helps, then work through it. Tell me what a specialist would nod at, what they'd wince at, and what's flatly wrong; where the piece has collapsed two claims that the serious literature keeps seperate, or quietly inflated a modest thesis into a sweeping one; and whether his final recommendation actually follows from the parts that survive scrutiny. Pay particular attention to two things: whether 'analogy is unreliable' is really a point about history specifically or something much broader, and what Thucydides is being made to say versus what the modern coinage is actually for.

And please be upfront about how confident you are in each part of the audit, and single out the one judgement you're least sure about. Ta.
