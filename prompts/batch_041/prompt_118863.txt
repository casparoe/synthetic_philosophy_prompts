I want to set a problem about what a rule of evidence presupposes concerning the nature of apologizing.

California's Evidence Code, section 1160, makes inadmissible "the portion of statements, writings, or benevolent gestures expressing sympathy or a general sense of benevolence" made to an accident victim or the victim's family, when offered "as evidence of an admission of liability in a civil action." The section adds, however, that "A statement of fault, however, which is part of, or in addition to, any of the above shall not be inadmissible pursuant to this section." Statutes of this shape — commonly called apology laws — have been enacted in many American states, and are usually defended on the ground that health-care providers will disclose and apologize for bad outcomes if the apology cannot be turned against them in malpractice litigation.

Two features of the drafting puzzle me. First, the rule protects the sympathetic half of an apology while expressly declining to protect the half that does the moral work; it treats an apology as a compound whose components can be severed and assigned different legal force. Second, the section defines the protected "benevolent gestures" as "actions which convey a sense of compassion or commiseration emanating from humane impulses" — a gesture must issue from a humane impulse, whereas a protected statement need only express sympathy, sincere or not.

Write an essay of no more than 700 words on what this practice presupposes. Reconstruct the implicit theory of apologizing that the rule requires, then interrogate it. Address, at least: (1) Does the rule presuppose that an apology is a composite of separable illocutionary acts, and is that picture defensible, or does the acknowledgment of fault survive inside the sympathetic half as an implicature that the drafter's scissors cannot remove? (2) The rule's rationale presupposes that jurors infer fault from an apology — otherwise there would be nothing to exclude — while its remedy presupposes that jurors can be prevented from drawing an inference they are said to be prone to. Are these commitments compatible? (3) Does the section presuppose that a speech act's legal effect is fixed by its content rather than by the speaker's intention, and if so, why does it impose a sincerity condition on gestures but not on statements? (4) Separate what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with: would a conventionalist theory of performatives license the same rule, and would a Gricean theory of communicative intention? State what the rule would have to look like under each.

Close by saying what would have to change in the practice if apologizing turned out to be indivisible — if there were no such act as apologizing without conceding fault. Lead with a single sentence stating what you take the rule's implicit theory to be, then defend it. Argue from the rule as given; do not cite secondary literature.
