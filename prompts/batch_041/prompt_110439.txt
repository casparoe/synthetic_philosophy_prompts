Write a dialogue between two philosophers of language on the relation between indicative and counterfactual conditionals. Both speakers are experts; neither is a strawman; each must engage the other's actual arguments rather than restate a position. Neither may cite or name any author, appeal to authority, or gesture at "the literature" — every move must be argued from the cases and phenomena themselves. Give the speakers names of your choosing, and have them define any technical term (modal base, ordering source, conditional probability, scope, entailment) the first time it appears. Aim for roughly 1,000–1,400 words of dialogue.

Assign the speakers these positions.

Speaker A holds that conditionals of both kinds have a single truth-conditional semantics: "if" restricts the domain of worlds over which a modal operator ranges, and the apparent difference between the two forms is a difference in which domain the antecedent selects — a body of information about the actual world in one case, a body of worlds held fixed as "how things already were" in the other — together with the tense morphology. On A's view both forms are true or false in the same way, and the difference is one of content, not of kind.

Speaker B holds that the two forms differ in kind: indicatives are assessed by how probable the consequent is on the antecedent and do not have truth conditions of the sort counterfactuals have, whereas counterfactuals are claims about what holds in the relevantly similar worlds in which the antecedent is true. B will insist that A's uniformity is purchased at the cost of the phenomena.

The two must work through all four of the following, and must not reach a comfortable consensus.

1. The dispensary case. A hospital keeps one remaining dose of an antitoxin in a locked dispensary. The log shows the dose was administered at 3 a.m. Only a person could have removed it; the building was locked; several staff members had keycard access; the entry records are incomplete. The investigators, who have no idea who did it, say both of the following:

(i) "If Ana didn't take the dose, someone else did."
(ii) "If Ana hadn't taken the dose, someone else would have."

They accept (i) and reject (ii). The speakers must explain why the two sentences come apart here, and must say precisely what does the work: the possible falsity of the antecedent in (i), the exhaustiveness of the ways the dose could have left the dispensary, the fact that the nearest worlds in which Ana refrains are worlds in which the vial simply stays in the fridge, or something else. Each speaker should say whether their own account predicts the split and why.

2. The future pair. "If it rains tomorrow, the fête will be cancelled" and "If it rained tomorrow, the fête would be cancelled." Both sentences are about tomorrow, neither antecedent is known to be false, and the second uses a past-tense form about a future event. What exactly is the difference, if it is neither the falsity of the antecedent nor the time of the event? Have the speakers connect this to the dispensary case: is it one distinction or two?

3. Negation and embedding. Have them argue about how each form behaves under "it is not the case that" and under expressions like "probably" or "I doubt that," and about whether the two forms behave alike in these environments. They should say what each pattern would show if it went one way and what it would show if it went the other, and which of them it in fact favours.

4. The cost of each view. Each speaker must name the phenomenon their own account handles worst, say what they can say about it, and concede at least one point to the opponent that genuinely costs them something. B must say what it takes for an indicative to be true if it has no truth conditions of the ordinary sort; A must say what constrains the choice of domain in each case, and whether that choice is a matter of the speaker's information, the world's history, or the sentence itself.

They should end with the disagreement sharpened rather than settled, and each should be able to state the other's position in terms the other would accept.

After the dialogue, output a single JSON object with exactly these fields and nothing after it:

- "protagonists": an array of two objects, each with "role" (the position in one phrase), "core_commitment" (one sentence), and "costliest_concession" (the point they gave away, at most 25 words).
- "dispensary_verdicts": an object with "indicative" and "counterfactual", each an object with "speaker" (the name of the speaker offering the explanation) and "explanation" (at most 35 words).
- "cruxes": an array of two or three objects, each with "question" (the unresolved question), "answer_A" and "answer_B" (at most 25 words each).
- "difference_proposed": an object with "formulation" (the sharpest statement of the difference between the two forms that the dialogue produced, at most 40 words), "accepted_by_both" (true or false), and "why_not" (at most 30 words; write "n/a" if both accepted it).
- "left_over": an array of one or two short strings naming what neither speaker accounted for.
- "verdict": an object with "better_placed" ("A", "B", or "neither") and "reason" (at most 40 words).
