ok so I've never studied any of this formally, I just read about religion-and-politics stuff on my own when something catches my interest, and I've gotten stuck on a conceptual thing that I cant seem to think my way out of.

The four cases everyone brings up are England, Germany, France and the US, and they're always laid out as four positions on one scale from religious state to secular state. But when I look at what they actually do, the scale stops making sense to me. England has an established church — the monarch is the supreme governor of it, a couple dozen bishops get seats in the legislature automatically — but nobody pays a tax to it and hardly anybody goes. Germany has no established church and the state is supposed to be neutral about religion, but the state collects a church tax for the big churches as a surcharge on your income tax, and to make it stop you have to go down to the registry office and formally declare that you're leaving, which I think can also mean the church won't do your funeral or let you be a godparent for your nephew. Then France, the country everyone points to as the truly secular one, still has Alsace-Moselle, where the state pays the salaries of the priests and pastors and rabbis because some old arrangement from before the separation law is still in force there — while the same country tells kids in public school they can't wear religious stuff. And the US has the no-establishment rule, but churches are tax exempt and the motto on the money is religious.

What I want isn't a country-by-country history, I can get that anywhere. I want the concept underneath explained. Is secular actually one property that countries have more or less of, or is it like three or four different properties that everyone keeps cramming under one word? Because it seems to me that "the state doesn't pick a winner among religions" and "the state stays out of religion entirely" and "the state keeps religion on a short leash" are not the same thing at all, and the four countries score differently on each.

Two specific things I cant settle on my own:

1. Is the German church tax a form of establishment? The state is doing the churches' billing and payroll for them, which sounds like entanglement. But you could also say the state is just acting as a neutral collection agency, so people can fund whichever religion they want without the state favoring any of them — and that refusing to collect it might itself be the state taking a side. Which way should I think about this, and does the answer change if the state takes a cut as a fee?

2. If England's church gets no tax money and nobody's in the pews, is it really established in any way that matters, or is establishment there just a costume? What would have to be true for it to be doing real work — and is the honest answer that "established" is a fact about legal status rather than about power or money?

Then the thing I actually care about: how would you even tell which of the four is more secular, without smuggling in one country's setup as the definition of secular? Every time I try, I catch myself treating whatever my own country does as the neutral baseline and everything else as a deviation. If it helps, my half-baked theory is that the real dividing line isnt separation but direction of dependence — whether the church depends on the state or the state depends on the church — which would put England and Germany on the same side and France and the US on the other, and that feels wrong to me but I cant say why. Tell me if that theory survives contact with the actual cases, and where exactly it breaks.
