I run grantmaking at a small foundation — about forty grants a year, twenty to two hundred grand each, decided by a team of five with no training in this stuff, signed off by trustees quarterly. We've started asking what our grants do in the long run and its turned into a mess: once the far future is in play, the case for and against the same grant is dominated by stuff we're inventing, and two of us can reach opposite conclusions from the same facts. One trustee says this is just cluelessness and we should stop pretending to rank grants at all.

I don't want to stop. Write me a procedure we can run on a proposal in an hour: questions in order, what counts as a good enough answer, where it stops with a no, and where it hands the decision to a person because the disagreement is about values rather than facts. Build in a sorting step I can teach: every consideration goes into one of three buckets — things we have evidence for, things we can reason about but not measure, and things that only work if you accept an assumption we can't defend — and the procedure should say how much of a case can be the third bucket before ranking by expected value stops meaning anything, and what we do then (reversibility? track record? admit it's a tie?).

Then run it on two proposals: a scholarship program whose near-term effects we can measure but whose long-run effects on who ends up powerful nobody agrees on, and a grant to people working on slowing down the reckless development of AI, where nothing is measurable and even the sign of the effect is contested. Tell me where it gives an answer I won't like, which steps are heuristics dressed up as rigor, and which are values choices I have to own myself.
