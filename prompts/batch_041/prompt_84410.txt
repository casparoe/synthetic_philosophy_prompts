A philosophy graduate student I know gave me the following summary of the Meno problem, and I would like to know how much of it to trust. The problem, he said, is why knowledge is worth more than mere true belief, by which he meant more useful, since both get you to the same destination when you act on them. Plato's answer in the Meno, he continued, is that true belief is like the statues of Daedalus, which run away unless they are fastened down, whereas knowledge is the same content tethered by an account of why it is true, so knowledge is more valuable because it stays put. He then said that contemporary epistemologists have largely settled the matter by appealing to the justified-true-belief analysis: because knowledge is justified true belief, the justification is what adds value on top of the truth, and the value it adds is reliability. He concluded that the Meno problem is really an early version of the Gettier problem, so that once Gettier showed justified true belief to be insufficient for knowledge, the Meno's answer was no longer available. Please separate what a careful epistemologist would accept, what is a harmless simplification, and what is an outright mistake, and say whether his concluding claim follows from whatever is accurate in the rest. Answer in continuous prose, without headings, lists, bold text, or tables.
