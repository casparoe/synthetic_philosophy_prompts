I am a novelist, and the book I have been writing is set partly inside a small research institute on a northern headland; the chapter I am stuck on turns not on a scene of action but on an argument. One of my characters is a physiologist who has reasoned himself into the view that experiences are perfectly real and entirely idle -- he has adopted the word epiphenomenalist and is a little vain about it -- and in the scene I have drafted, a doctoral student pins him to a whiteboard and reconstructs his position as a proof that no one can be trusted to report what they are experiencing. I want the scene to be honest: I would rather have my physiologist lose for the right reason than win for a bad one. Here is the whiteboard as I have written it. (1) Every physical event that has a cause has a cause that is wholly physical, and that cause is sufficient by itself to produce the event -- once it is in place, the effect is guaranteed. (2) An experience is not identical with any physical event; the smell of rain on hot asphalt is not the pattern of activity in my brain. (3) If an event already has a cause sufficient to produce it, nothing further is needed to produce it, and nothing that is not needed to produce it is among its causes. (4) So my utterance that I can smell the rain is not caused by my experience of the smell. (5) Yet the utterance is a reliable report of the experience: I say those words when the smell is present and not otherwise. (6) A report cannot be reliable unless the state it reports is among the causes of the reporting; otherwise the correspondence would be a coincidence. (7) The epiphenomenalist can grant (6) without surrendering (4): the experience and the utterance are siblings rather than parent and child, both produced by one and the same physical state, so the utterance registers the experience without being caused by it. (8) But if being a common effect of a common cause is enough for one state to register another, then a barometer registers the coming storm and tree rings register the drought and a bruise registers a fall; so either registering requires causation, in which case (7) fails and (6) and (4) reinstate the contradiction, or registering is that cheap, in which case the utterance is about the brain state and the pressure system and the weather and is not distinctively about the experience at all. (9) Therefore the epiphenomenalist cannot keep both his thesis and the reliability of his own introspection; he must abandon (2), or (5), or the thought that his reports are about his experiences.

What troubles me is less any single line than the reach of (3): it never mentions experiences, and if it holds, then no mental state is ever a cause of anything, and the student's own conclusion is not caused by his premises either -- reasoning itself would become a shadow, which is more than he set out to prove and more than I want my novel to claim. Of the claims on the board I would most like to keep (2) and (5), and I would sooner give up (1) than either, though I suspect that is expensive. So: which step or premise should go, why did it look so plausible when the student wrote it up, and what exactly does rejecting it cost -- what does the epiphenomenalist have to pay, and what do the rest of us have to pay who are not epiphenomenalists? If the argument is sound and only the price is in question, say so and itemize the price; if some step is quietly doing something illegitimate, name it and say what it was pretending to be. I would rather have the diagnosis than a tour of the literature.
