I spent forty years as a cosmologist, and I have lately retired into philosophy, where I keep discovering that questions my old colleagues filed under bookkeeping turn out to be the whole of the matter. The one that has taken hold of me is this: if the universe is spatially infinite and more or less uniform, then everything that happens here happens infinitely many times over; and if the future of life is unbounded, then the number of lives is infinite as well. My training gives me no purchase on the arithmetic of that, and I have been trying for months to write down what troubles me in prose, without success. So I am asking you for a poem instead.

Here is the trouble in the form I have got it into, and I would like the poem to work at this level of detail rather than gesture at infinity in general. Take two possible worlds, each containing infinitely many people. In the first, every life goes well by a fixed and modest standard; in the second, every life goes exactly twice as well, and nothing else differs. Any accounting I was taught for finite populations says the second is better. But the welfare summed over each world is infinite, and infinite is infinite, so the accounting returns equality — and worse, it will return equality between an infinite world of flourishing and an infinite world of unrelieved misery with a sufficient quantity of good tacked on. If I try to repair this by counting people, I am told that an infinite collection can be placed in one-to-one correspondence with a proper part of itself, so a world in which half the people simply never exist has exactly as many people as one in which they do, and adding infinitely many new happy lives to an infinite population leaves the population no larger. If I try to repair it by comparing lives one at a time, in some order, the order does the work: a series that converges only by cancellation can be rearranged to converge to any value I choose, or to none, so it is my sequencing and not the welfare that decides the verdict. If I adopt the most conservative patch available — comparing only worlds that differ at finitely many people, or where the differences can be summed in a way that does not depend on order — then every comparison I actually care about falls outside the principle. And the consequence that keeps me awake is this one: if the future holds infinitely many lives, then adding a finite number of lives to it, or subtracting a finite number, leaves the total where it was; the difference between having saved a thousand people and having saved none is, on this accounting, no difference at all.

I want a poem that makes this visible rather than merely naming it. Write a pantoum: quatrains in which lines two and four of each stanza reappear as lines one and three of the next, and a closing stanza whose second line is the third line of the first stanza and whose fourth line is the first line of the first stanza, so that the poem ends by re-entering its own opening and could be begun again — no more than six quatrains, and the recurring lines word-for-word identical each time they appear, with the change of sense carried entirely by what surrounds them. Among the recurring lines, two should carry the whole argument between them: one stating the aggregative principle in its most natural and unassuming form, and one recording what becomes of it on contact with an infinity. I would prefer these to be the second and fourth lines of the opening quatrain, so that the reader meets them first as remarks about an ordinary, finite world and only later, without any alteration of the words, as remarks about an unbounded one; the repetitions should leave the reader with the sense that the problem has not been solved but re-entered one level up, which is precisely what I cannot get prose to do. Write it in the voice of someone keeping the accounts rather than of a philosopher announcing a result — I have in mind a clerk of lives who has spent a career on finite measurements and is now asked to balance something without end — and keep it accurate enough that a specialist in infinite aggregation would find nothing to wince at, while a reader who has never considered the question should still come away with a poem rather than an argument in verse. No names, no attributions, no footnotes.
