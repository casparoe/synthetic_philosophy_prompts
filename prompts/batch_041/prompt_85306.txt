I've been teaching the nudging/paternalism stuff for years and I'm sick of the same argument: the libertarian-paternalist crowd says a nudge is fine so long as its transparent and doesn't ban anything, the critics say it bypasses deliberation and thereby disrespects autonomy, everyone shakes hands and goes home. I want you to argue for a different line and actually commit to it.

Heres the position. What makes an influence objectionable isn't that it bypasses deliberation, its that it isn't *answerable*. Call an influence answerable when the person it lands on can (a) represent it as one of the considerations bearing on their choice, rather than just as something that happened to them, and (b) defeat it by taking it up — i.e. deciding against it actually works. Argue that answerability, not bypassing, does the moral work here, and that it reorders the usual cases. A silent auto-enrolment default should come out permissible on your view (inertia is not answerability's enemy — you can opt out, and you can reason about the opt-out), while a mandatory cooling-off period — the intervention that *forces* you to deliberate — should come out as more objectionable than most people think, because it constrains the space of deliberations instead of feeding into it. Pick at least one further case of your own where the two criteria come apart, and say which way yours goes and why.

Then take on the hard bits properly, don't just gesture at them. (i) If answerability is what matters it looks agent-relative: the same auto-enrolment default may be perfectly answerable for a savvy 40-year-old and not for someone who's never read a pension statement, so does your view force per-person rather than per-policy verdicts — and is that a reductio? (ii) Doesn't every influence become answerable the moment you tell the person about it, which would make the criterion vacuous? (iii) Is answerability just autonomy with a new name? Meet whichever of these you think is strongest.

No reading list and no citations — I know the literature, I want the argument itself. And tell me where you'd bet your own view is wrong.
