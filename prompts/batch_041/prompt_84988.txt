I teach a seminar on nationalism and constitutional theory, and I'm designing an exercise in which students have to project a canonical nineteenth-century thinker onto problems he never lived to see. The figure I've chosen is John Stuart Mill, and the text we'll have read is his discussion of nationality in Considerations on Representative Government. As you probably recall, Mill treats the coincidence of state boundaries with national feeling as a practical condition for stable free government, and treats the principle of nationality as a real force in politics rather than a sentiment that enlightened statesmanship can simply dissolve. He died in 1873, well before the secession referendum became a standing constitutional device, before supranational legal orders existed, and before constitutional courts began issuing doctrines about the clarity of referendum questions. That gap is exactly what I want you to work in.

Please give me a sustained projection, roughly eight hundred words, of how Mill would have responded to the secession questions that now arise in Scotland, Catalonia, and Quebec. I don't want a generic survey of self-determination theory; I want Mill's likely reasoning, reconstructed from his own commitments, brought to bear on cases he couldn't have anticipated. Some specific things I'd like you to address along the way. Would he regard a unilateral declaration of independence by a regional parliament as legitimate, or would he insist that separation requires the consent of the wider state whose territory is being divided? Would he have accepted something like the Canadian Supreme Court's later doctrine that a referendum on secession must pose a clear question and produce a clear majority, and if so, on what principle would he have drawn the line between a clear and an unclear majority? How would he handle the nested-minority problem, where the seceding region is itself internally divided, or contains an indigenous nation or a linguistic minority that does not wish to leave? Would he locate the unit of self-determination in the existing administrative region or in the national community, and what would he do when those two come apart? What would he make of the fact that the European Union effectively requires a member state's consent before one of its regions can leave, given that the Union is itself a voluntary association of nations? Would he distinguish the negotiated Scottish referendum of 2014 from the contested Catalan process of 2017, and if so, on what grounds rather than on grounds of mere legality? And how much weight would his utilitarianism carry against a nationality's claim, if conceding independence promised to reduce aggregate welfare or to weaken liberal institutions in the successor state?

Two conceptual tools may help you sharpen the projection, and I'd like you to use them explicitly rather than gesturing at them. The first is the distinction between a primary right of secession, exercised by a national majority simply because it prefers independence, and a remedial right, exercised only in response to serious injustice or the denial of political voice. The second is the distinction between plebiscitary and constitutional conceptions of self-determination: on the one hand, the will of the people expressed in a vote; on the other, a set of legal procedures that specify in advance who may vote, on what question, and with what threshold. Mill's writings sit awkwardly with both, and I'd like you to say where he fits and where he doesn't.

Because this is a reconstruction and not a work of scholarship, please do not quote Mill at all, and do not cite chapters, page numbers, or editions. Paraphrase his positions in your own words rather than putting sentences in his mouth; I would much rather have an honest paraphrase than a quotation you cannot guarantee. Where your projection is well grounded in things Mill plainly says, say so. Where you are extrapolating from his premises to conclusions he never drew, mark that as extrapolation. Where his texts are silent or pull in two directions, say that rather than manufacturing a verdict, and where Mill scholars themselves disagree about how to read him on nationality, tell me which parts are genuinely contested rather than presenting one reading as the consensus.

Finally, please write this as continuous prose, the way you would write a long email to a colleague. No headings, no bullet points, no numbered lists, no bold or italicized labels, and no tables. Just paragraphs.
