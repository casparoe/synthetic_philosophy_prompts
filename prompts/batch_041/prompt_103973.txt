I teach a graduate decision-theory reading group (about a dozen students, one 90-minute session) and want an in-class game that makes the group feel the force of Good's result that the expected value of information is non-negative for an expected-utility maximizer -- the point where acquiring a free signal cannot leave you worse off in expectation -- and then feel precisely where that result is fragile. Design a betting game in which players are repeatedly offered a free look at a signal before committing to an act. In the early rounds the payoff structure should make looking obviously safe, so the theorem's prediction is borne out and no one who looks can end up behind in expectation; in a later round the same act of looking should be genuinely damaging even though the information still costs nothing. The damage must come from the structure of the decision problem rather than from deception or a hidden charge -- after the game the players should be able to name which of the theorem's assumptions they had quietly dropped (a fixed decision problem, the information being used to pick the best available act, the agent maximizing expected utility, and the set of available actions or the agent's own preferences staying the same). Give me the rules, materials, payoff tables, and round-by-round structure, plus a debrief that maps each moment onto the theorem and its loopholes; say what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle it; and be frank about which part of the result the game cannot reproduce and that I must supply myself. Points are fine, no money needs to change hands. End with a single line beginning Verdict: naming the assumption whose relaxation most reliably makes the value of information negative.
