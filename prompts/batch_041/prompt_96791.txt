Write a formal essay in distributive ethics on the following controversy. Telic egalitarians hold that equality is intrinsically valuable: a distribution is better, in at least one respect, the more equal it is, quite apart from how well off anyone is in absolute terms. The levelling-down objection contends that this view refutes itself. If equality is intrinsically valuable, then a change that makes everyone worse off while leaving them equally off, or that drags the better off down to the level of the worse off without improving anyone, is an improvement in one respect; and that verdict is absurd, since nothing is made better by making someone worse off and no one better off. Prioritarianism is usually offered as the repair: it gives extra weight to gains in the position of the worse off but assigns no independent value to equality, and so is said never to license levelling down. Your task is to decide whether prioritarianism genuinely escapes the objection or merely appears to. Argue the issue on its merits rather than surveying the literature, and do not cite authors.

To fix a target, take the prioritarian value function to be the sum, over persons, of the square root of each person's welfare, so that the marginal value of a gain falls as a person's welfare rises, and consider four distributions over two people: (a) both at 25; (b) one at 100 and one at 1; (c) one at 100 and one at 25; (d) both at 9. Compute what the utilitarian, the telic egalitarian, and this prioritarian must say about the relevant pairwise comparisons, and use the results throughout.

Then reach a verdict on each of two questions. First: does prioritarianism escape levelling down? Determine whether any change that improves no one can count as an improvement on this view, and state what it must say about the levelling-down of (c) to (a) and of (b) to (a). Note also whether the view can prefer a change that makes the worse off worse off, as the comparison of (b) with (a) appears to show, and explain what that implies about its relation to egalitarianism. Second: is the escape too cheap? The levelling-down objection seems to presuppose that an outcome cannot be better unless it is better for someone, a person-affecting restriction; if so, does the objection beg the question against the egalitarian, who denies the restriction? And if prioritarianism secures its verdict only by denying that inequality is ever bad in itself, does it thereby forfeit the ability to condemn inequalities that leave no one worse off in absolute terms — for instance an inequality consisting solely in a standing relation of domination, where the subordinate party's welfare is no lower than it would be under equality? Assess whether that loss is acceptable.

Close with a single verdict: state whether the levelling-down objection refutes telic egalitarianism, whether prioritarianism escapes it, and whether the price of escaping it is worth paying. Defend the verdict against the strongest reply open to the losing side, and make explicit and argue for any substantive principle on which you rely.
