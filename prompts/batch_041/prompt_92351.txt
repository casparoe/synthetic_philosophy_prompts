For two terms now a small seminar I run has been going over the exchange discussion in the justice book of the Nicomachean Ethics, alongside the first book of the Politics on acquisition and usury, and we have stalled twice in exactly the same place. The dispute concerns what Aristotle is doing in that stretch of argument where he holds that exchange requires a proportion, that need is the single thing that binds otherwise incommensurable goods together, that money is a conventional stand-in for need, and that coin is called nomisma because it exists by law rather than by nature and can be made useless by us. Three readings circulate, and each has a standing move against the other two.

On the first reading, Aristotle is attempting a theory of exchange value: he is trying to say what fixes determinate exchange ratios, and he fails. The evidence is the ending, where he concedes that in truth things so different cannot be commensurable even though practice treats them as if they were, and reaches for a common measure that he cannot quite produce; the proportion he sets out, whose terms mix the exchangers with the things exchanged, is either the ingenious or the incoherent part. This reading descends from Marx, who in Capital credits Aristotle with the first analysis of the value-form while holding that he could not have completed it in a society resting on slavery, where human labour could not yet appear homogeneous. Its stock move against the others is that only it explains why the passage ends in an admission of failure.

On the second reading, Aristotle is not doing value theory at all but justice: the proportion is a criterion of fair reciprocity, and need and money are conditions of the possibility of a fair exchange rather than determinants of a price. The placement of the discussion inside a treatise on virtue, and the reciprocity vocabulary, are the evidence. Its stock move is that the first reading has to treat a chapter of ethics as a botched economics paper.

On the third reading, Aristotle is doing neither: money is a unit of account whose function is to render heterogeneous goods commensurable for practical purposes, so his answer to the commensurability puzzle is institutional and conventional all the way down, and what looks like an aporia is in fact a thesis. Its stock move is that the first reading requires a natural measure, while Aristotle says outright that the measure is by law and within our power to revoke.

My own hypothesis, offered as a hypothesis rather than a conclusion, is that this is a question-conflation. Two questions are being run under one heading: what makes goods commensurable, a conceptual question about the possibility of exchange, answered by need and by money; and what makes an exchange just, a normative question about fair terms, answered by the proportion. Aristotle moves from the first to the second without marking the transition and borrows the terms of the proportion from the commensurability discussion, so each side can point to sentences the other must explain away and neither reading can be refuted from the text alone. If that is right, the disagreement is not substantive but a matter of which question the passage answers; yet the parties are not merely talking past one another, since the choice of question is itself a claim about what the text is for.

I want two further candidates discriminated rather than merely listed. The first is methodological: the sides may differ about the criterion of a good reading, one favouring charity, one internal coherence, one fit with fourth-century market practice, in which case the impasse is a disagreement about what would count as settling it. The second concerns what need is, a psychological fact about the strength of wants, a social fact about what a community treats as exchangeable, or a normative requirement of reciprocity; each reading needs a different answer and the text supplies none. And I want one specific argument pressed, because it seems to cut against the first two readings at once. If money is conventional and revocable, then any standard of just exchange stated in money is itself conventional, so the justice reading owes us a non-conventional standard the text never provides; while the value reading needs a natural measure, which the remark about law and revocability appears to deny. That would leave the third reading as the only one on which the passage is coherent, at the price of making Aristotle no value theorist at all, and with a further puzzle: if commensurability is purely institutional, what is the proportion in the justice argument measuring? Is that a genuine argument, or does it equivocate on convention?

Give me a diagnosis rather than a survey, and do not settle the exegetical question for me. What kind of disagreement is this, in the end: verbal, a difference of question, a difference of method, or a genuine substantive dispute about whether there can be a measure of value that is not itself conventional? Is the persistence of the impasse evidence that the question is ill posed, or only that it is hard? Given your diagnosis, what would count as progress here, a distinction, an argument that one reading makes the passage and the Politics material coherent where the others leave it broken, a reconstruction of the arithmetic of the proportion, or the conclusion that nothing should move and why? Assume I know the texts and need no summary; rank the candidate diagnoses by how much of the stall each actually explains.
