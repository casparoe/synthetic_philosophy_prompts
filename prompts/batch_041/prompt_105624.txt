A friend and I have been working our way through Cicero's dialogue on old age, the one in which Cato the Elder, then in his eighty-fourth year, is made to explain to the younger Scipio and Laelius why old age need not be wretched — that the sensual pleasures one loses were a doubtful blessing at best, that the mind keeps its edge so long as it is kept in use, that the old retain a real authority and can still do worthwhile work, that memory is not in fact the ruin it is reputed to be, and that death, when it comes, is either the end of everything or the threshold of something better. For the final meeting of our small reading group I have been asked to open a discussion on a question that of course never arose for Cicero himself, and I should like your help in preparing what to say. The question is what he would have made of the modern ambition to do away with ageing altogether. To make it concrete: imagine an intervention, safe, cheap and universally available, that arrests senescence at roughly the prime of life, so that a person might go on for centuries, or indefinitely, in full bodily and mental vigour, never arriving at the condition that Cato is at such pains to defend. I do not want a summary of the dialogue's argument as it stands, nor a survey of what others have said about it; what I want is a reconstruction of how its speaker would actually respond to this prospect.

Work through it as a genuine prediction rather than a paraphrase. Which of his commitments would survive the change in circumstances and which would be quietly undermined by it — for instance, if the consolations of old age depend on there being an old age to console, what is left of his position once that stage of life is abolished? Where would he be most likely to entrench himself, and what would he find newly troubling about a world in which people simply declined to die? I am especially interested in how his attitude to death would shift once death had become something one could refuse, and whether he would accept the familiar modern worry that a life without an end loses the shape that gives it meaning, or would dismiss that worry as one more instance of the very fear of death he sets out to dissolve. Be candid, too, about the limits of the exercise: tell me where you are extrapolating from things he does say, where you are inferring a temperament rather than a doctrine, and where the prediction is frankly a guess. Please write it as continuous prose, with no headings, bullet points, bold text or tables, in the manner of a letter, and in British English, so that I can read it aloud to the group and then defend it in discussion.
