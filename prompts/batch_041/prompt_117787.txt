I'm running a reading group for grad students in philosophy of biology and next month we're doing the what-is-life thing. Every time we do it people just recite whichever definition they heard first and then we argue about viruses for an hour, so this time I want a decision tree instead of a list of definitions. Can you build me one?

Shape: the internal nodes are questions about concrete cases, the leaves are definitions of life. Seven or eight leaves, no more, and at least a couple of the leaves should be positions that don't usually get a name in the encyclopedia version of this debate.

Cases that have to show up somewhere on the tree, because these are the ones that actually split people:

- a mule: alive, sterile, no descendants ever
- a prion: makes more copies of itself by reshaping other proteins, no metabolism, no nucleic acid anywhere
- a virion sitting on a bench, versus the same virion's genes running a host cell
- a bacterial spore: metabolically inert, not growing, can sit for a century and then wake up
- fire
- a transposon shuffling copies of itself around a genome
- a stripped down lab bacterium with a few hundred genes that dies unless you feed it exactly the right medium
- a lichen, which is two organisms tangled into one thing that behaves like one organism
- something from a shadow biosphere with a completely different biochemistry

Some ideas I'd like you to work in, because they cut across the usual textbook axes:

1. The no-definition-give-me-a-theory move: the thought that any definition we write now is premature, since the whole point of a theory of life is to tell us what to look for, and that we should maybe adopt a neutral placeholder term for whatever the theory eventually picks out instead of fighting about the word life. Which of the cases above does that move refuse to answer, and does refusing count as an answer?

2. A how-hard-was-this-to-build criterion: count the minimum number of joining steps you'd need to make the molecule out of simple building blocks, and say anything past some threshold is too improbable to have arisen without a long history of selection behind it. Ask what that criterion says about the prion, and about fire, which is presumably very cheap to build.

3. Teleology: life as the kind of system that maintains itself and for which things can go well or badly, as opposed to something you can describe purely chemically. Fire consumes and spreads. Can fire do well or badly?

4. The weird fact that bacterial and archaeal membranes are built from mirror-image versions of the same backbone and joined with different linkages, so it's genuinly unclear whether the last common ancestor had a normal membrane, a leaky one, or whether membrane chemistry got invented twice. Use that for the different-biochemistry node if it's useful.

Rules for the tree:

- phrase every node as a contrast or a case someone can answer without knowing which leaf it leads to. Bad: do you think viruses are alive? Better: the spore and the virion are both inert and both come back to activity; is the difference that the spore carries its own restart program, that the spore is a stage of something that was already alive, or neither?
- build it from the logical space first and drop the actual positions into it afterward. Note which combinations of answers end up unoccupied and say whether that's because they're incoherent, unattractive, or just unexplored.
- flag any leaves your tree can't separate. My suspicion is that the autopoiesis-ish leaf and the plain metabolism leaf won't come apart on any of these cases, but see what you find.
- each leaf gets a name, or unnamed if nobody defends it, plus one sentence on its most awkward commitment.
- no citations, no reading list, I don't care who said what.

Audience is grad students, a few with a bio background, so you can assume they know what a transposon is, but write the node questions so a smart non-biologist could still answer them.
