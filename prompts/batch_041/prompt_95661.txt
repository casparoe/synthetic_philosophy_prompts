I am a novelist at work on a book whose first-person narrator repeatedly avows a conviction — his loyalty, his contentment, his having no regrets — in a register the reader has already learned to distrust. A philosopher friend read the draft and told me I was writing Moore's paradox; I am not certain I am, so I have gone back to Kazuo Ishiguro's *The Remains of the Day* as the strongest case I know of a novel that seems built on that structure. I would like you to read the novel through the lens of Moore's paradox: the peculiar absurdity, not of contradicting oneself, but of asserting something and in the same breath denying that one believes it — the omissive form, in which one asserts that p while denying that one believes that p, and the commissive form, in which one asserts that p while avowing the belief that not-p. Stevens, the butler, insists on his dignity, on his loyalty to Lord Darlington, and on the absence of regret for the life he has surrendered, while the reader — and, I would argue, the novel's own accumulating pattern of detail — delivers the opposite verdict.

What I want from the reading is a genuinely critical one. Does the novel stage a Moore-paradoxical state in the strict sense — a narrator who could sincerely voice both halves of the conjunction — or only the weaker thing, an unreliable narrator whose avowals and conduct diverge over the course of a life? The distinction matters to me as a novelist because the first would dramatize a thesis about the relation between assertion and belief, whereas the second is a technique of characterization, and I need to know which I am actually attempting. Does Stevens's narration bear on the standing dispute over whether the absurdity is semantic, the two halves genuinely contradicting one another, or merely pragmatic, the two halves being consistent but impossible to assert together? And does the novel, by making such a narrator inhabit an entire life rather than a single sentence, support or embarrass the view that one cannot so much as coherently believe a Moore-paradoxical conjunction — or the parallel claim, in the literature on self-deception, that the self-deceiver cannot really hold contradictory beliefs at once? Where does the reading strain? If there is a scene or a turn in the book that resists being forced into the paradox, I would rather you say so plainly than smooth it over. The reading should be faithful in both directions: it should not flatten the novel into an illustration of a puzzle, and it should not soften the puzzle to make the novel fit.

Conclude by telling me what the novel adds to the philosophical discussion that the puzzle, stated as a puzzle, cannot — what it shows, or makes felt, that argument alone leaves out.

Finally, be explicit about how confident you are in each of your main claims, and single out the one point you are least sure of, with your reasons for the doubt.
