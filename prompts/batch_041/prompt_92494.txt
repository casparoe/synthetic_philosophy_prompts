I teach a graduate seminar on moral responsibility, and I want my students to work through Strawson's reactive attitudes using an institutional case rather than a puzzle case, so I've stipulated a hospital system's error-disclosure policy and I want you to reconstruct what it presupposes. The policy, as I've stipulated it, has five elements. First, any clinician who reports their own error or near-miss through the safety portal within twenty-four hours is immune from disciplinary action regardless of the harm caused; the event goes to a system-level review that produces process changes, not findings about the individual. Second, errors that surface any other way — chart audit, a colleague, the family — go to a departmental review that can sanction the individual, with the severity of sanction scaling to the harm. Third, clinicians involved in a serious event are assigned a peer supporter under the system's second-victim program and offered a facilitated conversation with the family; participation is voluntary, but the review board treats a refusal to participate as evidence of insufficient accountability. Fourth, the same system operates a no-fault compensation stream for a defined class of birth injuries: families are paid without any finding of fault, and the clinicians in those cases are not reviewed at all. Fifth, sanctions are calibrated to what the clinician knew or should have known rather than to the outcome — except that the immunity in the first element applies even where the clinician was negligent in a way that would otherwise draw sanction, so long as they reported it themselves.

Reconstruct the implicit theory of responsibility this arrangement presupposes. Distinguish carefully what the policy strictly requires from what it merely fits well with, since I suspect several theories would license most of it. Address these in particular: does the policy presuppose that the reactive attitudes are legitimate responses to the quality of will an agent expresses — so that the self-reporting clinician's honest stance is precisely what exempts them — or is it a frankly instrumental regime that treats blame as a lever for safety, and can those two readings coexist without incoherence? Strawson holds that the reactive attitudes neither require nor admit of an external justification; is a policy that deliberately engineers whom we resent and whom we exempt a counterexample to that, or is it consistent once we see the attitudes as trained rather than justified? Does the practice treat the objective stance as something adoptable at will by a committee — rather than something we lapse into toward the incapacitated or the alien — and what follows for Strawson's own account if it does? Which rival positions does the arrangement quietly rule out, and where is it genuinely underdetermined? Finally, describe the practice that would result from taking the objective stance throughout, and say whether the human losses Strawson emphasizes would be a reason to reject it or merely beg the question against it.

Separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who know this literature, and do not present a contested reading of Strawson as the consensus; where the stipulated details of my policy are doing load-bearing philosophical work, say so. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give the reasoning. No preamble, and no restating of my question.
