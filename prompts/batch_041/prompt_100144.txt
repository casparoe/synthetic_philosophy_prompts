I host a small interview series on practical philosophy, and next week's guest is a philosopher who has spent several years developing what she calls the bicameral model of moral uncertainty. The model is meant to sit between two familiar positions: maximizing expected choiceworthiness, on the one hand, and a simple parliamentary or voting model, on the other. On her account, each moral theory that one takes seriously receives a vote weighted by one's credence in it, but those votes are then filtered through a second chamber that registers the magnitude of what is at stake from each theory's point of view. That second chamber can reweight or veto a decision, but only up to a cap, and the cap is justified not by a brute aversion to fanaticism but by a diachronic integrity constraint: the agent must be able to endorse the pattern of her choices over time from within each of the theories she holds. She also insists that moral uncertainty is not merely empirical uncertainty about a fixed value function, because the theories themselves supply the standards by which value is compared, and because they are not mutually exclusive in the way empirical hypotheses are.

Draft eight to ten interview questions that go beyond having her restate the view. I want at least one question each on whether the bicameral model collapses into expected choiceworthiness with extra steps; on how the second chamber's cap avoids fanaticism without becoming arbitrary or ad hoc; on what work the claim that moral uncertainty is not empirical uncertainty is doing, and whether the model can survive if that claim is rejected; on how the model handles intertheoretic value comparisons and cases of incomparability or parity; and on what the model implies for collective or political decision-making, where the relevant theories may be held by different people rather than by one uncertain agent. For each question, include a follow-up keyed to the answer she is most likely to give, and make the sequence progressively harder for her to deflect with the response that this is a research programme rather than an objection. End with a question about what evidence or argument would count against the bicameral model, and a follow-up about whether any such evidence could be recognized from within the model itself. No generic questions about how she became interested in philosophy or what moral uncertainty feels like.
