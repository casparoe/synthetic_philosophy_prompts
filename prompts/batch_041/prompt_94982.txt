I am a retired medical researcher — thirty-five years in gastrointestinal physiology — who has spent the last several years reading philosophy and history with more enthusiasm than discipline. My own field supplies the anecdote that got me into the present trouble: for most of my working life it was simply understood that bacteria could not colonise the stomach, and the people who claimed otherwise were not merely mistaken but faintly disreputable; the reversal came well within my career, and I have never quite got over it. I therefore take the possibility of a despised minority being right more seriously than most of my former colleagues do, and I have been trying to square that disposition with what I have read about historical revisionism and its disreputable twin, denialism — chiefly in connection with the Holocaust, the Armenian genocide, and Nanjing.

Two quite different criteria seem to be on offer for the line between the two, and I would like them compared and adjudicated.

The first I have imported from the philosophy of science, from Lakatos: a research programme is progressive when it anticipates novel facts and grows by them, degenerating when it merely patches itself to accommodate what is already known, and the verdict is provisional — a judgment about the trajectory of an inquiry, revisable in the light of what the inquiry does next. Call this the trajectory criterion.

The second is what I find in the writing on denialism itself: that the difference lies not in the programme's trajectory but in the conduct of the argument — a systematic asymmetry in the standard of proof demanded of congenial and uncongenial claims, the treatment of absent documentation as disproof, the relocation of the goalposts each time a demand is met, and the construal of a settled consensus as itself evidence of conspiracy. Call this the conduct criterion.

What I want is a comparison of the two, and a verdict. Do they pick out the same cases? Where do they come apart — is there a degenerating programme that is not denialist, or a denialist enterprise that is in good methodological order by Lakatos's lights? Which of the two, if either, is fundamental, and does one explain why the other matters? And a question I cannot settle alone: the trajectory criterion yields a fallible, revisable verdict, whereas denialism as the term is actually used is a verdict that no future evidence could overturn. Are these two versions of a single concept, or is denialism a different kind of thing — a political practice in the costume of a research programme — in which case the comparison I have set up is itself misconceived and I would rather be told so. I am also unsure whether the trajectory criterion survives the crossing from experimental science into history, where one cannot intervene and where the evidentiary base is finite and largely fixed; if "novel fact" means nothing in that setting, say so, and say what should replace it.

Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence and then give the reasoning, without preamble and without restating my question.
