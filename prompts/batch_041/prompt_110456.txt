I am a retired ecologist — forty years in animal behavior, most of it spent on signaling and mate choice — and since retiring I have been reading philosophy and speculative fiction at a rate my former self would have found indecent. My reading group has handed me the dark forest hypothesis to present next month: the claim that the galaxy looks silent because any civilization that advertises itself is destroyed preemptively, since nobody can verify anybody else's intentions and the cost of guessing wrong is extinction. I would rather bring in a short story of my own than another summary, so I am asking for your help in writing one. What I want from you is the story itself, not an essay about the story.

The thing I keep noticing is that the argument leans on a premise my old field would never grant for free, namely that no civilization can send a signal of peaceful intent that a stranger could rationally trust, because anything the peaceful could send, the hostile could send too. Behavioral ecology has a standard reply to that worry, the handicap principle: a signal is credible only when it is costly in a way a cheater cannot afford to fake. So I want a story that asks whether the dark forest survives that reply, and in particular whether the very cost that makes a peace signal believable might be the thing that makes the sender worth destroying. A civilization can only afford to be seen, I suspect, if it can survive being struck first; but a civilization that can survive being struck first is precisely the one its neighbors cannot afford to let keep growing. I want the story to sit on that paradox.

A few things I would like built into it. The setting should be a single hiding civilization, not a galactic council and not a war, and the argument should be conducted by people who must decide something concrete within a few days: whether to transmit, and what to transmit. Let both sides be genuinely good — the faction that wants to broadcast should have the better biology on its side, and the faction that wants silence should have the better game theory — so that I cannot tell from the prose which one you take to be right. The physics and the biology should constrain the plot rather than decorate it: the signal has to cost the senders something real, and the difficulty a receiver would have in distinguishing an offer from a feint has to be a genuine problem inside the story, not a misunderstanding that a clever character clears up. I would also like the story to turn, at least partly, on whether a first strike in this universe can be decisive, since if retaliation is possible the case for shooting first weakens; that technological question should be live in the characters' debate rather than settled by the narrator.

Please do not resolve it. No twist revealing that the neighbors were peaceful after all, no last-minute message from an elder civilization, no narrator's verdict that hiding was wise or that contact would have worked. I would rather finish the story unable to say whether the transmission should have been sent, with both readings still available. And please do not have anyone recite the hypothesis as a slogan; let the characters reason their way to it in their own words, without the stock vocabulary, so that it feels discovered rather than quoted.

Roughly fifteen hundred to twenty-five hundred words. Write in American English. No headings, no bullet points, no bold text, no tables — continuous prose only, the way one would write a letter.
