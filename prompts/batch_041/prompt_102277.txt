Having read about Bertrand's paradox, I am convinced it establishes something stronger than is usually admitted. Because the principle of indifference yields three mutually incompatible probabilities — 1/3, 1/2, and 1/4 — for one and the same event, namely that a randomly chosen chord is longer than a side of the inscribed equilateral triangle, the principle cannot be a sound rule of reasoning; it can only manufacture contradictions. From this I conclude that no objective prior over a continuous quantity is defensible, and that whenever I assign such a prior I am merely expressing a subjective preference with no better claim to rationality than any other. The same trouble arises in my own work: when I try to set a prior on the unknown proportion of defective items coming off a production line, being indifferent over the proportion and being indifferent over its logarithm give me different priors, which I take to be one more instance of the identical defect. Is my conclusion correct? And if it is, how am I to choose a prior in practice, given that none of them can be justified as objectively correct? Please answer in American English.
