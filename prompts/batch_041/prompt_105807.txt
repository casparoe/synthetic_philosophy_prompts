Write a short story about a state-appointed biographer in a fictional republic who is commissioned to compose the official Life of a recently deceased general. The republic treats public biography as a form of moral instruction, and rival factions each want the general remembered as a different exemplar: the soldier's soldier, the reluctant conscience, the martyr for the old order. As the biographer gathers letters, testimony, and mutually incompatible anecdotes, she confronts what she begins to call the exemplar's dilemma: the more a life is shaped to be imitable, the less it resembles any life a person could actually live. The story should explore whether moral learning from lives is possible when the life has been curated for that purpose, and whether the biographer's responsibility is to the person, to the truth, or to the civic good. Include a scene in which she compares her subject with a figure from an older tradition of Plutarchan parallel lives and hagiography, and a scene in which a student asks her whom he should imitate.
