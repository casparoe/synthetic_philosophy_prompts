I run a monthly discussion evening for adults at a community hall: about sixteen people, mixed ages and jobs, no philosophy training, and nobody who wants to be lectured at. I have one hundred minutes, one room, paper, pens, a hat, and a small refreshments budget that I am willing to let the group spend for real. I would like an activity that makes them feel, rather than hear about, the thought that there is no neutral way to arrange a set of choices — that whoever sets the pre-ticked option, the order of the list and the cost of opting out has already decided something, even when nobody lies and every option is on the table. I started thinking about this after noticing that the only reason I walk anywhere most days is that a step counter came pre-installed on my phone.

Three things I want them to run into before anyone names them. First, that a well-meant arrangement of options moves a group away from what that same group said it wanted twenty minutes earlier. Second, that the identical mechanic feels like helpful design when it points where they already wanted to go and like being managed when it doesn't. Third, that the strongest lever is usually not the pre-ticked option but the price of looking at the rest.

Roughly, I imagine splitting the group in two, having each half fill in a short survey of what they would pick, then having each half design the other half's choosing environment — the pre-ticked option, the order of the list, and the effort required to deviate — with no deception permitted and each designer handed a secret goal slip. Then the choices are made for real. Please treat that as a sketch: tell me whether it can carry the three discoveries, and replace it with something else if it can't. I need roles, materials, a timeline with timings, a way of recording what happened without money changing hands, and a view on how the goal slips should be written so that the contrast between the same mechanic feeling benign and feeling like management actually lands. Nothing should be explained to them until afterwards; the vocabulary should arrive only in the debrief. Please stay away from the organ-donation and cafeteria examples, which my group has heard.

The debrief should get from what they have just done to the difference between shaping how someone pursues their own ends and substituting your ends for theirs, to why being steered by someone else's will can be objectionable even when it leaves you better off, and to the question I suspect nobody in the room will ask: who gets to be the designer, and how could the people living inside an arrangement ever consent to it.

Finally, tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time — the half that treats it as a competition, the participant who refuses to design anything, the group that games the survey — and how to handle each. And be honest about which part of the idea the activity cannot reproduce and that I will have to say out loud myself.
