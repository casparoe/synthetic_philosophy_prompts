I have just taken up a seat on my region's public-health prioritisation panel, and the first substantive decision before us has left me uneasy. The panel must commit a fixed budget to one of two programmes. The first would spare a small, precisely identified group of patients a severe and lasting harm, at a cost that is defensible only because the harm is so grave. The second would lift a mild but genuine burden — a few days of discomfort, some lost time, a low background of worry — from a very large number of people, on the order of tens of millions, at the same total cost. The first programme does nothing for the many; the second does nothing for the few. We can fund one, not both.

Please set out the most important implications of the philosophical debate about aggregating tiny harms — the family of cases that opposes one person's undergoing a severe ordeal against an enormous number of people each undergoing a trivial irritation — for how I should reason about the decision in front of me. I am not asking for a survey of who has defended which side, and I do not need citations or reading suggestions; I want the consequences of that debate for a decision-maker in my position.

Please address the following features of our case explicitly, since I suspect they do more work than the headline numbers:

- the mild burdens in the second programme are heterogeneous rather than a single uniform nuisance, so it is unclear how, or whether, they may be summed;
- the large number of people is finite and known, rather than astronomically large;
- the severe harm falls on identified individuals, whereas the mild burdens are statistical and, for any given person, unlikely;
- the mild burdens, if not lifted, would fall repeatedly on the same people rather than once each;
- and it is a public body, not a private individual, that would be imposing whichever programme we choose.

For each feature, say whether it strengthens or weakens the case for the aggregating answer, and conclude with the two or three considerations that should, in your view, actually decide the matter.
