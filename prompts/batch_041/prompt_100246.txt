In my metaphysics seminar we have spent the past few weeks on causation by omission, and the final assignment is an essay that takes a stand on one of the seminar's unresolved disputes rather than merely surveying it. I would like help drafting that essay, and I would prefer it to end with a defended verdict rather than a survey that trails off into "it depends."

The dispute is this. On the familiar difference-making picture, a condition counts as a cause of an outcome when the outcome counterfactually depends on it: had the condition been different or absent, the outcome would have been different or absent. This handles ordinary positive causation well, but it also seems to admit omissions. The gardener's failure to water the plants is followed by their death, and had the gardener watered them, they would have lived; so the omission seems to qualify as a cause of the death on the very same footing on which the watering, had it occurred, would have qualified as a cause of their thriving. Many philosophers resist this. They insist that an omission — a mere absence, something that never happened — cannot enter into the causal relation at all, and that omission talk is either a useful fiction or a distinct, second-class notion (something like a quasi-cause) rather than genuine causation.

I want the essay to adjudicate between two theses, which I will label for convenience. The parity thesis holds that omissions can be causes in whatever sense positive events are causes, and that any felt asymmetry between the two is a pragmatic or normative artifact — a matter of what we happen to care about, or what expectations are in play — rather than a metaphysical difference. The deflationary thesis holds that omissions are not causes, or are causes only in an attenuated sense, and that the appearance of parity comes from the counterfactual test being too permissive.

Please do the following. First, set out the strongest version of each thesis before adjudicating, so that neither is a straw man. Second, confront a specific challenge to the parity thesis: if the counterfactual test licenses omissions as causes, it seems to license far too many — not only the gardener's failure but also the failure of every bystander, of the owner, of the seed supplier, and indefinitely many others. Explain whether the deflationary theorist can turn this profligacy into an argument against parity, and whether the parity theorist has a principled way to keep the class of omission-causes from exploding. Third, consider a case of my own devising. A child is drowning in a pool. A stranger standing at the edge could easily have saved her and did not; a lifeguard on duty could easily have saved her and did not. The two omissions are counterfactually indistinguishable with respect to the death. Does parity commit us to saying the two are equally causes? If our intuitions diverge here, is that divergence evidence about causation itself, or only about blame and responsibility? Say what you think the divergence shows.

Then defend a verdict. If you think the truth lies between the two theses, specify precisely the respect in which omission-causation is genuine and the respect in which it is not, rather than gesturing at a middle ground.
