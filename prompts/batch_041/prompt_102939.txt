I'm assembling materials for a small workshop and need the joint statement of an adversarial collaboration on whether we may attribute beliefs — and other propositional attitudes — to large language model-based systems.

The two parties are, first, the intentional-stance realists: those who hold that attributing beliefs to such systems is not merely a convenient shorthand but is licensed whenever the attribution earns its keep by supporting prediction and explanation of the system's behavior, in the way the intentional stance is supposed to work for any system complex enough to reward it. Second, the functional-organization skeptics: those who hold that genuine belief attribution requires a certain kind of internal organization — a stable, revisable, action-guiding representation that the system is sensitive to as evidence — and who argue that current systems lack whatever it takes, so that belief-talk about them is at best a façon de parler and at worst a category error.

Write the document the two camps could actually both put their names to. I want:

1. An agreed-claims section, numbered: what belief attribution is for; what would count as evidence for or against it; what is common ground even though it sounds like a concession (that the systems produce fluent and often useful text; that engineers and users routinely and profitably talk as if these systems believe and want things; that nobody is claiming the systems have inner experiences merely in virtue of being described this way).

2. The precise statements of the remaining disagreement, each phrased so that its own side would accept the wording of its own view, with a note wherever the two formulations turn out to be about words rather than about the world.

3. The cruxes — the sub-questions such that settling one would move at least one side — and, for each crux, a case, test, or argument that both sides agree bears on it, with each side's prediction about it written down in advance.

So that the statement has teeth rather than trading slogans, I want it to work through three instruments, and I want you to say whether each is well-formed or whether it quietly begs the question in favor of one side:

(a) A revision test: a system may be said to believe that p only if being presented with evidence against p alters not just its next utterance but its downstream dispositions across contexts — and, if it does not, whether we should conclude that it never believed p or only that it has stopped asserting p.

(b) A commitment test: whether the system will let the putative belief constrain behavior when doing so is costly or inconvenient, or whether the belief is idle except when queried.

(c) A distinction between believing that p and being disposed to assert that p in the contexts one is prompted in. I suspect a large part of this dispute is really about which of these two things is being claimed, and if so I want the statement to say so and to say why the remaining disagreement, if any, is not merely verbal.

Then apply all three to a hard case that I think is where the camps most often talk past each other: the difference between a belief encoded in the weights and a belief supplied only in the context window — say, a falsehood the system has been told this once versus a falsehood absorbed in training — and the further case of two copies of the same model fine-tuned on divergent data, where we want to say they believe different things even though their architecture and prompt are identical. Include at least one case where a downstream automated system treats the model's output as a fact, so that the attribution has consequences and cannot be shrugged off as a manner of speaking.

Format: a numbered agreed-claims section, then a table of disagreements with a column for each side's own wording, the crux, and the predicted outcome of the relevant test, then a short closing section titled where the dispute is verbal.

Do not give me a bibliography, reading list, or citations; I want the argument, not the literature, and I have no way to check references anyway. And I would rather you push back than comply smoothly: if the framing assumes there is a single fact of the matter about whether a system "really" believes something — an assumption I am not sure survives contact with the intentional stance itself — or if one of my three instruments is rigged toward the skeptics or toward the realists, say so plainly instead of drafting around it.
