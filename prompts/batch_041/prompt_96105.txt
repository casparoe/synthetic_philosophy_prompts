I am a novelist at work on a book set in a fictional kingdom that borrows heavily from the intellectual world of classical Mithila. The plot turns on a court scribe who must decide whether to record a dying merchant's utterance as testimony. The utterance is grammatically complete, its words are spoken in close succession, its meanings are mutually compatible, and the scribe has no doubt about what the merchant intended to say. Yet the scribe later learns that the merchant was merely repeating a phrase he had heard from a notorious liar and had no personal knowledge of the matter. I want the novel's philosophical climax to hinge on a precise conceptual issue, not a vague worry about trust. In the background, I am drawing on the Nyāya account of testimony as a pramāṇa, and on the four conditions that Nyāya philosophers of language standardly require for a sentence to yield verbal understanding: expectancy, compatibility, contiguity, and speaker's intention. My difficulty is that I need a concrete example of a conceptual issue—a puzzle or tension—that a character can articulate in dialogue, and that arises precisely because those four conditions are all satisfied while the epistemic status of the resulting cognition remains genuinely unsettled. Could you give me an example of such a conceptual issue? Please make it as precise as possible, and feel free to invent a small variation on the scenario if that helps isolate the issue.
