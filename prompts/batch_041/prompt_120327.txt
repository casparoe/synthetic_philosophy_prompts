Every year I promise myself I'll finally fix the animal ethics week in my third-year applied ethics module, and every year it turns into me at the whiteboard drawing supply chains while eighteen students quietly check their phones. So this time I want it to be a game — something where they work out for themselves that if you count deaths per calorie, the beef on their plate comes out looking better than the chicken, and then have to sit with the fact that this result is manufactured entirely by the metric they were handed. Please don't let the point arrive by lecture. I'd rather the facts were spread unevenly around the room — different teams with different data sheets, or different briefs, or both — so that the argument that breaks out is about what to count, not about what's true.

Setting: eighteen third years, ninety minutes, a normal seminar room with chairs we can shove about, no laptops, and I'll bring a one-page data sheet with the figures I want them to use (deaths per hectare at harvest, deaths per animal, how much of the animal is actually eaten, typical lifespans, that sort of thing). I'll fiddle with those numbers before the session, so the design needs to make it obvious where each figure enters the arithmetic, in case I want to flip a ranking on the day. The ideas I want them to collide with, roughly: that counting deaths and counting suffering-years give you different menus; that cattle mostly eat grass and chickens mostly eat crops, so the field-death arithmetic isnt symmetric; that an animal which only exists because we bred it is an odd thing to count as a victim, and that this is also the reasoning that lets bad farms off the hook; that the whole comparison collapses if wild animals' lives are mostly not worth living, or if invertebrates are in the frame; and that preferring to eat whatever killed fewer animals can look like rigour when it's really just the metric you found most convenient. The debrief should prise apart three things they'll definately mush together — which facts are disputed, which metric was chosen, and which moral-status assumptions got smuggled in.

I'd also like the plan to be honest about what the game can't do. My guess is it can't make anyone feel the force of the claim that a wild animal's life may not be worth living, or of the uncertainty about insect sentience, and that I'll have to lecture that bit myself; but tell me if I'm wrong. And tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it — there's a faction in the class from the effective altruism reading group who will try to short-circuit the whole thing in the first five minutes, and I want to know what I'm supposed to do about them. Give it back to me as a JSON object with the fields: title; setting; materials; roles (an array, each with name, what they want, what they know that the others don't, and what they're able to do); rounds (an array, each with the round number, minutes, what happens, and what I'm meant to be doing while it happens); scoring (how teams win, and why the payoff scheme pushes them towards incompatible menus); debrief (an array, each with the moment in the activity, the concept it maps onto, and the question to put to the room); failure modes (an array, each with what goes wrong and how to handle it); and cannot reproduce (a list of the things the activity can't deliver, which I'll have to supply myself).
