I did a logic-heavy CS degree years ago and these days I work on data platforms, the kind of job where you're constantly merging tables from three teams who disagree with each other, so I spend a lot of time with Belnap's four-valued logic — the setup with four values instead of two, true, false, both, neither, where both is what you get when two sources flatly contradict each other and neither is what you get when no source has anything to say, and the whole appeal is that you don't have to pretend the contradiction isn't sitting there in the table. Two orderings, one for truth and one for how much information you've got, designated values are the true one and the both one, negation flips true and false and leaves the other two alone, and the merge is just a lattice join. That's my home turf and I'm fluent in it, though I dont read Sanskrit and everything I know about Indian philosophy is secondhand.

The reason I'm asking is that my partner is doing an MA in South Asian religions and has been reading me bits of the Jain stuff on anekantavada, many-sidedness, and I got weirdly hung up on the formal part, syadvada, conditioned predication. The way I understand it: you never just assert that a thing is thus, you assert it under a syat, a somehow or in-some-respect, and then the tradition says there are exactly seven things you can say about any object once you're doing that. Roughly: somehow it is; somehow it isn't; somehow it both is and isn't; somehow it's inexpressible; somehow it is and is inexpressible; somehow it isn't and is inexpressible; and somehow it is, isn't, and is inexpressible. Seven and no eighth, supposedly exhaustive. It looks so much like my four values that I keep wanting to say fine, both is slot three, neither is slot four, done — except they obviously don't line up, because my both is generated by a conflict between two sources whereas their both is supposed to hold of one object at one time in one respect, and then there's that inexpressible slot that I have no idea what to do with, and the whole standpoint business (naya, is that the word?) sitting underneath it that I haven't even tried to fit.

So what I want, and I care much more about the second and third parts than the first: (a) restate the seven-slot scheme in my vocabulary as faithfully as you can manage — the four values, the two orderings, the designated values, what a merge does, whatever else in the lattice seems relevant — and say plainly which of the seven come across clean. (b) Then the residue report. What in the Jain scheme has no counterpart at all in what I do (I'd guess the standpoint theory, and the fact that the whole apparatus seems to be aimed at loosening your grip on your own opinion rather than at storing information, but tell me)? What only comes across with distortion (my suspicion is the inexpressible slot — I genuinely can't tell whether it's meant to be my neither, or a remark about language rather than a truth value, or a gesture at the omniscient kevala knowledge that supposedly can't be put into words at all)? And going the other way, what does my four-valued setup let me say that the seven-slot scheme forbids — an unqualified assertion, or treating both as a fact about the world rather than about whose sources you're holding? (c) Last thing, and this is the bit I actually lie awake over: is that residue a defect in my vocabulary, or a sign that the Jain thing is built for a different job than assigning truth values? And why exactly seven slots and not four or nine — does anything in the four-valued lattice generate that closure, or is the number coming from somewhere else entirely?
