My colleague has spent several years on a question I find hard to think about cleanly: whether one should bear a cost for the sake of agents one can never causally affect, when the only thing linking one's choice to theirs is the way both parties reason. Her published work records verdicts on a series of cases, and before she writes up the next one I would like an independent prediction of what she will say about a case of my own devising, together with a candid account of how much of that prediction her record actually forces and how much I am reading into it.

Her verdicts so far, as she states them:

1. Two agents are exact duplicates of one another, the same decision procedure realized twice, sealed in separate rooms with no channel of influence between them and no predictor observing either. Each faces a one-shot prisoner's dilemma in which defection pays better whatever the other does, and the payoff to defection is large. She holds that each should cooperate, and that this is not a mistake.

2. A traveller is stranded in a remote place; a driver will stop for her only if he is confident she will pay him on arrival, and he reads dispositions well. Once she is in the car he has no way to punish her. She holds that the traveller should decide to pay, and pay.

3. The same traveller, except that the driver's willingness to stop turns on a coin toss rather than on any prediction of her. She holds that the traveller should take the ride and keep the money.

4. A counterpart that duplicates her reasoning, memory and values exactly, except that in this instance its choice is fixed by a coin it tosses before either of them acts. She holds that she should defect, although the counterpart is in every other respect her and will cooperate half the time.

5. A counterpart of a wholly different kind, which defects against cooperators as a matter of settled policy, and whose choice is independent of hers in every respect she can identify. She holds that she should defect.

The case I want predicted is this. She is one of a very large number of agents, on the order of ten to the fortieth, who will each at some point face a choice of the same anonymous shape: bear a small private cost of one unit that confers a benefit of a thousand units on some other member of the population, or take the smaller but surer gain of defecting. No agent can identify, influence or predict any other; none of them runs her decision procedure, and none is a duplicate of her. What is true is that they all belong to a broad class of reasoners who tend to converge on the same verdict for dilemmas of the same shape, so that her bearing the cost is weak evidence, on the order of one part in ten to the thirty-fifth, that any given other agent bears it too. Multiplied across a population that size, that shift corresponds to something like a hundred thousand further agents bearing their costs, each conferring a thousand units of benefit, against a cost to her of one unit.

What I would like from you: the candidate rules that fit her five verdicts and survive them, stated in your own terms rather than under the name of any school; what each of those rules predicts about the large-world case; your best guess at her verdict, with the reasons for preferring one rule over the others; and the further case, or cases, that would most cleanly separate whatever survives. If two rules agree here but come apart elsewhere, say where. And if her record genuinely underdetermines the new case, say so plainly, and say which principle she would be adopting with each verdict.
