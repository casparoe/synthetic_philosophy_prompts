I am preparing a handout for a graduate seminar on rationality. I need a decision tree: a flowchart whose nodes are questions and whose leaves are positions, taking a student from a commitment they can check to a named view about what a rational agent should do in a one-shot Prisoner's Dilemma against a counterpart who is a mirror of them. The entry point is a student who accepts the payoff structure, accepts that neither player can communicate, commit, or enforce anything, and is willing to grant that the two players' choices may nevertheless be dependent on one another.

The distinctions I care about, which the tree must express, are these. First, the route of the dependence: your choice causing theirs, the two of you sharing a cause (same designer, same training run, same selection process), your two procedures being logically or algorithmically equivalent, or your counterpart running your procedure as a subroutine. Second, whether that dependence is taken to be a reason with normative force or merely information to be exploited, which is where accounts that cooperate because of any correlation come apart from accounts that cooperate only when the correlation is routed through the other player's decision procedure. Third, the unit to which the demand is addressed: the act, the policy or procedure, or the standing disposition or character. Fourth, whether the relevant agent is the individual or the pair, with the question being what we should do rather than what each of us should do. Fifth, whether the cooperative verdict survives imperfect similarity, and if so whether it degrades gradually or collapses at a threshold.

Every node must be phrased as a question a student could answer without already knowing where it leads, and each answer branch should be labeled with the concrete case or claim that makes it vivid. The tree must be able to handle at least these cases: exact duplicates; near-duplicates differing by one line of code or by a random seed; players with no causal connection whose choices are correlated only because both were produced by the same process; a counterpart who simulates your procedure and then does what it predicts; a case where the correlation between your act and a good or bad outcome does not run through your decision procedure at all; two agents that can never interact causally but can each predict the other's policy perfectly in advance; and a population of many agents all running your procedure, each in a pairwise dilemma with the rest.
