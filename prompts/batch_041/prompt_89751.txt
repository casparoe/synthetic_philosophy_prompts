I have been reading rationalist blogs and their comment threads for close to two decades now, long enough to have watched the same argument about reductionism play out a dozen times and long enough to have a lingering affection for Dennett's picture of cranes and skyhooks alongside a suspicion that the picture does less work than its admirers think. Last week our reading group produced an unusually careful version of that argument, and I would like it adjudicated rather than summarized.

The occasion was a short essay by one of our members, Devon, who argued that the felt sense of a piece of reasoning being valid is nothing over and above the phenomenology of fluent processing: the "click" of an argument landing on you is the subjective correlate of a low-effort, high-fluency computation, and there is no further fact to explain. Two other members, Hana and Owen, spent the following hour arguing about whether calling that greedy reductionism is a substantive criticism or a dressed-up complaint about tone. Dennett's formulation in Darwin's Dangerous Idea, which both of them quoted at each other, is that "Good reductionists suppose that all Design can be explained without skyhooks; greedy reductionists suppose it can all be explained without cranes." I found myself agreeing with each of them in turn, which usually means I am the wrong person to referee.

Their exchange went roughly as follows.

(1) Hana: Devon's claim is greedy reductionism. He does not derive the phenomenon, he dissolves it. The cranes he skips are the intermediate levels — the psychophysics of fluency, the developmental story, the cultural transmission of norms of argument — and he jumps straight from "there is a fluency signal" to "that is all there is to the feeling of validity." The tell for this kind of explanation is that it always predicts the phenomenon should be less remarkable than it seems, and then never checks whether the predictions it generates are actually borne out.

(2) Owen: That cannot be a distinct error. Watch what your test does: when a reduction succeeds you call it a crane, when it fails you call it greedy, but "succeeds" and "fails" are doing all the work, so the real charge is just that the explanation is wrong or incomplete — something we could have said without the metaphor. The metaphor is also asymmetric. Nobody ever has to prove a reduction greedy; the label simply shifts the burden onto the reducer to supply more, and since more intermediate levels can always be demanded, no reduction can ever discharge it. On that reading the term is itself a skyhook for critics — a way to claim the high ground without naming the missing step.

(3) Hana: You are conflating incomplete with greedy. An incomplete reduction is a research program; a greedy one spends the phenomenon. And there is an independent test: greedy reductions have to help themselves to the very thing they claim to reduce. Devon needs "fluency" to be evaluable as fluency at getting the inference right, which presupposes the notion of validity that he said was nothing over and above fluency. That is circularity, and circularity can be demonstrated without any infinite regress of demands for further levels.

(4) Owen: Then your real charge is "circular" — again, a defect statable without cranes and skyhooks. And the circle is not obviously there: he can define fluency over the computational process without presupposing validity, and if he cannot, that is a formal failure, not a special variety of reductionism. Note also where the term comes from. It was introduced by a reductionist partly to distinguish his own book from the accusations of explaining consciousness away — it is a shield for a reductionist, not a weapon against reductionism in general, and the older name for the same idea, "nothing-buttery," was at least honest about being a complaint. A concept that lets each author say "not me, that other fellow is the greedy one" is a boundary-drawing device, and boundary-drawing devices tend to be about status rather than truth.

(5) Hana: Fine — let us make it diagnosable rather than merely assertable. An explanation is greedy when its work is front-loaded: it earns its apparent success by choosing a description of the target that already contains the explanation. Devon picks "the feeling of validity" as his target and offers "the feeling of fluency" as the explanation, but he has defined the target as the feeling you get when processing is easy. That is not an explanation, it is a renaming. Genuine crane-building re-describes the target in terms the lower level does not already contain. So the test is checkable: ask whether the lower-level story could have been told, in the same words, about a case in which the higher-level phenomenon is absent, and would still have predicted its presence.

(6) Owen: Your relabeling test detects vacuity, not greediness — it catches empty theories of every stripe, reductionist or not. And notice where it leaves your Dennett: on your account, greedy reductionism turns out to be nothing but explanatory vacuity. Which is precisely my point. The crane-and-skyhook machinery is an evocative picture that keeps being recruited to do work done by ordinary criteria — circularity, vacuity, underdetermination — and its distinctive contribution is a vibe, the vibe of "you are being too quick." And vibes are how we end up calling unwelcome explanations greedy and welcome ones crane-building.

What I want from you is not a general essay on reductionism but a referee's report. Rate each of the six moves from clearly mistaken to clearly right, with a brief justification for each, and be explicit where a move is right about one thing and wrong about another — Hana's criterion shifts between (1), (3), and (5), and I want to know whether that is a repair or a retreat. Then answer three questions. First, is either of them right that "greedy reductionism" names a distinct error, or is it, as Owen claims, ordinary explanatory failure plus a picture, and does anything turn on which it is? Second, is Devon's specific claim greedy in whatever sense survives the exchange, and what would have to be true of his argument for the answer to come out the other way? Third, what is each of them missing that the other is not seeing? Close with a bottom line on who had the better of the exchange overall, and how confident you are.
