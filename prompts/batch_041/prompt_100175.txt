I run planning for a mid-sized city — zoning, transit, housing, the usual — and I keep hitting the same wall. Some of what lands on my desk is genuinely technical (will this rezoning actually produce the units we're claiming, does this interchange meet the safety spec) and some is a fight about what the city should be, wearing a lab coat and pretending to be technical. My team signs off on maybe fifteen of these a month and none of us are philosophers.

A friend who teaches philosophy keeps telling me Carnap — the Vienna Circle guy — has a political/planning side nobody reads, and that it's wierdly on point for exactly this. The three things she handed me: first, explication — you take a mushy everyday word, decide what job you actually need it to do, then define a sharp replacement, and you're judged by whether the sharp version does the work, not by whether it matches the mushy original. Second, the split between questions internal to a framework (given our rules, does this follow?) and external questions about which framework to adopt in the first place (are we a car city or a transit city?), which she says he thought wasn't a factual question in the same way. Third, tolerance — the idea that in logic there are no moral constraints on which framework you pick; you choose by what it's for, not by which one is true. (She also muttered something about Neurath being the actual planning guy in that circle and the whole scientific-worldview project being in the service of rationalizing society, but I don't need the history, I need the procedure.)

Turn that into a procedure my office can actually run. I want the questions in order, what counts as a good enough answer to each, where the procedure stops with a no, where it deliberately hands off to the elected officials or to a public process because the question is external and not ours to settle, and where it's a value call dressed up as a technical step. It has to be something we can run in an afternoon before a hearing, and it should say what to do when we can't get a number. Then run it on three proposals: converting a big downtown surface parking lot into dense housing; widening a highway to cut commute times; and putting license-plate readers on the arterials. I especially want the one where the procedure tells me something I don't want to hear — I have a feeling at least one of these comes back as not our call to make, and I'd rather hear that from you than from a councilmember.

Flag the places the procedure will misfire — where explication smuggles in a political choice, where internal and external won't cleanly seperate — and say plainly what the procedure can't do. And give me a one-page version I can hand to the staff.
