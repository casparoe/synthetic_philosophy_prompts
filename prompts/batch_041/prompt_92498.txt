Consider a sequence of decision-makers facing a common binary question — which of two states of the world obtains — who act in a fixed order. Each shares the same prior, cares only about being correct, receives a private signal that is informative but never decisive, and, before acting, observes the actions but not the signals of everyone who went before. All are rational expected-utility maximizers, and this is common knowledge. Call a decision-maker cascaded if the action he takes is the one he would have taken regardless of which private signal he had received; in the usual analysis, once the first few actions agree, everyone thereafter is cascaded, and the private signals of the rest leave no trace in the public record.

I want a worked example, not a survey. Construct a concrete instance — fill in the two states, the signal accuracies, and the step-by-step reasoning of the first several decision-makers — that exposes a conceptual issue about the notion of a cascade itself. Your example should bring out that being cascaded is a fact about what a person would have done under signals he did not receive, and so need not leave any mark on the actions he did take; and it should show that a cascade built out of nothing but correct Bayesian reasoning can be indistinguishable, from the record of actions alone, from a run of mere imitation. Then assess, briefly and separately, two claims often made about such sequences: that a cascade is a failure of collective information aggregation, and that an observer can diagnose one by watching the decisions. For each claim, say which parts your example supports and which it leaves untouched, and be explicit about where the example does no work.
