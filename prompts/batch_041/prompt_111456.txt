I retired from a career in analytical chemistry about five years ago and have been sitting in on philosophy seminars ever since. Last week a few of us got stuck on the absent-minded driver problem: you're driving home, there are two intersections, you can't tell which one you're at when you reach it, turning off at the first pays 0, turning off at the second pays 4, and going straight past the second pays 0 (or maybe crashes). The standard puzzle is that a policy of always going straight gets you nothing, always turning gets you nothing if you're at the first, and the ex ante best policy seems to be to randomize—continue with probability one-half, turn with probability one-half—which feels like it can't be right because at any given intersection you either are at the first or the second. I've come around to a view I'd like you to attack: the whole thing is an artifact of treating the driver's two possible locations as two separate decision problems. Once you see the driver as choosing a policy in advance, under a known amnesia structure, the answer is just the policy that maximizes expected utility from the ex ante standpoint, and randomizing 50/50 is that policy; there's no remaining paradox, and any intuition that says "just go straight and turn at the second" is smuggling in information the driver doesn't have.

For instance, does the view make different predictions than evidential or causal decision theory in some variant, like one where you can leave a note to yourself that survives with some probability, or where there are three intersections and the memory wipe is probabilistic? Which of those would a defender of my policy-choice line have to concede as a real problem, and which could they dismiss as changing the subject? What I'd personally find most persuasive is a variant where the policy-choice answer gives a result that is intuitively absurd even after I've fully bought the framing.
