THE QUESTION

Q3 (25 pts). In 2016 ProPublica reported that a recidivism instrument used in courtrooms across the US (COMPAS, made by a company then called Northpointe) made mistakes that fell unequally across racial groups; the company replied that its test was as accurate for one group as the other. Assume the tool outputs a risk score and the court uses a single cut-off to flag defendants "high risk."

Define three properties such a tool might have:

(A) Predictive parity: among defendants flagged high risk, the proportion who actually reoffend is the same for black and for white defendants.
(B) Equal false positive rates: among defendants who do not reoffend, the proportion wrongly flagged high risk is the same in both groups.
(C) Equal false negative rates: among defendants who do reoffend, the proportion wrongly not flagged is the same in both groups.

(a) Explain why (A), (B) and (C) cannot all hold at once when the two groups' base rates of reoffending differ, and say how many of the three a tool can satisfy at most.
(b) A colleague says the real culprit is that the model uses race, or proxies for race, through correlated features; strip those out and the three properties become jointly satisfiable. Is that right? Explain.
(c) A second colleague proposes a different criterion, "error-ratio parity": require that the ratio of the false positive rate to the false negative rate be the same in both groups, so that the tool makes the two kinds of mistake in the same proportion for everyone. Assess this as a fairness criterion, and say whether adopting it gets around the problem you identified in (a).

STUDENT'S ANSWER

ProPublica and the company were both reporting real things about the same instrument, and they weren't in conflict. ProPublica was measuring (B) and (C): of the defendants who didn't go on to be arrested again, a much larger share of black defendants had been flagged high risk, and of those who did go on to be arrested again, a smaller share of white defendants had been flagged. The company was measuring (A): among the people actually flagged, the share who went on to reoffend was about the same in both groups. So the score was reasonably well calibrated in that sense, and the two reports can both be true.

(a) The three properties are linked through the base rate. At a fixed cut-off, let p be the base rate and let FPR and FNR be the two error rates. Then

PPV = p(1 − FNR) / [p(1 − FNR) + (1 − p)FPR]

so once FPR and FNR are pinned down, PPV is a function of p alone. Take p = 0.4 for one group and p = 0.2 for the other, with FPR = FNR = 0.3 for both. First group: 0.28/(0.28 + 0.18) = 0.61. Second: 0.14/(0.14 + 0.24) = 0.37. Identical error rates, visibly different predictive parity. So a tool can have any two of (A), (B), (C) but never all three once the base rates differ, and which one you give up is a policy choice rather than a technical one. If I had to choose I'd keep (A), because a judge who can't interpret what a "high risk" flag means can't use the tool at all, and unequal error rates are a price we should be willing to pay for that.

(b) Yes, and I think this is the practical upshot of the theorem. The problem isn't the score's accuracy; it's that the feature set lets the score stand in for race — prior arrests, neighbourhood, employment history, that sort of thing. Once you drop race and the features carrying the most of it, the score is genuinely group-blind, and the three properties come back into alignment, at worst with small residual gaps from sampling noise. That's why so much of the practical literature is about auditing features and removing proxies.

(c) Error-ratio parity is a sensible proposal, because it takes seriously that the two mistakes aren't equally costly — a false positive is someone sitting in jail before trial, a false negative is a warning that never got issued — so rather than insisting the error rates match one by one, it asks only that the tool strike the same balance between them for both groups. It's also weaker than (A)–(C), so it's compatible with failing all three, which is what we need after (a). And since it holds the proportion of the two mistake-types fixed across groups, it effectively equalizes the total harm the tool does to each group: the group that gets flagged more often is also the group whose flags are wrong less often, so it evens out. Adopting it does dissolve the impossibility in (a), since the impossibility was only ever about demanding all three parity conditions at once.

So my overall answer is that the 2016 dispute was a disagreement about which fairness property to prioritize, not a disagreement about facts, and the lesson is that fairness has to be specified politically before it can be measured technically.
