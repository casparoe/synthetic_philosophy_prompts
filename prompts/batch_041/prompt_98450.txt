Fine. But here's whats been nagging me. Accuracy is always accuracy on a particular set of propositions; you score a credence function against an algebra. You could be maximally accurate and perfectly coherent while your entire credal life is spent on gerrymandered disjunctions and arbitrary predicates, never touching anything that matters. Meanwhile someone with just a few credences, but about the things that actually carve nature at the joints, looks epistemically better — and nothing in the framework seems to say so. There's a related smell: since accuracy on a proposition that's logically entailed by others comes for free, two agents who agree on all the coarse-grained facts can differ in accuracy purely because one has a finer-grained credal agenda, which makes me wonder whether that's an epistemic difference at all or just a bookkeeping one.

So my question is roughly this: is that a genuine hole in accuracy-first epistemology, or can the framework absorb it without giving up accuracy's status as the sole fundamental epistemic value? And if it can't, what's the least disruptive patch — say, does relativizing accuracy to a 'question' or a partition fix it, and does that break the standard vindications of probabilism?
