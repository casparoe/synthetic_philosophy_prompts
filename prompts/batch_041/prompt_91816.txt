I am preparing a ninety-minute session on vagueness for second-year philosophy students who have had a unit of propositional logic and know what bivalence is, and I want a plan for the explanation rather than the explanation itself. Set out the order in which the material must be introduced — what has to be in place before epistemicism and supervaluationism can appear as the two serious escape routes from the sorites rather than as two entries in a list of positions, and what depends on what.

The carrying example must not be grains of sand, and I would rather avoid baldness and height, which they have all met. I want to build the session on the geologists' grain-size vocabulary, where the boundaries are stipulated rather than discovered: on the Udden–Wentworth scheme a boulder is anything over 256 mm and a cobble is 64–256 mm, whereas ISO 14688-1:2017 instead makes a boulder 200–630 mm and reserves a further class for anything larger. Tell me whether this can carry the whole session — what it gives me that the sand example does not, and what it distorts. My worry is that because these lines are fixed by committee, the example may quietly teach that boulder is a precise term and so make epistemicism look easier than it is.

Then the misconceptions, in the order the students will form them and the point in the sequence where each should be headed off. The ones I anticipate: that supervaluationism amounts to the claim that there is no fact of the matter; that it introduces a third truth value; that if no particular sorites premise is false then the universally quantified tolerance principle must be true; that epistemicism's sharp boundary is merely something better instruments could reveal; and that higher-order vagueness is a technicality rather than a problem for both views.

Finish with one question I can put at the end that would show whether they have understood what the two views disagree about, and a short list of what I should openly refuse to cover in the time available.
