I cover science and ideas for a general-readership magazine, and I'm preparing a long feature on whether the sciences really form a ladder — whether chemistry, biology, psychology and economics are ultimately "nothing but" their lower-level constituents, or whether the higher rungs have a standing of their own — together with an interactive flowchart that readers can work through to discover where they actually stand. The problem I keep running into is that the familiar labels have gone slack: readers cheerfully call themselves physicalists and emergentists at once, or reductionists and pluralists, because none of the terms forces a definite commitment on the cases that matter. So I don't want the flowchart organized around the labels at all. I want it built from the bottom up, from the logical space of answers rather than from a list of schools: each branching node should be a question a reader can answer about a concrete case without already knowing which camp it drops them into, and each leaf should be a position that actually survives once the combinations of answers are worked out, named where a standard name exists and marked "unnamed" where it doesn't.

The distinctions I want the tree to turn on are these, roughly in the order I'd branch them. First, when two descriptions of the same system differ in grain — a gas as pressure and temperature versus as molecules, a market as supply and demand versus as individual transactions, a flock of starlings as a shape versus as so many birds, a glider in the Game of Life as a moving object versus as cells obeying rules — is one description more fundamental than the other, or do they simply answer different questions? Second, does the higher-level description ever earn its keep by explaining or predicting something the lower-level one cannot even state, and if so does that show the higher-level kinds are real or only useful? Third, is there downward causation — do higher-level features make a difference to lower-level outcomes over and above what the parts already do? And fourth, a distinction I keep wanting to draw in the piece but cannot find a settled name for, so please treat it as the working vocabulary: the difference between a level being autonomous (its generalizations cannot be derived from the level below) and a level being independent (its entities could exist even if the lower-level ones did not). I suspect that many readers who call themselves non-reductive physicalists are really asserting autonomy while denying independence, and I want the tree to make that split explicit rather than letting it hide inside the word "emergence."

Please give me the instrument itself: first the branching questions in order, phrased so each can be answered on the concrete cases rather than in the abstract; then the leaves, with one sentence each on the position and its most awkward commitment; then a note on which combinations of answers turn out to be unoccupied, and for each whether that is because the combination is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored; and finally any pairs of leaves your tree genuinely cannot separate, flagged rather than papered over. Aim for something a reader can finish in a few minutes, so no more than about eight questions and a dozen leaves, and keep every node answerable by someone who has never heard of any of the schools it eventually sorts them into.
