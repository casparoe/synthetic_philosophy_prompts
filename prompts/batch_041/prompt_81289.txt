I'm running a ninety-minute session of our environmental ethics reading group on a question the syllabus states flatly and the room has never taken seriously: whether destroying habitat is actually bad for wild animals — not bad for biodiversity, not bad for ecosystems, but bad for the animals themselves, counted one at a time. The attendees are mostly ecologists with two philosophers mixed in, and they will arrive with the conservationist's reflex — habitat lost, welfare lost — which is exactly the reflex I need them to suspend long enough for the question to look genuinely open. I don't want you to argue the topic for me; I want a plan for how to teach it. Assume the audience already grants that animals can be harmed and that suffering matters, but has never entertained the thought that a wild animal's life might on balance go badly, and has no experience with the population-biology style of accounting that the question seems to demand.

The device I've been using to pry it open is a ledger, and I'd like the plan built so that the audience arrives at it rather than being handed it at the door. The ledger has two columns — one hectare of old-growth forest and one hectare of cattle pasture — and in each column I refuse to count the animals alive on some arbitrary day. Instead I count every animal that lives and dies over a five-year window: every nestling, every parasitoid, every mayfly, every rodent taken by an owl. Then I ask what fraction of those lives contained more suffering than satisfaction, and whether the forest really wins once you stop counting standing bodies and start counting lived lives. My sense is that the enormous turnover in the forest is the hinge of the whole session, since most of those lives end by starvation, exposure, or being eaten alive, and the ecologists' intuition that "more animals" means "more welfare" quietly assumes the opposite. But I'm not sure the ledger is the right thing to build the session around — it may be too bloodless to carry the room — so I want you to tell me whether it should be the carrying case or whether something smaller and more visceral should do that work, and what the carrier has to have and has to lack for the payoff to land.

Give me the plan in five named sections, in this order. First, "Sequence": the order in which the components have to be introduced, with a sentence on what each step depends on and why the conclusion would be unearned if the steps were reordered — spend roughly a third of your answer here, and tell me where the ledger enters the sequence and why it can't enter earlier. Second, "The carrying case": your verdict on the ledger, and if it should not carry the session, what should, described concretely enough that I could draw it on a whiteboard, plus what that case must include and what it must deliberately omit. Third, "Misconceptions": the wrong turns this audience will predictably take — my guess is the one that bites hardest is "fewer animals simply means less suffering, full stop," but I expect several others — each stated as the audience would actually phrase it, with the exact point in the sequence where I should head it off and what the move is. Fourth, "The check": one question I can put to them in the last ten minutes that would show whether they've understood the shape of the question rather than merely been made uncomfortable by it, and what a good answer to it would sound like as opposed to a bad one. Fifth, "What to leave out": the threads I should openly refuse to settle at this level — and say so out loud — because pulling on them would either require empirical claims I can't support in the room or drag us into disputes that presuppose the very thing we're trying to establish. Please write in American English, and don't send me reading recommendations; the plan itself is what I need.
