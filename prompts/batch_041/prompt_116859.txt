For the last few months a reading group I'm in has been working through the cluster of arguments meant to show that a rational agent's preferences must be complete and transitive, and that such an agent must choose in accordance with expected utility — the money-pump constructions, the representation theorems that take completeness and transitivity, together with independence and continuity, as axioms, and the more recent revival of all this in the literature on whether a sufficiently capable artificial agent is bound to behave as if it were maximizing a utility function. We keep arriving at the same standoff, and I'd like a diagnosis of it rather than another summary of the positions. The case for the arguments is a piece of means-end reasoning: if you would pay to escape a position you could have avoided for free, or if your choices cycle so that you can be led to hand over a good and then pay to get it back, then by your own lights you are doing worse than you need to, and on the austere view of practical reason that is the only kind of irrationality there is to be had — the theorems are not axioms smuggled in as norms but consequences of taking your own ends seriously. The case against is a loose coalition: that the pump only bites against an agent whose choices are determinate and stable enough to be exploited, so an agent with genuinely incomplete preferences, who simply declines to rank two options, need not be pumpable at all; that a resolute or forward-looking chooser can defuse the sequence without revising any preference; that the whole construction slides from the synchronic demand for an ordering to a diachronic story about a series of trades, and so shows at most that a certain pattern of behavior is costly, not that incoherence is irrational; and that expected-utility maximization is defined only relative to a partition of states that the theory itself does not supply, so the norm falls silent exactly where the critics press.

My hypothesis — and I would like it tested rather than endorsed — is that the two sides are not really disputing the mathematics, which they both accept, but something upstream: whether a norm of practical rationality answers to a standard of internal coherence or to a standard of getting what you want; whether "preference" names a determinate mental state or a pattern discerned in choice; and whether the argument is meant as a theorem about agents who already have a complete ordering or as a constraint bearing on agents who may not. If that is right, which of those upstream questions is doing the real work, and is any of it more than bedrock — a formal result, a cleaner counterexample, or a distinction that would let one side show the other is answering a different question under a shared name? If it is not right, tell me where the genuine substantive disagreement lies — about the possibility of incomplete preferences, about the small-world idealization, or about whether the pump actually functions in the cases the critics care about — and what would count as progress on it, or why none should be expected.
