Next month I'm giving a colloquium in a physics department, defending a deflationary line on Wigner's puzzle about the unreasonable effectiveness of mathematics: that the effectiveness is largely an artifact of a selection effect on our mathematical library, since we retain, teach, and cross-link the branches of mathematics that have proved applicable while the vast majority that never find a use quietly drops out of view, so that the genuine residue of surprise is much narrower than usually claimed — essentially the wholesale transfer of a single formal structure between two domains that had no causal contact. The audience is mostly condensed-matter and particle physicists who treat the puzzle as a live and important mystery and are allergic to being told it is a non-problem, plus two mathematicians: a logician who suspects my selection story is either unfalsifiable or simply false, and an applied mathematician who thinks I underestimate how often mathematics with no apparent use later turns out to matter. Prepare my Q&A sheet: the objections I will actually get from this room, phrased in their vocabulary and roughly in the order they will arrive, ordered by how much damage each would do; for each, a two-minute reply, what I should concede up front, and where the reply is soft. Include the one question I am hoping nobody asks. Throughout, keep what is settled separate from what is genuinely contested among people who work on this, and do not hand me a contested position dressed up as the consensus.
