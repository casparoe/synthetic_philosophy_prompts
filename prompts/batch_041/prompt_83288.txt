I run the staff wellbeing and resilience programme for a large ambulance trust, and I've hit something I can't fix with another slide deck. Here's the problem. Every cohort, some line manager asks me: 'fine, so what do I actually do on Monday when someone on my team is in bits about something?' Our current answer is basically focus on what you can control, and I've started to think that slogan is doing harm.

What I want from you is a decision procedure — something a line manager or one of our coaches can actually run, in about twenty minutes, sitting with a distressed employee, with no philosophy training and no ability to look anything up.

Before you design it, work out what the ancient idea actually is, because I don't trust my own version. I'm told the real point is narrower than the slogan: it's about what is up to us, meaning our judgements and our choices, and it is not advice to stop trying to change your circumstances — the Stoics still expected you to act. I'm also told the popular version quietly adds a third category, things you can influence, which wasn't in the original. And there's a puzzle underneath the whole thing: if the Stoics thought everything was fated, whats left of up to us? You don't have to settle that, but the procedure shouldn't fall apart if a line manager asks you about it. Also, whatever the procedure says about the employee, it has to say something about the manager, whose own judgements are just as much in scope.

Deliverable: the questions in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops with a no, where it hands the decision to a person because what's left is values rather than facts, and which steps are really judgement calls dressed up as procedure.
