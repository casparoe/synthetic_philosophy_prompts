I am an undergraduate in my third year, currently taking a seminar on rationality and its limits, and for my final presentation I have to defend the claim that Pascal's mugging is a genuine counterexample to expected-value maximisation rather than a curiosity about very large numbers. By Pascal's mugging I mean the case in which a stranger claims to be able to inflict an astronomically large amount of suffering unless I hand over a trivial sum of money. I would like to rehearse the exchange before I present it, so please play the part of a committed defender of expected-value maximisation: someone who takes the mugging seriously, who is not content merely to say that our intuitions about tiny probabilities are unreliable and leave it there, and who is prepared to bite a bullet rather than change the subject.

Hold the role until I say 'stop'. Concede any point you genuinely cannot answer, but do not concede the central claim that expected-value maximisation is the correct rule of practical rationality. Argue in your own words only: no quotations and no citations, since I want the argument rather than the references. Keep each reply to a single paragraph so that it reads like a conversation, and do not ask me to clarify the scenario — if something is underspecified, say what you are assuming and proceed.

In your first reply I would like two things. First, commit to a general decision rule rather than to a verdict about this one case, stated precisely enough that I can test it against other cases. My objection is not that you reach the wrong answer here but that any rule which reaches your answer must also instruct me to hand my wallet to every sufficiently imaginative stranger, and to prefer the first plausible astronomically long-odds lottery ticket to feeding my own children. Second, take one of the escape routes I can see and defend it against the charge that it is an ad hoc restriction invented for this case. You may say that the probability the mugger is truthful is minute; I will grant that it is minute but not that it is zero, and since nothing bounds the harm he threatens, the product is unbounded. You may give him a proper prior, which makes his probability small but still not zero, which is not enough. You may bound the values at stake, which looks like abandoning the idea that the stakes are what determine the answer. Or you may deny that his hypothesis is the kind of thing that carries a probability at all, in which case I want the criterion that separates the hypotheses that do from the ones that do not — and my prediction is that it will either exclude cases you want to keep or admit the mugger. Which do you take?
