You are a philosopher whose specialty is the ethics of paternalism, choice architecture, and the line between persuasion and manipulation. I want an argued answer, not a literature survey—don't cite anyone, just reason. Work carefully: begin with a short brainstorm in which you lay out the candidate dimensions along which the cases below might differ (who the intervener is, what the mechanism is, whose ends are served, whether the intervention would survive disclosure), say which of those dimensions you expect to do real work and which you expect to be red herrings, and then commit to a position.

Here is what I actually think, case by case, going on gut:

1. An employer auto-enrols new staff into a pension, with a one-click opt-out. Fine.
2. A supermarket places candy at child eye level beside the checkout. Not fine.
3. A government requires graphic health images to cover half of a cigarette packet. Fine.
4. A government requires graphic health images to cover half of a soda advertisement. Not fine. I flag this one because I cannot say offhand what separates it from 3—I don't think it's addiction, and I don't think it's children.
5. A doctor gives a patient a saline injection for chronic pain and tells her it is a genuine analgesic. Not fine.
6. The same doctor gives the same saline and tells her it is a placebo that often works through expectation. Fine.
7. A city rebuilds a dangerous intersection with narrower lanes and a raised crossing, so drivers slow down without being ticketed. Fine.
8. A city builds a footbridge over a busy road and closes the at-grade crossing, adding several hundred metres to pedestrians' journeys. Not fine.
9. A casino tunes its machines to display frequent near-misses. Not fine.
10. A government taxes sugary drinks. Fine.
11. A government bans a food additive that is safe in itself but, on the evidence I accept, contributes to poor health. Not fine.
12. A government requires motorcyclists to wear helmets. Fine.

My own best guess at a principle is this: an intervention is acceptable exactly when it makes it easier for the person to act on the ends she would endorse if she were fully informed and thinking clearly, and unacceptable when it makes it easier for her to act on ends she would disavow under those conditions. A friend offers a rival I will call the disclosure test: an intervention is acceptable exactly when, were the person told afterward precisely how it worked and what it was for, she would endorse having undergone it. I suspect my guess is too permissive in one place and too strict in another, and that the disclosure test flips 4 to acceptable, which I resist but cannot yet justify resisting.

What I want from you:

First, the principle, or a small set of rival principles, that comes closest to capturing the whole set. State each precisely enough that I could apply it to a new case without having to ask you what you meant.

Second, for each clause of your preferred principle, name the case that forces the clause, and say what that clause costs you elsewhere.

Third, tell me which of my twelve verdicts your principle cannot accommodate, and, separately, which verdict the simplest rival principle would force me to abandon. I would rather see the price than have it smoothed over.

Fourth, say which apparent conflicts in my list are real and which dissolve once the right distinction is drawn—I have in mind 1 against 9, 7 against 8, and 11 against 12, but tell me if I have the wrong suspects.

Fifth, apply your principle to a case I have not listed: an intervention that is fully disclosed, serves the person's own endorsed ends, and is carried out by a private firm for its own profit. Give your verdict and say whether it sits comfortably with the rest.
