Ok, I'm drafting a short paper for a reading group and I want comments before I commit to the line. Topic is higher-order evidence and epistemic akrasia — roughly, believing something while also believing that your own evidence doesn't really support it, or that you're probably getting it wrong. Here's the gist, in note form:

The standard setup: there's evidence about the world (first-order stuff) and evidence about how good you are at handling the first-order stuff (higher-order evidence). I take the consensus to be that higher-order evidence can rationally knock your confidence down. The puzzle is whether you can ever rationally *stay put* — believe p while acknowledging your evidence doesn't support p. Call someone who does that akratic.

My move: I want to seperate higher-order evidence into two kinds. Token-level: evidence aimed at *this particular* judgment — e.g. someone tells you the exact calculation you just did is one you always botch. Population-level: evidence that people *with your profile* (same training, same field) are systematically overconfident by some factor, say 20%.

Claim: token-level higher-order evidence rationally defeats the first-order belief, no controversy there. Population-level is different, and here's why: if I discount my first-order credence by 20% because I'm a person of my profile, then by the same logic I should discount my credence in the *study* that told me so, since the researchers share the relevant profile and their estimate is itself a confident judgment. Discount again, and again — regress. My fix: the correction is scoped to token-level intuitive judgments, and a population statistic isn't a token-level intuitive judgment, so the correction never gets to apply to itself. The asymmetry is principled, not ad hoc, because the two kinds of judgment have different error profiles.

(2) Does my population/token distinction really track anything, or can it be collapsed into ordinary first-order vs higher-order evidence? (3) Is the case even epistemic akrasia, or am I changing the subject? (4) Strongest thing I'm missing. And honestly, is this interesting, or is it just restating the old regress with new labels?
