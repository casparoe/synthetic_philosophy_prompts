I have just come out of a long argument with a friend about standing to blame — about whether hypocrisy genuinely costs a person the standing to blame, or whether the whole notion is moralizing decoration. I am not asking who won. I want to know how much of my position is exposed, and where.

My position, as precisely as I can state it. Standing to blame is a genuine normative condition on the act of blaming, not a prudential or rhetorical matter: the point is not that the hypocrite's blame will be ineffective or embarrassing, but that past some threshold it is not properly addressed to anyone at all. Standing, though, is norm-specific and scalar. A person's standing to blame another for a given failing is diminished, not extinguished, in proportion to how closely her own conduct falls short of the very norm she invokes; failings of other kinds leave it untouched. Once the diminution is great enough, the blame becomes something the target may set aside without answering — not because it is false, and not because answering would be imprudent, but because it no longer constitutes the kind of demand that calls for a reply. The case my friend kept returning to was a supervisor, herself careless about the very safety procedure at issue, reprimanding a subordinate for skipping it. He maintained that the subordinate's retort — you are one to talk — is a dodge rather than a defeater, and that whatever is objectionable in the supervisor's conduct, it is not a want of standing.

What I would like from you:

- The cases, distinctions, arguments, or observations that tell against this position, ranked by how much damage they do, with a short explanation of the weight you give each. Include the ones that arise from the view's own commitments, not only the ones an opponent would press.
- For the more serious of these, whether a committed defender of the position would concede that it counts against the view or would dismiss it as question-begging, and whether that dismissal holds up.
- Whether the position predicts anything its rivals do not, such that some case could actually decide between a scalar, norm-specific account and a binary or global one. If nothing could, say so plainly.
- A candid verdict on insulation. The scalar formulation appears to let a defender redescribe any counterexample as a case in which standing was still sufficiently present, or in which the target's disregard was merely rhetorical. Is that a real risk of unfalsifiability, and how much does it matter for a view of this kind?
- What would move me most. The thought I would find hardest to accommodate is that standing may have nothing to do with the blamer's record and everything to do with her relationship to the wrongdoer — whether the reproach is hers to make at all — in which case hypocrisy is doing far less work than I have assigned it. Tell me whether that is the real fault line, and what turns on it.

Reason from the position itself; I do not need citations or a reading list, only the shape of its exposure.
