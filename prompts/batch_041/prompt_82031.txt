For years now I've been reading the rationalist blogs and forums — enough to have absorbed the standard references without ever having sat down with the underlying literature — and there is a knot of ideas around the common prior assumption that I have never been able to pull apart. The folklore as I have it runs roughly as follows. Two agents who begin from a common prior, and who are both rational, cannot agree to disagree; there is a theorem, attributed to Aumann, that settles the matter; and since real people disagree constantly, it follows that in any live dispute at most one party is being rational. Layered on top of that I have picked up three further claims that seem to sit oddly with the first. One is that Bayesians who begin from different priors and receive the same evidence are supposed to converge on the truth over time, so a shared prior is not needed after all. Another is that the common prior assumption is the load-bearing device that makes equilibrium analysis in games of incomplete information tractable, which suggests it is modelling apparatus rather than a fact about rational minds. The third is that the assumption is a straightforwardly empirical claim about actual people, that it is obviously false, and that its falsity is therefore a standing problem for Bayesian decision theory. I have also seen the no-trade theorem cited to explain why speculative trading is impossible, which strikes me as absurd on its face given that markets exist and are busy.

The particular case that has pushed me to ask is this. A colleague and I, both reasonably numerate, both Bayesians in the loose sense, have been arguing about the outcome of a policy decision we both follow closely. We have read much of the same material and come away with materially different credences — his in the high sixties, mine in the low thirties, in percentage terms. I had taken this to be the ordinary condition of two people reading the same evidence differently. But on the version of the Aumann result I have been working with, this is precisely the configuration that cannot obtain between two rational agents who share a prior, and our priors certainly differ in all sorts of ways: different training, different reading, different priors over how institutions behave. I have also read, in a game theory textbook or a blog post about one, that genuine differences in prior belief are supposed to be traceable to differences in information, which would make our dispute a dispute about information after all, and would explain why the theorem applies to us. So I cannot tell whether the divergence between us is what licenses the disagreement or what corrupts it. What I want is a way to determine which of us is the one whose prior is doing the damage: whether there is a principled procedure, something like calibration but applied to priors rather than to forecasts, that would tell me that my low thirties are the product of an indefensible starting point, or whether the honest answer is that I should simply defer to him, or he to me.

Please tell me what the common prior assumption actually asserts, what the agreement theorem actually needs in order to go through, and what follows for a disagreement like ours. If my framing has imported something these results do not say, I would much rather hear that than have the question answered on its own terms, and I would like the same treatment for the convergence claim and the no-trade theorem, since I suspect they have been doing rhetorical work in my head that they cannot support. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence and then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restatement of my question; and say how confident you are in what you claim, singling out the point you are least sure of.
