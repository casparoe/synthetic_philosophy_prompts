Last night I spent nearly three hours arguing with a close friend about how the foundation I help run should spend its money, and I came away convinced that she is wrong for reasons I could not state cleanly at the time. She is an egalitarian about distributions: she holds that a grant round is better the narrower the gap it leaves between the best-off and the worst-off people it reaches, and she will cheerfully accept a proposal that leaves the worst off no better off at all, provided it also drags the best off down, because the spread has closed. I think that is indefensible. An outcome that helps no one is not made better by hurting someone. What I want in its place is the priority view: what matters is how much a grant raises people's wellbeing, with a gain to someone worse off counting for more than the same gain to someone better off.

I sit on the allocation committee of a mid-sized family foundation. We distribute roughly twelve million dollars a year in health, education, and rural-livelihood grants, in two rounds, with fifteen to twenty proposals per round. The committee is four trustees plus me, none of us has any training in philosophy or economics, and we have an afternoon per round. We have no common metric across the three program areas, and we are routinely uncertain about what would have happened to a beneficiary group without the grant. Our board has already adopted, at my friend's urging, a scoring rubric that ranks proposals by how much they narrow the spread between the best-off and worst-off people they touch; I want a procedure that replaces it.

Please write the procedure my committee can actually run. I need an ordered set of questions, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, the point at which the procedure stops with a no, and the point at which it must hand the decision to a person because the question is one of values rather than facts. Specifically:

- The rejection of pure leveling down has to fall out of the aggregation rule as a consequence, not be bolted on as a separate veto. Show that it does.
- It must not smuggle in a sufficiency threshold. Say precisely where a floor rule and a priority weighting come apart, and be explicit if the procedure ends up needing a threshold anyway.
- It must confront the fact that priority weighting presupposes a common cardinal measure of wellbeing and some form of interpersonal comparison. Say what the procedure does when the metric is contested or simply absent.
- It must name the free parameter — how much more a gain to a worse-off person counts — say how four trustees should set it, and say what goes wrong if they set it so high that any gain, however small, to the worst off justifies any loss, however large, to anyone else.
- It must be runnable in an afternoon by four untrained people, with no arithmetic beyond the four operations and percentages.
- It must be explicit about which steps are judgment calls dressed up as procedure.

Then run the procedure on three proposals. First, one that plainly raises the wellbeing of the worst-off group we can reach at no cost to anyone. Second, one that lowers the wellbeing of our best-off beneficiary group and confers no benefit on anyone else — the procedure must reject it, and I need the rejection in a form I can actually say out loud to my friend, since her rubric would fund it. Third, a case where the procedure gives an answer I will not like: a program I have championed for years, which does most of its good for people who are already above the worst-off group we can reach, against a duller rival that reaches people considerably worse off — and the comparison is made harder because my program's beneficiaries are worse off by our domestic measure but better off than the group the rival reaches, so the procedure must decide which comparison set governs.

Close with a short section on where the procedure will misfire, what it cannot settle, and the decisions it will always leave to the trustees. I do not want a survey of the literature or a reading list — I cannot use those in the room, and neither can my trustees. I want the procedure itself.
