I have read a great deal of the rationalist-adjacent writing on the ethics of the far future, and I have come to believe that the standard practice in welfare economics rests on a confusion. I would like either to be talked out of this or to be told what follows from it.

My starting point is Ramsey's remark, in the paper where he sets up the optimal-saving problem, that discounting a later enjoyment merely because it arrives later is ethically indefensible and reflects nothing but a failure of imagination. I take this to be close to settled. A pure rate of time preference is just a bias in favour of whoever happens to be standing where we are, and the weight we assign to welfare should be the same at every date.

But then I look at how the number is actually arrived at. The Ramsey rule gives the consumption discount rate as the sum of a pure time preference term and a term equal to the growth rate times the curvature of the utility function, and the familiar point is that even with the pure term set to zero you still get a positive rate, because future people will be richer and a marginal dollar will buy them less. This is where I become suspicious. If what is being compared is the welfare of people at different times, then shrinking the weight of a future person's consumption on the ground that they will be richer seems to accomplish exactly what Ramsey forbade, only indirectly: it makes the value of a future life depend on how it compares with ours rather than on how good it is in itself. And if that is not what it does — if the growth term is really only about the diminishing marginal utility of money — then it cannot bear the weight economists place on it, because a dollar is not a unit of welfare, and the exchange rate between the two is precisely the thing in dispute. Either way, a positive discount rate looks like normative content smuggled in under cover of a technical adjustment.

So my question is this: is the growth-and-diminishing-marginal-utility defence of a positive consumption discount rate either a disguised pure time preference or else a category mistake about the relation between money and wellbeing? If it is, what is the right rate to apply to the far future, and what would have to change in ordinary cost-benefit practice?
