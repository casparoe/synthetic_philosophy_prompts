A colleague in law and economics puts the following argument to me: the employment relation is simply a market exchange like any other. An employer wants a service, a worker supplies it, the two agree on a price, and both parties expect to gain; to call the result exploitation is either empty moralizing or a roundabout complaint about bargaining power, not about the transaction itself. Marx, notoriously, does not deny that the wage bargain is formally a free exchange between equals, and he locates exploitation somewhere this analogy cannot reach. I would like you to audit the analogy rather than adjudicate the politics.

Set out the correspondence explicitly: what plays the role of the buyer, the seller, the commodity, the price, and the contract, and how exact each of these correspondences is. Then identify where the mapping breaks — attending especially to the distinction between a worker's capacity to labour and the labour actually performed, and to what becomes of the commodity once the exchange has been completed. Next, say which features of an ordinary market exchange the analogy quietly imports into the employment relation, and which of those the relation genuinely lacks. Finally, tell me whether the colleague's conclusion — that no exploitation worth the name survives once the bargain is seen as voluntary — still stands, or whether Marx can grant every premise of the analogy and still reach the opposite conclusion, and if the latter, exactly where the additional step is inserted. If the same audit bears on the worker's relation to the product and the process of production, note briefly how.
