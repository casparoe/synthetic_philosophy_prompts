I am a retired experimental physicist. I spent thirty-five years in a laboratory measuring things and worrying about calibration, and I have spent the last four years reading philosophy with more appetite than training. I am not interested in being told that my questions are confused, at least not without being shown where; what I want is to be told what my own verdicts commit me to.

I have been reading about the Sleeping Beauty problem, and I have come to suspect that it is one member of a family of cases that all turn on a single question: when does the fact that I am having this experience rather than some other one give me evidence about the world? Here are five cases and my verdicts on them. I am reasonably confident about the first four and thoroughly unsettled by the fifth.

Case 1. A fair coin is tossed. If it lands heads, I am awakened once, on Monday. If it lands tails, I am awakened on Monday and again on Tuesday, and between the two awakenings I am given a drug that erases all memory of the earlier one, so that at each awakening I have no idea which awakening it is. I know all of this. I wake up. My verdict: my credence that the coin landed heads should be one third. What convinces me is a calibration argument of the kind I would run on a detector. Imagine the whole experiment repeated a thousand times. Two thirds of all the awakenings in that long series follow tails. If at every awakening I were offered even money on tails, with the payoff settled at the end of the experiment once for each awakening at which I accepted, I would come out well ahead. I do not see how an experimentalist could ask for more than that.

Case 2. A fair coin is tossed. If it lands heads, one person is created and placed in a room. If it lands tails, ten people are created and placed in ten identical rooms. They are qualitatively identical, none of them has any memories, and none of them will ever meet the others. I am one of the people created and I have no idea which one. I wake up in a room. My verdict: my credence that the coin landed heads should be one half, unchanged. My waking up in a room was certain whichever way the coin fell, and I cannot see how the bare existence of nine strangers could be evidence about a coin.

Case 3. A fair coin is tossed. If it lands heads, a world is created containing one hundred people; if it lands tails, a world is created containing one hundred thousand. Everyone is created in order and given a serial number. I am one of the people created; I wake up with no memories and find that my serial number is 87. My verdict: this should leave my credence in heads at about one half. I am told that this reasoning, pursued consistently, is something like an argument that the human race will end soon, and I am not persuaded by that argument either. That I happen to be the eighty-seventh person in a world does not seem to me to be evidence about how many people there will be.

Case 4. Two cosmological theories are on the table. Theory A says the universe contains about a billion observers. Theory B says it contains about a quintillion. Both are equally good by every other measure, neither makes any prediction about anything I have observed or ever will observe locally, and I have no other evidence bearing on them. My verdict: I should regard them as equally likely. That there are vastly more observers in one of them is not by itself a reason to think that one true, and I would regard a cosmologist who reasoned otherwise as having made a mistake about what evidence is.

Case 5. Back to Case 1, with one change. If the coin lands tails, the Tuesday awakening is not mine: the experimenter makes a duplicate of me from my brain state, and it is the duplicate who wakes on Tuesday with all my memories. If the coin lands heads, there is only me, awake on Monday. I wake up, unable to tell which situation I am in. My verdict: I do not have one. I am pulled in two directions. On one side, nothing about the case has changed except the labels, so I should still say one third. On the other, the coin no longer makes any difference to how many times I am awake; it makes a difference to how many people are awake, and the other person, whoever he is, is not me.

My hunch, which I offer to be demolished, is that Cases 1 and 2 differ in the following way. In Case 1 the coin determines how many experiences I have. In Case 2 it determines how many other people exist. Perhaps self-locating reasoning is legitimate only when the hypothesis in question bears on the number of my own experiences, and not when it bears on the number of observers besides me. If that is right, it would save all four of my confident verdicts at once, which is suspiciously convenient.

What I would like from you:

1. State as precisely as you can the principle or principles that would generate my verdicts, and say for each one which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate. If nothing can accommodate all of them, say which verdict I should give up and what I should replace it with.
2. Say whether my hunch is coherent, and if it is not, what is wrong with it. If it is coherent, state it in a form that does not lean on the vague notion of my own experiences, and tell me what it commits me to.
3. Tell me what your preferred principle says about at least two cases I have not listed, chosen because they put the most pressure on it.
4. Tell me which of my verdicts I should regard as the least secure, and why.

Please write in American English, and do not worry about citing literature. I am not asking for a reading list, and I would rather have your reasoning than a survey of who has said what.
