I run a metaphysics/ethics reading group, about 10 people, 90 minutes, mostly grad students who've skimmed a bit on panpsychism but nothing technical. I want an activity that makes them feel in their gut why pan-sentience is ethically explosive and also why it's so hard to hold on to cleanly — the sense that if experience goes all the way down, then ordinary physics (a photon absorbed, a nucleus decaying, a transistor flipping) is already a moral disaster scene, and the only thing between that conclusion and sanity is a bridging principle nobody can justify.

Here's my rough sketch, improve it or replace it. Give each person a hand of cards, each card naming a physical process at a different level — say: an electron changing orbital, a molecule breaking, a neuron firing, a rat flinching, a person deciding. They privately assign each card a "moral weight" (0 up to some max) using a rule they have to write down and defend in one line. Then the twist: I announce a lab is deciding whether to run an experiment that will trigger an enormous, given number of each event type (I'll hand out the counts — the low-level counts are astronomically bigger than the high-level ones), and the board has to vote yes/no on the experiment using the summed weights. The arithmetic does the work: anyone who gives a nonzero weight to the low-level processes gets a total that dwarfs everything they care about, so to keep a sane answer they have to zero out the quark or the orbital and defend a sharp cutoff. Then a second round where they have to justify that cutoff with a principle that isn't just "it's the level I already care about." To stop them from dodging, stipulate up front as a working assumption that panpsychism is true and that experience intensity tracks something like degree of informational integration, with a floor of some experience everywhere — so the question is only how to weigh it.

Debrief that maps each moment onto the idea (the zeroing = the arbitrariness of any moral-status cutoff and the combination problem; the exploding total = the horror case; the second round = the search for a bridging principle), what predictably goes wrong the first time (someone checks out with "but panpsychism is false," someone reverse-engineers weights to get the verdict they wanted, and people slide between "has experience" and "has a welfare" — tell me how to handle each), and an honest note on what the activity can't reproduce: it can't touch whether panpsychism is actually true, and it can't convey why combining subjects is metaphysically hard rather than just morally awkward, which I'll have to supply myself.
