Background: I've been chewing on the thing I've seen called the dogmatism paradox. Roughly: if I know that p, then I also know that any future evidence that seems to tell against p must be misleading -- because p is true and I know it, so anything pointing the other way is leading me astray. So knowing something seems to license me to ignore, or explain away in advance, whatever counterevidence shows up. That's obviously a terrible way to think. Most treatments I've seen try to rescue knowledge by saying something like: knowing p gives you a reason to expect that counterevidence will turn out to be explicable, but it doesn't give you a reason to think this particular piece of counterevidence is misleading -- you still have to actually look at it. That feels right, but it also feels like swatting the paradox away rather than explaining why the original reasoning seemed so compelling in the first place.

My idea, in notes:

1. Knowledge claims are time-indexed and, once true, irrevocable. If I knew p on Tuesday, then no matter what happens later it stays true that I knew p on Tuesday. Later evidence can make it true that I no longer know, or that I'm now obliged to give up the belief, but it can't reach back and turn Tuesday's knowledge into mere opinion.

2. The dogmatist's inference is a move from a past-tense fact to a present-tense license. "I knew p" is being used as authority over a question -- what does this new evidence show? -- that is settled by the evidence arriving now. My Tuesday knowledge doesn't get a vote on Wednesday's question.

3. On this reading the paradox is a fallacy of tense, not a deep problem about knowledge. It's the epistemic cousin of treating a promise I made last year as an argument that I don't have to decide anything today.

4. Predictions this makes: the paradox should lose its bite when the "misleading" evidence is itself already in the past, since then we really can assess it against the old knowledge. And it has an exact mirror image nobody worries about -- "I didn't know p yesterday, so no evidence today could make p known" -- which is just as bad and strikes nobody as a paradox. If my diagnosis is right, the two should be equally puzzling or equally banal, and they aren't.

5. What I can't sort out: if knowledge is dated and irrevocable, but whether I knew on Tuesday depends on facts that only get settled on Friday (if Friday's evidence turns out to be genuine, I never knew p at all), then knowledge's past-tense status depends on the future, which undercuts point 1 and maybe sinks the whole thing. This might be where it collapses. Tell me if it does, and if so, whether there's a version of the view that survives.
