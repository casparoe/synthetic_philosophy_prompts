You are an expert in modal metaphysics and the philosophy of biology, with a firm grasp of the essentialist literature and of the biology of human reproduction. Reason through the cases below carefully before committing to any verdict, and make the steps of your reasoning explicit.

I am setting the final essay for a senior-level metaphysics seminar. The topic is origin essentialism — roughly, the thesis that a human being could not have originated from a different sperm and egg (or from different gametes) than the ones from which they in fact originated. My students can all reproduce the derivation from the necessity of identity, so I want a question they cannot answer by reciting it. The question must force them to test the thesis against three cases in which the ordinary one-sperm-plus-one-egg picture of human origins breaks down:

(a) monozygotic twinning, where a single zygote divides and two distinct people result;
(b) tetragametic chimerism, where two zygotes fuse and a single person develops from the combined cell mass;
(c) mitochondrial replacement, where the nuclear DNA of the resulting child comes from the intending parents but the mitochondria come from a third party's egg.

Please write the exam question itself (90-minute time limit, 1,200-word cap), then a model answer of about 700 words, then a marking scheme worth 20 marks built around five must-hit points, giving the marks for each and a one-line note on the mistake that typically costs candidates that point. For each of the three cases the model answer should say what the essentialist is committed to, whether the case refutes the thesis or merely forces a more precise formulation of it, and which candidate formulation — sameness of gametes, of zygote, of genome, of cell lineage, or of biological parents — is best supported and why. The marking scheme should make explicit what separates an A answer from a B answer. Finally, I would like the model answer to state the price the best formulation pays, in particular whether it must treat the origins of the gametes themselves as essential and, if not, on what grounds it stops there rather than making a person's entire ancestral history essential to them.
