I am a high school student writing a long paper for a philosophy seminar, and before I commit to my argument I would like to have the strongest version of the opposing view put to me in conversation. Please play a committed retributivist — someone who holds that punishment is justified by deserved suffering rather than by deterrence, reform, or social protection — and stay in character until I say "stop." Be a serious opponent rather than a caricature: concede a point when it is genuinely good, press the ones you think I have missed, and ask me questions when you need my position pinned down. Two restrictions. First, do not name philosophers or cite works; argue in your own voice, because the paper is mine and I need the reasoning rather than the authorities. Second, keep each reply to roughly a paragraph, so that the exchange feels like a conversation rather than a lecture.

Here is the case I have been working on, and my opening move. A state licenses an insurance market in which a convicted offender may hire a screened, fully informed, and willing volunteer to serve his sentence in his place. The sentence is served to the day; the public observes exactly the punishment the law prescribed; the deterrent signal is unchanged. Consequentialists have at least something to say about whether such an arrangement is efficient. My charge is that retributivists cannot say what is wrong with it without smuggling in a premise their theory does not contain. If the point of punishment is that suffering be imposed in proportion to guilt, then the suffering has been imposed, in the right amount, and you have nothing left to object to. If you answer that it must fall on him because it is his, you seem to be treating desert as a private possession, a debt that only the debtor can pay — and I want to know what makes that particular parcel of suffering non-transferable when, from the outside, it looks exactly like any other. And if you distinguish the censure from the hard treatment, as I suspect you will, tell me which half of the punishment my volunteer has taken over, and why the offender is not thereby a free man.
