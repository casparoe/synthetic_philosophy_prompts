Okay, so: I'm a fifth-year philosophy grad student, and through a chain of events I'd rather not go into (my advisor moved departments, I agreed to sit on one committee, and here we are) I've been volun-told into giving the opening speech at our university's annual peer-education training weekend. It's a Saturday morning in September, about ninety undergrads who've volunteered to be dorm "consent and community" peer educators, plus the dean of students and her staff, a handful of residential-life supervisors, and — because she sits on the advisory board and never misses this thing — an emeritus professor who taught Kant here for thirty years and who, I'm reliably informed, will be in the front row watching me like a hawk. The dean's office has given me five to seven minutes and a takeaway they'd very much like me to deliver: the slogan is "only yes means yes," and I'm supposed to send everyone out of the room feeling that consent is really pretty simple once you stop being euphemistic about it. Warm, a little funny, no jargon.

The trouble is that I've spent alot of the past year reading and arguing about this — I've been on LessWrong-adjacent forums since I was like fifteen, so I have a bad habit of wanting to dissolve slogans instead of repeat them — and I've come to think consent is not simple at all, though not in a way that should leave anyone paralyzed. I also don't want to give ninety nineteen-year-olds a philosophy seminar at nine in the morning, or leave them thinking "well, it's all ambiguous, nobody can really know." So what I need is a speech that can be honest about the hard cases without litigating them, and that keeps the practical norm intact while quietly refusing to pretend the slogan is airtight.

Here's what I'd love it to somehow make room for, without ever flagging it as "the philosophy part": that a threat doesn't have to threaten anything wrong to do real work — "have sex with me or I'll leave you" and "have sex with me or I'll send everyone those photos" feel completely different, and I've never seen a clean criterion for why, so I'd rather the speech be honest about the murk than invent a bright line. That a sincere yes can fail to count if it was bought with a false picture of what you were agreeing to (the standard case is someone lying about whether they can have kids, but the shape of it is what I care about). That being too drunk to sign a lease and being too drunk to... well, you know. That consent can be fully present and the thing still rotten — the agreed-to-but-exploitative cases — and fully absent and the thing still fine, like a surgeon who doesn't wake you up to ask. And the one I actually care about most: "yes means yes" makes consent sound like a magic word, as if once the syllable is out the moral question closes, when I think the truer and more useful picture is that consent is something two people are trying to keep track of together, and its failure modes look like one person being surprised, or one person not being able to say no without losing something they need. Can you write me the speech? Make it sound like a person talking — I'm not saying "normative" out loud — and remember it has to land for the dean's office, who are listening for the slogan, and for the Kant professor, who will notice instantly if I fudge the hard cases. No citations or reading lists please, I'm speaking from memory and I'd get them wrong anyway. Under about a thousand words if you can, and please don't let it turn into a list of rules.
