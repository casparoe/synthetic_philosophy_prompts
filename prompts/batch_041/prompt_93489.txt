I'm a novelist, and I've hit a wall — not in the manuscript, but in my own family, where a disagreement has quietly turned into something I can only describe as a philosophical problem I can't resolve. My cousin Dana has cut off all contact with her mother, my aunt, who is now seriously ill; Dana says the reasons are old and real and that she won't explain them to people who have already decided. Since then the family hasn't argued with her so much as closed over her. She's spoken of in the past tense at gatherings, her name is skipped in the round of toasts, and my aunt has taken to saying, only half in jest, that Dana is no longer one of us. I've been asked to speak at a dinner that will effectively ratify this — to say a few words about family and who belongs — and I've stalled, because I can feel the pull of both sides and I can't tell whether that's cowardice or a genuine standoff.

What makes it feel like more than a family quarrel is a thought I keep running into, in a course I'm taking and in a novel I admire: that a person is not a self-contained thing but something held and kept in being by other people, that we become and remain persons only through the recognition of those around us. If that is right, then what my family is doing to Dana is not mere coldness; it is closer to unmaking her, and I can't decide whether that makes their judgment weightier — they may see something about her that I don't — or monstrous, since it makes one person's standing depend on a vote at a dinner table. I also notice the idea comes in different strengths: as a plain fact about how we develop, as a claim about what personhood actually consists in, and as a claim about what we owe one another. I suspect my relatives are sliding between these, which is why nothing anyone says lands. And there is a strand of it I find disturbing — that personhood is something achieved over a life and can be forfeited — which would seem to hand the family exactly the verdict they want.

So I would like a committed answer rather than a tour of the options. Taken at its strongest, does the idea that we are persons through other persons support what my family is doing to Dana, or condemn it? Does it matter whether personhood is a status you either have or lack, versus something graded that a person can lose? And whatever you conclude, tell me what I should actually do at that dinner and why — I would rather be told I am wrong than be handed the considerations and left to sort them myself. Please write in American English.
