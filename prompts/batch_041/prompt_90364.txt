I sit on the council of a mid-sized medical research charity — we hand out about £40m a year, mostly lab grants. We've had two fabrication cases in eighteen months and our chief exec has come to us with a proposal she's rather attached to. I have to circulate a short written opinion to comittee before we vote on the 14th.

Her proposal has two bits. First, every grant-holder lodges a £60k good-conduct bond with us; it comes back in full after five years if there's no misconduct finding, and no intrest in the meantime. Second, the PI and one postdoc on each grant have to do a two-week residential data-integrity programme, which we won't pay for — their institution eats the cost. Her argument is the handicap principle: anyone can write in a cover letter that they're careful, so the only thing that carries information is a cost that a lab which doesn't really believe its own data wouldn't be willing to bear. She's been reading about peacocks and about the sorting condition in signalling models and thinks this is textbook.

Half the council says it's just a wealth filter — the endowment-rich labs shrug, the small honest ones can't play, and a determined fraudster with a well-funded PI posts the bond and carries on. The other half says the waste is the point and we shouldn't be sentimental about labs that won't risk anything. We also have a pilot from last year, where the bond was optional in one round: 40% took it up, and take-up tracked both big endowments and clean retraction records, so both sides are quoting it at me and it proves nothing either way.

Write the opinion I'll circulate. Recommend a decision — adopt, reject, or adopt with conditions — give me a timeframe, and say what we'd do instead if you'd reject it. Engage the signalling argument on its own terms rather than waving it off, and tell me what evidence would actually separate the handicap story from the wealth story. No references needed, I just need the reasoning to hold up.
