I edit the science-and-ideas desk at a mid-size magazine, and the call I get wrong most often — maybe twice a month, usually late at night before we go to press — is whether to keep a long-running investigation or series alive or kill it. Some of these have already eaten six months of a reporter's time and a big slice of the budget, and the whole newsroom keeps telling me the money's gone so decide on the future only. Fine, except that advice always feels like its missing something. Sometimes the fact that we've spent so much is telling me something real about the story. Sometimes killing it now blows up a promise to a source, or teaches my reporters that I fold when it gets hard. Sometimes quitting is just panic. Can you turn this into a procedure I can actually run — the questions in order, what counts as a real answer to each, where the procedure stops with a flat kill-it, and where it has to hand the call back to me because it's a values question and not a factual one. I don't want the textbook version where sunk costs are simply irrelevant; I want the honest one, including the arguments that honoring a sunk cost can be defensible (as a commitment device, as a signal to sources and to my own staff, as a way of not being the kind of person who flinches) and how to tell those apart from plain self-justification — and the point where the question becomes which agent we're even talking about, me now or the magazine over years. Run it on three: a big investigation that's expensive, going nowhere, and whose central premise I've quietly stopped believing; a reader-favorite series that loses money every month; and a reporter's four-year pet project where my own history of killing her stories is part of what's actually being decided. Include the case where the procedure tells me something I won't want to hear. Constraints: I have about an hour, no outside help, and I have to justify the decision to the publisher. And tell me what the procedure can't settle — where I'm just going to have to choose.
