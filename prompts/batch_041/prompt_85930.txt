I am a novelist finishing a book that contains a chapter in which a myrmecologist has to decide whether to say, out loud, that the colony she has studied for eleven years has a mind. I would rather the chapter's ambiguity be principled than atmospheric, so I want you to build me a case to order — engineered, not merely described — and then show me exactly how the disagreement falls out of it.

Two conditions. Use them as I define them here rather than substituting the textbook versions.

Global Availability: a state of a system is globally available when the information it carries can be brought to bear on the whole of that system's behavioural repertoire — whatever the system can do, it can do in the light of that information — and when one such state can be set beside another for comparison.

Stake: a state carries a stake when its biological function is to track a fact about the system itself (its reserves, its location, its survival), such that the system is harmed when the state is wrong, and the harm is traceable to the misrepresentation rather than to some other failure.

Build the case in the direction that is hard to build: a colony that has Stake but not Global Availability. Constraints. The mechanism must be one a myrmecologist would not laugh at, and must be given in a single paragraph of plain prose; no science fiction, no invented organs, no exotic physics; no appeal to what the colony is secretly feeling. The absence of Global Availability must be structural rather than a matter of degree — not something that more ants or more time would repair. Do not let any part of the colony understand the whole. And do not stipulate the split; the reader should be able to see why the two conditions come apart here.

Then, in a short labelled section each, give me the verdicts on two questions — first, does the colony have states that represent anything? second, does the colony have a mind? — from three positions, which you may phrase in your own words but should treat as fixed:

the Instrumentalist, for whom the only question is whether the colony's behaviour is best predicted by treating it as an agent with beliefs and desires;
the Functional-Role theorist, for whom a state is a belief only if it plays the right part in the system's causal economy, Global Availability being part of that part;
the Stake theorist, for whom states are about things only when they have the function described above, and for whom a mind requires a subject for whom things can be so.

Finish with three things, briefly: which single feature of the case is doing the work; what becomes of the verdicts if that feature is removed — in particular, whether the case collapses back into the ordinary question of whether a colony could have a mind, with the ordinary answers; and how the position that comes out worst would attack the case.

Keep the whole reply under 700 words, and put the scenario first, before any analysis. No citations and no names of philosophers: I cannot check them, and I want the positions rather than the literature.
