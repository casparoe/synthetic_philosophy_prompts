I spent most of last weekend in a long, increasingly bad-tempered argument with a friend about G. E. Moore, and I came away convinced that almost everything people think they know about him is a cartoon: that he was a prissy Edwardian who decided that beauty and friendship were the only two things that mattered, that he refuted hedonism simply by insisting pleasure isn't the good, and that his famous open-question argument is just the is/ought gap wearing a waistcoat. The argument started because I've agreed to lead a session of an adult continuing-education reading group — people with no philosophy training but a great deal of patience, roughly ninety minutes, no texts in front of them — on Moore's account of value, and specifically on why he treats the enjoyment of beauty and the pleasures of personal affection as the two things that are good in themselves, and, more interestingly, on why he thinks those goods are organic unities whose value is not the sum of the value of their parts. I want you to plan the session for me rather than explain Moore to me. Give me the order in which the ideas have to be introduced, with the reason for each ordering constraint — what genuinely has to be in place before the next thing will make sense. In particular, I want to know how much of the open-question argument this group needs before the phrase "good in itself" means anything to them at all, and whether the organic-unity point can be felt before they have the isolation test, or whether that order is forced. Then tell me the single carrying example the whole session should hang on. I have a hunch it should be something small and domestic rather than an abstract isolation-test comparison of a beauty-rich world against a beauty-poor one, and I want you to say whether that hunch is right, what the example has to have and what it must not have — it must not smuggle consequences back in, and it must not let them hear "organic unity" as the bland claim that a description of a whole mentions more than a description of its parts — and why the obvious alternative, walking them through the enjoyment of a beautiful object as Moore himself sets it up, fails at this level.

Second, I want the misconception map. I'm expecting at least these four: that Moore is a hedonist in disguise because the appreciation of beauty involves pleasure; that "intrinsic" means "by nature" or "innate"; that an organic unity means the parts change their value when they are combined, rather than the whole's value simply not being their sum; and that Moore's ranking of beauty and friendship is a piece of effete aestheticism that quietly ignores suffering. For each, tell me exactly where in the sequence it will form and the precise move that heads it off, and flag any misconception I haven't listed that a group like this will predictably bring with it. Finish with one question I can put to the room at the end that will actually tell me whether they have grasped the difference between a good valued for itself and a whole whose value exceeds its parts — something they cannot answer by parroting my phrasing back at me. And tell me what I should openly refuse to do in ninety minutes: which parts of the surrounding apparatus — the naturalistic fallacy read as a thesis about definition, the ideal-utilitarian calculus, the contrast with hedonism as a rival theory — I should leave out and say I am leaving out, so that the session has a clean shape instead of turning into a survey.
