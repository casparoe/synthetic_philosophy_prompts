I sit on the board of a small housing co-operative, and for the past few months I have been unable to stop thinking about our constitution. It is three rules long, and everyone treats each of them as obviously right. (1) Anything shared — the roof, the garden, the parking, the paint on the façade — is settled by majority vote, and the minority is expected to comply. (2) Each household is sovereign inside its own private sphere: what it reads, whom it invites, how it furnishes its rooms, and what colour it paints its own front door. No vote may override this. (3) Some things cannot be done even if every member agrees to them: a member cannot sell or transfer their vote at a meeting, and cannot sign away their unit in perpetuity.

My trouble is that I can no longer see how (1) and (2) fit together, and I suspect (3) is an admission that they do not. Our neighbour has painted her door a colour the rest of us find ugly, and a clear majority wants it changed. Rule (1) says the majority may decide anything shared, and the façade is shared; rule (2) says the door is hers. We argue as though there were a fact of the matter about which rule governs, and I doubt there is. The sharper case is this: suppose a scheme repainting the whole row one colour is one that every household, including hers, would genuinely prefer to the present mess — her included, and she would even prefer the scheme's colour to the one she chose. Rule (1) would carry it unanimously; rule (2) still gives her a veto she is entitled to use, so the co-op can be stuck on an outcome that every single member would rather swap for another. I have a nagging sense that this knot is a famous one in the theory of voting and collective decisions, and if so I want to know which of our rules the result actually indicts.

What I want is not a tour of what celebrated philosophers have written about liberty, but a reconstruction of what our little document commits us to. What theory of rights, and what theory of whose preferences count, does it presuppose — in particular, does it presuppose that my preference about my neighbour's door weighs less than my preference about my own, and is that a principled line or a fudge? Is the document internally consistent? If it is not, take a definite position on which rule should go and what the co-op would lose by dropping it; if you think all three survive under a narrower reading, say exactly what that reading is. I would also like to know whether the document genuinely forces one answer, or whether several different theories of rights would each let us keep all three rules, so that the trouble lies in how we apply it rather than in what it says. Give me your own verdict rather than a menu of options, and flag anything you are unsure of. Keep it to 600 words or fewer.
