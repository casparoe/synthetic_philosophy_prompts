I'm in a little reading group that does one weird text a month, and last time someone brought in the guy who invented the word genocide — Lemkin, the Polish-Jewish lawyer, writing in 1944 — and the thing that's been bugging me since is how much wider his version of the crime was than the one that actually made it into law. For him it was the destruction of a group's whole pattern of life: its language, its churches, its schools, its property, its ability to reproduce itself as a group, with the killing as one technique among several. Then the 1948 convention narrowed it down to five specific acts done with intent to destroy a national, ethnic, racial or religious group, in whole or in part, as such — the drafters deliberately voted down the cultural clause, and cut political groups too, and what's left turns on proving a special intent to destroy the group *physically*. So you can apparently erase a language, a church and a whole way of life without ever committing genocide in the legal sense, and you can commit it while your memos talk about resettlement and security. There's also this strange detail I keep chewing on: forcibly transferring a group's children is one of the five enumerated acts, which means a policy whose real aim is assimilation can still land inside the definition if it's done the right way. I want a short story that lives in that gap — not an essay with characters in it, but a story where the law is a wall the characters keep running into.

Here's the setup I have in mind, though you should feel free to make up whatever names, places and details work. Invent a small landlocked country — please make up the country and the minority group, I don't want this to be a thin disguise for any real atrocity — where a minority with its own language and its own church is being squeezed: some killings by uniformed units, some by irregular militias who seem to be acting on their own, villages burned in a way that's officially about land and insurgents and only unofficially about who lives there. Your protagonist is a mid-level analyst in the early-warning office of a regional body, herself from that country though not from the minority, and her actual job is to draft the internal memo that decides whether the word gets used in the organization's next statement. Around her: a legal adviser who says the file doesn't come close to the intent standard and who is not a coward about it; a delegation head who wants the word for reasons that are about his own career and his own history; a cousin in the diaspora NGO that has already put the word on its fundraising posters; and someone from one of the burned villages who has very strong views about whether the word helps or hurts the people still there. One of them should know the definition better than she does and still disagree with her about what to do with it.

What I want out of the story is that the reader ends up feeling the pull of naming it *and* the reasons for restraint, without being told which one to feel. So: let the legal definition be a genuine constraint rather than a bit of bureaucratic fussiness — the reader should understand why intent is so hard to prove when the violence is decentralized and the stated motives are security and land, and why proving it matters beyond pedantry. Give me at least one scene where cultural destruction is happening in plain view and everyone in the room knows the treaty doesn't care, and one where something looks like one of the enumerated acts but is motivated by something else entirely. Don't give me a wise character who explains the law; let me pick it up from what people do. And end with her actually deciding — sign the memo or gut it — but without the story telling me she was right. A few pages is fine, and no real countries, real leaders or real cases please.
