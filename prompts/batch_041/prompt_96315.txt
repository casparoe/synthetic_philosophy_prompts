I run a small reading group at my company — mostly engineers, about 14 of us, we get 90 minutes on Thursday afternoons — and I want to do a session on this idea from the AI-alignment world that I keep chewing on. Roughly: take me, and say that whenever I hit a question that's too hard to answer in one go, I'm allowed to spin up copies of myself to work on the sub-parts, and each of those copies can do the same, all the way down, and then I just read their answers and synthesize. The claim is that this idealized me — infinite patience, infinite copies of myself — is the right yardstick for what I "really" think, or for what a good answer even looks like, because it's just me all the way down, no extra magic added. And there's a second claim baked in: that any hard question can be chopped into small enough pieces that some bounded copy of me can handle each piece, and that recombining the pieces doesn't lose anything. I'm suspicious of that second part but I can't say why.

Can you design an activity for the group that makes us actually feel this — where the rules force us into that recursive consultation tree and we hit whatever it is that breaks, before you name it? I want the rules, the materials (cheap — paper, sticky notes, whatever), a rough timeline for the 90 min, and a debrief that maps what happened onto the idea and its assumptions. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to salvage the session when it does, and be honest about which part of the idea the activity just can't reproduce and that I'll have to explain myself.

Two constraints: nobody should have to "act" or perform, people just sit and pass notes or talk in whatever channels the rules allow, and I'd rather the question they deliberate on be something with a checkable answer we can compare against afterwards, not a fake "what's the meaning of life" thing. And I dont want a bunch of references or further reading at the end, just the activity.
