I am in a graduate seminar on personal identity, and we spent last week on Daniel Dennett's suggestion that the self is a center of narrative gravity. The version our professor gave us runs like this: a center of gravity is not a part of a physical object, it is not made of anything, and no amount of cutting a body open will reveal it, yet claims about where it lies are objectively true or false and they do real explanatory work; by analogy, the self is not a part of the brain or a thing in the head, it is an abstraction over the stories a body tells and has told about it, and yet claims about a self are truth-apt and explanatory. The professor's point was that this blocks the question of where the self is the way the question of where the center of gravity is gets blocked. A classmate objected that this is eliminativism with a nicer name, and the professor moved on before I could work out whose side I was on.

Two loose ends are nagging me. First, a friend of mine who did a physics degree says the professor's version of the analogy is too tidy: he says the center of gravity is only pinned down relative to a gravitational field, that in a non-uniform field there need not be any point about which the gravitational torque vanishes and that if there is such a point it need not be unique, and that where it does exist it shifts as the body changes position and orientation, so it is not the stable, always-available, uniquely determined feature the analogy seems to need. I am taking his word for it; if he has the physics wrong, I want to be told. Second, another student proposed that the better analogy is the legal person: a corporation has no location and is made of nothing, it can be sued, fined, and held to its promises, it survives the complete turnover of its employees, and it exists only by virtue of a dense set of institutional practices — charters, accounting, liability — that no one can point to in a warehouse. That analogy seems to restore the normative dimension the center-of-gravity picture appears to strip out, since nobody blames a center of gravity, so it is not obvious to me that the two analogies are doing the same work or that the professor's can simply be swapped for it.

Please audit the center-of-gravity analogy rather than defend or attack Dennett's view in general. Give me the correspondence table first — what plays the role of the body, the mass distribution, the gravitational field, the torque, the theory that licenses talk of the point, and the observer — then tell me where the mapping breaks, separating the breaks that matter for the job the analogy is being asked to do (licensing the claim that there is no further fact about the self, while leaving us free to treat one another as responsible agents) from the breaks that are merely decorative. Say explicitly what the analogy smuggles in, whether the conclusion survives once the disanalogies are on the table, and how the corporation analogy compares, including whether the relativity and non-uniqueness my friend mentioned make the self-analogue better or worse. If neither analogy is the right one, sketch what a better one would look like and what it would have to preserve. I do not need citations or a reading list; I will add those myself.
