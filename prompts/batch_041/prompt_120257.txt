A member of my reading group has been arguing that Rawls's difference principle is internally unstable — that once you grant the reasoning Rawls uses to motivate it, you are pushed past it, either into a demand for strict equality or into a position that quietly abandons its own foundation. He has set the argument out as follows, and I cannot find the loose thread.

(1) The difference principle is motivated by the thought that the distribution of natural talents and of social starting positions is arbitrary from a moral point of view. Behind the veil of ignorance the parties do not know where they will land in that distribution, and no one can claim to deserve a larger share merely because of the family, body, and society into which they happened to be born.
(2) If a feature is arbitrary in that sense — if it cannot ground desert — then it cannot by itself justify anyone's holding a larger share of social goods.
(3) Arbitrariness is conserved under voluntary action. If your starting endowment is arbitrary, then whatever you end up with by acting from that endowment is arbitrary too; effort, striving, and hard bargaining do not launder an undeserved starting point into a deserved result.
(4) By (2) and (3), the extra productivity that the difference principle rewards with a larger share is itself arbitrary, and so grounds no claim to that larger share.
(5) The difference principle nonetheless permits the better off to keep a larger share, provided the arrangement raises the position of the least advantaged. But to permit a person to keep more is to treat that person as having a claim the rest of us would otherwise be entitled to override; the proviso only makes sense as the price of buying off that claim.
(6) By (4), there is no such claim to buy off.
(7) Therefore the difference principle is either inconsistent — retaining an inequality-permitting clause that presupposes entitlements its own motivating premise denies — or it must be read as demanding equality, since any inequality it allows would rest on exactly the arbitrary facts it set out to neutralize.

He maintains that (1)–(3) are Rawls's own thought carried one step further, and that (5) is a truism about what it is to 'permit' something. I am unwilling to surrender the arbitrariness argument; it is the strongest thing Rawls has going. But I am equally unwilling to conclude that the difference principle collapses into strict equality, since the whole point of the principle is to license inequalities that work for the worst off.

Which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost? I want to know whether the trouble lies in the inference from arbitrariness to the absence of a claim, in the conservation step (3), or in the reading of 'permit' in (5) — and if it lies elsewhere, where. For whichever step I give up, tell me why it looked compelling in the first place. Then give me the price list. If I keep the arbitrariness argument, what must I say justifies an inequality, and how much of Rawls's architecture do I have to give up to say it? Compare at least the institutional reading (the principle governs the basic structure rather than individual claims), the reciprocity reading (inequalities are justified because they benefit everyone, the worst off included, and so express fair terms rather than desert), a strict-egalitarian reading that embraces the conclusion, and a desert-based reading that rejects (1). For each, say what it preserves, what it surrenders, and whether it leaves step (2) or step (3) intact.
