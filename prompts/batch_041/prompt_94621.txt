I host a small interview podcast on the philosophy of medicine, and next month's guest is a philosopher who has spent about a decade defending a position she calls constrained naturalism. The core of it is descriptive: a state counts as a disease when it is a subnormal departure from species-typical functional efficiency, a matter of biological fact rather than social valuation. What separates her from the older biostatistical tradition is two amendments. First, she fixes the reference class by the environment in which the trait was selected rather than by present-day demographics, so that near-universal myopia in a reading-heavy population still counts as disease, and the sickle-cell trait remains a dysfunction even though it confers protection against malaria. Second, she drops the harm requirement that hybrid analyses of disorder retain, on the grounds that harm belongs to illness — the patient's experience and the social role built on top of it — and not to disease, which is the underlying biological fact. She has one stock reply to most objections: that her critics are running the two ledgers together, disease and disvalue, and then treating the second as evidence about the first. She also likes to observe that the threshold problem — where on a continuum of blood pressure or depressive symptoms does pathology begin? — is a problem for every taxonomy, including her critics', and so cannot be used to choose among them.

I would like you to draft the questions for that interview: ten to twelve of them, clustered and sequenced, phrased the way a host actually asks them aloud, each with a follow-up keyed to the answer she is most likely to give and a one-line note on where the follow-up should go if she takes the exchange somewhere else. None of them should invite her to restate the position or to survey the literature, and none should ask her who said what; I want the questions to stand on their own content. The sequence should press where the literature actually presses: on how the selective environment that fixes her reference class is identified, and whether it can be identified without appealing to what we care about; on what work is left for disease to do once harm has been reassigned to illness, and why a value-free classification should have any grip on what medicine ought to treat; on the possibility of universal or near-universal disease, and the converse case of a rare deviation nobody regards as pathological; on the declassification of homosexuality as a mental disorder and the removal of the bereavement exclusion for major depression, which she will want to read as science correcting itself; on whether the notion of function her account relies on is itself normative, and whether dysfunction inherits that normativity; on the line-drawing problem, given her insistence that her thresholds are natural facts rather than conventions; on where the account draws the line against enhancement, aging, pregnancy, and ordinary variation; and on what would actually change in the clinic, in insurance, or in the definition of health care if her view were adopted.

Three final points about the shape of the set. Her two-ledger move is very hard to dislodge, so order the questions so that it becomes progressively more expensive to use — ideally so that by the end she must either concede something or repeat herself — and say explicitly which questions do that work. Close with the one question I am most afraid to ask her, and with a question about what evidence would count against constrained naturalism; if she answers that nothing could, I want a follow-up ready for that answer. And tell me which two questions to cut if we run short on time.
