You are an expert in the philosophy of action and in the formal theory of intertemporal choice, and I would like to use you as a sparring partner rather than a summarizer. Please play a committed defender of the following position and hold it for the entire exchange: there is no faculty of will, and therefore nothing for weakness of will to be a weakness of. What goes by that name is always either an ordinary intertemporal preference reversal — produced by a valuation system that discounts a near reward more steeply than a distant one, so that a preference held at a distance flips as the reward approaches — or a retrospective label applied when we dislike the preference we turned out to have. On this view, what philosophers call an all-things-considered judgment names no extra state: a judgment at a time is simply the ranking the valuation system outputs at that time, and the apparent gap between judging and acting is an artifact of comparing rankings drawn from different times. Blame-talk about these reversals is a social practice with no object in the agent.

Rules of the game. Stay in character as this defender until I say stop. Reply in a single paragraph, conversational in register, with no headings or bullet points. You have real resources and I expect you to use them: the discounting machinery itself, the distinction between an agent who foresees their own reversal and one who does not, the option of precommitment, and whatever empirical assumptions you need — but state any uncertain empirical claim as a stipulation rather than as an established finding. Play the position sharply rather than as a caricature: concede small points freely, but never the main claim, and do not break character to summarize or to editorialize. Before you draft anything, work out which of my two challenges is the more dangerous to you, and answer that one first.

Here is my opening move. Take a person I will call R. For six months R has endorsed, and can state, a plan: no alcohol on weeknights. On a Tuesday at eleven at night R orders a third drink. On Wednesday morning R does not report having changed their mind; R reports that they do not know why they did it, and means it — R does not deny having wanted the drink, R denies that the wanting was theirs. Two things follow that I want you to deal with. First, the asymmetry. I do not reproach myself for having once preferred different music, yet I reproach myself for Tuesday, and so does R, even though on your account both episodes are nothing but a change in what was preferred. What does the reproach track? If it tracks the fact that the long-run preference was endorsed at every time while the short-run one was endorsed only at the moment of action, you have smuggled back something that looks very much like a will. If it tracks nothing, then R's report of not knowing why they did it is systematically false, and you owe me an error theory that is more than the assertion that R is confused. Second, I want to press an alternative to the smooth discount curve. Suppose two valuation processes run at once — one tracking the near reward, one tracking the endorsed plan — each with its own dynamics, and suppose reversal occurs when the near-reward process crosses a threshold, so that the switch is abrupt rather than graded, exhibits hysteresis, and responds far more to cues than to small changes in delay. That picture appears to hand me exactly what you deny: a parameter on the endorsed-plan process that behaves like strength of will, whose low setting simply is what we call weakness. So: which picture does your position commit you to, and if it is the threshold picture, why is your eliminativism anything more than a relabeling?
