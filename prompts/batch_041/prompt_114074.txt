I got into an argument with my German friend Jonas that started as the stupidest possible thing — whether you're allowed to cross an empty street on a red light at 3am — and turned into what i'm now pretty sure is a real, substantive arguement about what a morally decent person even is. (I'm a longtime lurker on the rationalist blogs so my instinct is to call everything a coordination problem and move on, but I actually want the argument here, not the deflationary move.)

His picture: the moral achievement is following the rule you agreed to, no exceptions, especially in the cases where you privately think this particular rule is silly — because the alternative is that every official gets to carve out an exception for the case in front of them, and that's how you get favoritism, arbitrariness, and eventually a whole class of people who are quietly confident that the rules are for other people. Conscience belongs upstream, where the rules get made; once they're made it's supposed to shut up. My picture: conscience is the seat of the whole thing, authority is a hint at best, and the worst thing you can be is the person who did the bad thing because someone told them to — the entire point is that you personally refuse. A distinction I came up with and would like you to actually use: in his picture conscience gets a vote, in mine it gets a veto. Or put it this way, which I like better: the two pictures disagree about who bears the burden of justification when someone deviates from the rule — the deviator, or the rule itself.

So compare these two as actual philosophical positions rather than as national stereotypes, and tell me which has the better of it. Specifically: (a) does the conscience/veto picture have any non-question-begging answer to the person whose conscience is sincerely monstrous — the shopkeeper who won't serve a customer on conscience grounds, or, to make it harder, the official who refuses to enforce a law he thinks is unjust — or does it just have to eat that and hope the monsters are rare? (b) does the rule/vote picture have any answer to the captured procedure, where the rules themselves are the injustice and everybody involved can honestly say they only did what they were told? and (c) are these even rivals, or are they answering different questions — what makes an action right vs. who gets to decide — so that the apparent disagreement is downstream of that, and if so does that dissolve the disagreement or just relocate it somewhere less comfortable? Please lead with your bottom line in the first sentence — which one has the better of it, in one line — and then give the reasoning. No preamble, and don't restate the question back at me.
