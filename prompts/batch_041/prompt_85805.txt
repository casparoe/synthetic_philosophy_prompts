I have been trying to get clear on the free energy principle, and I want to check whether the following reading of it is correct before I rely on it. As I understand the claim, any self-organizing system — a cell, an organism, a brain — acts so as to minimize the discrepancy between the predictions it generates and the sensory input it actually receives, and it is this minimization, rather than any goal or reward, that explains its behaviour. If that is right, the principle seems to carry an immediate and awkward consequence: such a system should gravitate towards whatever surroundings would least often falsify its predictions, and the limiting case of that is a dark, silent, unchanging room, in which nothing ever contradicts an expectation. Yet no organism behaves this way; organisms pursue novelty, hazard, and stimulation. I can see only two ways to respond. Either the principle is false, or it has been formulated so permissively — with enough unconstrained parameters and enough scope for post hoc repair — that no observation could count against it, in which case it is not an empirical theory at all. Which diagnosis is correct? And if the principle is in fact defensible, is that because it was never really a claim about minimizing prediction error, and if so, how does the quantity such systems are actually said to minimize differ from the one I have described — and is the expected free energy I have seen mentioned merely that same quantity averaged over possible futures?
