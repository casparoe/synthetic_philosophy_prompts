I am a novelist, and I have two chapters that turn on the same piece of bad arithmetic. In the first, a man receives a letter whose whole content is that exactly one of two things is true — his house is infested with termites, or he has paid a stranger a thousand dollars — and he understands that his own payment is what decides which; the letter would not have reached him otherwise. In the second, a man is asked for a hundred dollars after a coin has already fallen, and is told that the payment would have earned him ten thousand in the world where the coin fell the other way, and that he will never stand in that world to collect. I want a single poem to serve as the epigraph to both, and I want it built so that it has to be read twice.

Write it as two equal halves of ten to fourteen lines each, in the same metre and rhyme scheme, the second half a near-mirror of the first — same scene, same order of images, only a handful of words changed. One refrain line must appear in both halves and mean something different in each. Within either half, the reader must not be able to tell whether this is the world where the coin came up heads or the world where it came up tails; that fact has to remain outside the poem. The man who pays and the man who is paid must never share a half, and the coupling between the letter and the payment must be recoverable from the poem's own structure. Use no decision-theory vocabulary: the difference between reckoning by what an act causes and reckoning by what an act reveals about the world has to be carried entirely by the substitutions between the halves. Close without settling whether the payment was folly or obligation, and let a reader who knows these puzzles be able to say which half speaks for which reckoning.
