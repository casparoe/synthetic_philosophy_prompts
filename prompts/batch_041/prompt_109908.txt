Exam question: Consider this case. A hiker is trapped under a boulder in a canyon. Two park rangers, Ana and Ben, each have a winch. Either winch alone would be sufficient to lift the boulder, but the winches cannot be used together (the cable geometry means only one can be rigged at a time). Ana is on duty and responsible for the canyon. Ben is off duty but happens to be nearby with his personal winch. Ana sees the hiker, thinks "not my problem today," and does nothing. Ben sees the hiker, thinks "Ana is on duty, she'll handle it," and does nothing. The hiker dies. In lecture we distinguished: (i) counterfactual dependence: an omission O is a but-for cause of outcome E iff, had the agent not omitted (i.e., had she acted), E would not have occurred; (ii) causal status: whether something is a cause at all; (iii) causal selection: among the but-for causes, which one we call "the cause" based on norms, roles, or abnormality; and (iv) the distinction between "omission as cause" (an absence causing an event) and "causation of an omission" (an event causing an absence). Question: Are Ana's and Ben's omissions causes of the hiker's death? Is either "the cause"? How does the distinction between causal status and causal selection bear on your answer? Does the fact that omissions are absences rather than positive events show that they cannot be causes? Is this case a case of overdetermination? Defend your answers.

Student answer: Ana's omission is a but-for cause of the death: had she rigged her winch, the hiker would have survived. Ben's omission is not a but-for cause. It's true that if Ben had acted, he would have lifted the boulder, but Ana would still have omitted—she was on duty and had already decided not to act—so Ben's acting would not have changed the fact that Ana omitted. Since the winches can't be used together, Ben's action would just have replaced Ana's action, not added to it. So the outcome would still have occurred. Thus only Ana's omission has causal status. Causal selection is a separate issue. Ana is "the cause" in the ordinary sense because she was on duty, whereas Ben was off duty. This supports the norm-based account: causal status is about counterfactual dependence, while causal selection is about norms and roles. It also shows that omissions can be causes despite being absences. An omission is not a positive event, but it can still be a cause because causation relates facts or propositions, not just events. The only exception is when the omission is itself caused by something else, as Ben's omission was caused by Ana's being on duty; that is a case of causation of an omission, not omission as cause, so it doesn't count as a cause of the death. Finally, this is not a case of overdetermination, because overdetermination would require both omissions to be but-for causes, and only Ana's is. If anything, the case shows that counterfactual dependence is necessary for causation: the one omission that lacks it (Ben's) is not a cause. So I'd say Ana's omission is the cause, Ben's is not, and the absence objection fails.
