For thirty-five years I was an experimental physicist. I retired four years ago and have since been reading philosophy with more enthusiasm than system, and I have now been asked to run a single session in a lifelong-learning course on the philosophy of science. The class is about eighteen adults — a few with scientific or medical training, most without — the session lasts ninety minutes, and the room has movable chairs and a whiteboard but no projector I can count on. I must design the session myself, and I would like it to be something the participants do rather than something they listen to. The topic is not neutral for me: in the laboratory I was always already assuming that tomorrow's readings would resemble today's, and I never once had to justify that assumption to a grant panel.

The topic is the no-free-lunch theorems in machine learning and the problem of induction. I have come to think two claims are usually run together, and I want the session to keep them apart.

The first is a theorem about averages. Take any two prediction rules. If you average their performance over all the worlds there could be, weighting each world equally, their expected error is the same. That is a claim about an average over problems, not about any single problem: on any given problem one rule may be very much the better. So the theorem does not say that all methods are equally good; it says that an advantage on some problems is always paid for by a disadvantage on others, and that the equal weighting of worlds is itself an assumption — and a very strong one — about which worlds there are.

The second is Hume's point. Whatever we offer as a justification for trusting induction appears to presuppose induction already, and no quantity of data removes the circularity.

I do not want the first claim to be swallowed up by the second. By the end of the ninety minutes I want the group to see the first as the claim that the lunch is never free: generalisation has to be bought with an assumption about the world, and that assumption cannot be read off the data. They should feel the price, not merely be told it.

Please design the session. The rules and the scoring should need no money, and the discovery must be theirs. Three things matter to me particularly. First, at some point they should be holding identical data and writing down incompatible predictions, each backed by a rule that fits the data perfectly, and only afterwards realise that the tie was broken by something they brought with them and not by the data. Second, there should be a round in which one team is allowed to design the world after seeing everyone's rules, so that every rule fails, followed by a round in which the world is a natural one, so that the rules whose bias matches it win. Third, the debrief must make clear that the second round's advantage was purchased as well. I imagine the data as short strings of two symbols, since everyone can follow those, but you may choose otherwise. Please do not use 'grue', and please do not let the phrase 'problem of induction' be spoken before the debrief.

Give me the whole thing as one JSON object, with exactly these fields:

- title: the name of the activity, no more than ten words.
- aim: one sentence stating what participants should come away seeing.
- experience_arc: an array of strings, in order, describing what the participants feel or notice at each stage — the sequence of experiences, not the sequence of instructions.
- materials: an array of strings; everything needed, down to cards and pens.
- room_setup: how the room should be arranged.
- timeline: an array of objects, each with phase (string), minutes (number), facilitator_does (string) and participants_do (string); the minutes should sum to ninety, with the debrief appearing as its own phase or phases.
- rules: an array of strings giving the formal rules — how predictions are committed before any data are seen, how teams are formed, how the world-designer is chosen and what they are allowed to know, and how a round ends.
- scoring: an object with per_round, overall and ties, each a string.
- withheld_vocabulary: an array of strings — the words the facilitator must not say before the debrief.
- debrief_map: an array of objects, each with moment (a moment in the activity), idea (the idea it maps to) and question (the question that does the mapping). At least one entry must map to the averaging claim and at least one to Hume's point, and the entries must say which part of the activity corresponds to which.
- predictable_failures: an array of objects, each with what_goes_wrong, why and remedy.
- cannot_reproduce: an array of strings — what the activity cannot show, and which part of the idea I shall have to supply in words.
- briefing_corrections: an array of strings, at most three — anything in my statement of the two claims that you think is wrong or misleading.
