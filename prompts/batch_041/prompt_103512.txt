For most of my working life I was a field biologist, and my research concerned insect behavior, principally among the social Hymenoptera. Since retiring I have been sitting in on an upper-level undergraduate course in the philosophy of mind, and this term I have been drafted into grading. The unit we have just finished dealt with mechanical versus flexible behavior and its bearing on the question of free will; it turned on Hofstadter's coinage "sphexishness" and on the provisioning behavior of the digger wasp Sphex. The natural history is familiar ground to me, but the philosophical literature is not, and I would rather not return a script until I have a second opinion on it.

The examination question the students were set was as follows.

Explain the phenomenon that Hofstadter calls sphexishness, taking the behavior of the digger wasp Sphex as your illustration. Then address two further questions. (i) What does the wasp's behavior establish, if anything, about the wasp's own mental life? (ii) Does the example bear on the question whether human action can be free? In answering, engage with at least one philosopher who has made use of the example.

One student's answer is reproduced in full below.

The term sphexishness was introduced by Hofstadter, and it names a kind of behavior that looks purposeful and even intelligent from the outside but is in fact the execution of a fixed program, with no genuine choice at any point. The standard illustration is the digger wasp Sphex. The wasp was studied by Fabre and later discussed by Dennett, who quotes an account of it from Wooldridge's Machinery of the Brain. A female Sphex digs a burrow, stings a cricket to paralyze it, and drags it back to the nest by one of the cricket's legs. Before taking the cricket inside, she leaves it at the threshold and goes into the burrow alone to inspect it. If the cricket is moved a few inches during that inspection, the wasp will retrieve it, but only as far as the threshold, where she drops it again and re-enters to inspect. The sequence can be repeated indefinitely, because the inspection has to be completed before provisioning can proceed. The point of the example is that the wasp cannot register that nothing about the burrow has changed; the sight of the prey outside the burrow simply re-triggers the routine.

Hofstadter's use of the example is to make us uneasy about ourselves. If behavior this apparently deliberate can turn out to be mechanical, then much of what we take to be thoughtful human conduct might be sphexish in the same way, and we might have no way of telling from the inside.

As for the wasp's mental life: the experiment shows that the wasp has no inner life at all. If what we are watching is a fixed program, then there is nothing it is like to be the wasp; there is no subject undergoing the experience, only a mechanism running through a routine. That is why the case is such a serviceable one for philosophers who wish to deny consciousness to insects.

As for human freedom: Dennett uses the example to argue that free will is an illusion. The wasp's behavior is rigidly determined by its programming, and Dennett holds that human behavior is determined in the same way, by evolution and by the wiring of the brain. The wasp is therefore a picture of ourselves, and the felt sense of choosing is an epiphenomenal by-product. On this view the difference between the wasp and us is one of complexity, not of kind, and the word freedom names nothing real.

The example does, however, illuminate moral responsibility. Because the wasp acts from instinct and not from deliberation, we do not hold it responsible for what it does. By the same token, when a human being acts from instinct — in a fit of rage, say — we regard the act as less than fully free. So the wasp's sphexishness points to the general principle that responsibility requires the capacity to have done otherwise.
