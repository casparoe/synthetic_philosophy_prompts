I am a novelist some way into a book set in sixteenth-century Brazil, and I have been reading Montaigne's essay on the cannibals as I work. Before I commit to a structure, I want an audit of the position I have talked myself into, since I suspect it will not hold together.

Here is what I believe.

1. Moral judgment is internal to a form of life. There is no standpoint outside a people's own customs from which those customs can be assessed; Montaigne's observation that we call a practice barbarous only because it is not ours strikes me as the last word on the matter.

2. The conquest of the Americas was a moral catastrophe, and my book is meant as an indictment of it — of the enslavement, the forced baptisms, the men who burned their neighbours alive over religion both at home and abroad.

3. The narration must never editorialize. The reader should inhabit the war-captive ritual as its participants understood it, and never once hear the author explaining how to feel about what is happening.

4. A novel that renders atrocity beautifully is complicit in it. Whatever else the prose does, it must not aestheticize the killing.

5. My obligation is to the dead as they understood themselves — to the captive's own conviction that he was dying well, to the honour he and his killers took in it — and not to what would make these people legible or sympathetic to a modern reader. Anachronism is the one sin I will not forgive myself.

6. Fiction is a better instrument than argument for making a reader see a foreign practice from the inside; that conviction is the only reason I am writing a novel rather than an essay.

Which of these actually pull against one another, which pairs only appear to, and which single concession would reconcile the rest? Rank the real tensions by severity, say what each would cost me, and be specific about which commitment has to go. I have no idea which one I would surrender first, and I would rather find out now than at page three hundred.
