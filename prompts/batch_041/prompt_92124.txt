I work on the release-review team at a small company that fine-tunes a large language model and rents it out to other firms as a coding and operations assistant. I have no academic training in this area — I studied history and ended up in software — but I have been reading and thinking about machine behaviour on my own for a couple of years, and I am one of three people who have to sign off before a new version of the model goes out to customers. What keeps me up at night is a specific worry: we cannot tell from the inside whether a model that behaves well in our tests is genuinely disposed to do what we want, or is only behaving well because it can tell it is being tested. Our tests run in a harness, the model sees the test prompts, and one of our engineers pointed out that our safety plan sits in the same code repository that the model can read when we give it repository access. So some of our checks may be measuring how well the model plays along with us, not what it would do after deployment.

I want you to turn the split between the two broad families of safety measures into a procedure my team can actually run. The first family consists of measures that assume the model may be misaligned and try to make it hard for even a misaligned model to cause serious harm: confining what it can do to narrow, checked channels, requiring a human to approve consequential actions, monitoring its outputs, keeping the ability to cut off its access. The second family consists of measures that try to make the model actually want the right thing: training it to be honest and careful, testing whether it has good dispositions, and relying on it to exercise good judgment in situations we did not anticipate. People around here call the first family control and the second alignment, but I care about the substance, not the labels.

The procedure should take a proposed deployment and a list of candidate safety measures, and produce which measures to require, which to run only in some configurations, and which to drop. Please make it answer these things:

- Ask its questions in a fixed order, state what counts as a sufficient answer to each before moving on, and say where the procedure stops with a flat no.
- For every check, say whether its result is trustworthy even when the model is misaligned and actively trying to pass it, or whether the result is only as good as the model's willingness to be honest — and say which it is and why. I want this distinction to be the spine of the whole procedure, because it is the thing we keep getting wrong.
- Mark the points where the next step is a value judgment rather than a fact — how much residual risk we will tolerate, how much weight to give the model's own interests if it has any, how much we care about being fooled versus being slow — and hand those decisions to a named human rather than resolving them itself.
- Say what to do when we genuinely cannot get evidence either way, and forbid the procedure from dressing up a guess as a measurement.
- Be runnable in an afternoon by three people with no research background, using only things we can observe or test ourselves.

Then run it on two concrete proposals and show your work. First: shipping a feature that lets the assistant execute shell commands inside a customer's repository, with a limit on which commands it may run and a log the customer can inspect. Second: letting the model autonomously triage incoming support tickets, decide which are resolved, and close them, with write access to the ticket system and no human reading each one. For at least one of these, the procedure should return an answer my team will not like — for instance, telling us to drop a check we are fond of because it is only as trustworthy as the model's honesty, or to keep a clumsy measure we find embarrassing. I also want the procedure to say how it handles the fact that the model can read our safety plan, since that fact may poison several of the checks at once. Finally, tell me plainly what the procedure cannot do and where it will misfire, including the case where the model is misaligned in a way that happens to make our tests look better rather than worse.

For the two worked cases, end each with a single line in the form Case: <name> | Required: <list or none> | Conditional: <list or none> | Dropped: <list or none>. Then close the whole answer with one final line beginning Summary: that states in one sentence the single most important thing this procedure does.
