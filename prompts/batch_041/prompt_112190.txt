I run a fortnightly reading group on philosophy of mind, and we have just spent two sessions on Hofstadter's Aunt Hillary: the dialogue in which a colony of ants, every individual member of it as thoughtless as a single neuron, is nevertheless credited with a mind of its own — with beliefs, moods, and something like memory, none of which belongs to any ant. Two of us left convinced that she thinks; a third came back the following week with an argument meant to show that the whole idea is worthless, and I have not been able to say where it breaks. Here it is.

(1) What makes something a mind is its functional organization: if its parts stand to one another in the relations in which a brain's neurons stand to one another, then it has the mental states that organization supports, whatever those parts happen to be made of. (2) The ant colony is such a system: the traffic of the ants — the signals that recruit, alarm, repair, and recruit again — mirrors the signal traffic of a cortex closely enough that the same functional description applies to the colony as a whole, which is precisely why the beliefs and the moods are credited to the colony rather than to any ant. (3) So the colony has a mind. (4) But the organization mentioned in (2) is something we supply rather than something we find: for any physical system whatever — a waterfall, a heap of sand, the population of China wired together with radios so that each person stands in for one neuron — there is some way of pairing its states with the states of some machine under which it satisfies (1). The description is always available; the world does not have to cooperate. (5) So (1) and (4) together license us to attribute beliefs and moods to waterfalls and heaps of sand. (6) An account of mentality on which everything has a mind draws no distinctions and explains nothing. (7) So something here has to give.

Tell me which premise or step to reject, why it looked innocent, and what each of the alternatives costs. I would rather keep Aunt Hillary than concede that a heap of sand has moods, but I have no idea what that preference commits me to. In particular: if the repair is to strengthen the notion of functional organization so that the colony still qualifies while the waterfall does not, what exactly has to be added, and is that addition principled or an admission that minds need the right kind of parts after all? If the repair is to deny (2), what is the colony doing, if not thinking — and what then becomes of the thought that a brain is itself only a colony of cells, with nothing in it but neurons that think nothing? And if we keep (1) and (4) and simply swallow (5), what are we saying about the ants, whose inner lives we began by denying, and about ourselves? I would rather not be told that this is merely the problem of other minds; if that is the answer, say what follows from it and what it leaves untouched.
