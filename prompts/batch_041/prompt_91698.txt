I'm trying to wrap my head around infinite ethics and I keep hitting the same wall, so I want the actual explanation rather than just the name of the puzzle. Set it up like this: world A has infinitely many people, each at welfare level 1. World B is A plus one extra person, also at 1. World C is A plus one extra person whose life is utterly miserable, say at -1000. Here's what I can't get past: under the total view, B and C both come out infinite and so are apparantly indistinguishable from A; under the average view, every one of them has an average that converges to 1, so they're all identical again. An infinity of extra happiness, or one person's bottomless misery, makes no difference whatsoever. Please explain why both views fail here, and whether it's a defect in the views or something about infinity itself — I want the mechanism, not the label.

Then stress-test a fix, because a friend of mine is convinced it works. The fix: compare worlds lexicographically by welfare, starting from the worst-off person and working upward — better worst-off wins; if they tie, compare the second-worst; and so on. If a world has no worst-off member (say the levels are 1, 1/2, 1/3, ...), fall back on the infimum. Does this dodge the problem above? Where does it break? I already suspect it does something grotesque in cases like: one world where the single worst-off person sits at 0 and everyone else is at a billion, versus a world where everyone is at 0.001. And I'm worried the whole thing secretly presuposes that people come in a nice ordered line — what happens when they don't, or when the welfare levels have no minimum at all?

Last thing, the meta question I actually care about: is there any way to compare infinite worlds that preserves the intuition that adding a happy person is a real improvement, or do we just have to give that intuition up?
