I have been asked to give the commencement address this June at the small college in the town where I have spent my whole life. I am not an academic: I worked at the paper mill here for nineteen years until it shut down, and for the eleven years since I have driven a school bus on the same streets I walked to school on. The committee asked me because I am a familiar face around here, and I said yes before I had any idea what I would say. What follows is what I would like to say, and I would like you to write it.

For the last several years I have been working my way through the popular books that promise to explain the whole of human history in a single arc — the ones that trace the fates of continents to geography, the ones that locate the decisive turn in one change in the human mind, and the one that argues the familiar sequence of farming, then cities, then kings and states, is something we inherited rather than discovered. I have also read enough of the criticism of those books to believe that the trouble is not mainly this or that claim but the shape they all share. The story always runs from small to large and from simple to complex, and it always arrives, at the end, at us, which is convenient for us. I do not want to hand a graduating class that shape. I also do not want to stand at a podium and tell them history has no shape at all: that is not true as far as I can tell, and it is not a thing you say to people on the morning they are handed their diplomas.

So: a commencement address of roughly eight to ten minutes, in my voice — plain, first person, no jokes I would not actually make. Give the audience a genuinely large view of where human beings have been, and build it around a test I have been applying to these books for years: whether a person can leave, whether a person can refuse, and whether a person can change the rules they live under. I would like that test to be the spine of the speech rather than a topic in it — the thing that decides what can and cannot be said, the way a sermon is decided by its text.

Two things I would like you to use, because they are what convinced me the arc is a choice rather than a finding. First, the archaeological record of early cities: some settlements of tens of thousands of people show no palaces, no royal tombs, no central storehouses, no written administration — nothing that looks like a ruler — and at least one very large early city appears to have begun as a stratified settlement and then moved in the other direction. Second, that many communities deliberately lived one way in one season and another way in the next, the same people organizing themselves communally at one time of year and under something closer to command at another, on purpose. I do not want the graduates lectured on archaeology; I want one clear image of each, briefly, so that the rest of the speech is honest.

The audience is about a thousand graduates, their families, and the faculty, among them a handful of historians who will wince if I overclaim, and many parents for whom this is the first degree in the family. The local paper prints the address in full every year. So it has to be a real speech for the occasion, not an essay read aloud.

Constraints, and I care about these more than about anything else:

- Do not name authors or books. The graduates have not read them, the historians will hear it as a lecture, and I would rather the ideas stand on their own.
- Do not quote anyone. I cannot check a quotation and I will not stand up and misattribute a sentence to somebody.
- Do not let geography be destiny, and do not let farming be a trap or a one-way door.
- Do not imply that the present arrangement of the world was inevitable, or earned, or the end of the story.
- Do not end by telling them that the future belongs to them, that they are the next chapter, or that history is on their side.
- Do say something about what happened to this town when the mill closed. I have never managed to say it in a way that did not either file it under progress or inflate it into a tragedy of cosmic proportions, and I would like the speech to find the third thing.
- End with a charge, because the occasion requires one, but a charge that does not rest on a promise about how things will turn out.

If it comes out a little strange for a commencement address, that is fine. I would rather it be strange than familiar.
