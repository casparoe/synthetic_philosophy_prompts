I have been reading rationalist blogs and forums long enough to be tired of the standard summaries of the legal-moralism debate, so I want a dialogue that goes further into the machinery. Two equally competent legal philosophers are arguing at a small workshop over a case study from a fictional jurisdiction.

The first is a Hartian. She distinguishes the positive morality a society happens to share from the critical morality that can be justified, holds that the criminal law may not coerce people merely in order to keep the positive morality intact, and treats the claim that a shared morality is one fabric that unravels from any loose thread as an empirical hypothesis that carries the burden of proof. The second is a Devlinian. He holds that a society has the same standing to defend its moral code that it has to defend its political institutions, that the benchmark of that code is the morality of the ordinary reasonable person rather than the philosopher's or the reformer's, and that the law's interference is governed by a sliding scale of tolerance — deep and widespread indignation, and a practice well beyond the limits of tolerance — rather than by a bright line at harm.

The case they are arguing about: in the fictional Republic of Vantia, a community of four hundred people practises the returning, in which each member who has given written consent in advance has their body dismembered and composted in a private ceremony on the community's own land. No outsider has ever attended, and no living person is coerced. Parliament, responding to public revulsion, is considering a ban. Its committee report cites polling in which four-fifths of Vantians call respect for the dead central to their shared morality and call the returning far beyond what they can tolerate, and notes that in the neighbouring Republic of Kesh, which legalised a similar practice fifteen years ago, reported cases of hospital staff mishandling corpses and of fraud against the estates of the elderly rose sharply over the same period — though Kesh also rewrote its probate law and cut mortuary funding at the time.

Let them have genuine back-and-forth, each answering the other's actual moves, each conceding at least one point that is awkward for his or her own side and pressing at least one that is awkward for the other's, and neither winning by invoking an authority. I want them to locate a real crux, and in particular to reach ground the summaries never cover: whether the criminal law is itself one of the things that makes a morality shared, so that the claim that a shared morality requires legal protection threatens to be circular; and whether a legal-moralist regime is a ratchet, each law it passes becoming part of the shared morality it then claims a right to defend. End with each saying what evidence would move them. No citations or reading lists.
