I'm in a grad seminar on punishment theory -- the whole thing is basically organized around the retributivism vs. consequentialism split -- and our final paper has to "read a film through the theory," and I've claimed A Clockwork Orange becuase it's the obvious pick and now I'm worried I've picked something so obvious that I'll just write the paper everyone writes. So I want help getting a genuinely good reading out of it, and I'd rather it be in the weeds than safe. Here's where I've got to. The Ludovico technique is a consequentialist program in almost pure form -- deterrence and reform and incapacitation rolled together, with no pretense that Alex is getting what he deserves, only that he'll be made unable to do harm. The film seems built to make you recoil from it, and the chaplain's objection (roughly, that a man who can't choose the good isn't good, he's just a clockwork orange) is the retributivist complaint in its most sentimental form. But I don't think that's the whole story, and I want a reading that takes both camps seriously and then maybe turns on them. Two things I keep circling and can't resolve. First, there's a real position in criminology that says we should just stop asking about desert altogether and manage dangerousness the way we manage a disease -- so is Ludovico a fair reductio of that view, or does the film cheat by making Alex so monstrous that the humane version of the view never actually gets tested? Second, there's the retributivist line (the one that runs from Hegel through Herbert Morris's "Persons and Punishment") that being punished is itself a way of being respected as a responsible agent, that we have something like a right to be punished rather than merely treated -- and I can't tell whether the film dramatizes that line or quietly exposes it as comfortable self-flattery. There's also the thing that Burgess's novel ends with Alex outgrowing violence on his own and Kubrick cuts that chapter and ends on Alex fantasizing about it again, which I'm pretty sure changes what the film is saying about whether the state or the man is the real problem.

So what I want: a philosophical reading of the film that (a) shows how retributivism and consequentialism each actually show up in the Ludovico plot and in how the other characters respond to Alex, (b) says honestly where the film stacks the deck and where it resists both theories, (c) uses the right-to-be-punished idea and the "just manage dangerousness" idea as pressure tests rather than as illustrations of points I've already decided, and (d) ends by telling me what the film adds to the debate that the arguments on their own don't. Please don't just tell me the treatment is bad because it takes away his free will -- I know that and I don't think it's the interesting part, and I'll be annoyed if that's the whole answer. No citations needed, I just want the reading itself.
