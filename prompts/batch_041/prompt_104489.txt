I retired from a biology department about six years ago and have been working my way through philosophy ever since, mostly on my own, which means I'm constantly half-lost and constantly suspicious of my own instincs. Right now I'm stuck on Popper's The Poverty of Historicism -- the argument that you cant have laws of historical development the way physics has laws, that the historicist is chasing something that was never there. What keeps turning up in the secondary stuff I've been reading is a picture I recognize from my own field: the claim that a society, or a civilization, or "history" itself, is an organism. It grows through stages, it has a youth and a maturity and a decline, its parts are subservient to the whole and can't be understood in isolation, and its development unfolds by something like an inner logic rather than by anyone's choices. Whoever reaches for it -- I've seen it in writing about Spengler, and in a lot of Marxist writing, and honestly in some pop-science stuff -- uses it to license a big conclusion: that there are laws (or at least lawlike patterns) of historical development, that we can predict where things are heading, and that the right stance toward the future is diagnosis and prophecy rather than tinkering.

I'd like you to act as a philosopher of science with a serious background both in the philosophy of history and in theoretical biology, think this through carefully before you answer, and audit the analogy for me. What corresponds to what -- is "society" the organism, is the state an organ, is a class or an institution a cell, is "development" ontogeny or phylogeny or neither? Then tell me where the mapping breaks, and be specific about which breaks actually matter for the conclusion the analogy is being used to support and which are just decoration. In particular I want to know which features the organism picture quietly imports that a society plainly lacks (does a society have a determinate lifespan, a single germ line, a boundary between self and environment, a genome, a mechanism of reproduction?) and whether the historicist conclusion survives once those are on the table. I have my own suspicion, as a biologist, that "development" in organisms isn't law-governed in the sense the historicist needs, and that the analogy is doing the work rather than reporting a discovery -- but tell me if I'm wrong. If a better analogy is available for whatever the historicist is really reaching for, say what it is and why it does the job better; if you think no analogy will do it, say that too. Start by just listing the correspondences and the possible disanalogies before you commit to a verdict -- I'd rather see the working than the polished answer.

Finish with a line that starts "Verdict:" and gives me one of holds, holds-with-caveats, or fails, plus a short phrase naming which single disanalogy did the most damage to the conclusion.
