I teach a second-year seminar on decision theory and philosophy of economics, about 18 students, and I've got a two-hour session coming up on the centipede game and backward induction. I want to run it as an activity they play before I so much as say the words backward induction, because every year I explain the rollback argument and they nod, and then in discussion it's clear they don't feel it at all -- they think the argument is a trick and that passing is obviously the sensible thing. So I definately need a runnable game, not a lecture outline. My rough idea is pairs, four or five rounds, a pot of points that starts small and grows each time someone passes, and on your turn you can either take the pot now or pass it to your partner. If you take, you get the bigger share and your partner gets the smaller share, and the game ends; if you pass, the pot gets bigger and it's their turn. The last round has to be set up so that taking is clearly best for whoever moves there, and the round before that has the same shape, and so on back to the start. I want them to play it a few times with different numbers of rounds, then in the debrief see that the unique backward-induction answer is to take immediately, even though almost nobody does, and that the people who pass often end up with more points than the rollback predicts. Could you design the rules, the payoff table (readable at a glance, no algebra on the board), the materials, and a timeline that fits 45 minutes of play plus 30 minutes of debrief with 18 students? I'd also like a variant where each pair writes down a public promise before playing about what they'll do in the last round, and the promises are read aloud, so we can see whether cheap talk changes anything or just makes the backward induction more uncomfortable.

Then I need a seperate debrief that maps what actually happened onto the backward-induction argument -- not just announces the conclusion. I want them to notice the exact moment where their own reasoning says take and their own behaviour says pass, and I want them to see why the rollback is supposed to be forced even though it feels absurd. Please also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it: pairs colluding, someone taking early and killing the interesting bit, people misunderstanding the last-round incentive, the fake money being too low to make anyone care, that sort of thing, and what I should do when it happens. Be honest about what the activity can't reproduce -- e.g. whether it can really generate the paradox or only a tension between individual and joint payoffs, and what I'll have to supply by talking. And write it in British English, please, since I'll be handing it out here.
