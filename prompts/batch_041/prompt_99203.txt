So my mate Dan has been reading about expressivism for a seminar and we spent basically two hours on the bus arguing about it, and now I can't tell if he's actually got me or if he's just built a machine that eats itself. His argument goes like this, numbered, because of course he numbered it. (1) The meaning of a sentence is whatever mental state it's used to express. (2) "Lying is wrong" is used to express disapproval of lying. (3) Now take "If lying is wrong, then getting your little brother to lie is wrong" -- the bit "lying is wrong" shows up inside it, but the person uttering the whole conditional doesn't have to disapprove of lying at all; they might not care one way or the other. (4) So inside that conditional, the bit "lying is wrong" isn't expressing any disapproval. (5) But by (1), if it isn't expressing a state it can't be carrying its meaning, so it must either mean something different in there or mean nothing at all. (6) If it means nothing the conditional is gibberish; if it means something different then "lying is wrong" is ambiguous, and the argument "lying is wrong; if lying is wrong then getting your brother to lie is wrong; so getting your brother to lie is wrong" commits a fallacy of equivocation. (7) Either way expressivism can't explain why that little argument is so obviously valid. (8) So expressivism is false.

Dan thinks this is checkmate and honestly it does feel pretty tight, but something smells. I really want to keep expressivism for the plain moral sentences -- when I say "lying is wrong" I don't think I'm describing some fact out there, it feels more like I'm booing. And I definately want to keep the modus ponens; that inference is fine and anyone who denies it is being weird. But then I noticed his argument seems to work just as well against "but" and against slurs, and I'm fairly sure those embed fine ("If she's poor but honest, then...", "If that word is a slur, then you shouldn't say it"), and nobody concludes from that that "but" has no meaning.

So now I can't tell whether the problem is premise (1), or (4), or the step from (4) to (5), or whether expressivists just have to swallow a bullet somewhere. Tell me which step you'd give up, why it looked so propperly plausible in the first place, and what it actually costs the expressivist to abandon it -- and if the fix saves the moral case, does it also handle the "but"/slur cases for free, or is that a separate patch? Keep it under 400 words, and lead with the step you'd reject and the price tag in one sentence before you unpack it.
