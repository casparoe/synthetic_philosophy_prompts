ok so I'm in this seminar on AI alignment and we spent two weeks on low-impact / impact-measure ideas — the whole family of proposals that says an agent should try to avoid side effects, keep its footprint small, leave the world more or less as it found it, that kind of thing. The professor keeps saying this is supposed to be a way of getting safer behavior *without* first solving the whole value-specification problem, and a friend of mine thinks that's impossible. I tried to write up his argument cleanly and now I actually believe it, which is bad, because the conclusion is that the entire research program is either redundant or incoherent and I don't buy that. Here's the argument:

(1) An agent should avoid side effects, where a side effect is any change to the world beyond the one the agent was asked to bring about.
(2) You can only tell whether a given change is "beyond what it was asked to bring about" by checking it against what it was supposed to bring about.
(3) What it was supposed to bring about is set by its objective, and real objectives — "make me coffee," "clear my inbox" — never specify which of the many changes that follow are the ones that count.
(4) So to sort a change into "intended" versus "side effect," the agent has to fill the objective in, i.e. work out what the person would actually want, all things considered.
(5) Filling it in that way is just consulting a fuller specification of value.
(6) If the agent already has that fuller spec, the side-effect rule is redundant — it can just optimize the spec directly.
(7) If it doesn't, the rule is unusable — there's nothing for it to classify.
(8) So "avoid side effects" adds nothing on top of a full value spec, and does nothing without one.

What makes me nervous is that (9) nothing in there is special to side effects — swap "side effects" for "lying" or "breaking a promise" and the same steps seem to run, which would mean no short, finitely-stated norm can ever guide action until you've already got the complete axiology. That can't be right. So I'm pretty sure I'm supposed to reject something, I just can't see what. If I have to give something up I'd rather keep (1) — I think "don't mess with stuff you weren't asked to mess with" is a real, usable instruction and not just a disguised value spec — but I want to know what that costs me. Which step do you reject (or, if the argument is actually valid, which premise is cheapest to sacrifice), why did that step look so obviously true while I was writing it, and what exactly do I pay by giving it up? Give me the runners-up too, since I suspect you'll push me toward a different step than the one I'd pick.
