I'm a 4th-year phil PhD student and I've drawn the short straw on one session of a seminar called Proof, Games and Rationality — 14 students, about half philosophy and half CS, 90 minutes, room with movable tables and a whiteboard. They've had the usual dose of Nash equilibrium and backward induction, no combinatorics. The session before mine is on non-constructive existence proofs in maths, and my job is to give them a case where they actually feel the thing instead of nodding at it. What I want to build the session on is the strategy-stealing argument: you show that the second player in a symmetric game can't have a winning strategy, so if the game can't be drawn the first player wins — and you've proved that without having the faintest idea how to win.

The game, and please keep the rules exactly as I've got them (I've already printed boards, 4x4 and 7x7, happy to change sizes if you've got a reason): it's Hex, reskinned as ferries on a map so the CS half don't pattern-match it. Rhombus of hexagonal cells, n cells on a side. Players alternate putting a ferry on an empty cell; ferries never move or get removed. One player owns the two opposite shores running north-east/south-west, the other owns the other pair, and you win the moment your ferries form an unbroken chain joining your two shores. There are no draws in it — that's a topological fact about the board, they'll believe it on small boards but they can't prove it in 90 minutes and I'm going to have to just assert it.

What I want them to walk out with: (1) they've felt in their hands that a guaranteed win with no findable strategy is a real thing and not a riddle; (2) the argument comes out of their mouths, in their words, before I've named it; (3) they see that it has three load-bearing premises — the two players' options have to be symmetric, the game has to have no draws, and an extra move has to be something that can't hurt you — and that each of the three can fail. They should also notice that what the argument actually hands you is "the second player can't win", and that the last step to "the first player wins" is exactly where no-draws gets smuggled in. I don't care about the history, skip the Nash anecdotes, and nothing that requires looking anything up.

Hard constraints: no lecture up front; I don't say the conclusion before they do; nobody says strategy stealing until the debrief. I want at least one moment where they enact the steal with their bodies rather than talk about it — some way to feel "I made a throwaway move and now I'm playing your strategy" — and if you can't stage that honestly, say so and give me the next best thing. Also I want them to leave able to turn down a bet: I'm going to offer a chocolate bar to anyone who can beat me in a game where they have to move second, and after the argument they should all refuse it even though not one of them can tell me how to win going first. And I want a break-it round: three pre-printed rule cards, each one changing a single thing about the game, with the group working out for each which premise dies and whether the argument still gets them anything. One of the cards should be a game where the first player genuinely looses with best play — I've got one in my back pocket (four tokens, you take one or two, whoever takes the last token loses; I'm fairly sure the first player is just lost there) but if you've got something better for the same job, use it and show me it works. And tell me which of my three cards is the one that doesn't really break what its supposed to, because I'd rather hear it from you than from 14 students.

Give it to me in these sections, in this order, with roughly these lengths:

- Materials and setup (~150 words): what I print, board sizes, how the tables go, anything I need to prep.
- Run of show (~500 words): minute by minute for the full 90, with my lines only where they matter, including the seperate nudge sequence I use if they stall on the argument and what each nudge is for.
- Break-it cards (~300 words): the three cards as the students would see them, and for each, what premise it breaks, what to expect, and what the argument still gives them.
- Debrief (~350 words): the general schema as a template with blanks they fill in, plus a moment-by-moment map from what happened in the room onto the parts of it, and what I still have to assert rather than let them discover.
- What goes wrong the first time (~200 words): what to watch for and what to do about it, honestly.
- What the activity can't do (~150 words): the part of the idea the game can't reproduce and that I have to say out loud, and what I should not pretend the activity showed.
