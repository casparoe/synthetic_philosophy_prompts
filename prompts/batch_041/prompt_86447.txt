Ok, so I'm a TA for a second-year metaphysics course and I'm putting together a handout for the week on counterpart theory vs. transworld identity, and I keep hitting the same wall. My students read the Lewis and Kripke material and come away convinced the whole debate is one binary — either a person literally exists in more than one possible world or they don't — when really the live positions are combinations of about four or five independent comittments, and half of section is people defending the same view under different names while thinking they disagree. So I want you to build me a decision tree (or taxonomy, whatever's cleaner) that starts from commitments a student can actually have an opinion about and ends at a named position, built from the logical space first and the list of names second. Every branch node should be a question phrased so a reader can answer it without already knowing where it leads — no node that's just "do you accept counterpart theory?". Here are the axes I care about in particular: whether the other worlds are concrete universes like ours or abstract representations of ways things could be, or whether the reader thinks there's nothing there to argue about at all; whether an actual individual itself shows up again at other worlds or whether cross-world talk is really about distinct world-bound individuals related by resemblance; whether an individual's modal profile is fixed by something intrinsic to it or is a partly extrinsic, context-shifting matter of overall similarity; whether the reader thinks two worlds could differ with no qualitative difference between them at all (perfect duplicates, swapped roles); and whether the only possible worlds are recombinations of what already exists or whether there can be genuinely new things. Also please pull apart people who take the counterpart idea as a mere semantics for modal talk from people who buy the whole metaphysical picture, because those two get run together constantly and it drives me up the wall.

I need at least these leaves: full-on counterpart theory with concrete worlds; the actualist who still helps themselves to counterparts; transworld identity together with a real essentialism; the actualist who hands every individual an individual essence and locates modal properties in world-indexed predicates; the primitive-thisness view on which worlds can differ with no descriptive difference; a combinatorialism; and a quietist/deflationist leaf for the student who thinks de re modal claims aren't really truth-apt. For the diagnostic cases, use concrete ones — the statue and the lump of clay, a person who could have had entirely different parents, a world that duplicates ours up to some point and then diverges, a chair that could have been made of ice, and at least one case that forces the reader to say whether a perfect duplicate of them at another world is them or merely resembles them — rather than asking people which ism they like.

Where the tree can't actually separate two leaves, say so instead of fudging it; my suspicion is that transworld identity plus essentialism and the individual-essence actualism will collapse into each other, but tell me if I'm wrong. And flag any branch combinations nobody in the literature seems to occupy, with a one-line guess at whether that's because the combo is incoherent, just unattractive, or simply unexplored. Please write it in American English.
