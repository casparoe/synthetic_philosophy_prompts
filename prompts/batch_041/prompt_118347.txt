I get the boring version of the idea that an action only has moral worth if you did it for the right reasons — the shopkeeper who's honest just because it's good for business gets no credit. What I dont get is the stronger version, where the reasons you acted on have to be the very reasons that made the action right in the first place. Is that meant as a claim about the actual causal history of the action, or just about the agent — like a sensitivity condition in epistemology, where what matters is whether her motive would have tracked the right-making features across nearby cases? Explain what the stronger requirement is supposed to add over the boring one, and how each reading copes with three cases: someone who acts rightly from a decent motive that isn't the right-making one (she helps a stranger because she can't stand to walk past, when what makes helping right is that it's owed); someone who's overdetermined (she acts for the right reason but would have done it anyway out of vanity if that reason had been absent); and someone in whose deliberation the right consideration figures but who'd have acted identically without it, so it seems to do no work. Also, does any of this survive if the right reasons were never consciously before her mind at the time?
