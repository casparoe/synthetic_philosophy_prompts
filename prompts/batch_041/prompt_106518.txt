I sit on a working group at a large secondary school that is rewriting the 'learning dispositions' section of our end-of-year reports — the part where a teacher, alongside the grade, is asked to assert things like 'perseveres when a problem gets hard', 'contributes confidently in group discussion', or 'does not yet work independently'. Two years of complaints have convinced me we are asking teachers to make claims their evidence cannot support: one set of parents produced a child we had described as lacking confidence in class and who runs a lunchtime club with thirty members, and a selective school has asked us to justify a report saying a pupil had 'become a diligent worker' in Year 11 after two years of disengagement. The awkwardness is not that individual judgements are sometimes wrong; it is that we have no procedure for making them at all. A teacher sees each pupil four hours a week in a single setting, gets roughly ten minutes per pupil to write, has no training in any of this, and knows the report is signed off by the head and read by parents and admissions tutors. From my own reading, the trouble starts with the simple conditional analysis — a thing is disposed to produce a manifestation in conditions C exactly when, if it were placed in C, it would produce it — because the test conditions can be finkish (the disposition is genuinely present now, but the arrival of the conditions would itself destroy it), masked (the conditions obtain, but something blocks the manifestation while the disposition is left intact), or the manifestation can be mimicked (it occurs, but something other than the disposition produces it). A disposition can also be present and never manifested in anything we happen to see, which is roughly our situation with every pupil.

Please turn this into a procedure a teacher can actually run. I want the questions in the order they should be asked, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, what the teacher does when an answer is missing, mixed, or would take a term to get, and where the procedure stops with a no and the claim is simply not written. It must be usable in ten minutes by someone who has not read any philosophy, and it must be explicit about the points where the remaining question is no longer evidential but a value judgement — how much weight the report should carry, how much risk of a false claim we are prepared to accept, what counts as a fair opportunity for the disposition to show itself. Include a step for the mimic case, since a pupil can earn good reports for reasons that have nothing to do with the disposition, and a step for the common case where we cannot observe the pupil in the conditions the claim is about. Then run the procedure on three of ours. Amara never speaks in English seminar and three teachers have written that she lacks confidence in group settings, though she runs the chess club and chaired the meetings that organised last term's charity tournament. Devon was disengaged for two years and now works hard, but only since he joined a tutor group whose tutor checks his planner every morning. Yusuf is described by three teachers as collaborative and generous with his time, and the behaviour they cite is entirely confined to the weeks when group marks are awarded. Finish by telling me what the procedure cannot do, and where it will give the working group an answer it does not want to hear — I expect at least one of those three. I do not need a reading list or attributions; I need something the staff can follow.
