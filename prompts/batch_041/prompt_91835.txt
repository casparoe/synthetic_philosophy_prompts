I'm leading a session of a reading group on the metaphysics and ethics of virtual worlds, and for our next meeting I've been asked to run the case of the incident in the text-based world LambdaMOO in March 1993, the one Julian Dibbell wrote about. Please take my account of the facts as given rather than worrying about the history, since I can't check anything while we're talking and neither can you: a user whose character was called Mr. Bungle exploited a small program another user had written — a kind of voodoo doll — to take control of several other users' characters and describe actions on their behalf, and in a public room he made them perform sexual acts with him and with one another while others watched. No body was touched; what happened was text appearing on screens, produced by a program running on a server in California. The reactions are what I care about. The participants and most onlookers treated it as a real wrong that had really been done to someone, in a place, by a person; after several days of argument the offender's character was destroyed — the punishment included turning it into a helpless amphibian in front of everyone — and within a few months the community had built itself a formal petition-and-ballot system of self-government. A vocal minority, then and later, insisted that nothing had happened to anyone at all: the victims were characters, not people, the offender was a character too, and a character cannot be wronged, so there was nothing for anyone to punish.

I want the episode analyzed through a particular position, and then the position tested against the episode. Take as your starting point the realist view of virtual objects — that a virtual room or a virtual sword is not a fictional entity like a sword in a novel, but a genuine artifact, a real computational object with real causal powers, so that what happens in a virtual world is not merely represented but occurs. Granting that much, then separate the harm question into three accounts and hold them apart sharply. First, the fictionalist or authorial account: the episode is a piece of jointly enacted fiction, no character is a locus of harm, and any wrong lies in the real-world act of authoring or describing, not in what was described. Second, the experiential account: the harm is real because the experience of the people involved is real, but it is at bottom psychological — a matter of what it was like for those players to read those lines and to know that others read them. Third, the constitutive or performative account: the act was a real act of a distinctive kind, something in the neighbourhood of a speech act, whose wrong consists neither in fiction nor merely in distress but in the uptake — in what it did to the participants' standing with one another, in a community, before witnesses. For each account, say what it implies about what was harmed and in what sense, why the community had standing to respond at all, whether destroying the character (and that particular form of destruction) is intelligible as a response, and how it handles three features of the case: the publicness of the act, the fact that the apparent victim was a character whose player may have been away from the keyboard, and the fact that the instrument of the wrong was itself a virtual object.

Then reverse the direction: what does the episode show about the theory? Which of the three accounts is confirmed, which is embarrassed, and where does the realist premise about virtual objects end up doing no work, or too much work, in explaining why anyone thought a wrong had been done? Say where the theory is silent rather than making it speak, and tell me what would have to be true for your preferred account to be correct and what would count as evidence against it. I don't want a survey of the literature, a reading list, or citations; I want the analysis. Give me that in prose, then a table with exactly these columns — Account; What was harmed, and in what sense; Why the community had standing; What it explains in this episode; What it cannot explain; Verdict (supported, strained, or silent) — with one row for each of the three accounts and a fourth row for the realist premise about virtual objects itself, and close with a single sentence saying whether the case is, on balance, an argument for or against that premise.
