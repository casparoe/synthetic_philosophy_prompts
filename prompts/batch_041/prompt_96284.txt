I'm in a third-year seminar on philosophy of social science and we've just spent two weeks on what the readings call multipolar traps — the pattern where everybody would prefer things went differently, but no one can unilaterally stop, so the bad outcome is stable. (The reading keeps calling it Moloch, which I take it is just the poetry version of the same thing.) For my essay I want to use a distinction I came up with myself instead of just re-explaining the standard examples again, and I want to check that it's actually doing work. My distinction is between end-able races and treadmill races. An end-able race is one with a finish line: whoever gets there first ends the whole thing, so the trap is at least self-terminating — arms races to build the weapon first, a land grab, that kind of thing. A treadmill race has no finish line, because the payoff is purely relative: you have to keep paying just to stay where you are, and if you stop you don't just forfeit a prize, you lose ground continuously. I'm thinking of the way everyone in my department now answers email at midnight, or the way every streaming service has to keep buying content it can't really afford.

What I'd like is the most important implications of that distinction. Concretely: if I'm someone who wants to actually do something about a trap rather than just diagnose it, does the distinction change what I should try? My hunch is that end-able races are the ones where people panic and try to "stop the race" and it never works, whereas treadmills might be more tractable than they look, because there's no winner to hand the trophy to and the participants are all visibly miserable — but I dont know if that's a real argument or just a nice-sounding line. I'd also like to know whether the distinction predicts anything about intervention types: does making exit cheap for a single player help in one kind and not the other, or does reducing the number of players matter more in one? And please include the implications I won't like, like if it turns out my two categories collapse into one, or if the treadmill cases are the genuinely hopeless ones. One more thing: flag which implications depend on empirical assumptions I'm smuggling in without noticing, since I have to defend this in seminar next week and there's a guy in the group who always asks exactly that and I want to have an answer ready.
