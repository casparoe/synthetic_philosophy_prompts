I am a journalist who covers social policy and the ideas that underpin it, and I am writing a long read on how the benefits appeals system reasons at its margins — the cases where the medical evidence is thin and something else is doing the work. To that end I have spent three months sitting in on the hearings of an independent panel that hears appeals against decisions on a disability benefit payable only on proof of incapacity for work. The panel issues short reasoned decisions, so I have a usable track record. Over the past year it has:

- allowed the appeal of a 38-year-old woman with severe anxiety and agoraphobia who leaves home only in her sister's company, but who volunteers two mornings a week in a charity shop with that sister beside her;
- allowed the appeal of a 61-year-old man in the early stages of dementia, still able to perform long-familiar routine tasks but unable to learn a new till or rota system;
- allowed the appeal of a 29-year-old man whose migraine incapacitates him for roughly eight days in twenty-eight, and who has twice been dismissed for sickness absence, on the ground that no employer in the real labour market would keep him on;
- dismissed the appeal of a 54-year-old former roofer whose knees no longer permit kneeling or climbing but who can sit at a desk for an hour at a stretch, on the ground that desk work exists;
- dismissed the appeal of a 45-year-old man with chronic low back pain, an unremarkable scan, a GP note that describes pain behaviour rather than demonstrable pathology, and two abandoned courses of physiotherapy;
- dismissed the appeal of a 33-year-old woman with well-controlled type 1 diabetes and no complications, on the ground that a managed condition is not an incapacity.

Next month the panel hears a 47-year-old man with a long history of alcohol dependence, now abstinent for fourteen months and attending a weekly mutual-aid group; mild alcohol-related changes in the liver, but no cirrhosis; no qualifications and six years out of paid work; and some fifteen hours a week spent caring for his mother, who has advanced Parkinson's disease. His GP supports the claim. He says he could not hold down a job and do the caring.

I do not need the statute or the case law, and I am not asking you to reconstruct the law. What I want is the panel's own logic. Predict how it will decide; set out the two or three rules that fit its existing decisions equally well and say what each of those rules predicts about this case; give me your best guess and the reason for preferring it; and name the case I should watch for in the coming year that would tell those rules apart. If you think the existing decisions genuinely underdetermine the outcome, say so, and tell me what principle the panel would be committing itself to on either reading. Please write in British English.
