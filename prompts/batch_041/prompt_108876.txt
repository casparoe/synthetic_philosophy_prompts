so in philosophy we did this peer grading thing where you swap answers with someone and mark theirs, and i ended up with this one. the question the teacher set was:

"Goodhart's law is the idea that a number that was a decent way of keeping track of something stops being a decent way of keeping track of it once people start being judged on it. Explain why that happens, using a school that's judged by the share of its students who pass the state test, and say what it shows about running schools by numbers."

and here's the answer i have to mark:

"Goodhart's law is about a measure going bad once it's used as a target. Say a school is judged by the share of students who pass the state test, and its funding and its rating depend on that number. Then everyone's behaviour shifts: teachers spend more weeks drilling the tested material and drop art, music and science because those don't count; the school steers weaker students into easier tracks so they don't drag the average down; classes practice the format of the test instead of the subject. Nobody has to lie for this to happen, the teachers are just doing what they're now paid to do. The result is that the pass rate stops tracking how much the students actually know. And since the pass rate went up while the real learning went down, the law is really about the thing you care about getting worse, not just about the number getting less useful. So the lesson is that you should measure the thing you actually care about — with the right measure the problem never comes up."
