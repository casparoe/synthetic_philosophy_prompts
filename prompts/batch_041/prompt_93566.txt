I have no formal training in politics or philosophy, but a single word has been bothering me for years, and I would like to see the problem worked out as a dialogue.

I grew up in the United States, where "liberal" names the left-of-center coalition: pro-welfare-state, pro-civil-rights, at ease with regulation. When I later lived in the Netherlands, I found that the people around me used liberal — liberaal — for something close to the opposite: free markets, small government, distrust of the nanny state. In Britain the word seemed to mean something else again. What struck me is that each side treats its own usage as the plain one and the other's as a provincial drift or a corruption. I have also noticed a few smaller oddities that I cannot fit together: the American sense seems to have no clean European equivalent, so that the nearest matches are "social democrat" or "progressive"; the European sense has no clean American equivalent, though "libertarian" is not quite it either; and the word flips depending on what it is attached to — a "neoliberal" is a free-marketeer, a "liberal interventionist" is a hawk, an "illiberal" is someone who breaks the rules of a free society.

I would like a dialogue between two people, one speaking for the American usage and one for the European, each genuinely convinced that the other's usage is the aberration. Neither may be a fool or a caricature; both should be equally sharp and equally fair, and each should answer what the other actually said rather than talking past them. They should not merely trade definitions. They should press each other with concrete cases — a ban on face coverings in public, a hate-speech prosecution, a wealth tax, drug legalization, epidemic lockdowns, or whichever of these actually do the work — and test whether they are disagreeing about a word or about politics. I do not want them to shrug and declare that it is all just semantics; if part of the dispute is verbal, I want them to isolate exactly which part is verbal and which part survives as a genuine clash of commitments. One of them should also press the point that the word does not merely divide America from Europe but splits Europe against itself, and the other should push back on that.

Please write it in the following sections, in this order:
- Setting: two or three sentences establishing who the two speakers are and where they are talking. About 50 words.
- The dialogue: the conversation itself, in alternating turns of roughly ten to fourteen exchanges, each speaker responding to the other's last point. About 900 to 1,200 words.
- Where they landed: a short coda, in a neutral voice, stating what the two turned out to be disagreeing about once the labels were set aside, and whether any of it was merely verbal. About 150 words.

Please do not include citations, references, or a reading list — the dialogue and the coda are all I want.
