So I somehow ended up on the welfare review committee at the research institute where I work. It used to be just the animal-care people, but last year our director expanded its remit to cover "non-biological systems" as well, which is how I now spend my Tuesday nights reading proposals about reinforcement learning. I'm a cell biologist. This is not my field.

Anyway, there's one case on the agenda for Thursday and I have to circulate the committee's written opinion beforehand, so I need you to draft it. Here's the situation.

A group in the computational neuro wing wants to build two kinds of artificial agent and train both to avoid a noxious input signal. Agent A is basically a reflex: signal comes in, agent avoids it, and thats it. No model of itself, no representation of the signal as meaning anything, just a controller keeping a variable in range. Agent B is built differently. It maintains a running model of its own condition and "integrity," the noxious signal is fed into that self-model as a threat to it, and the agent can trade off avoiding the signal against other things it wants. The team wants to run both through thousands of aversive training episodes, look for patterns we associate with suffering (generalized avoidance, flattening of behavior, willingness to give up other rewards to make the signal stop), and then delete most of the instances.

Our policy — written by a philosopher the director hired, and honestly I think it quietly assumes the answer — says a system gets protection if it "has a stake in its own condition." It goes on to say that having a stake is more than running a control loop that keeps a variable in range; the system's own states have to be able to go better or worse from its point of view.

The committee is split three ways and I can't get them to converge. One member says that if B satisfies that criterion then this is essentially vivisection on a possible person, and we should reject it flat out. Another says "point of view" is precisely the sort of thing you can never verify from the outside, and a welfare rule built on an unverifiable criterion will end up protecting a thermostat, so we should approve and let the science run — finding out is the whole point. The team themselves argue that refusing just exports the work to labs with less oversight, and that you cant know whether these systems matter without building them.

There's also a worry I keep raising and can't get a straight answer on. The study's design seems to presuppose that the difference between A and B — feedback loops, a self-model — is the difference that matters morally. But the two might be running the same computation laid out in different wiring, and I'm not at all sure wiring is where the moral weight lives. Or that it isn't.

Draft me the opinion I'll read out Thursday. It needs to land on a concrete decision — approve, reject, or approve with conditions — with a time frame and conditions the team could actually act on, and it has to give the strongest honest version of whichever side it rules against. Also say what evidence would make the committee revisit this later, because I am not doing this every quarter.
