I'm a backend engineer at a mid-size payments company, about 900 people, and I somehow ended up on our incident review board. Seven of us: two SREs, a PM, our support lead, someone from legal, and a rotating engineer seat, which is me this quarter. We vote Thursday on a case and I'm supposed to write the opinion that gets circulated beforehand. I have no philosophy background at all, which will matter in a second.

The case. March 3rd we had a 22 minute partial outage. A feature flag flip killed a cache-warming job, retry traffic piled up, and the outage ended when our circuit breaker tripped and drained traffic on its own. Nobody rolled anything back, the breaker did it. But our published postmortem has a line that reads roughly: at 14:02 the on-call, Priya, decided to hold and keep watching; at 14:07 rollback initiated. Priya says that is not what happened. She says she was still reading dashboards at 14:02, that she wrote a message in the incident channel saying she was holding off, and that she wrote it after the breaker had already tripped. So the timeline has her deciding something before the thing that supposedly prompted the decision.

I dug into the timeline tool and it's worse than that. It merges log streams from three services and sorts them by each service's own clock. The gateway's clock runs about 90 seconds ahead. One service only emits its lines when a batch flushes, so its entries carry the flush time and not the event time. And the human decision in the timeline is really just the timestamp Slack put on a chat message. Two of our engineers pulled the raw logs and got two different orderings of the same events. The timeline isn't a recording of what happened, it's a rendering of it. And I can't tell you whether the record of Priya's message is wrong because the system rewrote it after the fact or because nothing was ever recorded in the first place. From where I sit those two failures look identical in the output, and that bugs me more than it probably should.

Why it's a fight: one action item from the postmortem is assigned to Priya and it's being cited in her mid-year review as evidence of a judgment problem. She's appealed to us. Three of us want to strike the individual action item, and two of those three also want to kill the single root-cause field and the decision-point line in our template entirely. The other four say that if the board can't say who decided what and when, nothing gets fixed and the next incident is worse, and that a pile of contributing factors is just a way for everyone to escape.

One more thing, which is probably the real reason I'm writing you. We're separately considering adding a field to our config service so every state change gets attributed to a decider, human or bot, with a timestamp. Our staff engineer says that just rebuilds the thing Dennett spent a whole book demolishing, she calls our current template the Cartesian theater of incident review, and another engineer says Dennett's multiple drafts model already settled that there's no moment where the decision happens, so the template should stop pretending there was one. I don't know if either of them is using it right. My instinct is that a claim about consciousness doesnt automatically tell us what belongs in an incident template, but I also don't want to be the guy who waves off a real point because it came from a book about brains.

Draft the opinion I'll circulate. One page, 700 words or so. It needs:

- a clear recommendation on the Priya finding and on the template, with a date for doing it (we vote Thursday, her review cycle closes on the 20th)
- the actual proposed wording of whatever template lines you want to change, not a description of them
- a plain-language account of what the multiple drafts / Cartesian theater thing does and doesn't imply for a review process like ours, and where the analogy breaks down. Half the board will definately glaze over if you wave at a philosopher, so no citations, and if you bring up an idea, one sentence on what it is
- an honest run at the strongest version of the accountability side's argument, not a strawman
- a direct answer to whether attributing every state change to a decider in the config service just rebuilds the problem, and if it does, what you'd log instead
