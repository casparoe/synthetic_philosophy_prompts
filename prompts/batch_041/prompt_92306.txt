I am a novelist, and in three weeks I am to give the wedding toast for two of my oldest friends, both of whom I met in graduate school — one was writing on the metaphysics of modality, the other on the philosophy of biology — and the room will be split almost exactly down the middle. Half of the guests are people from that period, who will hear every modal verb in the speech and flinch at any of them that is loose; the other half are the couple's families, warm and funny and without a day of metaphysical training between them, who will rightly expect a toast and not a seminar. My trouble is that the ordinary furniture of a wedding toast has been taken away from me. I cannot say that the two of them were meant to find each other, or that the universe conspired, because I have spent too long with a view I have seen called origin essentialism — the claim, usually traced to Kripke, that a person could not have come from any sperm and egg other than the ones that actually produced them, that your very identity is welded to that single pairing. If that is right, then each of them was, in the strongest possible sense, inevitable from the moment those two cells met, while the meeting of the two of them was a wild contingency that very nearly did not happen. The toast has to honor both, and the stock phrases collapse them into one.

Worse, the view has soft edges exactly where I need firm ground, and I would like the speech to be honest about that rather than tidy. There is the old problem of twinning: if a single fertilized egg can divide into two people, then origin from that zygote cannot be what makes each twin the individual they are, or else the two would be one person; and there is the puzzle of whether what matters is the particular sperm, the particular egg, or only the combination — whether, in the counterfactual where the same egg met a different sperm, we would have the same person with different genes or a different person altogether. I would rather gesture at that instability than pretend it away. What I want from you is the toast itself: five minutes, delivered by me, warm, funny where it can be, and rigorous enough that the philosophers in the room will not wince. Please build it so that the modal structure does the work. I have in mind a single sentence that recurs three or four times across the speech with only the modal verb changing — they could have, then they could not have, then they would have — so that the audience feels the difference between the necessity of a person and the contingency of a meeting without ever being lectured about it. It should end as affection and not as an argument, it should be honest that the metaphysics underneath is contested, and it should not, under any circumstances, contain the words meant to be.
