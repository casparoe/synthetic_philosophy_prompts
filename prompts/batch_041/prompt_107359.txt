I'm in a third-year seminar on Kant's practical philosophy and last week we did his reply to Constant about the murderer at the door — the one where he says you mustn't lie even to save the victim. Pretty much everyone in the room thought it was insane, which made me want to defend something in the vicinity, so I wrote a draft. My supervisor's feedback was "either original or confused, I genuinely can't tell which," which wasn't super helpful. Can you give me comments? I care most about (a) whether I've actually got Kant's position right or whether I'm beating up a strawman, (b) whether the central move is genuinely interesting or just a relabelling of stuff that's already in the literature, and (c) anywhere the thing just breaks. Be blunt.

Here's the draft, more or less as it stands:

The usual fight goes: Kant says don't lie, common sense says obviously lie, and then everyone either decides Kant is a monster of rigorism or rescues him by saying he never really meant it and the example is just about the general duty not to lie. I think both camps are answering the wrong question. The interesting question isn't *whether* you may lie to the murderer, it's *to whom* the duty of truthfulness is owed, and *who has standing to demand it*.

My proposal: split the duty of truthfulness into two parts. First, a duty owed to humanity (or to oneself as a rational being) not to corrupt the practice of truthful communication — on Kant's own picture this one is unconditional. Second, a claim that a *particular* interlocutor has on you to be told the truth — and this one is not unconditional, it's conditional on the interlocutor not himself being engaged in a wrongful project that uses you as a tool. The murderer has forfeited his claim, because you can't invoke the norm of truthful communication to hunt down your victim and then complain when the norm isn't honoured toward you. So the lie is still a violation of the first duty — Kant is right that you do wrong — but it isn't a wrong *done to him*, and that's why our intuitions here aren't as crazy as they look. He has no standing to resent it.

The part I think is new is the extension: I claim this same distinction explains a whole range of cases where the "Kant says no lying" line seems obviously false. Lying to a court under oath is different from lying to the murderer, not because of the consequences, but because the court is an institution we've authorised to demand truth, so the claim component is intact even when the stakes are trivial. Lying to a kidnapper about where the money is, lying to a nosy neighbour, lying to a border guard about your religion — all differ in whether the second component is present, not in how bad the fallout is. And I want to say the second component is present exactly when the interlocutor is participating in good faith in the practice of communication, i.e. isn't exploiting your truthfulness to do wrong.

Then there's a harder case I'm stuck on: the murderer asks where the victim is, and you say "I don't know" — which is true, because you saw him leave five minutes ago and genuinely don't know where he is now. Most people say that's fine. But on my view it's the *same* violation of the first duty as an outright lie (you're still trying to make him believe something false), yet it isn't a lie at all, so I get the wrong verdict unless I say the first duty is about misleading rather than lying — and if I say that, I think I've just become a consequentialist about communication. Is there a way out of that, or is that the crack that sinks the whole thing? Also, does the standing idea have any traction, or am I just inventing a fancy word for "he deserved it"?
