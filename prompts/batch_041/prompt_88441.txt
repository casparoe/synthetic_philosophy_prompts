I'm in a graduate seminar on scepticism and I've been asked to defend a position on Moore's proof of an external world, but I want to do it with numbers rather than rhetoric, so I'd like you to assign a credence to a proposition I'll state as sharply as I can. The proposition is this: that Moore's proof — or the most charitable reconstruction of it, in which the premises are ordinary perceptual claims of the form "I know that here is a hand" and the conclusion is the existence of an external world — succeeds as a proof, in the sense that it would give a rational agent who begins with genuine but non-dogmatic sceptical doubt a net reason to accept that an external world exists. By "succeeds" I do not mean merely that the argument is formally valid; I mean that it actually moves the epistemic needle for the agent in question. I'd like your own credence, stated as a number between 0 and 1, but what I care about far more is the derivation, so please decompose the question rather than defending a verdict.

In particular, I want a rough probability and a one-line justification for each of the following terms, or for whatever list you think is better: (a) the probability that an ordinary perceptual premise of the relevant kind can be known antecedently to any proof of the external world, rather than only on the far side of one; (b) the probability that the inference transmits warrant from premise to conclusion rather than merely presupposing the very thing it purports to establish — I have in mind the worry that a proof of this shape can only hand you the conclusion if you have already helped yourself to it; (c) the probability that a proof which cannot, as a matter of psychology or dialectic, convince a determined sceptic is nonetheless a proof, which is really a question about what kind of thing a proof is for; (d) the probability that the sceptical hypothesis is even a single coherent alternative, given that "the external world" may not name one proposition; and (e) the probability that the demand for a proof that does not beg the question against the sceptic is itself a legitimate demand rather than a rigged standard. Feel free to add a term if you think the decomposition is incomplete, but tell me what it is and why it belongs. Then give me the combination, tell me which single sub-question the final number is most sensitive to, and say what evidence or argument would move that term the most.

Two further things. First, distinguish clearly between the "dialectical" reading of the proof — where success means winning the argument against an actual or imagined opponent — and the "warrant" reading, where success means conferring justification on a believer regardless of what any opponent would concede; I suspect the number comes out very differently depending on which we are scoring, and I want you to say which reading you are answering under, or give me both. Second, separate what is genuinely settled in this literature from what is contested among people who know it well; where you are reporting a position that reasonable specialists dispute, mark it as disputed rather than describing it as the consensus, and flag any place where the reconstruction of Moore's argument you are using is one contested reading among several.
