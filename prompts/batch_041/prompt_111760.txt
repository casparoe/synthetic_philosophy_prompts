I hold a strong form of value incommensurability: for some pairs of options there is no fact of the matter about which is better, worse, or equally good, and the gap is not a failure of information — it would survive full information and unlimited reflection, and it is not merely vagueness in the comparative. I also reject the further claim that such items are comparable but neither at least as good as the other; I say they are simply unranked. What would count against this?

I want the map of the view's exposure rather than a survey of the literature. Rank the defeaters by how much they should move someone who holds the view: which arguments, cases, intuitions, or formal results are decisive, which merely awkward, and which the view's own defenders would concede as counting against them rather than dismissing as a change of subject. Say also what the view predicts that its rivals do not — rivals including the claim that such cases are comparable but neither better, the claim that the comparatives are merely vague, and the claim that hard choices are simply underdescribed. And say whether the view has been insulated into unfalsifiability: in particular, whether the claim that there is no fact of the matter can absorb any apparent comparison by redescribing it as the agent's projection or confusion, and, if so, whether that is a defect or an acceptable price.

Two things I especially want assessed. First: take a pair I call unranked and improve one member by an amount too small to notice — an extra afternoon of satisfaction added to a life. Either the improved option is now ranked against the other, in which case rankability turns on differences too slight to matter, or it is not, in which case repeating the improvement cannot restore rankability either, and a version of the option that is enormously better in every respect remains unranked against the other. I cannot see which horn to take, and I suspect my choice here determines whether the view is robust or merely local. Second, a distinction I have only recently begun to take seriously: it is one thing to say that two values — a kind of liberty and a kind of equality, say — admit no common measure; it is another to say that two options are unranked. Options come with determinate amounts of whatever values they carry, so the absence of a common currency for the values does not by itself entail that any pair of policies is unranked. If that distinction holds, my thesis is about options while the arguments for it are about values, and the inference between them is the weakest joint in the view. Is it?

I do not accept the money-pump line, since I deny that practical rationality requires a complete ordering, but tell me if I am dismissing it too quickly. I would also like to know what I would personally have to find persuasive for the view to be worth abandoning.
