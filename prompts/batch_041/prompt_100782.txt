Below is an examination question from an undergraduate philosophy of mind paper, followed by a student's answer to it. I am marking the script and would like a considered assessment rather than a general impression.

Question: Explain Searle's Chinese Room argument, state precisely the thesis it is intended to refute, and assess two of the standard replies. Does the argument succeed?

Student's answer:

Searle asks us to imagine a man who knows no Chinese locked in a room with a large book of rules. Chinese characters are passed in to him, and by consulting the rules he passes other characters back out. To an outside observer his answers are indistinguishable from those of a native speaker, so the room as a whole passes the Turing Test for understanding Chinese. But the man inside understands nothing; he is merely manipulating symbols according to formal rules. Searle concludes that running the right program cannot by itself produce understanding.

The thesis the argument is meant to refute is what Searle calls strong AI: the claim that a suitably programmed computer literally has a mind, with understanding and intentionality — not merely that a computer can be used to model mental processes (weak AI, which Searle accepts). The nerve of the argument is the distinction between syntax and semantics. A program is a set of formal, syntactic operations; understanding requires semantic content. No amount of symbol-shuffling generates meaning, so no computer running a program can understand.

Two replies are standard. The systems reply concedes that the man does not understand Chinese but insists that the understanding belongs to the whole system, of which he is only a part. This reply fails: understanding has to be located somewhere, and if the man does not have it and the rulebook does not have it, then no part of the system has it, and so the system as a whole cannot have it either.

The robot reply grants that symbol manipulation is not enough and adds that the system must be embodied — placed in a robot that interacts causally with the world. Searle's rejoinder is that this merely adds further inputs and outputs to the same formal manipulation, and so changes nothing. In fact the robot reply is really just a version of the systems reply, since both locate the understanding somewhere other than the person in the room.

The argument therefore shows that no computer could ever understand anything, and that functionalism is false. Mental states cannot be identified with computational states, because computation is defined purely syntactically.
