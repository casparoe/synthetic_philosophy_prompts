I'm a third-year philosophy undergraduate, and this term I'm taking a seminar on paradoxes of the infinite. My professor has assigned each of us to lead one session for the department's open reading group — forty minutes, a whiteboard, an audience of about fifteen that includes graduate students from economics, physics, and law alongside a few undergraduates. None of them have any set theory, and most will arrive believing that "infinity" just means "a very large number." My session is on the problem of infinite ethics, and what I want from you is a plan for how to teach it rather than an explanation of it: the order the ideas have to come in and what depends on what, the single example that should carry the whole session and what it must have and lack, the misconceptions I should expect and the exact point in the sequence where each one should be headed off, the check at the end that would show the problem has landed rather than merely been heard, and what I should openly refuse to cover at this level, with a word on why.

To be concrete about the target, so you know what the plan has to reach: I want them to see why the ordinary assumption that value can simply be totalled over a population becomes incoherent once the population is infinite. Take a world with infinitely many people, all at a decent level of wellbeing, and then a second world identical except that it also contains one additional person living a very good life. The natural thought is that the second is better; but if a world's value is the total of its inhabitants' wellbeing, both totals are infinite and the extra life is simply swallowed — and the same reasoning says that adding one person in agony does not make things worse either. Now change the second world so that everyone in it is worse off than in the first, and the totals are still both infinite, so the totalling approach now says the two worlds are equally good, which is plainly false. I can get them to feel that something has gone wrong. What I cannot see is how to sequence the material so that the arithmetic feels like it is doing philosophical work rather than sitting alongside it as a mathematical curiosity, and I keep worrying that the example I find most striking is the one they will read as a puzzle about maths rather than about ethics — so if the case I have sketched should not be the carrying example, say what should replace it and why.

Please make committed choices: pick the ordering and the carrying example and defend them, rather than laying out three possible approaches for me to choose between. Lead with the spine of the plan in a single sentence — the one-line statement of the order in which the ideas must go — and then give the reasoning, without preamble or restating the setup. And take a definite stance on one point rather than hedging: whether the session should end by resolving the problem or by deliberately leaving the audience with it unresolved, and why that is the right ending for this particular audience.
