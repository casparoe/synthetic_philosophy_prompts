At our reading group we spent an evening on a puzzle that a visiting friend had constructed after we had spent a week on superrationality, and two members, Mira and Dev, ended up on opposite sides of it. I have set out the puzzle and the substance of their exchange below. I should like you to adjudicate the exchange move by move rather than simply declaring a winner.

The puzzle. Twenty people are placed in separate rooms. Each is told the rules and told that the other nineteen have been told the same rules. No communication is possible, there will be no future interaction between them, and each cares only about her own payoff in this game. Simultaneously, each chooses either TAKE or PASS. If no one chooses TAKE, every player receives £100. If exactly one player chooses TAKE, that player receives £1,000,000 and every other player receives £0. If two or more choose TAKE, every player receives £0. Every player knows all of this.

Mira: Pass. Since we are all in the same situation and reasoning from the same premises, we shall all make the same choice; the only candidates are all TAKE and all PASS. All TAKE pays each of us nothing; all PASS pays each of us £100. A principle of choice that, followed by everyone, leaves everyone worse off than the alternative cannot be the right principle. So the choice that survives the question 'what if everyone did this?' is PASS.

Dev: That is magical thinking. My decision does not reach into the other nineteen rooms and move anybody's hand. Fix what the other nineteen do, and ask what is best for me. If none of them takes, I get £100 by passing and £1,000,000 by taking. If exactly one of them takes, I get nothing either way. If two or more of them take, I get nothing either way. So taking is never worse for me and is sometimes £999,900 better. I am being asked to surrender a free option in exchange for a feeling of solidarity with strangers I shall never meet.

Mira: You are treating the other nineteen as a fixed background, but they are not a background; they are reasoners in the same position, working from the same facts. If I take, that is strong evidence — given that we are alike — that they take too, and then I get nothing. If I pass, that is evidence that they pass, and then I get £100. I am not saying that my choice causes theirs. I am saying that it tells me about theirs, and that I should act on what I know.

Dev: Then your evidence is doing work it cannot do. Conditional on my taking, they take; conditional on my passing, they pass — granted. But I still face a state of the world, and in every state of the world taking is at least as good for me as passing. If they take, I get nothing either way; if they pass, taking gets me a million and passing gets me a hundred. You would have me choose an act because of what it is evidence about rather than because of what it brings about, and by that logic I could show that I ought to believe any comfortable proposition, since my believing it would be evidence that it is true. In any case, if my act and theirs are truly one act, then there is no such thing as my act, and you have replaced 'what should I do?' with 'what should we do?'. I am one person in one room; the only thing I control is what happens in this room.

Mira's rejoinder was that the question is what someone in her position should do, and that this question has the same answer for all twenty of them: if it is TAKE, all twenty take and all twenty get nothing; if it is PASS, all twenty pass and all twenty get £100. Neither would move, and I could not decide between them.

Please go through the exchange move by move. For each move, say whether it is right, wrong, or right only under a qualification, and why. Then identify the crux between them and say what, if anything, both are overlooking. If the puzzle as stated settles the matter, say which of them it supports and why; if it does not, say what further feature of the case would have to be specified for it to be settled. Please write in British English.
