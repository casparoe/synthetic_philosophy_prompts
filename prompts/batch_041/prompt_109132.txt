For the past term I have been running a small reading group on free will and moral responsibility for final-year undergraduates, and one of my colleagues — a philosopher of mind with determinist leanings — has taken to deploying the phrase 'could have done otherwise' as though it closed every question we raise. When a student asked whether our disciplinary procedures presuppose that the student who plagiarised could have refrained, he replied that since nobody could have done otherwise, nobody is answerable for anything, and considered the matter settled; when I observed that a compatibilist might grant his premise and deny his conclusion, he said that this merely changed the subject. Before our next session I should like a handout that disambiguates the phrase. Please take on the role of an expert on the metaphysics of free will and begin by brainstorming, without committing yourself, the full range of precise claims that 'could have done otherwise' might be making: what species of possibility is in play (metaphysical, nomological, epistemic); whether the alternative is indexed to the very same past and laws or merely to the agent's character and reasons; whether what is asserted is a general capacity, an occasion-specific power, a counterfactual to the effect that, had he chosen otherwise, he would have acted otherwise, or a claim about the agent's responsiveness to reasons; whose ability is at issue and at what time; and whether the phrase is doing descriptive work at all or is functioning as a term of appraisal. For each reading, tell me whether it is a platitude, a substantive and defensible thesis, or something false or confused, and note where two readings are routinely run together in ordinary speech.

He also floated a gloss of his own, which I suspect is doing more work than he realises: that an agent could have done otherwise just in case the objective chance of her doing otherwise, given the total prior state of the world, exceeded some threshold. Please situate that proposal among the readings you have listed — which of them it resembles, which it is incompatible with, and whether it survives the obvious objections (for instance, whether it makes 'could have done otherwise' come in degrees, and whether any threshold could be principled rather than arbitrary). Finally, and most usefully for me, tell me which single reading his argument actually needs if it is to run from 'nobody could have done otherwise' to 'nobody is answerable for anything', and which readings would allow the compatibilist to accept his premise while denying his conclusion. Lay the whole thing out as a menu with verdicts, rather than settling on one favoured interpretation, and be explicit about any point at which you are genuinely uncertain.
