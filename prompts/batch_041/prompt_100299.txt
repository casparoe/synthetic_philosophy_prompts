Two people in my methods reading group have been going at each other for months about Simpson's paradox and I want them to actually produce a document instead of another shouting match. Person one is the causal-inference type: there's no paradox at all, just a failure to specify your causal question -- draw the DAG, decide whether your stratifying variable is a confounder or a mediator, and the right table is determined, end of story. Person two is the decision-theory / pragmatist type: the DAG does not pick the table for you, what picks the table is what you're trying to predict or control and for whom, so the reversal is a permanent feature of inquiry rather than a mistake anyone made.

Write the joint statement they could both sign after a real adversarial collaboration. I want a numbered list of claims both accept; then a table of the remaining disagreements, with each side's position written in wording that side would actually accept; then the cruxes, ie the sub-questions such that settling one would move at least one of them; and for each crux a concrete case or argument both agree bears on it, with each side's prediction written down in advance. If part of the fight turns out to be about words -- what counts as a "paradox", whether "the true effect" is even a well-formed thing to ask for -- say which part and why.

To stop this from being all vibes, both of them have agreed to work through this made up study. Treatment X vs treatment Y, recovery as the outcome:

- Mild at intake: X recovers 19 of 20, Y recovers 72 of 80.
- Severe at intake: X recovers 16 of 80, Y recovers 3 of 20.
- Overall: X recovers 35 of 100, Y recovers 75 of 100.

Now here's the twist they have to take seriously. In reading A, the mild/severe split is measured at intake, before treatment, and clinicians gave X mostly to the severe patients. In reading B, the exact same numbers come from splitting on whether the patient got worse in the first 24 hours after treatment started, which is downstream of the treatment itself. Same table, different causal story. Both of them should say what each reading implies, whether their disagreement survives reading B, and whether there's anything left for the decision context to settle once the causal structure is pinned down -- or whether that was always just part of specifying the estimand. I'd guess one crux is whether the "correct" table can depend on the target population as well as the causal structure, and another is whether conditioning on a post-treatment variable is ever the right thing to do when the question is about a subgroup you can only identify after the fact, but you two will find others.

Casual, direct, no hedging into "both sides have a point" mush -- if they genuinely agree on something, say so plainly, and if the residual disagreement is smaller than they think, say that too. Around 900 to 1200 words.
