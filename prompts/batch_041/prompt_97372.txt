I am a cognitive-science graduate student, and a friend and I have spent the last several evenings arguing about Robert Zemeckis's 1997 film Contact — specifically about what the alien signal is supposed to mean and where that meaning is supposed to live. We keep talking past each other, so I want a proper critical reading of the film rather than another round of traded intuitions, and I am asking you to write it. Approach this as a philosopher of mind and cognitive scientist who knows Hofstadter's Gödel, Escher, Bach well, has a solid grasp of the philosophy of language and of information, and can read a popular film closely without condescending to it or over-crediting it.

The framework I want the film read through is Hofstadter's account, in the part of GEB that deals with the location of meaning, of the three levels at which a message can carry meaning. As I understand it — and I am paraphrasing, so there is no need to quote the book — there is the frame message, which is what tells a receiver that there is a message here at all and how to begin making sense of it; the outer message, which tells the receiver how to extract and interpret the content; and the inner message, which is the content itself. The load-bearing claim is that no level interprets itself: each presupposes a decoder of the right kind, so the meaning of a message is not a property lodged inside the physical signal but a relation between the signal and a suitably structured receiver. Hofstadter's own examples, if I recall, run from a record and a phonograph to the genetic code.

Now the film, as I remember it. Ellie Arroway, a SETI researcher, detects a repeating signal from the star Vega, whose first component is a sequence of prime numbers — plainly artificial. Embedded further into the signal she finds a television transmission, footage of the 1936 Berlin Olympics. And buried still deeper, in the structure of the signal, are thousands of pages of engineering schematics for a machine. The machine, once built, turns out to be a transport device; Ellie rides it, has an experience she cannot prove, and returns to a world that doubts her because no instrument recorded anything — though there is that final suggestion, in the static, that something did in fact happen.

Here is what I want from you. Read Contact through the three-level analysis, and make the reading do real work rather than decorate the plot. Which layer of the signal is the frame message, which the outer message, which the inner message — and does the film's own drama depend on the fact that the humans and the senders share almost none of the frame that would make this assignment obvious? I am especially interested in whether the film is quietly committed to a thesis about the location of meaning: that the signal's meaning was never in the signal, but in the meeting of two decoders who happened to be isomorphic enough to understand one another; or whether, instead, the film cheats by letting the meaning turn out to be intrinsic after all. Test the reading against the rival interpretations you can think of — the religious reading that runs through the Joss character and the leap of faith, the reading on which the whole transmission is a test or a trap, the reading on which the message is really about the sender's own loneliness — and say honestly which of these the film supports and which it merely flirts with.

Be faithful in both directions. Do not reduce the film to an illustration of the theory, and do not bend the theory to save the film. Where does the triad genuinely illuminate a scene or a choice — the primes as a handshake across an unshared frame, the nested layers as a decoder bootstrapping itself from almost nothing, the unrecorded journey as meaning that cannot be transmitted? And where does the film resist the analysis, so that an honest critic has to concede that the three-level story does not fit? Finally, tell me what the film adds to the philosophical discussion that argument alone does not: is there something about the sheer difficulty of establishing a shared frame — about how fragile a decoder is when it has to be built from scratch — that Hofstadter's tidy triad understates, and that the film dramatizes better than the theory states it?

Take your time, and work through the rival readings before you commit to one. Begin with the reading itself: lead with your central claim about Contact in the first sentence, then develop the argument. No preamble, no restating my question, no throat-clearing about how interesting the topic is.
