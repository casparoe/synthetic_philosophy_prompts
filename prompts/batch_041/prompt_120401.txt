I'm doing a philosophy MA after a psychology degree, which is probably why this particular thing has been nagging at me, and we've just finished the week on luminosity and the KK principle — luminosity being roughly the idea that if you're in a mental state you're thereby in a position to know you're in it, and KK being the claim that if you know something then you know that you know it. What I want is to take a real episode out of the history of psychology and read it through that lens, because I have a suspicion the seminar's treatment of it was glib. The episode: in 1977 Nisbett and Wilson published a long review arguing that people have very poor introspective access to the causes of their own behaviour — the shoppers who explain their choice of stockings by the colour and the fabric when what actually moved them was where the pair was sitting on the table — and that when we're asked to account for ourselves we confabulate something plausible rather than report anything real. They were careful to seperate mental contents from mental processes, and to claim only that the processes are hidden from us. Ericsson and Simon pushed back in 1980, arguing that verbal reports can be perfectly good data if you handle them properly: they distinguish reports given while the task is underway from reports given afterwards, and they distinguish the case where a thought is already in words and can simply be spoken from the case where it has to be recoded out of some non-verbal format, where the translation itself is what does the damage. I may be garbling that — my notes are a disaster — but that's the shape of it.
