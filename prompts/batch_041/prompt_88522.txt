I'm a machine learning engineer who has spent the last several years working on ensembles and uncertainty, so my mental furniture is bagging and boosting, weak learners, the bias-variance-covariance decomposition of ensemble error, decorrelation, calibration, and the familiar fact that averaging a thousand models helps you only to the extent that their errors are not shared. Over drinks after a departmental workshop, a friend in political theory and I got into an argument about whether the Condorcet jury theorem actually tells us anything about democracy or whether it is just bagging with extra steps. I want to understand her position on its own terms before I decide whether I have been missing something, so please restate the theorem and the surrounding debate about what it does and does not show -- the competence assumption, the independence assumption, the fact that the result reverses when voters are worse than chance, the standard complaints that real electorates are correlated, incompetent, or strategically motivated -- entirely in my vocabulary. Give me the dictionary explicitly, item by item: competence becomes the accuracy of a weak learner, independence becomes the error decorrelation my work lives and dies by, majority rule becomes hard voting, and so on. Be as faithful as you can, and where the theorem's own framing is more careful than mine -- for instance if there is a distinction between a body answering a question that has a right answer and a body choosing a policy, or between the probability that a verdict is correct and the authority that verdict carries -- spell that out in my terms rather than assuming I already know it. I would much rather have a translation that is awkward and honest than one that is smooth and wrong, so do not quietly import ensemble-theoretic assumptions (that there is a loss function, that someone holds the labels, that we could in principle reweight the voters) and then present the result as if it were the political claim.

Then give me the residue report, which is the part I actually care about: which parts of the debate translate cleanly into ensemble terms, which translate only with distortion, and which cannot be expressed at all. I am especially interested in four candidates for residue. First, in my world a supervisor holds the ground truth, whereas in politics nobody does, so the correct answer the theorem is about may not exist until after the vote; tell me whether that difference is fatal or superficial. Second, one person, one vote forbids exactly the reweighting and stacking an ensemble engineer would reach for, and I want to know whether that constraint is a defect of the political case or a feature of it. Third, there is a tension between the jury theorem's reassuring asymptotic result and the cycling majorities Condorcet is also famous for, since a set of individually sensible rankings can aggregate into an incoherent one; I want to know whether that has an honest analogue in model aggregation or is a genuinely different phenomenon that I am conflating because both involve voting. Fourth, there is everything about legitimacy, authority, and obligation, for which my vocabulary seems to have no slot at all. For each of these, say whether the failure is a defect of my framework, a sign that the political claim was doing more work than it advertised, or a sign that I have been misreading the theorem all along. Close by telling me what my friend can legitimately claim on the basis of this theorem and what I should stop saying.
