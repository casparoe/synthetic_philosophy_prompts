My friend is doing a PhD in cosmology and we got into a dumb argument about this at a bar on Friday and it's still bugging me. Here's the thing I can't square. Everyone in physics says the second law of thermodynamics isn't really a law, it's statistical — entropy can in principle go down, its just absurdly unlikely that anything macroscopic would. Fine. But nobody actually treats it that way. Every time someone proposes a scheme that would violate it — Maxwell's demon, some ratchet, whatever — the response is never 'well, that's a fantastically improbable fluctuation, sure, maybe', it's always to go find the missing cost, usually by shoving entropy into the demon's memory and dragging in something like Landauer's principle. So in one breath the law is violable in principle and in the next it's treated as sacred, and an apparent violation is taken as proof that we've missed some new physics rather than as an instance of exactly the statistical violation we said was allowed. And it cuts the other way too: for small systems you can literally see individual runs where entropy goes down, all those fluctuation theorem results, and people are perfectly happy about it. So small systems break it, big ones can't, and the difference isn't in the law, it's that we've decided the odds are too small to care about? That seems like a strange place to park a physical principle.

Then there's the other half. When you ask why time has a direction at all — why we remember the past and not the future, why ice melts in coffee and not the reverse, why there are traces and records — the standard move is to point out that the universe started in a state of extraordinarily low entropy. Which is fine, except nobody wants to explain that, it just gets posited. Sometimes I see it dressed up as a law, the 'past hypothesis', and that strikes me as cheating, because if it's a law then we've explained the arrow by adding a new law to the pile, and if it's a boundary condition then we've explained it by stipulating a fact, and I can't tell how you'd even distinguish those two options when in both cases you write the thing down and put it in the box marked 'don't ask'. So what I want to know is what all of this actually commits us to. What view of laws, of probability in a deterministic system, and of what counts as a good explanation do you have to hold for these practices to hang together? Do they presuppose entropy is something real and mind-independent, or just a useful coarse-graining that depends on what we choose to keep track of? Do they need objective chances, or only typicality with respect to a measure nobody derives from anything? And which rival pictures do they quietly rule out — that the arrow is a perspectival artifact of creatures built like us, or that the low-entropy past is just one more fact about a block universe with no special explanatory standing? I'd like to know which bits are strictly forced by the practice and which are just habits of the field, and what would actually have to change — in how it gets taught, in what gets counted as a successful explanation, in what we're willing to say when a demon shows up — if we took the statistical line completely seriously and stopped patching.
