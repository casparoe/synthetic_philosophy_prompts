Exercise:

An often-repeated argument about insect welfare runs as follows. There are on the order of 10^18 insects alive at any time. The probability that a given insect is sentient in a morally relevant sense is small — suppose it lies somewhere between one in ten thousand and one in a hundred. Multiply the two and you get an expected 10^14 to 10^16 morally relevant subjects, which is four to six orders of magnitude more than the roughly 10^10 human beings alive. Therefore insect welfare should dominate our moral priorities.

Write a short answer that (a) explains how the expected number of morally relevant subjects is to be computed from these numbers, and what role, if any, the independence of the underlying events plays in that computation; (b) says what kind of probability the estimate in the premise is, and what follows from your answer for the strength of the argument; and (c) states the strongest objection you can construct against the conclusion, and gives a verdict on whether it succeeds.

Submission:

(a) This is an expected-value argument, and the arithmetic is the easy part. With N ≈ 10^18 insects and probability p that a given insect is sentient, the expected number of sentient insects is N·p, which for p between 10^-4 and 10^-2 gives between 10^14 and 10^16. That is four to six orders of magnitude more than the roughly 10^10 humans. Counting heads is not the whole story, though: an insect's experience, if there is one, is plausibly thinner than a mammal's, so the morally relevant quantity is not the number of subjects but the number of subject-moments weighted by their intensity. The scale survives a large discount. Even if only 10^14 of those insects are sentient, the per-insect weight would have to fall below about 10^-4 of a human's before the comparison reverses, and nobody is in a position to defend a number that small.

(b) The probability in the premise is not a chance. There is no physical randomizer that decides whether a beetle is conscious; the fact is fixed and we simply do not know it. So the quantity is a credence — our degree of belief in a proposition about insect neurobiology — and the resulting figure is an expectation relative to our information rather than a count we could in principle go and verify. That does not disqualify it. This is exactly the kind of uncertainty that expected-value reasoning is for, and the fact that the number moves with the estimate is a reason to improve the estimate, not a reason to refuse to act on it. Since the premise gives a range rather than a point value, I would take the midpoint, 5 × 10^-3, as the working estimate, which yields about 5 × 10^15 expected subjects.

(c) The strongest objection I can construct is that the premise's probability is a property of a whole order of animals rather than of each animal separately. Insects share a nervous-system architecture, so whatever makes one of them sentient makes the others sentient as well; the events are correlated, not independent. Since we are entitled to multiply only when the events are independent, the expected number of sentient insects is not N·p. The honest figure is closer to the number of insects in the single most plausible scenario — either very nearly all of them or very nearly none — so the argument has no license to treat the middle of the range as an average over a huge sample. On this reading the scale argument collapses into one binary question about the insect nervous system, to be settled by neuroscience rather than by arithmetic.
