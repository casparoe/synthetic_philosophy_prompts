I'm in a grad seminar on Bayesian epistemology and we spent most of last week on a puzzle the prof calls the Judy Benjamin problem, and I think I follow the setup but not what I'm supposed to take away from it. The setup: Judy is lost in a tent somewhere in a territory divided into a Blue sector and a Red sector, and she thinks she's equally likely to be in either. The Red sector is itself split into Red-1 (the first company's area) and Red-2, and she thinks that if she's in Red she's equally likely to be in each, so her credences are Blue 1/2, Red-1 1/4, Red-2 1/4. Then the radio crackles and a reliable, clear-headed voice says: "I don't know whether you're in the Blue sector or the Red sector, but if you're in Red, the chance is 3/4 that you're in Red-1." (Something about Red being mostly Red-1 territory; the details don't matter I think.) The obvious thing to say is that she keeps Red-vs-Blue at 1/2, since the message is only about the inside of Red, and shifts the Red split to 3/4-1/4, giving Blue 1/2, Red-1 3/8, Red-2 1/8. But someone in the seminar ran some minimum-information machinery over it and got Blue at about 0.53, and we couldn't agree on whether that's a bug or the actual answer.

My friend Dara, though, has an argument that goes way past that, and I cant tell if it's clever or broken. Here it is as she wrote it up: (1) When you get new information, your job is to land on the credence function that fits it — one that respects everything the information says and adds nothing of its own. (2) What the radio tells Judy, in credence terms, is P'(Red-1 | Red) = 3/4. (3) That's the whole content of the message. It says nothing about Blue, and nothing about whether Judy is in Red at all; it's a claim about the inside of the Red sector, which is the same fact either way. (4) So her credences afterwards must satisfy P'(Red-1 | Red) = 3/4, and are otherwise unconstrained. Blue 1/2, or Blue 0.6, or Blue 0.4 are all exactly as faithful to what the message said — the message simply has no opinion on the matter. (5) If you prefer one of those to another, you must be importing something the message didn't contain: either a bare preference for resembling your earlier credences, which is just deference to your past self, or a principle like "minimize information," which you can only motivate by already assuming the answer it's supposed to deliver. (6) So there's no fact of the matter about what Judy should think about Red vs Blue. And the same holds for any message of the form "if A, then probably B": it fixes something inside the A-worlds and leaves the rest open, so conditional reports can't constrain you, and reason is silent about them. (7) Which is obviously wrong — when my doctor says "if you carry the gene, the test comes back positive 9 times out of 10," that's information, and I'd be irrational to shrug it off.

Two things bug me. First, (6) seems to prove so much that it refutes itself, and I'd rather keep (1) and find what's wrong earlier. Second, (6) also seems to say a conditional report can never tell me anything about its antecedent, and I don't believe that — surely there are cases where hearing "if A then probably B" raises my credence in A (if the only transmitter in range is one that only broadcasts from the Red sector, doesn't hearing it say something about whether I'm in Red?). Though maybe that's a seperate point and Dara would say it isn't the same message. Anyway: which step should I give up, why does it look so innocent, and what does giving it up cost me? I'd really like to keep (1) if I possibly can.
