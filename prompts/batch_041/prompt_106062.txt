So I spent 35 years doing marine biology, retired a couple years back, and now I'm doing a philosophy degree for fun (my wife is convinced I've lost it). Anyway I've gotten stuck on this thing about tying your own hands, and before I read any more about it I want to know what a proper account of it would even have to look like.

Here's the shape of the puzzle as I see it. In the lab we had our own version. We preregistered: before we ran anything we wrote down the hypothesis, the stopping rule, the analysis, and lodged the thing somewhere time-stamped, precisely so that the future us — the one who has seen the data and now really wants it to be a positive result — couldn't quietly move the goalposts. Same structure as Ulysses having himself lashed to the mast, if you'll forgive the obvious one. And same structure, apparently, as a constitution: some people in 1789 or whenever write down rules meant to stop some other people in 2024 from doing whatever the 2024 majority fancies.

What I can't get straight is what the binding is supposed to add. If I can untie myself, the rope did nothing. If I can't, then in what sense is the future me the same agent as the past me who tied the knot — and if he isn't, who gave the past me the right to boss around a stranger? With the political version it's worse: the whole point of democracy is that the people get to decide, and the whole point of a constitution is that on some questions they definately don't.

So what I want from you is not a theory, it's the spec for one — the list of things any decent account of precommitment (personal or political, I suspect it's the same problem wearing different hats) has to get right. For each item, tell me the case that forces it, and tell me which ones you think are just preferences of one camp dressed up as requirements. And I'd particularly like to know whether some of them can't all be had at once, because I have a suspicion that's where the real action is. If there's a pair that's jointly unsatisfiable, say so and say which one you'd give up.

Things I want on the list, or at least want you to tell me whether they belong:

- the difference between a commitment that works by removing your options and one that works by changing what other people expect you to do (burning the boats vs. announcing you'll never negotiate) — one phenomenon or two?
- the fact that Ulysses needed the crew. The tying was done by other people, on his orders, and they were the ones who could have untied him. Does a commitment that needs a third party to enforce it still count as self-binding, or is the self doing no work there?
- preregistration-type cases, where the enforcer is a community of strangers who'll punish you with reputational damage if you deviate. Precommitment, or just an incentive?
- what to say about binding a future self who won't merely be weak-willed but will have different values — the kid who tattoos something at 19, the 80 year old who wants out of the contract he signed at 30. Is paternalism across time different in kind from paternalism across people, or the same thing at a different scale?
- the dead hand problem — why a long-dead generation's rules should bind the living, and what the best answer is that doesn't just amount to "tradition"
- and the one that really bends my brain: a rule for changing the rules. Can a constitution's own amendment clause be used to amend the amendment clause? Can a contract contain a clause saying it may never be revoked, and is such a clause valid? I have a feeling this is the same question as whether you can promise never to make another promise, and I want to know if the answer is a real result or just a paradox people enjoy chewing on.

Then, briefly, because I know you'll want to: how do the usual suspects do against your list — the picture where a person is just a sequence of time-slices with conflicting preferences, the picture where there's a deeper or real self that does the tying, the picture where commitment is purely a strategic move in a game with other people, and whatever the constitutional lawyers have. Two lines each is plenty, I mainly want to see which requirement does the most killing.

One more thing, keep it concrete. I'm new at this and don't have the vocabulary yet, so when you say something like the autonomy of the bound self, please give me the lab version of it. Ta.
