For a feature I'm writing on what computational complexity can and can't tell us about machine creativity, I keep running into one line: that checking an answer is easier than finding one. It surfaced twice last week in nearly opposite service — once from a theorist who took it to show that better machine verification will never yield genuine originality, and once from an engineer who took it to show that verification is the cheap part and so not where the value lies. I doubt both can be right, and I doubt either is using the sentence the way the other is. Lay out the distinct precise claims that line could be making — as a definition of a complexity class, as a worst-case conjecture, as a claim about average-case or practical difficulty, as a claim about search versus decision, as a claim about human creativity, as a claim about cryptography — say for each whether it is a definitional truism, a substantive open conjecture, or something we know to be false, and tell me which readings would actually license each man's conclusion. The theorist also insisted the asymmetry is purely worst-case while his own solver dispatches the same instances in seconds, and he said the line is why cryptography exists at all; tell me what those two remarks do to the readings. Please answer in American English.
