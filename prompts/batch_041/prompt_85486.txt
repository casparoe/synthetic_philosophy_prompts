I'm a master's student in a seminar on knowledge and practical reasoning, and this week's assignment is an unusual one: rather than an essay, we have to write a dialogue between two people who hold opposed views about a single case, on the theory that you do not really understand a disagreement until you have watched two competent partisans try to settle it with each other. I have been assigned the dispute between pragmatic encroachment — the view that whether a subject knows can depend on what is practically at stake for her — and epistemic contextualism — the view that the truth of a knowledge attribution varies with the standards in play in the attributor's conversational context. The bank queue and the notary case have been worked to death in this seminar, so I have constructed my own case, and I would like you to write the dialogue around it. Dana is a hydrologist on flood watch; on the strength of her gauge readings and years of calibration records she believes that the levee above the settlement will hold through the night, and she is the person who must decide, within the hour, whether to recommend evacuation. What nobody can determine before morning is how much water is coming: depending on which of two basins the flood takes, the population behind the levee is either a few dozen people or tens of thousands, and that number will not be known until well after Dana's decision has been made and acted on. Her colleague Priya, who has no stake in any of this and is packing up to leave, asks Dana in a relaxed, offhand way whether the levee will hold, and Dana says she believes it will. The two characters are a defender of encroachment and a defender of contextualism; they disagree about whether Dana knows that the levee will hold, and therefore about whether Priya's casual remark that Dana knows is true.

I want the dialogue to do real work rather than trade slogans, and I have four specific hopes for it. First, the contextualist should press the point that Dana's evidence is exactly the same in both versions of the case, so that on the encroachment view whether Dana knows turns on which basin the flood happens to take — a fact that no one, Dana included, could have found out — and should draw out the further consequence that Dana's own ignorance would then be something she is in no position to discover. Second, the encroachment theorist should press the point that how much justification a belief needs surely depends on what is going to be done with it, that Dana is the one who will act on it, and that it is strange for the epistemic status of Dana's belief to be settled by whether Priya happens to be anxious about it; they should also offer the best available explanation of why we feel uneasy about Dana acting on her belief in the version where tens of thousands are downstream. Third, each should put at least one objection that the other cannot absorb without a cost, and the cost should be named rather than waved away. Fourth, both should attempt to specify a case or observation that would decide the matter between them, and should give an honest verdict at the end as to whether the dispute is empirical or is really about which fact — the subject's stakes or the ascriber's context — deserves to be treated as the locus of the variation in 'knows'. Neither character should be a strawman: the encroachment theorist must be given the strongest version of the view rather than the caricature that knowing is just believing whatever pays, and the contextualist must not be allowed to win by quietly treating 'knows' as meaning merely that one can be conversationally challenged here. Each should concede something genuine to the other before the end. If the dialogue finishes without a winner that is fine, provided it finishes with the two of them having narrowed their disagreement to something specific. About two pages, and no citations or references to the literature are needed.
