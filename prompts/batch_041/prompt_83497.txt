I am midway through a survey seminar on American political thought, and this week's reading was a chapter from a course reader — the author is not a specialist, but he writes cleanly — that offers what I take to be the standard textbook account of American exceptionalism. Compressed to its essentials, the chapter runs roughly as follows: 'American exceptionalism is the belief that the United States is uniquely virtuous, a nation founded on liberty and chosen by providence to lead the world. Tocqueville coined the phrase in the 1830s, after his tour of the country, and it has served ever since as a patriotic self-description. As a historical thesis it holds that America followed a path no other nation did: no feudalism, no hereditary aristocracy, no class struggle, and therefore no socialism. Comparative historians have since refuted that thesis, showing that the United States was never as different from Europe as Americans like to believe. The term therefore survives today chiefly as campaign rhetoric — an ideology, not a description.'

Something about the passage feels slippery to me, and I cannot put my finger on where. I would like you to audit it the way a specialist would, working through it claim by claim: which parts a historian or a comparative political scientist would simply accept, which are defensible simplifications in a lecture but misleading in print, and which are wrong outright. Two matters interest me in particular. First, the chapter seems to slide continuously among three different things — a descriptive thesis about how America actually developed, a normative claim about its virtue, and an ideology or rhetoric — and I want to know whether that conflation is the source of my unease, and how the three are properly kept apart; the closing sentence, that the term has become an ideology rather than a description, strikes me as too neat, since scholars do appear to use the term descriptively. Second, I doubt the word refuted is fair to the comparative literature: my impression, though I am probably conflating two books, is that the leading comparative scholars were pressing a version of the thesis rather than demolishing it, and that the interesting question is what kind of difference from Europe they claimed and whether difference is the same thing as superiority. Does the chapter's conclusion — that the concept has collapsed into mere campaign rhetoric — survive once these matters are sorted out?

Finally, a half-memory I would like checked, since it may be a red herring. I have the impression, from a podcast I only half-remember, that the phrase itself was not originally a compliment at all but an accusation traded inside the Communist movement in the 1920s — the charge that American communists were treating the United States as exempt from the general laws of historical development — and that it migrated into mainstream and patriotic usage only later; and further, that the familiar attribution to Tocqueville is more doubtful than the chapter lets on. If that is right, does it bear on the chapter's account of what the term means and how it has been used, or is it merely an etymological curiosity? And if I have misremembered, please say so plainly rather than accommodating me.
