I am a novelist some way into a book whose narration insists on attributing purposes, strategies, and mistakes to things that have no minds of the sort that can entertain purposes: a fig wasp that pollinates and dies, a cuckoo chick that clears the nest it was hatched into, a slime mould that finds the shortest path through a maze, a flock of starlings that turns as one. A friend who teaches philosophy read the first hundred pages and observed that my prose is not merely decorated with these attributions; it is committed to them. The view she attributes to me, and which on reflection I hold, is that such rationales are neither figures of speech nor a convenient predictive idiom, but real features of the world — that there are reasons for what these creatures do which no creature has. The reason is present; the reasoner is absent. She calls them free-floating rationales, and says the phrase is a term of art rather than hers; I have not troubled to find out whose. She also says the notion is respectable but contested. I want to know exactly what it is exposed to.

Let me state the position as I would defend it, so that the objections can be aimed at something definite. First, the rationale is real rather than instrumental: it is not that I find it useful to write as though the wasp were reasoning, but that the structure of the wasp's situation and of its behaviour genuinely stands in a reason relation. Second, the rationale is fixed by the history of selection rather than by anything the organism represents, and yet I want to say it is not simply identical with that history — the causal story and the reason story are two descriptions of one thing. Third, it is normative in at least a thin sense: a creature can fail to do what it has reason to do, and a trait can go on carrying a rationale that its circumstances no longer answer to; the moth spiralling into the flame is still doing, in some sense, what it has reason to do, though the reason has gone bad. Fourth — and I suspect this clause is doing more work than the others — none of it requires that anything anywhere entertain or experience the reason.

What would count against this? I want the full map of its exposure, not a defence. Which observations, arguments, intuitions, or formal results tell against it, and which of those would a committed defender concede as counting against them rather than dismiss as a misunderstanding? Sort them by how decisive they would be, from fatal to merely awkward. Tell me what the position predicts that its austere rivals do not; if it predicts nothing that the bare causal-mechanical story does not already predict, say so plainly, and say whether that makes it idle rather than false. In particular I want to know: what it would take to show that the rationale and the mechanism can come apart, and whether anything could count as evidence that a rationale is present but never manifests; whether cases of accident, drift, or one-off mutation force a dilemma in which either anything at all has a rationale, so the notion says nothing, or the notion is hostage to a selection history that may be unknowable; whether I am committed to anything I would rather not be — free-floating experiences to match the free-floating reasons, a teleology that naturalism cannot fund, a normativity with no subject to be bound by it; and whether the view quietly collapses into one of its rivals, whether that be the view that a trait's function is simply its selection history, or the view that reason-talk is only a stance we adopt for the sake of prediction, or a merely linguistic point about how the word reason is used. The defeater I would personally find most persuasive is a case, or a result, in which the rationale cannot be read off the mechanism at all — or one in which every arrangement whatsoever can be assigned a rationale, so that the notion costs nothing and buys nothing. Rank these from fatal to merely awkward, and tell me honestly whether the ones I would have to concede leave the position standing, or only twitching.
