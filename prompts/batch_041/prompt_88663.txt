So I just left my phd and started at a small grantmaking outfit that funds animal welfare plus some longtermist stuff, and the part of the job I did not see coming is that my coworkers and I keep disagreeing about the underlying ethics. One of us is sure factory farming is the whole ballgame, another thinks it's wild animal suffering, we've got a utilitarian, two people who are basically Kantian about not using anyone as a means, and one woman who thinks the whole question is really about what kind of person you become by giving. And we still have to decide where the money goes on Monday. I know there's a literature on moral uncertainty but I dont want a reading list or cites, I want a map I can actually walk my non-philosopher coworkers through.

So: write me a decision tree (a flowchart) that starts from someones gut reaction to the fact that they and a smart colleague disagree about ethics, and ends at a named position on what to do about it. Build the branches out of questions a person can answer without already knowing the names of the positions, and make every node a fork where the two answers actually go somewhere different — if a node funnels everyone to the same leaf, cut it.

I want the tree to be forced to make these distinctions somewhere, in whatever order you think is right:

- whether it even makes sense to say one theory is more correct than another by some amount, i.e. whether the options can be compared on a common scale across theories, or whether at some point you just have no ranking at all
- whether your credences in the theories get to weight them, or each theory gets a voice regardless of how likely you think it is
- whether the act you end up doing has to be endorsed by at least one theory you're considering, or whether a compromise that no theory would pick on its own is fine
- whether moral uncertainty is really just a special case of not knowing some fact (so ordinary decision theory already handles it), or a different kind of problem needing its own machinery
- what the uncertainty is actually about — which theory is right, vs. what your own theory says about this particular case, vs. what your own future self will think, vs. whether there's a determinate answer at all. These should land in different places
- how finely you individuate theories, since if consequentialism is one entry or a thousand the credence-weighting question comes out differently — if a node's answer flips depending on that, say so right at the node instead of pretending it's clean

Every node needs a concrete case or claim attached, not an abstract question — a case where a deontological constraint and a big consequentialist payoff come apart; a case where two theories both permit an act but only one requires it (so one of them leaves you a gap to do more); a case where a theory you give very low credence is extremely confident, or where it says the stakes are infinite; a case where two theories endorse the same act for completely different reasons. No node may just ask whether someone is a consequentialist.

These have to show up as leaves, one sentence each on the position plus one sentence on its most awkward commitment: maximize expected choiceworthiness on a shared scale; credence-weighting that also punishes theories for disagreeing a lot (the variance/hedging-against-spread kind); the view that with no common scale some options are simply unranked and you may only rule out dominated ones; just act on the theory you think most likely; a parliament where each theory votes regardless of credence; deliberately doing a compromise act that hedges; maximize the worst case across theories; the reductionist line that this is just empirical uncertainty about a fixed moral truth; a rights-first line that says never violate a constraint even if the numbers say to; defer to whatever procedure or consensus your group can agree on; and the view that the real upshot is about blame and excuse rather than about which act is right.

Then, after the tree: list any combinations of answers your tree allows that no actual position occupies, and for each say whether you think nobody's there because the view is incoherent, because it's coherent but nobody wants it, or because it's just unexplored. And flag any two leaves your tree cannot really separate — where the branches don't touch the difference — rather than papering over it.

Audience is smart people who aren't philosophers, so keep the nodes in plain language and the whole thing around 8 to 10 branch nodes. A one-line gloss of each leaf is enough, I'll write the scoring myself.
