I serve on the educational policy committee at a large public university, and I am trying to make sense of a tension in our own rules that no one on the committee can articulate. In our introductory STEM sequence — the courses that decide who may continue in the major — grades are assigned on a curve: the syllabus fixes in advance the proportion of A's, B's, and C's, and an instructor who wishes to exceed the quota must justify the deviation to a calibration committee, which almost always refuses. The stated rationale is that the sequence is competitive and that only a fixed number of students can proceed. Yet the same university certifies competence in its advanced laboratories on an absolute standard: a student either can or cannot perform the required technique, and everyone who can is passed, no matter how many others also can. Our honors and merit scholarships are likewise awarded by absolute cutoff. And our faculty are now evaluated by value-added measures, which reward moving students up from wherever they started rather than ranking them against one another.

Please write an analysis, addressed to the committee, that reconstructs the theory of merit and desert these practices presuppose. Does the curve presuppose that academic merit is positional — that what a student deserves is always relative to a fixed distribution, as in a race — while the laboratories and scholarships presuppose that merit is absolute and can be certified independently of how others fare? Are those two presuppositions genuinely inconsistent, or can a single coherent theory of desert underwrite both? Which rival theories does our mixture quietly rule out — say, a view on which desert tracks effort rather than achievement, or a view on which there is no fact of the matter about who deserves what and the institution merely manufactures the ranking? Where is our practice underdetermined, in the sense that it merely fits a theory of desert without requiring it? Please also address whether the curve treats a rank as something a student deserves or merely as something the student is entitled to hold once the institution assigns it, and whether the whole apparatus assumes that the university discovers a pre-existing distribution of merit or instead creates one. Finally, if we replaced the curve with a purely criterion-referenced standard, or replaced the laboratories with a curve, what else in our practice would have to change? Distinguish carefully between what our practices strictly presuppose and what they merely make convenient, and take seriously the possibility that the inconsistency lies not in our theory but in our institutions. You need not survey the literature; I want the analysis to follow from the practices as I have described them.
