My friend Dana runs a small coffee roasting outfit — six retail counters plus a wholesale side — and for the last few months I've been sitting in on her hiring, partly because I ran a restaurant for a decade and she thinks I have good insticts about people. The job she's filling is counter manager, and the thing the job actually requires is staying calm and decent when a customer is furious at you about something that isn't your fault. So Dana built an exercise: her brother, who's a good sport, comes in and plays an irate customer, and the candidate has to handle him. She swears by it and has run it on the last twenty or so hires. Two things about it are making me doubt the whole approach. First, some of the people who are best on the floor in real life are terrible in the drill. There's a guy, Marcus, who we almost didn't hire because he went stiff and over-apologetic in the role-play and looked like he'd never spoken to a human being; he's now the best manager Dana has. Second, and this is the part that really itches, the reverse happens too: one candidate was smooth as anything in the drill, and then on her first real shift, when an actual customer started yelling about a refund, she went cold and walked off the floor. Dana says the drill reveals what's already there, so it was right about her and Marcus is a fluke. But it seems to me the drill might be doing something to people rather than just reading them off — like some folks are fine being yelled at but not fine being tested on how they handle being yelled at, and the testing itself is what undoes them. She's also floated putting a secret-shopper during the paid trial shift, and I have a separate worry about that one, since now we'd be tricking someone we're paying.

What I want from you is help on two levels. Practically: what should she do with this exercise — keep it, drop it, or change it, and if change it, how, including whether the secret-shopper idea is salvageable at all. I dont need the standard HR spiel about structured interviews and rubrics; I've read that stuff and it doesn't touch what's bugging me. Conceptually: is it even coherent to say a person "has" a quality like this, as if it sits inside them and we're just trying to see it, when the quality keeps showing up and disappearing depending on the setup we put them in? And tell me whether the Marcus case and the walked-off-the-floor case are one puzzle or two different ones, because Dana treats them as the same thing and I'm fairly sure they're not. If the honest answer is that from behavior alone you can never tell whether the quality is missing, or present but blocked, or present but wrecked by the test itself, then say that plainly and tell me what a person is supposed to do about hiring anyway.
