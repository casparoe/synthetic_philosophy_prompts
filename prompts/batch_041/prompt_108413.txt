For the consciousness unit of my graduate philosophy of mind seminar, I want to replace my lecture on higher-order theories with a decision tree the students can work through in pairs before we discuss. They will have had a single session on first-order representationalism and nothing else, so the tree has to begin from a commitment nearly all of them already share and end at positions they can state and argue about on the spot.

The starting commitment is this: a mental state is conscious in virtue of standing in some relation to a further, higher-order representation of it. Build the tree from the logical space rather than from a catalogue of published views. The branch nodes come first, each phrased as a question a student can answer without knowing which position it leads to, and the positions are located in that space afterwards, each carrying a name (or marked unnamed where the combination has no defender) and one sentence stating its most awkward commitment. A student answering honestly should be able to reach a leaf without recognizing any of the labels on the way, so no branch question may itself name a position. Give it to me as an indented outline I can photocopy, with every branch labeled by the answer that takes you down it.

Put these questions on the tree, in whatever order its logic requires; they need not all sit on the trunk, and some may arise only for certain answers:

- Must the higher-order state represent the first-order state as conscious, or need it only represent it — and if only represent it, is the first-order state's being conscious anything over and above its being represented?
- Is the higher-order state about the first-order state itself, about its content, or about the subject's having it?
- Must the higher-order state be a thought with conceptual content, or can a perception-like or attention-like state do the same work?
- Must the higher-order state be occurrent, or does a standing disposition to have one suffice?
- Must it be numerically distinct from the state it makes conscious, or can a single state represent itself?
- Must the first-order state exist for the higher-order state to do its work? If a higher-order state can have no target, is anything conscious in that case?
- Is the higher-order state itself conscious — and does the view require that, forbid it, or leave it open?
- Is the account offered as a metaphysical answer to what consciousness is, or as an empirical hypothesis about what accompanies it?

Then add one axis of your own that these leave out, and say in a sentence why it earns a place on the tree.

Aim for seven to ten leaves. Within that: include at least one position no one has defended, and say whether it is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored; mark any branch that turns out to be a dead end and say why nothing survives there; include one leaf that the tree reveals to be a first-order theory in disguise; and flag any two leaves the tree cannot separate, saying what further question would separate them or that none would. Do not name authors or works, and do not append readings — I will supply those myself. Open with two sentences on what the tree is sorting by and what it is not.

Last, tell me which leaf you expect most of the class to land on, and which leaf none of them will have considered.
