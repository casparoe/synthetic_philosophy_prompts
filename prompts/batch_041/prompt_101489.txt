For a few decades the debate over pragmatic encroachment has had a shape that bothers me. The participants divide into contextualists, who locate the stakes-sensitivity in the standards for the word "knows" as used by the attributor; interest-relative or subject-sensitive invariantists, who locate it in the knowledge relation itself, holding that whether a subject knows depends on what is at stake for her; relativists, who make the truth of a knowledge ascription depend on the attributor's context of assessment; and classical invariantists, who deny stakes-sensitivity altogether. What strikes me is that all four camps accept the same handful of cases — the bank cases, the train cases, the cases where the stakes change while the evidence stays fixed — and agree about what competent speakers would say and do in them. The disagreement is entirely about what those agreed data show. Nobody's argument has moved anybody in decades, and I have started to suspect that the reason is that the parties are not really answering the same question.

Here is my hypothesis, offered as a hypothesis. The camps have each tacitly assumed a different answer to a prior question — what the concept of knowledge is for. One camp treats it as an information-economy device, whose function is to flag reliable informants, so its application should track reliability. Another treats it as a device for regulating assertion and practical commitment, so its application should track what it is permissible to act on. A third treats it as a marker of a natural, discoverable psychological state, so its application should track whatever state plays that role in a subject's cognitive economy. If that is right, then the dispute over the cases is downstream of a dispute about function, and the semantic machinery is largely a distraction — the camps are answering different questions under one heading, and the apparent first-order conflict is partly verbal. But I can see the opposite reading just as well: that these are four answers to one genuine question, under what conditions does a subject know, and the impasse is simply that the case data underdetermine the answer, so the choice is being driven by theoretical virtues — simplicity, conservatism about the semantics of "knows," fidelity to ordinary practice — that the camps rank differently and cannot argue each other out of.

What I want from you is a diagnosis, not a survey. Which is it: a verbal dispute, a dispute about which cases count as the data, a hidden empirical question about how speakers actually use the word, a disagreement about the point of the concept, or a genuine first-order disagreement that is merely hard? Engage with what each camp actually says, and in particular with how each would treat two cases of my own: a case in which the subject does not know the stakes, and a case in which the subject's past and present selves disagree about whether she knew. Tell me whether those cases are even relevant on each view, since I suspect each side will simply declare the other's cases irrelevant and that this refusal is itself the symptom. Tell me also whether the disagreement looks different once we distinguish the proposition a knowledge ascription expresses from the proposition it is used to assess. Then tell me what, if anything, would count as progress given your diagnosis: a conceptual distinction, a formal result, an empirical study of usage, or a change of question. If you think no progress should be expected, say so and explain why. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give the reasoning; no preamble and no restating of the question.
