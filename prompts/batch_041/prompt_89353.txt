My friend did a masters in philosophy years ago and is extremely confident about Leibniz, and by now I've heard roughly the same story from two popular books and a podcast, so I'd assumed it was just standard. Here's how she put it. Leibniz held the identity of indiscernibles — she called it Leibniz's Law the whole time, which nags at me because I have a feeling I've seen that name attached to something else, but I might be making that up — and the principle says that if two things have all their properties in common then they aren't really two things at all, just one thing counted twice. Leibniz needed it because of his principle of sufficient reason: if there were two perfectly alike objects, God would have had no reason to put one here and the other there, and God never acts without a reason. Then in 1952 Max Black destroyed it with a thought experiment: a universe containing nothing but two iron spheres, same diameter, same colour, same temperature, sitting two miles apart, with no other facts about either of them. The spheres are obviously discernible — you can walk up to one and look at it and then turn around and look at the other — but there's no property that sets either one apart. So either Leibniz's principle is false, or else the two spheres are secretly a single sphere, which is absurd. She said that when people object "ah but they differ in position, one's here and one's there", Black already saw that coming, because the positions are defined only relative to each other and so are symmetric too, and the objection is dead on arrival. And the upshot is that the principle of sufficient reason, and with it the identity of indiscernibles, can't be necessary truths, and since Leibniz hung so much on those, the monadology comes down with them.
