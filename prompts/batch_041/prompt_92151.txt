Last night a friend and I spent three hours arguing about whether political parties are a defect of democratic life or a precondition of it. She thinks the party is the only mechanism that lets large numbers of strangers act together at all; I found myself defending something close to Simone Weil's position in her late writing on political parties — that a party is at bottom a device for manufacturing and sustaining collective passion, that its first concern is its own survival rather than the truth, and that the discipline of membership quietly corrupts even honest people's relation to what is true, which is why the remedy would have to be the abolition of the party form rather than the moral improvement of individuals. Neither of us could make the other feel the claim; in the abstract it sounds either banal or monstrous. I want to fix that in my teaching.

I run a political theory seminar of about twenty second- and third-year undergraduates, and I have one ninety-minute session to work with. Design an activity in which they come to feel the force of this view from the inside rather than hear it asserted. They should organize themselves into competing parties around a genuinely contested question on which the evidence is mixed and reasonable people disagree — something local and low-temperature, if you can, so that the dynamics I care about are not drowned out by convictions they arrived with. Each participant should have to record a private judgment before any party forms, then help produce a single public party position, under a scoring rule that rewards cohesion and winning adherents more than getting the answer right, and in which dissent is permitted but costs something. The moment I most want them to hit is the one where someone notices that their private view has moved but continues to defend the line anyway, and explains the gap to themselves as loyalty or effectiveness rather than as a falsehood. I would also like them to feel the attraction of belonging and efficacy, so that the proposed remedy registers as a loss and not as a free fix.

The idea should surface in the rules, incentives, and roles, before anyone names it: withhold Weil and her thesis until the debrief. Give me the setup, the materials, the scoring, a timeline, and the debrief — which should map specific moments in the run onto the parts of the view: the subordination of truth to the party's survival, the party as a collective being with a will of its own, the corruption of well-intentioned members, and the widening gap between the party line and what its members privately think. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time and how to handle it, and be honest about what the activity cannot reproduce — in particular the long-term shaping of character that Weil is really after, whatever she means by collective passion at the level of the soul, and the normative question of whether abolition is the right remedy at all; I will have to supply those myself.
