I just got promoted from volunteer to coordinator at the after-school tutoring program I've been with for three years, which means I now spend more time in meetings with our funder than in a room with kids, and I'm realizing I have no idea what I'm doing when it comes to measuring anything.

Here's the thing that's bugging me. Our funder wants us to "demonstrate impact," and I proposed that we pick one headline number so we can at least tell whether our sites are getting better or worse. Our most experienced site lead — retired schoolteacher, been doing this since before I could drive — shut it down immediately with: "When a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure." Everyone nodded, we moved on, and I sat there feeling like a slogan had just been used as a veto. I looked it up afterwards and apparently the original version is about monetary policy and goes something like "Any observed statistical regularity will tend to collapse once pressure is placed upon it for control purposes," which sounds like a much more specific claim than the version she quoted. So now I've got two sentences rattling around and I can't tell if they say the same thing.

What's actually making me want to get this straight is that I've seen two different things go wrong here and I don't think they're the same failure:

(a) A couple of years back we counted sessions delivered and reported that to the funder. Within a term, tutors had figured out that a one-kid session counts the same as a five-kid session, and a twenty-minute check-in counts the same as a two-hour block. Our numbers went up 40% and I'm pretty sure nothing changed for any kid. Nobody was lying. They were just doing what the number paid them in.

(b) Separately, the district gave us pass rates for the schools we serve. The sites with the best pass rates turned out to be the ones in the wealthier catchment, where the test is easier and the kids were mostly passing anyway. Then the board started talking about "scaling what works" by copying those sites, and I realized we'd be copying the thing that made them look good instead of the thing that makes kids learn. The number was fine as a description and became garbage the second we started steering by it.

And a third data point that cuts the other way: we started tracking whether tutors actually showed up, mostly so we'd know who to call, and attendance genuinely improved. So it's not like every number we touch rots.

What I want from you: you're a philosopher of science who works on measurement and incentive design, and I want you to take that one sentence apart properly. Please start by brainstorming — actually write out — all the distinct precise things "when a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure" could be claiming, from the nearly tautological to the very strong. Think about what's doing the work: is it about the measure losing correlation with the thing we care about, or about people responding to the measure, or about the selection of which cases you look at, or about what counts as "the target," or about something epistemic where the measure was never tracking the real thing to begin with? Then for each reading, tell me: is it a platitude (true but does no work), substantive and defensible, substantive and false, or just confused? Say which readings would actually justify what my site lead did — using it to kill a proposal to measure anything at all — and which ones don't come close to justifying that.

Two specific questions I'd like you to answer inside that framework. First, are my (a) and (b) the same phenomenon under the slogan, or two different ones that happen to share a bumper sticker? Second, does the monetary-policy formulation and the popular formulation come apart — is one a claim about statistics and the other a claim about people?

Finally, don't just tell me the slogan is overblown or that it's obviously true. Tell me the strongest version of it I should actually take seriously when I go back to design a metric next month, and what that version tells me to do differently. And please dont hedge everything into mush — I need verdicts, not a survey.
