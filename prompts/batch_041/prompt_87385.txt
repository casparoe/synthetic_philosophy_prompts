I am a PhD student working on health law and the graduate member of my university hospital's ethics committee. We are drafting the policy that determines when a patient's earlier written wishes about treatment take precedence over what the patient says in the moment, and nearly every case we see is a slow decline: nothing changes from one day to the next that anyone could point to, yet after a year the same patient plainly can no longer weigh the options. The committee keeps splitting on where to put the switchover. One member insists on a constraint: whatever rule we adopt, no single day's change, however small, may ever be what flips the verdict; otherwise something trivial is deciding something enormous. Another concedes the constraint in theory but argues that the hospital must act as though there is a definite answer in every case, and that a clear policy is worth more than a well-founded one. A third wants to leave the matter to the treating team's judgment, which I suspect only relocates the difficulty. I have to bring a recommendation to the committee next month, and I would like help seeing what this disagreement is really about and what I should actually propose. I am also uneasy that any criterion we write down, for instance that the patient can no longer appreciate the consequences, will itself need a boundary, and I cannot tell whether that is a genuine problem or merely my nerves.
