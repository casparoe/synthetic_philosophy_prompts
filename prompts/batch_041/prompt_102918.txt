I'm putting together the final exam for my upper-level seminar on pain and the mind (we've done the usual stuff — Descartes, the two-dimensions-of-pain picture, hedonic tone, a bit of Grahek), and I want one essay question on pain asymbolia that isn't just a recitation excercise. The thing I keep running into is that everyone can say "asymbolia shows the sensory and affective components of pain come apart" and then they stop, as if that settles whether the pain is bad for the person. What I actually want the question to force is the step after that: if someone feels pain but isn't bothered by it — the standard presentation, where patients report the pain is there but say it doesn't hurt or doesn't bother them, and might even smile or stay relaxed while you poke them — does that show the badness of pain lives in the disliking / affective reaction rather than in the sensation itself? And I want them to have to deal with the messy interpretive problem, because the patients' reports are doing a lot of work and it's not obvious we should take them at face value. I've been seeding this in class with two cases I'll hand them on the exam sheet: one patient who says the stimulus "hurts" and rates its intensity around 7 or 8 out of 10 but insists it isn't unpleasant and doesn't move away, and another patient who pulls her hand back reflexively when you pinch her yet still reports no bother. I want the question to make them say what each of those shows — whether either one is actually evidence about badness at all, or only about motivation, or only about report — since the sensory/affective split by itself doesn't tell you which of those is going on.

Could you write me: (1) the essay question itself, pitched so a good student can write it in about 50 minutes and can't just paraphrase Grahek's feeling-pain / being-in-pain distinction; (2) a model answer of roughly 650–750 words that takes a clear position on whether asymbolia supports an attitudinal account of pain's badness (badness = the disliking or the attitude) or a hedonic / intrinsic account (badness = the felt quality itself), and that also weighs the option that asymbolic pain might still be bad for the subject in some non-experiential way, e.g. that it still signals tissue damage or otherwise threatens the subject's interests; and (3) a marking scheme out of 25, built around named must-hit moves, giving the marks for each and a one-line note on the mistake that typically costs students marks on that point. In the model answer, please finish with a short honest paragraph on what would actually change its verdict — what piece of evidence or argument would push it the other way — and flag which step of its reasoning it's least confident in. No quotations from any source, just the argument in your own words; and don't assume the students have read anything I haven't put on the syllabus, so the question has to stand on its own.
