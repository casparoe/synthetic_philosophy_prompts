So I'm a journalist — science and ideas, mostly long features — and I'm deep in a piece about the whole is-the-world-getting-less-violent question. I've read the optimist bible and all the takedowns of it, so I dont need either of those rehashed, and I definitely don't need a reading list. What I need is the practical upshot, because the whole piece hinges on it.

Here's the setup. The city I've been hanging around in for the story is in a fight over its public-safety budget. One camp wants more cops, hot-spot patrols, violence-interruption teams — anything aimed at stopping the next killing. The other wants trauma centers, victim services, help for kids who've lost a parent, the whole long tail of people who've already been hurt. And both sides are quietly leaning on the decline story: the optimists to argue we can afford to be generous, the pessimists to argue the trend is fragile and we'd better not.

Now the wrinkle, which I think is the real story. A demographer friend of mine insists the entire decline debate is measuring the wrong variable. Everyone counts violence as a flow — fresh homicides, war deaths, assaults, per hundred thousand per year. She says what actually wrecks people and societies is the stock: the accumulated pile of trauma, disability, bereavement, displacement and fear left behind by violence that already happened, which keeps generating fresh damage for decades. Count the stock instead of the flow, she claims, and the great decline shrinks a lot, maybe disappears. I can't tell if she's onto something or just playing with a metaphor. (And yes, I know per-capita-versus-absolute is where alot of the fight lives, but that one feels argued to death already.)

So: assume the decline-of-violence thesis is roughly true, and take her stock-versus-flow point seriously even if it's not obviously right. What are the most important implications for how that city should split its public-safety money? I want the two or three implications that genuinely move the answer, plus a note on which ones are load-bearing — i.e., if you pulled that piece out, would the recommendation flip?
