I just had a long argument with a friend about whether Humeanism about laws of nature—the view that laws are nothing over and above true regularities—is actually Hume’s own position, or whether that label reads a later metaphysical thesis back into an earlier argument about the origins of our ideas. My friend kept returning to the following passage from Hume’s Enquiry Concerning Human Understanding, Section VII:

“When we look about us towards external objects, and consider the operation of causes, we are never able, in a single instance, to discover any power or necessary connexion; any quality, which binds the effect to the cause, and renders the one an infallible consequence of the other. We only find, that the one does actually, in act, follow the other. The impulse of one billiard-ball is attended with motion in the second. This is the whole that appears to the outward senses. The mind feels no sentiment or inward impression from this succession of objects: consequently, there is not, in any single, particular instance of cause and effect, any thing which can suggest the idea of power or necessary connexion.”

Help me understand exactly what Hume is claiming here. In particular, is his conclusion that there is no necessary connection in nature, or only that we cannot discover or form an idea of one? How does the billiard-ball example bear on that distinction, and what work is done by “in act” and by the final clause about what can “suggest the idea”? I would also like help seeing whether this passage can support the Humean regularity view of laws, or whether it is better read as an epistemic and psychological thesis that leaves the anti-Humean about laws untouched. Please be precise about the distinctions, since my friend and I kept talking past each other.
