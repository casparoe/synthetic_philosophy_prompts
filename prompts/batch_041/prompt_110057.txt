My friend and I burned most of last night arguing about qualia after a seminar on the Dennett piece where he tries to talk everyone out of believing in the things, and we got nowhere because neither of us ever put a concrete case on the table. So here's the ask. The standard package deal is that a quale is supposed to have four features at once: it's ineffable (you can't put it in words), it's intrinsic (the feel is fixed by the state itself and not by whatever relations that state happens to stand in), it's private (only the person who has it has any access to it), and it's known directly by that person (they don't have to infer what their experience is like, they just get it). My friend thinks building a case with all four is trivial. I think the second you try to build one where the experience changes and nothing else does, you either smuggle in a functional difference or you end up unable to say what changed at all. I want a case that settles this.

Build me a scenario where someone's experience and their attitude toward it come apart, and where nothing can tell you which of the two actually changed — not any third-person test, and not the person's own testimony either, even granting them perfect honesty, perfect memory, full articulacy, and no confusion whatsoever about what they do and don't like. Constraints: it has to be something that could plausibly happen to a normal adult in an ordinary week, no brain surgery, no inverted spectra, no zombies, no telepathy, no exotic drugs, and stay away from coffee and wine and food generally, and stay away from colors too — those are the two examples everybody reaches for and I want a fresh one that isn't already sitting in the literature. It also has to break exactly one of those four features and leave the other three standing, and ideally the one it wrecks should be the one that's hardest to give up.

Then do the work on it, and I mean actually do it rather than gesture at it. I want the realist's verdict, meaning what they have to say to keep the case from being a reductio of their whole position; the skeptic's verdict, meaning what someone who thinks the notion is a confused artifact of a certain way of talking says instead; which single feature of your case is doing the work and what happens if I pull that feature out (does it collapse back into a boring old functional change?); and the realist's best rescue that doesn't just stipulate the answer. Give it back to me as exactly five numbered points, a sentence or two each, no preamble and no wrap-up paragraph.
